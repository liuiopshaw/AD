Near-infrared II (NIR-II) imaging at 1100–1700 nm shows great promise 
for medical diagnosis related to blood vessels because it possesses deep 
penetration and high resolution in biological tissue. Unfortunately, currently 
available NIR-II fluorophores exhibit slow excretion and low brightness, 
which prevents their potential medical applications. An atomic-precision gold 
(Au) cluster with 25 gold atoms and 18 peptide ligands is presented. The 
Au25 clusters show emission at 1100–1350 nm and the fluorescence quantum 
yield is significantly increased by metal-atom doping. Bright gold clusters 
can penetrate deep tissue and can be applied in in vivo brain vessel imaging 
and tumor metastasis. Time-resolved brain blood-flow imaging shows 
significant differences between healthy and injured mice with different brain 
diseases in vivo. High-resolution imaging of cancer metastasis allows for the 
identification of  the primary tumor, blood vessel, and lymphatic metastasis. 
In addition, gold clusters with NIR-II fluorescence are used to monitor high-
resolution imaging of kidney  at a depth of 0.61 cm, and the quantitative 
measurement shows 86% of the gold clusters are cleared from body without 
any acute or long-term toxicity at a dose of 100 mg kg−1.

Near-Infrared II (NIR-II) imaging in the region of 1100–1700 nm 
has attracted wide interests due to lower tissue scattering than 
the traditional NIR-I imaging in the region of 750–900 nm.[1] 
The long wavelength in NIR-II with decreased optical scattering 
allows the photon to penetrate the deep biological tissue and 
therefore improves the spatial resolution.[2] To make the NIR-II 
imaging technology feasible, it is crucial to develop desired dyes 
that can afford functional and structural imaging in biology 
and medicine. The inorganic nanomaterials, such as single-
walled carbon nanotubes (CNTs),[3] semiconductor quantum 
dots,[4] rare earth nanoparticles,[1h,5] and small clusters,[6] have

The preparation of gold clusters is based on a reported method 
(Supporting Information).[10] The formation of Au25(SG)18 clus-
ters (SG denotes glutathione) was confirmed by the electrospray 
ionization mass spectrum in our previous work.[10c] Au25(SG)18 
clusters are composed of 13 gold core atoms and six Au2(SG)3 
shell structures, as illustrated in Figure 1a.[9b] As shown in the 
transmission electron microscope (TEM) image and the size 
distribution histogram (Figure 1b,c), the core size of gold clus-

NIR-II dyes, such as nanotubes (>161  nm),[1b] Ag2S quantum 
dots (28.6  nm),[4a] and conjugated polymer (17  nm).[7a] The 
hydrodynamic diameter of gold clusters in fetal bovine serum 
(FBS) was determined by using dynamic light scattering (DLS).

As shown in Figure 1h, the NIR-IIa fluorescence of gold clusters 
under an excitation of 808 nm exhibits good stability in deion-

temperature for a week. The excitation of 808 nm was chosen

and scattering to afford the maximum penetration depth for in 
vivo imaging.[7a] It is noteworthy that the fluorescence of gold 
clusters is enhanced in FBS, which may be ascribed to the inter-
action of proteins and gold clusters.[13] Furthermore, we also 
performed the photo­stability under 808 nm laser radiation with 
an exposure time of 100 ms. The gold clusters show good photo­
stability in PBS and FBS with acceptable decay under contin-
uous excitation for 30 min (Figure S2, Supporting Information). 
These results reveal that gold cluster is an ultrasmall, aqueous 
soluble, and stable NIR-II fluorescent agent, which could be 
very promising for in vivo biological imaging.
To improve the QY of gold clusters, we introduced the surface 
ligand and metal doping methods. As shown in Figure  2b,c, 
ligand effect on NIR-II fluorescence intensity of gold clusters is 
investigated. It is found that gold clusters protected by cysteine 
show the strongest fluorescence among all ligands. For the 
metal doping effect on fluorescence enhancement, Figure 2d–f 
and Figures S3 and S4 in the Supporting Information show that 
all the Cu, Zn, Ag, and Er doping can improve the fluorescence 
and the enhancement effect strongly depends on the species 
and proportion of doping atoms. Especially, Cu-doped clus-

the corresponding QY at the 4% atom doping concentration 
is ≈0.67% under 808 nm excitation by using a standard IR-26 
dye (QY = 0.05%) as a reference sample (Figure S5, Supporting 
Information). Compared with other renal clearable dyes, the 
QY of gold clusters is very close to FD-1080[2b] and IR-ERP

We further investigated the electronic structures of Cu and 
Zn-doped Au25 clusters. As shown in Figure 2g, the pure Au25 
clusters possess the highest occupied molecular orbital–lowest 
unoccupied molecular orbital (HOMO–LUMO) bandgap of 
1.18 eV, assigned to direct optical transition, which is consistent 
with the NIR-II emission center of 1120 nm in Figure 1e and in 
good agreement with the reported findings.[9b,19] LUMO energy 
is occupied by Au 5d from the Au atom, S 3p and S 3s electronic 
states from the ligand. The HOMO can be assigned to Au 6sp, S 
3p, Au 4d, and Au 5d. Due to deep energy of Au d orbital electronic 
states, the Au 4d and 5d electronic states show negligible contri-
butions to direct optical transition and NIR-II emission. Thus, 
the different ligands could induce the difference on S 3p states, 
which could modulate the electronic structure of Au clusters. 
With Cu or Zn doping, the bandgap of Au25 clusters decreases to 
1.11 and 1.08  eV, respectively (Figure  2h,i). In addition, the 
LUMO energy level splits into three sub-energy levels. Compared 
with Au atom, Cu and Zn atoms possess significantly less d 
orbital energy level,[20] and doping can introduce the empty band 
and increase the efficiency of radiative recombination, although 
no obvious energy level shift at HOMO is found. Therefore, the 
NIR-II fluorescence could be optimized by adjusting the ligand 
and metal doping separately, and the two factors are intertwined 
as different metal doping inevitably changes the electron transfer 
between surface ligands and metal cores.[21]

We then used the NIR-IIa imaging to evaluate the brain 
fluorescence imaging in vivo with LPS induced brain injury 
model. LPS is widely used to induce brain inflammation in 
neuroscience.[22] The NIR-IIa brain imaging of LPS injury 
with gold clusters is shown in Figure 3i–l using the same dose 
and injection route as the normal mouse. The arterial vessels 
of the brain show a signal similar to the normal mice after 
0.96 s injection (Figure 3i). The arterial and venous vessels are 
observed after 1.6 s (Figure 3j). However, no significant decay 
in vessel signal is found and NIR-IIa signal in the arterial and 
venous vessels is still strong after 84.8 s (Figure 3l).
The dynamic NIR-IIa fluorescence signal provides a route to 
identify cerebral blood perfusion by measuring average NIR-IIa 
intensity in the region of middle cerebral arteries. Slopes are 
obtained to monitor the blood flow by normalizing NIR-IIa 
signal. The quantitative analysis of blood perfusion shows 
that NIR-IIa signal in normal mice takes 2.7 s to diffuse into 
brain vessel, but it takes 4.6 s in the injured mice (Figure 3m) 
to reach the maximum. The determined blood perfusion is 
0.804 s−1 for the normal mouse and 0.304 s−1 for the injured 
mouse, respectively. Meanwhile, the NIR-IIa signal of the 
normal mouse shows a 92% decrease after 84.8  s compared 
with 1.6 s, but the injured mouse only exhibits a 12% decrease 
of NIR-IIa signal after 84.8 s (Figure 3h). The gold clusters in 
LPS-injured mice show a slower blood perfusion and longer 
retention in brain vessels than the normal mice. LPS has been 
demonstrated to influence the blood perfusion[23] and therefore 
affects the retention of gold clusters in the brain. Biolumines-
cence with luciferase has been used to monitor the brain injury 
after LPS, but the resolution was too low owing to short emis-
sion at 560 nm.[24] The wavelength of NIR-IIa imaging is longer 
than 1300 nm, which improves the spatial resolution and pro-
vides more detailed information for LPS and related injuries.
To further test the use of NIR-IIa imaging in the cerebro-
vascular related diseases, we also performed NIR-IIa in an 
ischemic stroke model. In this model, the left middle cerebral 
artery of stroke mouse was blocked by a microfilament and reo-
pened after 1 h. Brain vessel imaging with gold clusters (200 µL, 
5.3 mg mL−1) in NIR-IIa region was performed by tail intrave-
nous injection. Again, the brain arterial vessels are observed at 
1.6  s after injection, and many small arterial vessels can also 
be seen in the left hemibrain but no new vessels are found in 
the right side (Figure 3n). The intensity of neovascularization in 
the left brain gradually increases with injection time up to 2.56 
(Figure 3o) and 4.48 s (Figure 3p). The signal in the injured left 
brain shows decay at 8 s after injection. The injured left brain 
exhibits more arterial vessels compared with the right brain.
We also used the dynamic NIR-IIa fluorescence imaging to 
analyze blood perfusion by normalizing NIR-IIa signal in the 
arterial vessel of both the left and right brain. Blood perfusion 
rate of the injured left brain is 0.11 s−1, which is two times lower 
than the normal right brain of 0.24  s−1 (Figure  3r). Principal 
component analysis (PCA) is a powerful tool for analyzing time-
related activity, and thus it was applied to time-course images 
over a time-course of ≈30  s with 100 frameworks for distin-
guishing the injured brain and normal brain. Significant red 
signal (red arrow) is seen in the left brain (Figure 3q). The red 
vessels are arterial vessels and the blue vessels are venous ves-
sels. Stroke is a cerebrovascular accident due to a disturbance in 
the blood supply to the brain. Blocking and reopening of brain 
vessels cause brain ischemia and reperfusion injury, which leads 
to neuronal damage, vascular injury, and neovascularization.[25] 
Ultrasmall gold clusters can circulate to the reopened injured 
vessels in the left brain and then allow us to monitor cerebrovas-
cular injury in real-time by dynamic fluorescence imaging. Fur-
thermore, imaging with gold clusters also allows us to moni­tor 
neovascularization of damaged brain tissue after a stroke. In 
summary, NIR-IIa imaging in vivo provides better resolution 
and may be useful for fundamental research of neuroscience.

We further investigated the excretion of urine and feces of gold 
clusters using ICP-MS. The corresponding gold concentrations 
from urine collected after 1 and 2 h are 32.2 and 53.0% ID g−1, 
respectively (Figure 5q). As a contrast, the highest gold concen-
tration in feces is 3.1% ID g−1 after 6 h with slower clearance. 
The cumulative excretion curves show that 86.6% and 2.2% of 
gold clusters are excreted by urine and feces after 48 h, respec-
tively (Figure  5r). The biodistribution measured by ICP-MS 
shows that the kidney is dominant target organ and the 
concentration is very low after 7  d excretion. Meanwhile, the 
uptake of the brain is 0.19% ID g−1 for 1 d, decreasing to 0.09% 
ID g−1 after 7 d (Figure S9b, Supporting Information), which 
suggests excellent excretion from brain. The gold clusters did 
not cause any toxicological response at ultrahigh injection dose, 
such as body weight, biochemistry, hematology, and pathology 
(Figures S9a and S10–S12, Supporting Information), which are 
consistent with the previous work.[27]

and deep tissue penetration (>0.6 cm), which is very beneficial 
to noninvasively monitor the pathological process of kidney 
related diseases in real-time, such as kidney dysfunction.[30] 
Moreover, the NIR-II-emitting gold clusters exhibit low affinity 
to biomacromolecule and rapid clearance from background 
tissues such as skin, allowing for high-contrast fluorescence 
imaging of the tumor metastasis, brain blood vessel and kidney. 
The high signal-to-background ratio of gold clusters is compa-
rable to the recently reported NIR-II fluorophores,[5a,8f,31] which 
is prerequisite to image-guided surgery. In addition, the QYs 
of our gold clusters with atomically precise structure could be 
further improved by surface chemistry and doping approaches 
for potential ultrafast in vivo imaging, two-photon imaging and 
confocal imaging in NIR-II region.
In summary, we developed a water-soluble and photostable 
gold cluster with well-defined structure as an efficient NIR-II 
fluorophore. The long wavelength emission of gold clusters 
allows penetration of the skull and achieves NIR-II cerebral 
blood vessel imaging. Real-time fluorescence imaging analysis 
shows LPS injured mice have slower blood flow and longer 
retention time than that of the healthy ones. Further, gold clus-
ters based NIR-II imaging can monitor many small vessels 
owing to increased permeability of brain vessel after stroke. 
Cancer metastasis imaging shows that the primary tumor, blood 
vessel, and lymphatic metastasis can be identified. Finally, ultr-
asmall gold clusters can cross renal filter and show excellent 
excretion (≈86%) after 48 h without any toxicological effects at 
a high dose. It can be conceived that gold clusters can serve as 
an NIR-II dye in biological imaging and potential medical diag-
nose, especially brain imaging.

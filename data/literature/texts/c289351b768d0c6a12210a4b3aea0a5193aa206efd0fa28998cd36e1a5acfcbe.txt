Ultrasmall silver nanoclusters: Highly eﬃcient
antibacterial activity and their mechanisms
Jian-Cheng Jin,†a,b Xiao-Juan Wu,†a,b Juan Xu,a,b Bei-Bei Wang,a,b
Feng-Lei Jiang*a,b and Yi Liu*a,b
Silver materials have been widely used as antimicrobial agents. Notably, silver nanoparticles have emerged
as a new generation of nanoproducts for biomedical and environmental applications in recent years.
However, ultrasmall silver nanoclusters (NCs) (∼2 nm) have rarely been used to kill bacteria and their anti-
bacterial mechanisms have not yet been fully elucidated. Herein, we studied the antibacterial activities
of bifunctional ﬂuorescent DHLA-AgNCs against three types of bacteria. The results showed that
DHLA-AgNCs exhibited excellent antibacterial activities against Gram-negative E. coli, which could
eﬃciently inhibit the growth of E. coli DH 5α and E. coli DSM 4230 cells at a concentration of 15 and
10 μg mL−1, respectively. Meanwhile AgNCs demonstrated no apparent antibacterial activity against
Gram-positive S. aureus. Then, the antibacterial mechanisms of AgNCs were systematically investigated.
We found that AgNCs aﬀected the growth of diﬀerent E. coli strains in diﬀerent ways. AgNCs inhibited the
growth of E. coli DH 5α mainly through damaging the outer cellular membrane and permeating into the
cells, followed by the antibacterial eﬀect of the internalized AgNCs and released silver ions. AgNCs,
however, inhibited the growth of E. coli DSM 4230 cells mainly through diﬀusing into E. coli DSM 4230
cells and damaging their respiratory chain. These results clearly indicated that diﬀerent bacterial strains
(e.g. diﬀerent E. coli strains) should be taken into consideration in future studies. Our work facilitates
further investigation of the design of new antibacterial silver nanomaterials with diﬀerent sizes.
1.
Introduction
Noble metal (Pt, Au, Ag, Cu) nanoclusters (NCs) are usually
composed of a few to a hundred atoms. Due to their ultrasmall
sizes (<2 nm), which are comparable to the Fermi wavelength
of the conduction electrons, they exhibit discrete energy levels,
resulting in their molecule-like properties.1 Thus, these NCs
possess very distinct properties from large noble metal nano-
particles, attracting a great deal of attention in recent years.2
For instance, they have been widely studied in biolabeling
and bioimaging,3 medicine,4 catalysis,5 and nanoelectronics.6
Particularly, fluorescent silver nanoclusters (AgNCs), a member
of the NC group, could be additionally used as an dual func-
tional antibacterial agent owing to the specific characteristics
of silver and their fluorescent properties.7
Silver has been used to prevent microbial infections for at
least six thousand years. Herodotus mentioned that silver con-
tainers were used to transport drinking water to keep it fresh
in wartime.8 Hippocrates used silver preparations to promote
wound healing when he treated ulcers. Since then, metallic
silver, silver salts and silver nanoparticles have been widely
accepted in medical applications.7 Among them, silver nano-
particles have emerged as a new generation of nanoproducts
for biomedical applications.9 Meanwhile, they still retain their
glory in the field of killing microorganisms because of their
excellent antibacterial activities.
A lot of studies have stated that the size,10 shape,11 surface
coating,12 and surface charge13 of silver nanoparticles could
aﬀect eﬃciently their antibacterial activity. Thus, great eﬀorts
have been made in the controlled or precise synthesis of
silver
nanoparticles.
Recently,
Xiu
and
his
colleagues
suggested that the specific particle characteristic of silver
nanoparticles showed negligible eﬀects on their bactericidal
properties and the antibacterial activity of silver nano-
particles was due to the oxidation of silver by dissolved
oxygen and subsequent release of silver ions.14 However,
their results are apparent that the antibacterial mechanisms
need further investigation. Many researchers have studied the

Herein, we present a systematic study of the antibacterial
activities of biocompatible dihydrolipoic acid stabilized dual
functional silver nanoclusters (DHLA-AgNCs) against Gram-
negative E. coli
(DH 5α, DSM 4230) and Gram-positive
S. aureus. The results showed that DHLA-AgNCs exhibit excel-
lent antibacterial activities against E. coli, which can eﬃciently
inhibit the growth of E. coli DH 5α and E. coli DSM 4230 at a
concentration of 15 and 10 μg mL−1, respectively. However,
they could not inhibit well the growth of S. aureus even though
the concentration of AgNCs reached 50 μg mL−1. The antibac-
terial mechanisms of DHLA-AgNCs against E. coli were then
studied by a variety of techniques including absorption spec-
troscopy, fluorescence spectroscopy, flow cytometry, micro-
calorimetry and microscopy, and the antibacterial mechanisms
were related to the damage of respiratory chain of bacterial
cells and the release of silver ions from the internalized AgNCs
in E. coli cells. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first
systematic study on the antibacterial mechanisms of ultra-
small AgNCs.
2.
Experimental
2.1.
Materials
Sodium borohydride, lipoic acid, silver nitrate, peptone,
yeast extract, 3,8-diamino-5-(3-(diethyl(methyl)ammonio)propyl)-
6-phenylphenanthridin-5-ium iodide (PI), 1,6-diphenyl-1,3,5-
hexatriene (DPH), 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate
(DCFH-DA), and 1,3,5,8-tetramethyl-2,4-bis(alpha-hydroxyethyl)
prophine-6,7-dipropionicacid (HP) were purchased from
Sigma-Aldrich. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), sodium hydro-
xide, thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT) etc. were pur-
chased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. (Shanghai,
China). Ultrapure water with 18.2 MΩ cm (Millipore) was used
in all syntheses.
2.2.
Synthesis of DHLA-AgNCs
DHLA-AgNCs were synthesized according to the method
reported
by
Shang23
with
fine
modification.
In
detail,
0.3040 g of lipoic acid were dissolved in 75 mL of an aqueous
solution containing 0.9 mL of NaOH (2 mol L−1) with vigor-
ous stirring, followed by addition of 0.5 mL of an AgNO3
aqueous solution (0.2 mol L−1). After stirring for 5 min, the
pH value of the mixture was adjusted to 8.0, then 4 mL of
freshly prepared NaBH4 (0.12 mol L−1) was added slowly to
the mixture with rapid stirring. The color of the mixture
changed from dark to red brown gradually and the reaction
was stopped after 5 h. The crude AgNCs was then dialyzed in
deionized water using a 3500 Da membrane for 6 h. Finally,
ultrafiltration was conducted with a 30 kDa membrane for
15 min to remove the remained silver ions and this process
was repeated for 5 times with water washing. The purified
products were freeze-dried and re-suspended in deionized
water for experiments.
2.3.
Characterization of DHLA-AgNCs
UV-vis absorption spectra were measured on a UV-61 OOPC
double-beam spectrophotometer (MAPADA, China) at room
temperature. Fluorescence spectra were taken on a LS-55
Spectrofluorometer (Perkin Elmer, USA). The morphology of
the AgNCs was observed by a JEOL 2100 transmission electron
microscope (JEOL, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV.
The zeta potential of the AgNCs was measured by Nano-ZS
ZEN3600 (Malvern Instruments, UK). X-Ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS) were carried out on an XSAM800 X-ray
photoelectron spectrometer (KRATOS, UK) using Mg as the
exciting source.
2.4.
MTT assay
Human gastric epithelial cell line (GES-1) was obtained from
China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC, Wuhan,
China). GES-1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified
Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine
serum, 1% penicillin and 1% amphotericin at 37 °C in 5%
CO2. The cells were seeded into a 96-well plate and allowed to
adhere for 24 h. After incubation with diﬀerent concentrations
of DHLA-AgNCs for 24 h, MTT with a concentration of 0.5
mg mL−1 was added into each well; the cells were allowed
to grow for 4 h until a purple precipitate was visible. The
medium was then removed and 200 μL dimethyl sulfoxide
was added. The clusters were vibrated for 5 min to dissolve
completely the crystals. Finally, the absorption at 490 nm was
measured by an Elx800 Micropalte reader (BioTek, USA).
2.5.
Precultivation and measurement of minimum inhibitory
concentration (MIC)
Three types of bacteria, namely S. aureus, DH5α (E. coli),
DSM4230 (E. coli) were incubated with sterilized LB medium
containing 5 g of NaCl, 10 g of peptone and 5 g of yeast extract
per liter at a pH value of 7.2 and temperature of 37 °C. A
colony of the bacteria grown on LB agar plates was inoculated

2.6.
The fluidity of the E. coli cell membrane
Bacterial cells were treated with diﬀerent concentrations of
DHLA-AgNCs for 5 h at 37 °C. 6 μL of the fluorescent mem-
brane probe, DPH (2 mM stock solution in tetrahydrofuran),
was added to 3 mL of each cell suspensions and incubated
for 60 min in the dark at room temperature to allow the
fluorescent probe to be incorporated into the cytoplasmic
membrane. The fluidity of the E. coli cell membrane was
characterized by the polarization of DPH. The excitation
and emission wavelengths were 354 and 427 nm, respect-
ively. When probed with HP (7.5 μL, 2 mM stock solution
in ethanol), the incubation time was 5 min, and the exci-
tation and emission wavelengths were 520 and 626 nm,
respectively.
2.7.
The integrity of the outer membrane of E. coli cells
The eﬀects of DHLA-AgNCs on the integrity of outer mem-
brane of E. coli cells were studied by examining their sensitiz-
ation to detergent-mediated bacteriolysis. E. coli cells were
incubated with diﬀerent concentrations of AgNCs for 5 h at
37 °C. Then, the suspension was centrifuged and the super-
natant was discarded. The cell pellets were re-suspended in
the same volume of sterilized PBS buﬀer. Finally, sodium
dodecyl sulfate (SDS) (0.1% final concentration) was added,
and the OD600 was continuously monitored.
A PI uptake assay was also conducted to determine the
integrity of the outer membrane of E. coli cells. The bacterial
cells were treated in the absence or presence of AgNCs in
the dark for 5 h. Then, bacterial cells were collected after
centrifugation and re-suspended in sterilized PBS containing
10 μg mL−1 PI. The bacterial cells were incubated at room
temperature in the dark for 30 min and the levels of
PI incorporation were determined by C6 flow cytometry
(BD, USA).
2.8.
Microscopic investigation
Electron microscopic observation was conducted using a
TECNAI G2 transmission electron microscope (TEM). After the
pretreatment of bacterial cells, E. coli cells were washed with
PBS buﬀer. All ultrathin sections for the TEM observation were
prepared following the standard procedures for the fixing and
embedding of biological samples.
2.9.
Generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) detected by
flow cytometry
The ROS level of E. coli cells were detected by a C6 flow cyto-
metry (BD, USA). After the pretreatment of DHLA-AgNCs, bacterial
cells were washed with PBS buﬀer. The obtained cells were
incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA at room temperature for
30 min in the dark. Then, the mixtures were centrifuged and
the E. coli cells were re-suspended in PBS buﬀer for flow cyto-
metry experiments.
2.10.
Microcalorimetry
A TAM air isothermal microcalorimeter (Thermometric AB,
Sweden), a multichannel microcalorimetric system, was used
to record the heat flow rate of the microbial growth reaction of
these three types of bacteria cells, which was calibrated by the
release of electrical energy in a resistor and the heat eﬀect of
the sample ampoule. All calorimetric experiments were con-
ducted at 37 °C. The details of the experiment were according
to our previous report.24 All microcalorimetric measurements
were performed in sealed glass ampoules containing 5 mL of
LB medium incubated with these three types of bacteria and
diﬀerent concentrations of AgNCs. All experimental pro-
cedures were performed under aseptic conditions.
3.
Results and discussion
3.1.
Characterization of DHLA-AgNCs
During the synthesis process, the color of the reaction solution
changed from dark to red brown gradually, and red fluo-
rescence could be observed under irradiation with a UV lamp,
indicating the formation of AgNCs. The absorption spectrum
of the as-prepared DHLA-AgNCs was shown in Fig. 1a. It shows
three absorption peaks at 328 nm, 426 nm and 497 nm, which
is consistent with the results reported previously.25 The photo-
luminescence (PL) spectroscopy of DHLA-AgNCs demonstrates
three excitation peaks at 370 nm, 425 nm, 500 nm and an
intense emission peak at 630 nm (Fig. 1a). The insets of
Fig. 1a were the photographs of DHLA-AgNCs under ambient
and UV light (365 nm). Bright red fluorescence was observed
under excitation at 365 nm, which demonstrated that the fluo-
rescent AgNCs were successfully prepared. The zeta potential
of the as prepared AgNCs was −28.8 mV, indicating the AgNCs
had good stability.
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to
observe the morphology and size distribution of the AgNCs. As
shown in Fig. 1b, the AgNCs were quasi-spherical and mono-
disperse with a narrow size distribution. The average size of
the AgNCs is 2.0 ± 0.5 nm and the size distribution agrees well
with the Gaussian distribution (Fig. 1c). The average size of
the AgNCs is a little larger than the results reported by
Shang.23 This distinction should be due to the measurement
diﬀerence between TEM and Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)
since high energy electron-beam irradiation could result in the
aggregation of nanoclusters.26

3.3.
Antibacterial activity
The turbidity test is a common method to characterize bac-
terial numbers. Here, the inhibitory eﬀects of DHLA-AgNCs on
Gram-positive and negative bacteria were characterized by
measuring the optical density of a bacteria suspension at
600 nm. As shown in Fig. 3b, the AgNCs have no obvious
inhibitory eﬀects on the growth of S. aureus, but they can
inhibit eﬃciently the growth of E. coli DH 5α and E. coli DSM
4230 within 24 h at the concentration of 15 and 10 μg mL−1,
respectively (Fig. 3c and d). As shown in Table 1, the MIC (the
concentration causing 90% cell death) of AgNCs against
E. coli DSM 4230 cells is 10 μg mL−1, superior to the results
reported previously, indicating the as-prepared DHLA-AgNCs
possess excellent antibacterial properties against Gram-negative
bacteria.

Antibacterial mechanisms

various alterations in the biosynthesis and assembly of other
membrane proteins.32 So, the normal state of the outer mem-
brane is important to cell function. Diﬀerential scanning
calorimetry (DSC),33 electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR),33
X-ray
diﬀraction
(XRD),34,35
nuclear
magnetic
resonance
(NMR),36 and fluorescence polarization37 have been employed
to determine the fluidity of the cytoplasmic membrane in pre-
vious reports. Herein, we employed two commonly used mem-
brane fluorescent probes, DPH and HP, which are associated
with the lipophilic tails of phospholipids,38 the hydrophilic

3.4.2
Eﬀects of AgNCs on the integrity of E. coli. The outer
membrane of Gram-negative bacteria provides the cell with an
eﬀective permeability barrier against external noxious agents,
but it is also a target for antibacterial agents.40 AgNCs firstly
interact with the outer membrane of E. coli cells. Therefore,
the integrity of the E. coli cells should be considered when
investigating the antibacterial mechanisms of the AgNCs. The
integrity of the outer membrane of the E. coli cells can be
determined by bacteriolysis with SDS. The destabilization of
the outer membrane leads to an increase in the permeability
of amphiphilic molecules, which cannot penetrate an intact
outer membrane but can cause a bacteriolytic eﬀect under
these circumstances.41 In detail, E. coli is almost impermeable
to 0.1% SDS. However, 0.1% SDS could easily permeate E. coli
when the outer membrane is damaged by external substances.
Fig. 5 shows the bacteriolysis of E. coli cells by 0.1% SDS in the
presence of AgNCs, as demonstrated by a decrease in absorp-
tion of bacteria at 600 nm. It is obvious that AgNCs do not
have any eﬀect on the permeability of E. coli DSM 4230 cells,
while there is a dramatic increase in the permeability of E. coli
DH 5α cells. It is proposed that the diﬀerent results are caused
by the diﬀerent cell walls; that of the E. coli DSM 4230 cells is
thicker than that of the E. coli DH 5α cells. Therefore, E. coli
DH 5α cells are more sensitive to AgNCs than E. coli DSM 4230
cells.
To confirm these results, PI staining was used to character-
ize the permeability of these two strains of E. coli cells. Normal
cells with an intact membrane are impermeable to PI, while
the damaged cells can be permeable to PI since their mem-
brane is damaged. As shown in Fig. 6b, there is no obvious
change in the fluorescence intensity of PI in E. coli DSM 4230
in the presence of AgNCs. Meanwhile the percentage of PI

3.4.3
Microscopic study. Electron microscopic study is a
direct method to observe the morphological changes. Morones
et al. reported that silver nanoparticles with small sizes
(1–10 nm) could enter into E. coli cells and cause further
damage by possibly interacting with sulfur- and phosphorus-
containing compounds.43 In our previous study, we have also
found that silver nanoparticles with smaller sizes (6.1 ± 0.8 nm
and 7.3 ± 1.0 nm) could enter into E. coli cells after they
damaged the integrity of the outer membrane of the E. coli
cells. However, silver nanoparticles with a size of 23.4 ± 2.8 nm
were not found in the ultrathin section of E. coli cells.27 The
diameter of our prepared ultra-small AgNCs is about 2.0 nm,
so it may also enter into the E. coli cells. To prove this, ultra-
thin sections of E. coli cells treated with AgNCs were con-
ducted. Fig. 7 shows the ultrathin section of the E. coli cells in
the absence or presence of AgNCs. The E. coli cells without the
pretreatment of AgNCs were clearly clean. In contrast, silver
nanoparticles were clearly observed in E. coli DH 5α with the
incubation of 15 μg mL−1 AgNCs and the cell walls were
damaged. The silver nanoparticles should be the aggregates of
AgNCs after they entered into the E. coli cells. Increasing the

outer membrane permeability experiments of these two strains

the cell walls. Many events may occur when AgNCs enter into

3.4.4
Generation of ROS. Many reports stated that ROS and
release of silver ions are the two key factors responsible for the
death of bacteria in the presence of silver nanoparticles.19,44,45
Under aerobic conditions, the Ag0 can inevitably be oxidized to
Ag+, resulting in the release of silver ions. But the amount of
the released silver ions is hard to determine since the AgNCs
both exist in E. coli cells and in suspension. While the ROS
level of E. coli cells could be easily measured by using fluo-
rescent probes. DCFA-DA is the most commonly used probe to
detect the intracellular ROS level. DCFH-DA cannot emit fluo-
rescence itself. Once it penetrates into cells, it can be hydro-
lyzed by enzymes in cells to produce DCFH, which can emit
strong fluorescence after oxidation by ROS. Similar to our pre-
vious reports, only small parts of DCFH-DA could penetrate
E. coli DH 5α because of the cell wall barrier,27 and negligible
DCFH-DA entered into E. coli DSM 4230. The AgNCs induced
an increased ROS level of E. coli DH 5α in a dose-dependent
manner (Fig. 8). With the incubation of 20 μg mL−1 AgNCs,
the ROS level of E. coli DH 5α increased by 19.3%. When
increasing the concentration of AgNCs to 40 μg mL−1, the ROS
level of E. coli DH 5α increases by 36.3%. Compared with the
results of E. coli DH 5α treated with 0.4 mM hydrogen per-
oxide, which induced a dramatic increase (94.3%) in the ROS
generation of E. coli DH 5α, the AgNCs showed minimal eﬀect
on the ROS level of E. coli DH 5α cells. Therefore, ROS is not
an important event for the antibacterial activity of AgNCs
against E. coli DH 5α. However, the detection of the ROS level
of E. coli DSM 4230 cells requires the development of novel
methods and further studies.

3.4.6
Proposed antibacterial mechanisms of AgNCs against
E. coli cells. Combining the results of TEM ultrathin sections
and the integrity of the outer membrane of E. coli cells, we can
conclude that the AgNCs’ inhibition of the growth of E. coli
DH 5α mainly depends on damaging the outer membrane of
E. coli DH 5α and permeating into the cells, while they show
an excellent antibacterial property against E. coli DSM 4230
due to the inhibition of the respiratory chain. The antibacterial
mechanism of AgNCs on E. coli cells was shown in Fig. 10. The
AgNCs inhibit the growth of E. coli cells mainly by the uptake
of AgNCs into E. coli cells via two diﬀerent ways: (1) permeat-
ing into E. coli DH 5α cells by damaging the outer membrane;
and (2) diﬀusing into E. coli DSM 4230 through porin chan-
nels. Then, the internalized AgNCs inhibit the growth of E. coli
cells through three pathways: (1) the release of silver ions; (2)
the released silver ions, and the AgNCs which aggregate into
silver nanoparticles, damage the respiratory chain on the cyto-
plasmic membrane; and (3) interaction of silver ions and silver
nanoparticles with DNA.

Conclusion
We have studied the antibacterial properties of biocompatible
dual functional fluorescent DHLA-AgNCs against Gram-posi-
tive
and
negative
bacteria.
The
results
showed
that
DHLA-AgNCs exhibit excellent antibacterial activities against
Gram-negative E. coli cells, which can eﬃciently inhibit the
growth of E. coli DH 5α, E. coli DSM 4230 at a concentration of
15 and 10 μg mL−1, respectively. In addition, AgNCs aﬀect the
growth of diﬀerent strains of E. coli cells in diﬀerent ways. In
detail, AgNCs inhibit the growth of E. coli DH 5α mainly
through damaging the membrane of the E. coli DH 5α cells
and permeating into the cells, and then the released silver
ions and the internalized silver nanoparticles can interact with
DNA, protein and the respiratory chain. Meanwhile AgNCs
enter into E. coli DSM 4230 cells mainly through diﬀusion and
inhibit the growth of E. coli DSM 4230 cells by damaging the
respiratory chain. This study has demonstrated a new antibac-
terial mechanism of silver nanomaterials against Gram-nega-
tive bacteria. The ultrasmall silver nanoclusters herein will
inspire the future design of new antibacterial materials.

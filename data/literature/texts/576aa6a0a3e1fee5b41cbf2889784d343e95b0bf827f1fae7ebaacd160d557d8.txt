Intracellular carbon monoxide release unveils selective antibacterial
effects
Tengfei Ma1,2†, Shaoqiu Zheng1,2†, Jian Cheng1,2, Guoying Zhang1,2 and Jinming Hu1,2*
ABSTRACT
Carbon monoxide (CO) is recognized as a dif-
fusible
and
biologically
membrane-permeable
gaso-
transmitter. However, the question of whether extracellular
and intracellular CO delivery would yield similar or distinct
biological functions remains unresolved. In this study, utiliz-
ing nonmetallic CO-releasing micelles as a platform for loca-
lized CO delivery, we present evidence suggesting that selective
antibacterial effects against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus)
are exclusively evident upon intracellular CO release, even in
cases of extracellular release with higher CO concentrations
showing no comparable effect. To substantiate this assertion,
we systematically design micellar nanoparticles with varying
sizes, monomer sequences, and shell compositions. Among
these variants, only the micelles taken up by S. aureus and
capable of intracellular CO release exhibit efficient bacteria-
killing properties. We further demonstrate that the selective
bactericidal effect is closely linked to the production of hy-
droxyl radicals after intracellular CO release. Additionally,
intracellular CO release proves to be an efficient treatment for
S. aureus-induced skin abscesses without the need for addi-
tional antibiotics, showcasing synergistic antibacterial and
anti-inflammatory effects. These findings underscore the pi-
votal role of the spatial location of CO release, significantly
enhancing our understanding of the pathophysiological
functions of gasotransmitters.
Keywords: carbon monoxide, intracellular release, antibacterial,
anti-inflammation, cutaneous abscesses
INTRODUCTION
Similar to nitric oxide, carbon monoxide (CO) has gained
recognition as a significant gasotransmitter [1,2]. Consequently,
there has been an increasing focus on exploring the biomedical
applications of CO [3]. Thus far, CO has demonstrated diverse
biological functions, including anti-tumor, anti-inflammatory,
and antibacterial effects when administered through CO gas or
CO-releasing molecules (CORMs) [4–11]. CO has been recog-
nized as a potent antibacterial agent, showing unique advantages
in inhibiting biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance devel-
opment [1]. Despite the rapid development of various CORMs
providing the opportunity for localized CO delivery, it is note-
worthy that the biological outcomes may vary between CO gas
and CORMs in certain cases [12]. For instance, CO gas exhibits
no apparent bactericidal effect [13,14], while conventional
CORMs like metal carbonyls display broad-spectrum anti-
bacterial activities. However, it is crucial to acknowledge that the
antibacterial activity in metal carbonyls may be attributed to the
presence of transition metal ions [4,15,16].
On a different note, it is well-documented that CO is biolo-
gically membrane-permeable, suggesting that the spatial location
of CO release (extracellular vs. intracellular) does not sig-
nificantly impact its biological functions. To investigate this
further, Berreau and coworkers [17] rationally designed mem-
brane-permeable
and
membrane-impermeable
CO
donors.
These donors produced similar anti-inflammatory activities in
mammalian cells, with extracellular CO release found to be less
toxic. This preliminary result suggests that the spatial location of
CO release may indeed influence the biological functions of CO.
However, whether extracellular and intracellular CO release can
produce a similar or different antibacterial effect remains
unknown.
In this study, we have constructed a small library of non-
metallic CO-releasing micelles containing 3-HF moieties within
the cores as potential CO donors and investigated whether the
location of CO release affects its antibacterial activity. To our
surprise, despite releasing comparable CO contents under red
light irradiation, these micellar nanoparticles exhibit strikingly
different antibacterial activities compared to metal carbonyls
and CO gas. The CO-releasing micelles show selective anti-
bacterial activities against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), with
the effectiveness being highly dependent on their sizes, mono-
mer sequences, and shell compositions (Fig. 1a, b). Specifically,
for BP1 micelles with different sizes, small-sized micelles
demonstrate a higher antibacterial effect than larger ones (BP1-S
> BP1-M > BP1-L). Moreover, the antibacterial activity of CO-
releasing micelles is greatly affected by the monomer sequences,
with block copolymer (BP1) showing a much higher bactericidal
effect than random copolymers (RP1) with similar composi-
tions. Furthermore, shells such as poly(oligo(ethylene glycol)
methyl ether methacrylate) (POEGMA; BP1) and poly(2′-acry-
lamidoethyl-β-D-galactopyranoside) (PGal; BP2) show similar
antibacterial activity, while the antibacterial activity diminishes
for the micelles with poly(2-(methylsulfinyl)ethyl acrylate)

Antimicrobial assay
The antibacterial capacity was studied by the standard colony-
forming unit (cfu) counting method, in which S. aureus and
Escherichia coli (E. coli) were used as representative bacterial
strains. Specifically, varying concentrations of micellar nano-
particles (100 μL) were incubated with bacterial phosphate buf-
fer saline (PBS) suspensions (50 μL, 106 cfu mL−1) for 10 min,
followed by illumination for 30 min at 650 nm (26 mW cm−2).
Afterward, 15 μL of bacterial dispersion was spread on a gela-
tinous tryptic soy broth (TSB) agar plate for colony formation.
The bactericidal efficiency was calculated by counting the
number of bacterial colonies.
Observation of bacterial uptake of micellar nanoparticles by
confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)
S. aureus cells in the mid-logarithmic phase of growth were
diluted in PBS to 1 × 106 cfu mL−1. First, the bacterial cells were
incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC, 1 mg mL−1) at
37°C for 30 min. The bacteria were then collected and washed
twice with PBS to remove the dyes in the media. Subsequently,
the bacterial cells were incubated with micellar nanoparticles
(100 μg mL−1) at 37°C for 30 min. The bacteria were again col-
lected and washed twice with PBS to remove the micelles from
the media. Afterward, the cells were resuspended in PBS and
exposed to light irradiation (650 nm, 26 mW cm−2, 30 min) or
not exposed to light irradiation. Following this, the cells were
further incubated at 37°C for 20 min. Images were captured
using a Zeiss LSM 980 confocal laser scanning microscope. The
green and red channels were excited at 488 and 594 nm and
collected at 510–540 (green) and 640–700 nm (red), respectively.
Detection of hydroxyl radical in bacteria by hydroxyphenyl
fluorescein (HPF) probes
S. aureus cells in the mid-logarithmic phase of growth were
diluted in PBS to 1 × 108 cfu mL−1. The bacterial cells were then
treated with micellar nanoparticles (100 μg mL−1), and HPF
probe (5 μM). Then bacteria were incubated for 30 min at 37°C,
and then collected and washed twice with PBS to remove the
micelles and probes outside of the cells. After that, bacteria were
redispersed in PBS and were either exposed or not exposed to
light irradiation (650 nm, 26 mW cm−2). Changes in fluores-
cence intensity were recorded on a microplate reader (Spec-
traMax iD5) with an excitation wavelength of 490 nm and an
emission wavelength of 515 nm.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Synthesis of CO-releasing block copolymers
To mitigate the potential interferences of transition metal ions in
the assessment of CO’s antibacterial activities, metal-free CO
donors were applied [18–21]. 3-Hydroxyflavone (3-HF) deriva-
tives have shown good structural stability and undergone pho-
tolysis
under
ultraviolet
(UV)
or
near-UV
visible
light
irradiation, exhibiting almost quantitative CO release [22–26],
revealing potential anti-inflammatory and anti-tumor activities
[27,28]. In our recent work, we proposed an innovative
approach to selectively activate 3-HF derivatives under red light
irradiation through a photooxygenation mechanism, thereby
minimizing the phototoxicity associated with UV light and
effectively eliminating its adverse effects on bacterial growth
[29].
A meticulously synthesized small library of metal-free CO-
releasing micelles, featuring different hydrophilic coronas, was
employed in our study. Specifically, three block copolymers with
a consistent hydrophobic block of P(3-HF-co-TPP) but different
hydrophilic blocks, including POEGMA block (BP1), PGal
block (BP2), and PMSEA block (BP3) were crafted through
reversible addition–fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) poly-
merization (Fig. S1) [30]. Noteworthy was the incorporation of
3-HF moieties and meso-tetraphenylporphyrine (TPP) photo-
sensitizer into the core of micellar nanoparticles, facilitating CO
release under red light irradiation. In this process, the localized
production of singlet oxygen (1O2) from the TPP photosensitizer
efficiently oxidized proximal 3-HF moieties within the cores of
micellar nanoparticles [29]. This assumption was supported by
nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopic studies (Figs
S2–S4), revealing the conversion of 3-HF moieties to 2-(ben-
zoyloxy)benzoic acid derivatives concomitant with CO release
[26,29].
The chemical structures of BP1, BP2, and BP3 are shown in
Fig. 2. Additionally, block copolymers RP1 (analogous to BP1
but with a random monomer sequence) and C-BP1, featuring
caged 3-HF (CHF) moieties incapable of CO release under red
light irradiation, were synthesized as the controls. These block
copolymers underwent comprehensive characterization through
NMR spectroscopy, size exclusion chromatography (SEC), and
UV-vis spectroscopy (Figs S2–S8), with their structural para-
meters detailed in Table S1.

Consequently, micellar nanoparticles of varying sizes were

absence of irradiation (Fig. 3b).
Initial tests on the antibacterial activity of saturated CO gas
solution (~1 mM) revealed negligible bactericidal effects towards
both S. aureus and E. coli cells (Fig. S10), consistent with pre-
vious reports [13]. Interestingly, while BP1 exhibited no bac-
tericidal effects on E. coli, regardless of the presence of red light
irradiation (Fig. S11), BP1 micelles with different sizes displayed
both concentration- and size-dependent effects on bacterial
killing against S. aureus under red light irradiation. Remarkably,
the antibacterial activity of CO-releasing micelles was sig-
nificantly compromised with increased micellar sizes (Fig. 3c). It
is crucial to mention that the inactivated BP1 micelles (referred
to as i-BP1) with prior red light irradiation and CO release
exhibited no antimicrobial effects, regardless of the micellar sizes
(Fig. S12). Additionally, caged 3-HF moieties with 1O2-produ-
cing capacity but without CO-releasing capacity (C-BP1, Fig. S1)
did not demonstrate antibacterial activity. The bactericidal effect
of C-BP1 at 5 and 10 μg mL−1, wherein the concentrations are
lower than the critical micelle concentration (CMC), was ten-
tatively ascribed to the generation of reactive oxygen species
(ROS) that further killed bacterial pathogens. However, the
bactericidal effect was diminished at higher concentrations
(>CMC) (Figs S13 and S14), which was due to the short half-life
of ROS in situ generated within the core of micellar nano-
particles that cannot efficiently diffuse out from the micellar
nanoparticles [31]. Therefore, the observed bactericidal effects of
BP1 micelles should be solely attributed to the red light-trig-
gered CO release.
Bacterial cytometry data suggested that these BP1 micelles
demonstrated decreased bacterial uptake with increased micellar
sizes (Fig. S15). CLSM studies revealed that BP1-S micelles were
readily internalized by S. aureus cells, while BP1-EL micelles
primarily resided outside the S. aureus cells (Fig. 3d). Notably,
the red light-mediated CO release from both BP1-S and BP1-L
micelles permeated bacterial membranes, as evidenced by the
increased fluorescence of CO-specific probes within S. aureus
cells (Fig. S16). However, it was somewhat counterintuitive, as
only BP1-S micelles exhibited bactericidal effects against S.
aureus bacteria (Fig. 3c). This observation suggested that the

To further validate the size-dependent antibacterial activity of
CO-releasing micellar nanoparticles, BP1-S, BP1-M, BP1-L, and
BP1-EL micelles were diluted to 5 μg mL−1, a concentration
below their CMC (~22 μg mL−1, Table S1), and stirred for 72 h
at room temperature to ensure the disassembly of micellar
nanoparticles. All resulting unimers exhibited identical anti-
bacterial activities (Fig. 3e, f). We inferred that large-sized
micelles hindered their bacterial uptake, leading to decreased
antibacterial activities. In exploring the generality of size-
dependent antibacterial behavior of CO-releasing micelles, BP2
copolymers were self-assembled into micellar nanoparticles with
different sizes. Remarkably, while the CO release behavior was
unaffected by micelle sizes, the antibacterial activity significantly
decreased upon increasing micellar sizes (Figs S17 and S18).
Based on these results, we concluded that the antibacterial
activity of CO was highly dependent on its release location, with
intracellular CO release exclusively exerting a selective bacter-
icidal effect.
Sequence-dependent antibacterial activities of CO-releasing
micelles
In addition to micellar sizes, the monomer sequences within
copolymer chains can significantly influence the self-assembly
behavior and subsequent antibacterial activity of CO-releasing
micelles. Specifically, we synthesized RP1, a random copolymer
with compositions comparable to BP1 (Fig. S1). We postulated
that the different monomer sequences of BP1 and RP1 might
lead to distinct self-assembly behavior in aqueous solutions. For
example, BP1, unable to be directly dissolved in aqueous solu-
tion, self-assembled into micellar nanoparticles with a diameter
of ~51 nm (BP1-S, Fig. 3a). In contrast, random copolymers of
RP1 showed increased water solubility, forming micellar nano-
particles with a diameter of ~23 nm by direct dispersion in
aqueous media (Fig. 4a). TEM studies confirmed the formation
of uniform spherical micellar nanoparticles (Fig. S19a). Notably,
RP1 micelles showed similar CO release performance to BP1
micelles due to their comparable chemical compositions (Fig. 4b
and Fig. S19b). Considering that RP1 micelles exhibited smaller
sizes than BP1-S micelles (23 nm vs. 51 nm), we hypothesized
that RP1 micelles could be more readily internalized by bacteria,
resulting in better antibacterial activity. However, RP1 micelles
did not exhibit any bactericidal effects with or without red light
irradiation at all tested concentrations (Fig. 4c), whereas BP1-S
demonstrated excellent bactericidal effects at a very low con-
centration of 5 μg mL−1 (Fig. 3e).
To comprehend the distinct antibacterial behavior of BP1 and
RP1, we incubated both BP1-S and RP1 micelles with S. aureus
bacteria. Despite RP1 micelles having smaller sizes than BP1-S
micelles, BP1-S micelles exhibited significantly higher bacterial
uptake (Fig. S20). This result was further corroborated by bac-
terial cytometry analysis, suggesting negligible bacterial uptake
after incubation with RP1 micelles (Fig. S21). Consequently, we
inferred that the compromised antibacterial activity of RP1
micelles was correlated with their poor bacterial uptake, aligning
with the observation that only intracellular CO release exhibited
antibacterial activities (Fig. 3).
To gain further insights into the distinct antibacterial activities
of BP1 and RP1, both possessing comparable compositions but
different
monomer
sequences,
coarse-grained
molecular
dynamics (CGMD) was performed to study the structural
properties of BP1 and RP1 micellar nanoparticles and their
interactions with bacterial membranes (Figs S22 and S23 and
Tables S2–S13). The self-assembly processes of BP1 and RP1
copolymers were initially simulated, with the force field of the
synthesized polymeric molecules parameterized according to the
MARTINI rules [32]. For BP1 or RP1, hydrophobic effects
induced phase separation, resulting in the formation of micellar
nanoparticles, with the hydrophobic monomers being buried in
the core of micelles and the hydrophilic segments lying at the
outer of the micellar nanoparticles. In the case of BP1, hydro-
philic POEGMA segments were concentrated and unevenly
distributed on the surface of micellar particles, whereas they
were more evenly distributed for RP1 (Fig. 4d). This structural
discrepancy between BP1 and RP1 micelles was attributed to
their different monomer sequences within the copolymer chains.
Subsequently, we explored the interactions between BP1 and
RP1 micelles and bacterial membranes. A mixed bilayer with a
ratio of CL:PG = 42:58 served as the model of bacterial mem-
branes, with lipids modeled using the MARTINI v2.2 force field
[33]. BP1 and RP1 micelles were placed onto the bilayer
membrane with a distance of ~0.5 nm to avoid strong interac-
tions at the beginning of simulations. BP1 micelles loaded onto
the membranes shortly (~0.01 μs) after the simulation started,
strongly adhering to the bilayer membranes. This contact
exposed the hydrophobic core at the interface, with the hydro-
phobic segments inserted into the inner layer of membranes
(Fig. 4e, Video S1). Consequently, the membranes were strongly
negatively curved and deformed [34]. Notably, limited by the
simulation time, no uptake event was observed. In contrast, no
direct contact between RP1 micelles and the bacterial mem-
branes was observed in 1.25 μs MD simulations (Fig. 4f, Video
S2). These results suggested the interaction between micellar
nanoparticles and bacteria can be significantly affected by the
monomer sequences, supporting the bacterial internalization of
BP1 rather than RP1 micelles, consistent with the observed
bactericidal effect of BP1 micelles with intracellular CO release.
Shell-dependent antibacterial activity of CO-releasing micelles
In light of the pivotal role played by intracellular uptake in the
antibacterial properties of CO-releasing micelles, we extended
our investigation to examine the influence of micellar nano-
particles with diverse hydrophilic coronas on their antibacterial
efficacy. BP1-BP3 block copolymers were self-assembled into
spherical micelles with comparable sizes (~93–98 nm in dia-
meter, Fig. 5a and Fig. S24). Their sizes remained relatively
unchanged after red light irradiation, and these micelles released
similar amounts of CO under irradiation (Fig. 5a, b). Specifi-
cally, at a concentration of 5 μg mL−1, BP1 and BP2 exhibited
bacterial killing rates exceeding 98% and over 99%, respectively,
against S. aureus bacteria (Fig. 5c, d). However, BP3, featuring
PMSEA shells, displayed no discernible antibacterial effects even
at concentrations up to 100 μg mL−1 under identical irradiation
conditions (Fig. 5e). The inactivated BP1, BP2, and BP3 micelles
(referred to as i-BP1, i-BP2, and i-BP3, respectively) with prior
light irradiation and CO release demonstrated no antimicrobial
effects (Fig. 5f).
The selective bacterial killing of BP1 and BP2 micelles was
further corroborated by the LIVE/DEAD assay (Fig. S25).
Remarkably, despite the comparable sizes of BP1, BP2, and BP3
micelles, they exhibited distinct bacterial uptake capacities, fol-

aureus bacteria. This peculiar phenomenon has not been
observed for CO gas and metal carbonyl-based CORMs.
Proposed antibacterial mechanisms of CO-releasing micelles
It has been well-documented that CO gas permeates biological
membranes [17]. Consequently, it was initially intuitive to pre-
sume that the location of CO release should not influence the
antibacterial activity of CO. The unique size-, sequence-, and
shell-dependent antibacterial activities of CO-releasing micelles
prompted us to delve deeper into the antibacterial mechanism.
The CO release from nonmetallic micellar nanoparticles did not
significantly induce increased permeability of bacterial mem-
branes, as evidenced by the 1-N-phenylnaphthylamine (NPN)
assay, and protein and nucleic acid leakage assays (Figs S29 and
S30). The integrity of bacterial membranes was observed
through both scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and TEM
studies, with collapsed bacterial membranes noted in the groups
treated with BP1 and BP2 under red light irradiation (Fig. S31).
Moreover, ATP assays revealed that only BP1 and BP2 micelles,
when subjected to red light irradiation, led to increased ATP
concentrations,
whereas
no
ATP
content
increases
were
observed for BP3 and RP1 groups (Fig. S32), in agreement with
the observed antibacterial activities of BP1 and BP2 micelles
after red light irradiation.

In previous studies with metal carbonyls, such as CORM-2,
the generation of hydroxyl radicals (·OH) within bacteria was
observed [35]. Consistently, we observed increased levels of
ROS, particularly ·OH, within S. aureus cells after treatment with
BP1 and BP2 micelles under red light irradiation (Fig. S33).
Utilizing HPF as an ·OH-specific probe, it was noted that BP1-
and BP2-treated bacteria exhibited elevated ·OH levels under red
light irradiation (Fig. 6a). To further substantiate that the
intracellular CO release killing S. aureus was linked to the
intracellular production of ·OH, we used three ·OH scavenging
agents including salicylic acid, 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide
(DMPO), and isopropanol (IPA) to investigate whether the
presence of these agents could mitigate the antibacterial activity
of CO-releasing micelles [36–38]. Using BP1 as an example,
while the presence of these agents (100 μM) did not affect CO
release profiles and bacterial viability, the antibacterial activity of
BP1 micelles was diminished in the presence of these ·OH-
scavenging agents (Fig. 6b, c and Fig. S34). These findings
robustly affirmed that the antibacterial activity of CO-releasing
micelles was linked to ·OH generation after intracellular CO
release, and the toxicity of CO could be alleviated through the
addition of ·OH-scavenging agents.
To delve deeper into the understanding of the underlying
mechanism of CO-releasing micelles, we conducted RNA
sequencing on S. aureus post-treatment with BP1 and BP2
micelles (Fig. 6d and Figs S35, S36). Evidently, genes associated
with bacterial DNA repairs, Such as recA [35], alongside those
responsible for safeguarding DNA from the impact of oxidative
stress (e.g., dps) [39], and those governing oxidative stress
homeostasis (e.g., zurA) experienced a significant decrease fol-
lowing treatment with BP1 and BP2 micelles (Fig. 6e–g) [40]. In
addition, genes regulating the availability of free Fe2+ (e.g., fur)
[41], and the YHB1 gene associated with superoxide accumula-
tion and enhanced oxidative stress [42], demonstrated pro-
nounced changes compared to the PBS-treated groups (Fig. 6h
and Fig. S37) [43].
Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and
Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses revealed that S.
aureus treated with BP1 and BP2 micelles exhibited a significant
down-regulation in pathways related to DNA repair, such as the
SOS response and DNA replication, and pathways governing the
regulation of free Fe2+, including ferroxidase activity. Simulta-
neously, pathways related to the reduction of Fe3+ ions and the
conversion of O2
•−into H2O2 exhibited notable up-regulation
(Figs S38 and S39). These results suggested that following BP1
and BP2 treatments, intracellular CO release instigated an
augmentation of free Fe2+ ions and peroxides, thereby enhancing
the Fenton reaction and intensifying the oxidative stress. Col-
lectively, the internal DNA repair of bacteria was weakened,
leading to a reduced capacity to resist and regulate oxidative
stress homeostasis, and ultimately to bacterial death. Expanding
upon the aforementioned findings, we envisioned that intracel-
lular CO release may target metal-centered proteins and impact
the respiratory chain [44], leading to the accumulation of ROS
such as ·OH. These species, in turn, disrupted the intracellular
redox homeostasis, culminating in the demise of bacterial
pathogens (Fig. 6i).

ABSTRACT: Metal particle size has a significant influence on the
activity of nanozymes, yet size-dependent nanozymatic catalysis under
the same metal loadings in full scale (from single atom, cluster to
nanoparticle) is a challenging task and has been rarely reported.
Herein, porous SiO2 nanoflower surface-confined Pt-based nanozymes
with the same metal loadings but different particle sizes were rationally
designed and synthesized for antibacterial and cascade catalytic tumor
therapy. The particle size−activity relationship is well established,
presenting a volcanic curve, and the PtNC/SiO2 nanocluster (NC)
nanozyme exhibits optimized peroxidase-like activity and bactericidal
efficacy compared with SAzyme and nanozyme. Furthermore, a
synergistic therapeutic platform (Lap@PtNC/SiO2) is constructed
through the adsorption of the prodrug β-lapachone (Lap), achieving high-efficiency cascade enzymatic catalysis for tumor
therapy thereby. The cluster nanozymes not only present optimized activity during the full-size engineering but also
demonstrate great potential in next-generation antibacterial and tumor catalytic therapy.
KEYWORDS: atomic layer deposition, size-dependent nanozymatic catalysis, peroxidase-like activity, volcano-type relationship,
cascade catalytic tumor therapy
■INTRODUCTION
Nanozymes, as artificial nanomaterials with intrinsic enzyme-
like characteristics based on catalytic chemistry, have drawn
increasing attention due to their tunable structure and
activity,1−6 low cost, and high stability. These properties have
been widely utilized in various fields such as catalytic
therapy,7−12 analytical testing,13−15 and environmental treat-
ment.16,17 Particularly, nanozymes that comprise active metals
are highly sought after because of their tunable microstructure,
such as particle size, allowing for targeted optimization of
enzyme-like catalytic performance.18−25 Factually, size-depend-
ent catalysis has been widely investigated in the thermo/photo/
electrocatalysis field.26−29 Nevertheless, size-dependent nano-
zymatic catalysis across the full size range (from SA, NC to NP,
Figure 1a) and its application in nanocatalytic medicine have
been rarely reported.
There are only limited reports, to date, on the particle size
regulation of nanozymes centered on the nanoscale (generally
>1 nm),30−34 and still, the obtained conclusions are not fully
consistent due to the different synthesis methods, metal
loadings, and supports (e.g., reducible/unreducible oxide
supports). The overall conclusion from the literature is that
nanozymes with smaller/suitable particle sizes present higher
enzyme-mimicking activity.35,36 It is generally considered that a
decreased size of NPs leads to a significantly increased number
of exposed surface atoms/active sites, along with changes in
surface defects and electronic structure, finally resulting in
boosted enzyme-mimicking activity. The limit of nanozyme is
the so-called SAzyme, with maximized atom utilization
efficiency and an atomically dispersed active site, first reported
in 2019.37 Numerous studies on SAzymes have been reported
until now, and unique enzyme-mimicking activities and
biomedical applications have also been achieved.38−47 The
questions then become whether SAzyme is the most catalytically
active and whether larger-size cluster or NP nanozymes will

enzyme-mimicking activity of metal SAs, clusters and NPs, to
the best of our knowledge, has not been reported (Figure 1b).
The main challenge lies in the rational design and controllable
synthesis of full-sized metal nanozymes with the same metal
loading and unreducible supports.
To unravel the size-dependent structure-performance rela-
tionships of metal nanozymes, herein we designed and
synthesized a series of unreducible porous SiO2 surface-confined
Pt-based nanozymes with the same Pt loading but different
particle sizes ranging from SAs, and clusters to nanoparticles
(NP), through a combined atomic layer deposition (ALD) and
hydrogen activation strategy. Compared with the PtSA/SiO2
SAzyme and PtNP/SiO2 nanozymes, notably, the 1.84-nm-
cluster PtNC/SiO2 nanozymes exhibit optimized peroxidase-like
activity, presenting a distinct size-dependent volcanic trend
(Figure 1c). Multiple characterization techniques, including
high-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron
microscopy (HAADF-STEM), X-ray absorption spectroscopy
(XAS), and density functional theory (DFT) calculations, were
adopted to unravel the size-dependent structure-performance
relationship and nanozymatic catalytic mechanism. Further in
vitro antimicrobial experiments confirmed the size-dependent
peroxidase-like activity of Pt/SiO2 nanozymes, with PtNC/SiO2
clusters showing the optimized antibacterial performance,
followed by PtNP/SiO2 single atoms and PtSA/SiO2. To address
the potential issue of insufficient H2O2 concentration during
catalytic therapy, we designed a cascade catalytic therapeutic
system where the β-lapachone (Lap) prodrug was loaded into
the surficial mesoporous channels of SiO2 obtaining the desired
Lap@PtNC/SiO2. Both in vitro and in vivo tests demonstrate
that the constructed Lap@PtNC/SiO2 can be used as a high-
efficiency nanozyme for catalytic therapy to efficiently kill cancer
cells. This work provides a meaningful example of size-
dependent nanozymatic catalysis, and the further designed
cascade catalytic therapeutic system affords great potential for
next-generation antibacterial and tumor catalytic therapy.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Nanozyme Synthesis and Characterization
Amino-functionalized porous SiO2 nanoflowers were synthe-
sized by solvothermal synthesis (Figure S1) and subsequent
amino-grafting methods, which were used as supports to achieve
PtSA/SiO2 SAzymes (0.35 wt %) by well-controlled Pt ALD
process48−50 (Figure 2a−c). From the typical HAADF-STEM
image of PtSA/SiO2 SAzymes (Figure 2c), isolated SAs,
highlighted with red circles, can be clearly observed without
any observable NCs or NPs. The SAzymes were then treated
under a hydrogen atmosphere to modulate the particle
composition and size (Table S1). When the SAzymes were
activated at a low temperature of 200 °C, the obtained PtSA+AC/
SiO2 nanozymes with sporadic atomic clusters (ACs, high-
lighted with squares) composed of several Pt atoms with a mean
size of 0.36 nm could be observed besides the main species of
SAs (Figure 2d and 2i). With a further increase in activation
temperature, more number of NCs with a mean size of 0.71 nm
appeared (300 °C, Figure 2e and 2i), while Pt SAs still remained
dominant in number (ca. 63.7%, Figure 2i). Nevertheless, this
case begins to reverse for the PtSA+NC/SiO2 nanozyme, where the
number of Pt SAs decreased to 32.9%, and the NC size increased
to 1.45 nm (Figure 2f and 2i). As the activation temperature
reached 500 °C, Pt SAs on the SiO2 nanoflowers disappeared
completely, leaving only uniformly distributed Pt nanoclusters
(PtNC/SiO2, Figure 2g), as confirmed by the characterization of
energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy mapping (Figure
2j). Notably, Pt NPs with a mean particle size of 2.3 nm were
obtained under an activation temperature of 600 °C, and the
(111) crystal plane of the Pt species with a lattice spacing of 0.23
nm could be clearly observed (Figure 2h and 2i). The above
results show that we have achieved precise modulation of Pt size
and composition by a well-controlled hydrotreatment method
while maintaining the unchanged Pt content and support
properties (Figure S2). This provides a solid base for
investigating the size-dependent peroxidase-like activity of Pt
nanozymes with the same loadings.

characterized by multiple techniques. The N2 adsorption−

Evaluation of Peroxidase-Like Activity and DFT
Calculations
The peroxidase-like activity of Pt-based nanozymes with
different particle sizes was assessed in detail in various H2O2−
substrate reaction systems, including 3,3‘,5,5′-tetramethylbenzi-
dine (TMB), o-phenylenediamine (OPD), and methylene blue
(MB). For the typical H2O2−TMB reaction system, the time-
dependent curves of the nanozymes in H2O2−TMB reaction
system were evaluated (Figures 4a and S9). It can be seen that
with increased reaction time, the characteristic absorbance peak
gradually increases (Figure S10). Note that the bare SiO2 is
almost inactive, i.e., the Pt particles are the truly catalytically
active species. Impressively, the peroxidase-like activity is closely
related to the particle size of Pt, and a volcano-shaped
relationship is obtained (Figure 4b). While the activity increases
remarkably with increased particle size, the maximized activity is
achieved when catalyzed by PtNC/SiO2 cluster nanozyme (1.84
nm). Further increasing the particle size to 2.3 nm results in
decreased activity, strongly demonstrating the size-dependent
nanozymatic catalytic property. Furthermore, the peroxidase-
like activity of the nanozymes with different sizes was detected in
the H2O2−OPD, H2O2−TA, and H2O2−MB reaction systems,
and again the PtNC/SiO2 nanozyme presents the optimized
peroxidase-like activity (Figures 4c and S11−S14), demonstrat-
ing the universality of the nanozymes and the reliability of the
obtained volcano-shaped relationships.

For the optimized PtNC/SiO2 nanozyme, the peroxidase-like
activity was further evaluated by analyzing its steady-state
kinetics (Figures 4d and S15). The calculated maximum
reaction rate (Vmax) and Michaelis−Menten constant (Km)
determined by varying H2O2 concentrations, are 2.51 × 10−7 M
s−1 and 2.68 mM, respectively, and the higher Vmax and lower Km
account for its excellent catalytic efficiency. Notably, the PtNC/
SiO2 nanozyme not only exhibits excellent catalytic efficiency
but also maintains structural stability during the catalytic
reaction and in physiological environments (Figure S16). To
further evaluate the peroxidase-like activity of Pt-based nano-
zymes with different particle sizes, the specific activity value (U
per milligram) of the four representative Pt-based nanozymes
(SA, SA + SNC, NC, and NP) was quantitatively evaluated by
measuring the absorption intensity of the colorimetric reaction.
As shown in Figure 4e, the PtNC/SiO2 nanozyme again exhibits
much higher catalytic activity (50.05 U mg−1) than PtNP/SiO2
(37.36 U mg−1), PtSA+NC/SiO2 (26.83 U mg−1), and PtSA/SiO2
(15.98 U mg−1). The optimized peroxidase-like activity of PtNC/
SiO2 nanozyme can be ascribed to the high generation capability
of active ·OH (Figure 4f), contributing to the boosted activity.
Meanwhile, its peroxidase-like activity exhibits pH dependence
(Figure S17).
To further gain deep insights into the size-dependent catalytic
mechanism, DFT calculations were performed. The free
energies of H2O2 decomposition catalyzed by PtSA/SiO2 (Pt1/
SiO2), PtSA+AC/SiO2 (Pt1+4/SiO2), and PtNC/SiO2 (Pt13/SiO2)
are shown in Figures 4g and S18−S19. H2O2 molecules are
initially adsorbed on platinum to form activated *H2O2
peroxide, which undergoes a homogeneous reaction to form
two *OH(*H2O2 →2*OH). The energy required for the
reaction of PtSA/SiO2, PtSA+AC/SiO2, and PtNC/SiO2 in this step
is calculated to be −0.49, −0.84, and −1.17 eV, respectively,
indicating the most favorable dissociation capability of PtNC/
SiO2 toward H2O2. In addition, the diagram shows that the
generation steps of *OH on PtSA/SiO2 and PtSA+NC/SiO2 are
both uphill processes (rate-determining step, RDS), needing to
overcome higher barriers of 1.5 and 0.8 eV, respectively. In
contrast, the energy barrier in the RDS is only 0.44 eV for PtNC/
SiO2, which well accounts for the much higher ·OH generation
capability and optimized activity of PtNC/SiO2 (Figure 4g). We
then calculated the density of states (DOS) of the RDS over the
three nanozymes (Figure 4h). The calculated d-band center
value of PtNC/SiO2 is −1.26 eV, which is closer to the Fermi level
than that of PtSA/SiO2 (−1.85 eV) and PtSA+AC/SiO2 (−1.43
eV), indicating the stronger interaction between *OH and
PtNC/SiO2 (Figure S20a). This results in the obviously
enhanced redistribution of the charge density of *OH
intermediates on the PtSA/SiO2, PtSA+AC/SiO2, and PtNC/SiO2,
and efficiently enhances the interactions, which can be further
proved by the differential charge distribution diagram (Figure
S20b-d). The *OH molecule shows a significant increase in
electron density, while the electron density of the Pt is reduced.
The stronger electron transfer from PtNC/SiO2 nanozyme to the
adsorbed *OH molecule stabilizes the *OH intermediate and
lowers its formation energy barrier, thereby enhancing the
enzymatic activity.
Antibacterial and Antitumor Properties of Pt/SiO2
Nanozymes In Vitro
Based on the volcano-type peroxidase activity of Pt/SiO2
nanozymes and corresponding DFT calculations, their inhib-
itory effects on bacteria and tumors were evaluated. First, the
antibacterial efficacy against two representative bacterial strains
was investigated: the Gram-negative bacterium E. coli (Figure
5a) and the Gram-positive bacterium S. aureus (Figure S22). In
the presence of H2O2 (1 mM), PtNC/SiO2 demonstrated
optimal antibacterial performance against both bacteria,
achieving complete growth inhibition (100%) after 30 min of
coincubation. Under the same conditions, PtNP/SiO2 exhibited
inhibition rates of 86.8% for E. coli (Figure S21) and 88% for S.
aureus (Figure S23), while PtSA/SiO2 showed the lowest
antibacterial activity (E. coli: 43.4%, S. aureus: 59.8%). This
result can also be demonstrated by the morphological changes of
bacteria (Figure 5b); the structural integrity of both E. coli and S.
aureus was severely compromised after treatment with PtNC/
SiO2. Additionally, live/dead bacterial staining further con-
firmed the antibacterial performance of Pt/SiO2 nanozymes
(Figure 5c), i.e., the proportion of dead bacteria (stained red) in
the PtNC/SiO2 group was higher than that in both PtNP/SiO2 and
PtSA/SiO2 groups. These results again indicate a volcano-shape
trend in antibacterial experiments, with PtNC/SiO2 exhibiting
optimal activity, while it requires a certain concentration of
H2O2 (1 mM) to trigger the reaction. Although the
concentration of H2O2 in the tumor area is higher than that in
normal tissues, it is not sufficient to perform the chemical
reaction efficiently. In order to increase the level of H2O2, we
introduced a special H2O2-producing agent, β-lapachone (Lap),
which can produce hydrogen peroxide after entering cancer
cells. Under the action of overexpressed quinone oxidoreductase
(NAD(P)H Quinone Dehydrogenase 1: NQO1) and intra-
cellular oxygen molecules, redox cycles.52−56 Notably, NQO1 is
overexpressed in different cancer cells, allowing for specific
cascade catalysis therapy without harming normal cells that
present low NQO1 expression.
The abundant pore structure of SiO2 also provides a
prerequisite for the adsorption and loading of Lap (denoted
Lap@PtNC/SiO2, 1.4 wt %, Figures S24 and S25). PtNC/SiO2 can
still maintain similar peroxidase-like activity before and after Lap
loading (Figure S26a). The concentration of H2O2 is closely
related to the release of Lap, and the influence of different pH
levels on Lap release from Lap@PtNC/SiO2 at 37 °C was also
investigated (Figure S26b). The results show that Lap exhibits a
high release amount and high release rate under acidic
conditions. This is due to the increased solubility of Lap in an
acidic environment, where the interaction between the
phospholipid hydrophobic layer and Lap is reduced, which is
beneficial for the effective release of Lap in cancer cells. The
cytotoxicity of Lap@PtNC/SiO2 was subsequently examined in
NQO1-overexpressing cancer cell lines after the cascade release
properties of Lap@PtNC/SiO2 were confirmed (CT-26, Figure
5d). Lap@PtNC/SiO2 exhibits a concentration-dependent
cytotoxic effect and significantly enhanced antitumor efficacy
on CT-26 tumor cells, with a cell inhibition rate exceeding 50%
at a concentration of 18 μg/mL. Meanwhile, the L929 and
AML12 normal cells maintain high cell viability (survival rate
>80%, Figure S27) after treatment of Lap@PtNC/SiO2 due to
the fact that these normal cells do not highly express NQO1,
suggesting minimal cytotoxicity of Lap@PtNC/SiO2 toward
normal cells. Furthermore, the therapeutic efficacy of Lap@
PtNC/SiO2 was further evaluated via live/dead staining (Figure
S28). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images
reveal that treatment with Lap@PtNC/SiO2 results in near-
complete cell death in CT-26 cells compared to the free PtNC/
SiO2 and Lap groups (Figure 5e).

Cascade Enzyme Catalytic Therapy of Lap@PtNC/SiO2
Nanozymes In Vivo
On the basis of the remarkable enzyme-like activity of Lap@
PtNC/SiO2 at the cellular level, its therapeutic efficacy by
intratumoral injection into tumor-bearing mice is further
evaluated (Figure 6a). The hemolysis results of mouse
erythrocytes demonstrate that Lap@PtNC/SiO2 presents good
in vivo biocompatibility since the hemolysis rate remains low
across a wide range of Lap@PtNC/SiO2 concentrations (Figure
6b). Over the next 16-day treatment period, the treatment in
mice is thoroughly documented and assessed through measure-
ments of tumor volume, tumor weight, and imaging analysis
(Figure 6c−e). The relative tumor volume growth curve shows
rapid growth in the control group, whereas neither Lap nor
PtNC/SiO2 alone achieves an effective therapeutic outcome
(Figure 6d). In contrast, tumor growth is significantly
suppressed in the Lap@PtNC/SiO2-treated group, and this
result is further supported by the tumor weight measured on day
16 (Figure 6e), which can be attributed to the cascade enzymatic
catalysis triggered by Lap@PtNC/SiO2 in NQO1-high-express-
ing CT26 cells. Note that no obvious body weight changes are
observed in any of the groups throughout the treatment period
(Figure 6f), and no noticeable nanozyme residues, tissue
damage, or pathological side effects are found in the major
organs of each group after different treatments, based on H&E
staining (Figures S32−S33). Meanwhile, the absence of
hematological or biochemical abnormalities throughout the
observation period points to normal organ function and suggests
no underlying chronic diseases (Figure S34). All of the above
results fully corroborate that Lap@PtNC/SiO2 has excellent
biosafety and low systemic toxicity in the upcoming clinical
trials. The therapeutic efficacy is further evaluated through
histological tissue sections. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)
staining of tumor sections reveals the most extensive necrosis in
the Lap@PtNC/SiO2 group (Figure 6g). Protein phosphatase 1
(Ki67) staining images of tumor slices reflect a pronounced
reduction in tumor proliferation following Lap or PtNC/SiO2
treatment, with expression being nearly abolished in the Lap@
PtNC/SiO2 group compared with the control group. TUNEL
analysis reveals that the level of apoptotic cells in the tumor
tissue of the Lap@PtNC/SiO2 group is significantly elevated,
markedly exceeding that of the other three groups (Figure S35).
To verify that the therapeutic effect of the Lap@PtNC/SiO2
group originates from NQO1-mediated cascade enzyme
catalysis, we further examined the expression level of the
NQO1 protein in tumor tissues after treatment (Figure S36).
The results show that the NQO1 expression level in tumor
tissues of the Lap@PtNC/SiO2 group with cascade catalytic
capability is significantly higher than that of the PtNC/SiO2
group, which induces only ROS production. These findings
demonstrate that Lap@PtNC/SiO2 has an optimized antitumor
effect, and the cascade catalytic therapy presents significant
therapeutic efficacy against tumors.
Based on the above outcomes, both in vitro and in vivo, we
propose that Lap@PtNC/SiO2 can effectively eliminate tumor
cells through a cascade enzyme-catalyzed therapeutic mecha-
nism (Figure 6h). Specifically, Lap@PtNC/SiO2 releases the
therapeutic agent Lap under the acidic tumor microenviron-
ment, which triggers a self-sustaining redox cycle catalyzed by
the tumor-overexpressed Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide
Phosphate Hydrogen (NADPH): NQO1, leading to the
continuous generation of H2O2. Subsequently, PtNC/SiO2
functions as a nanozyme that further catalyzes the conversion
of H2O2 into highly cytotoxic ·OH radicals, thereby inducing
tumor cell apoptosis. In addition, Lap can induce a futile cycle
that depletes NADPH, which, in turn, suppresses the reduction
of oxidized glutathione (GSSG) to reduced glutathione (GSH),
synergistically amplifying oxidative stress within the tumor
tissue.54,56 Overall, this cascade enzyme-catalyzed strategy
effectively promotes NQO1-overexpressing tumor cell apopto-
sis and suppresses metastasis through multiple synergistic
mechanisms, providing a new direction for the development of
integrated antitumor strategies.
■CONCLUSION
In summary, we have achieved the full-size modulation of Pt-
based nanozymes with the same Pt loadings and revealed the
size-dependent enzymatic catalytic performance. The PtNC/
SiO2 cluster nanozyme exhibits optimized peroxidase-like
activity with a specific enzymatic activity of 50.05 U mg−1 due
to its upshifted d-band center and the lowest energy barrier in
the rate-determining step during the dissociation of H2O2,
significantly surpassing that of PtSA/SiO2 SAzyme and PtNP/
SiO2 nanozymes. The volcano-shaped size−activity relation-
ships are also fully verified in H2O2 catalytic system and
antibacterial experiments. The optimized cluster nanozyme also
achieves high-efficiency cascade catalytic tumor therapy in
combination with a prodrug. The maximized atomic utilization
efficiency does not mean optimized nanozymatic efficiency, and
the present findings will afford new guidance for the rational
design of nanozymes.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Synthesis of Pt/SiO2 Nanozymes
Amino-functionalized SiO2 nanoflowers were prepared on the basis of
our previous research.50 Pt deposition was conducted in a custom-built
ALD reactor using amino-functionalized SiO2 as the substrate. Pt
single-atom nanozymes (PtSA/SiO2) were prepared by alternating
exposures to (methylcyclopentadienyl)trimethylplatinum(IV)
(MeCpPtMe3, Strem Chemicals, 99%) and ozone (O3). The as-
prepared PtSA/SiO2 was then subjected to thermal reduction under a
hydrogen atmosphere (10% H2/Ar) at different temperatures (200,
300, 400, 500, and 600 °C) for 2 h, yielding a series of Pt/SiO2
nanozymes with tunable Pt particle compositions and sizes.
Synthesis of Lap@PtNC/SiO2
Initially, 5 mg of Lap was added to 5 mL of ethanol and stirred at room
temperature under light-protected conditions. Subsequently, 50 mg of

Peroxidase-Like (POD) Activity of Pt/SiO2 Nanozymes
The peroxidase-like activity of Pt/SiO2 nanozymes (200 μg/mL) was
evaluated using TMB (1 mmol/L) and OPD (20 mmol/L) as

the absorbance at 652 nm (characteristic peak of the TMB oxidation
product) and 450 nm (characteristic peak of the OPD oxidation
product) using UV−vis spectroscopy over a 0−10 min reaction period.
The intensity of the chromogenic reaction peaks was used to quantify
the peroxidase-mimicking activity.
Catalytic Kinetics Determination
Steady-state kinetic assays were performed in NaAc/HAc buffer (1 mL,
0.1 M, pH 5.0) at 37 °C with PtNC/SiO2 (200 μg/mL). The reaction
mixtures were prepared in separate tubes by adding TMB solution and
varying concentrations of H2O2 (final concentrations: 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, and
10 mM), followed by absorbance measurements at 652 nm at different
time intervals. Subsequently, while maintaining other conditions
constant, the experiments were repeated with fixed H2O2 concentration
and varying TMB concentrations (final concentrations: 1, 4, 6, 8, 10,
and 12 mM). The Michaelis−Menten equation was applied to
determine the maximum reaction velocity (Vmax) and Michaelis−
Menten constant (Km).

μg) were incubated in NaAc/HAc buffer solution (1 mL, pH = 5.0)

The production of hydroxyl radicals ·OH was qualitatively assessed

added. After 10 min of reaction, an aliquot of the mixture was
transferred to an ESR tube using a capillary and immediately subjected
to ESR measurement.
Lap Release Profile of Lap@PtNC/SiO2
The cumulative release of Lap from Lap@PtNC/SiO2 was investigated
under different microenvironmental conditions. The Lap@PtNC/SiO2
was incubated in PBS solutions with varying pH values (7.4, 6.0, and
5.0) for predetermined time intervals (0, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h).
After each incubation period, the suspension was centrifuged, and the
supernatant was analyzed by UV−vis spectroscopy at 432 nm to
quantify the released Lap content.
In Vitro Cytotoxicity
CT26 cells, L929 cells, and AML12 cells were plated at 104 cells/well in
96-well plates and grown overnight. The cells were then treated with
varying concentrations of PtNC/SiO2, Lap@PtNC/SiO2 (0, 3, 6, 9, 12,
15, and 18 μg/mL), and the drug control group of Lap (0, 0.04, 0.09,
0.12, 0.17, 0.21, and 0.25 μg/mL), respectively. After 24 h of
incubation, the medium was removed, and Cell Counting Kit-8
(CCK-8) solutions were added for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was
measured to determine the cell viability.
Live/Dead Cell Staining
CT26 cells were plated in 35 mm confocal dishes at 1 × 105 cells per
dish and grown overnight. The cells were then treated with PtNC/SiO2,
Lap@PtNC/SiO2 and Lap, respectively. After 12 h of incubation, the
cells were stained with calcein-AM and PI for 30 min in the normal
incubator, washed with PBS, and observed by CLSM.
Flow Cytometry
CT26 cells were plated in a 6-well plate at 2 × 105 per well and grown
overnight. The cells were then treated with PtNC/SiO2, Lap@PtNC/
SiO2, and Lap, respectively. After incubating for 12 h, the cells were
collected and washed three times with PBS, and then the cells were
stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI in the normal incubator for 15
min. The flow data was collected by a flow cytometer, and the cells were
divided into early apoptotic cells stained by Annexin V-FITC or late
withering/dead cells stained by propidium iodide (PI).
In Vivo Therapy
Mice (BALB/c, female, 6−8 weeks old) were purchased from
GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. Xenograft models were established by the
subcutaneous injection of CT26 cells (3 × 106) in 100 μL of PBS into
the right armpit region of each animal. The tumors were allowed to
grow to approximately 50−100 mm3 in size. The xenograft mice were
divided into four groups randomly (n = 3): (a) blank control group
(PBS); (b) drug control group (Lap); (c) material control group
(PtNC/SiO2); and (d) experimental group (Lap@PtNC/SiO2). Lap@
PtNC/SiO2 (2 mg/mL, 50 μL) and equal amounts of PtNC/SiO2, Lap, or
PBS were intratumorally injected into the xenograft mice of each group,
respectively. Body weight, tumor volume, and photographs of each
mouse were recorded every 2 days. The volume of the tumor was found
using the formula: V = 0.5 × a × b2 (a is the length of the mouse tumor,
and b is the width of the mouse tumor). After the treatment on the 16th
day, all mice were sacrificed, and the tumors and organs were collected
for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Tumors were also stained
with terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling
(TUNEL). Blood was collected for pathological analysis of blood
biochemical indicators. The animal studies were conducted in
accordance with the approved protocol of the Institutional Animal
Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Institute of Biophysics,
Chinese Academy of Sciences (Project number: SYXK2023168).
Hemolysis Test
Blood was taken from healthy mice and collected in an anticoagulant
tube. The blood was then centrifuged (3000 rpm, 15 min), the
supernatant discarded, and washed twice with PBS (3000 rpm, 5 min).
After discarding the supernatant, the red blood cell precipitates were
prepared into a 4% red blood cell solution with PBS. Negative control
(PBS), positive control (H2O) and materials of different concentrations
were mixed with the 4% red blood cell solution in a 1:1 ratio,
respectively, and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation (3000
rpm, 15 min), the supernatant was taken out from a 96-well plate, and
absorbance at 450 nm was measured to evaluate the hemolysis rate.

Ultrasmall metal nanoclusters (NCs) are a sub-class of metal nanoparticles (NPs) with a metal core size
of 3 nm, and display interesting molecule-like properties. Atomically precise metal NCs can be synthe-
sized by many solution chemistry methods in polar and non-polar reaction environments. Among these
methods, two-phase based strategies involving a water-oil binary reaction system have emerged as a
facile means to synthesize, process, functionalize, and apply metal NCs. This Review ﬁrstly presents an
overview of the two-phase based strategies in the synthesis of hydrophobic and hydrophilic metal
NCs, and aims to elucidate the underlying working principles of these strategies. Then, how a two-
phase system could process metal NCs for size/composition tailoring, luminescence generation, sorting
and puriﬁcation is discussed. Subsequently, several examples that utilizing a two-phase system to efﬁ-
ciently functionalize metal NCs with different surface chemistry are highlighted. Finally, two-phase based
applications of metal NCs in catalysis and self-assembly are also reviewed. The fundamentals and the
advances of the two-phase based strategies in the synthesis, processing, functionalization, and applica-
tion of metal NCs summarized in this Review may increase the acceptance of two-phase based strategy
in the metal NC ﬁeld.

1. Introduction
Ultrasmall metal nanoclusters (NCs), a sub-genre of nanoparti-
cles (NPs) with a metal core size of 3 nm and stabilized by a
monolayer of ligand molecules [1–16], have attracted numerous
attention due to their intriguing molecule-like properties (e.g.,
luminescence [17–28], HOMO-LUMO transitions [29–31], chirality
[32–35], quantized charging [36], and magnetism [37,38]) as well
as their promising application in ﬁelds of environment [39–41],
energy conversion [42–45], catalysis [46–55], and biomedicine
[56–67]. The history of thiolate-protected metal NCs can be traced
back to 3 decades ago. In 1996, Whetten et al. successfully obtained
several discrete Au NC species via the two-phase Brust method
[68], which became the cornerstone of NC building. Since then,
tremendous efforts have been devoted in developing various syn-
thetic strategies and engineering the physiochemical properties
of metal NCs [1–3,46,69–72]. Currently, atomically precise metal
NCs of different size and composition can be synthesized via solu-
tion chemistry methods in polar and non-polar environments
[1,2,12,73–76]. Both polar- and non-polar-solvent based methods
have their own unique merits and demerits (e.g., incontrollable
reduction kinetics for polar-solvent based methods, and the usage
of expensive organometallic salts as metal precursors for non-
polar-solvent based methods) [75,77,78]. To overcome these
demerits, a binary water–oil two-phase system where the ligand-
protected metal complexes are stored in one phase and the reduc-
ing agent is usually placed in the other phase, is widely applied for
NC synthesis with precise control in the size, composition, optical
property, surface chemistry, etc [51,76,79–84]. This is because that
the two-phase system could not only provide more control ways to
manipulate the reduction kinetics of metal NCs (e.g., through inter-
facial reduction or gradient diffusion of reducing agent from one
phase to another for controllable synthesis) [80,84,85], but also
make the use of inexpensive hydrophilic metal salts as metal pre-
cursors possible (e.g., water-soluble metal precursors can be trans-
ferred from aqueous phase to organic phase for subsequent
synthesis) [77,78]. Up to now, diverse strategies based on a two-
phase system have been developed to synthesize, process, func-
tionalize and apply metal NCs in the past two decades [79–
81,83–90].
In the NC ﬁeld, two-phase based strategies were initially devel-
oped
for
the
synthesis
of

metal

the presence or absence of phase transfer agents such as tetraocty-
lammonium bromide (TOAB) which are used to transfer hydrophi-
lic metal ions from aqueous phase to organic phase for later
reductive synthesis of metal NCs in organic phase [79,80,88,90].
Later, in order to engineer metal NCs for broader usability, many
two-phase based strategies were designed for processing metal
NCs for their luminescence generation [83,92], size/composition
tailoring
[51,93–95],
sorting/puriﬁcation

etc.
For

has been exempliﬁed to be capable in processing metal NCs (e.g.,
Au, Ag, Cu, and Pt) from non-luminescent to highly luminescent
[83,92]. In addition, to further tailor the surface chemistry of metal
NCs, some two-phase based strategies were also devised for
functionalizing metal NCs [100,101]. For example, the surface of
metal NCs can be shifted from hydrophobic to hydrophilic via a
two-phase based strategy, or vice versa [86,102]. Very recently,
two-phase based strategies have been demonstrated to be applica-
ble for the catalysis and self-assembly of metal NCs [82,103]. All
above mentioned studies exempliﬁed that utilization of a two-
phase system has been a versatile strategy for the synthesis, pro-
cessing, functionalization, and application of metal NCs. However,
these two-phase related studies of metal NCs are more like scat-
tered jigsaws without proper summarization. Moreover, the
understanding on the working principles of those two-phase based
strategies especially in the synthesis, processing, and functional-
ization of metal NCs looks rather limited. Therefore, it is urgent
to contribute a Review on the utilization of a two-phase system
in the NC ﬁeld.
In this Review, we ﬁrst provide an overview of the two-phase
based strategies in the synthesis of hydrophobic and hydrophilic
metal NCs, and aim to elucidate the underlying working principles
of these strategies. Next we discuss how a two-phase system could
process metal NCs for their luminescence generation, size/compo-
sition tailoring, sorting/puriﬁcation, etc. We then highlight several
examples to demonstrate that utilizing a two-phase system could
efﬁciently functionalize metal NCs with different surface chem-
istry. Finally, two-phase based applications of metal NCs in cataly-
sis and self-assembly are also reviewed. We believe that this
Review will be of great practical value to both metal NC scientists
and other researchers who are interested in synthesizing and engi-
neering metal NCs for speciﬁc applications. Hopefully this Review
will increasingly stimulate prosperous development of two-phase
based strategies for metal NCs with new attributes and inspire
incessant endeavours in this emerging research area.
2. Two-phase synthesis of metal NCs
Owing to the ‘‘metal core-ligand shell” structure of metal NCs,
the selection of surface ligand for metal NCs is critical prior to
the synthesis because the surface ligand determines the surround-
ing environment of metal NCs (e.g., solvents with different polar-
ity) [104]. This not only governs the terminal application of
metal NCs [105–107], but also dictates the design of their synthetic
methods [3,46,75]. Therefore, here metal NCs can be classiﬁed into
two types based on the hydropathy of their surface ligands:
hydrophobic metal NCs and hydrophilic ones. Several two-phase
based synthetic strategies with different design concepts have
been developed to synthesize either hydrophobic or hydrophilic
metal NCs [79,80,85,88]. Generally, in these synthetic methods,
water-soluble metal precursors (e.g., HAuCl4) are all used as metal
sources. Speciﬁcally, for the synthesis of hydrophobic metal NCs,
hydrophilic metal ions should be transferred from aqueous to
organic phase for subsequent complexation with hydrophobic
ligands [79,80,88]. By contrary, in the synthesis of hydrophilic
metal NCs, metal ions directly interact with hydrophilic ligands
in aqueous phase to form M(I)-ligand complexes (here M(I)
denotes metal(I) ions), which may be further transferred to organic
phase (through electrostatic interaction by phase-transfer agents)

for better control in the size or other attributes of hydrophilic
metal NCs [85].
In addition, the key of these two-phase synthetic methods is
that the two-phase system could make possible the controllable
reduction of M(I)-ligand complexes [75,79,108], which is critically
important for the synthesis of atomically precise metal NCs
[80,88]. In the following sub-sections, we will separately discuss
the two-phase based synthetic methods for hydrophobic and
hydrophilic metal NCs with elucidations about their underlying
mechanisms.
2.1. Two-phase synthesis of hydrophobic metal NCs
For the synthesis of hydrophobic metal NCs, there are two main
methods developed based on the two-phase system: the two-
phase Brust method [68,79,90,109–112] and the solvent-directed
phase transfer method [80]. It should be noted that the one-
phase Brust method will not be included in this Review [113–
115], and one may refer to other excellent review articles in this
regard if interested [1,116].
2.1.1. Two-phase Brust and Brust-like methods for the synthesis of
hydrophobic metal NCs
The two-phase Brust method was developed by Brust et al. in
1994 and initially applied to synthesize small sized thiolate-
protected Au NPs (6 nm) [109,117]. The key feature of the two-
phase Brust method is the employment of a quaternary ammo-
nium compound as phase transfer agent for transferring hydrophi-
lic Au ions from aqueous to organic phase [88,109,117]. A typical
two-phase Brust synthesis is shown in Fig. 1, an aqueous solution
of Au(III) precursor (e.g., HAuCl4) is ﬁrst mixed with the organic
solution of the phase-transfer agent TOAB (with a hydrophilic head
group and four hydrophobic tails), and the TOAB could transfer Au
(III) precursor to the organic phase via the formation of [Au(III)Cl4]-
[TOA]+ complexes. Afterwards, a hydrophobic thiolate ligand (e.g.,
dodecanethiol or DDT) as protecting ligand is added to the organic
phase. It is noted that the thiolate ligand has moderate reducing
ability, and the thiol group of such ligands could reduce Au(III) to
Au(I) via the formation of disulphide bond. Previously, researchers
commonly believed that the introduced thiolate ligand (SR for
short) could replace the TOA+ species and interact with Au(I) ions
to form polymeric Au(I)–SR complexes via strong Au(I)–S bonding
in the organic phase at this stage [77,109]. However, later mecha-
nistic studies revealed that the TOA+ species still bind with Au(I) at
this step [117,118]. Nevertheless, as the ﬁnal step, an aqueous
solution of strong reducing agent such as NaBH4 is introduced into
the aqueous-organic two-phase system, which could reduce Au(I)
complexes to small thiolate-protected Au NPs with core sizes of
2–2.5 nm [119].
Later, tremendous research efforts have been devoted to adjust-
ing the particle size though varying the feeding SR-to-Au ratios
[68,120–122]. In 1996, an innovative work of Whetten et al.
increased the feeding SR-to-Au ratio to 2 (Note: the ratio is 1 in
previous Brust method) under the two-phase Brust synthetic
regime
and
successfully
obtained
several
fractions
of
DDT-
protected Au NCs/NPs with a core size of 1.5–3.5 nm [68]. The frac-
tion of Au NCs had a core size of ~ 1.5 nm and its core mass was
determined to be ~ 28 KDa, which was later conﬁrmed to be
Au144 NCs [120,123]. In parallel, Hostetler et al. adjusted the SR-
to-Au ratio to 3 within the two-phase Brust system to synthesize
DDT-protected Au NCs with a core size of ~ 1.5 nm (~110 Au atoms)
[121]. Similarly, Schaaff and Whetten used hexanethiol as protect-
ing ligand and ﬁxed the SR-to-Au ratio to 3 : 1 to obtain
hexanethiolate-protected Au NCs with a molecular mass of 14
KDa based on the two-phase Brust method [122]. It should be men-
tioned that up to that date the two-phase Brust method was suc-
cessful in synthesizing metal NCs, but still unsuccessful in
controlling the NC size at the atomic level. Moreover, the low yield
of metal NCs was also a major issue, which demanded continuous
efforts to improve their yields.

A highlight of the two-phase Brust method is the synthesis of
chiral metal NCs by deploying chiral phase transfer agent. It is
known that chirality is a fundamental property of nanoscaled
materials [56,128–130], and is promising in wide applications such
as optical devices, enantioselective catalysis, separation, and
biosensing [91]. While synthesis of chiral metal NCs is of major
interest in the NC ﬁeld, many chiral metal NCs synthesized are
racemic mixtures which require additional separation or puriﬁca-
tion procedures [131]. Therefore, direct asymmetric synthesis of
chiral metal NCs is desirable. Very recently, Yan et al. demonstrated
the direct synthesis of optically active enantiomers of Ag28Cu12(-
SR)24 NCs based on the two-phase Brust synthesis [91]. The key
of their synthesis is the usage of chiral phase transfer agents such

synthesis of enantiomeric metal NCs. This strategy that using chiral
phase transfer agents for asymmetric synthesis of chiral metal NCs
is remarkable because it opens a new door for preparing chiral
metal NCs, which may facilitate the ﬁnal applications of chiral
metal NCs.
Although the two-phase Brust method has been developed and
studied for more than two decades, the fundamental understand-
ing on its underlying mechanism is rather limited, which curtailed
the applicability of the method for the synthesis of size-tunable
metal NCs. In recent years, several mechanistic studies in the
two-phase Brust synthesis of metal NPs have been reported
[117,118,132], which might assist for the controlled synthesis of
size-tunable metal NPs. However, such studies were all conducted
within the NP ﬁeld and might not fully cater to the NC regime.
Therefore, the synthesis of size-tunable metal NCs via the two-
phase Brust method was still a major challenge. A feasible way is
to dig the underlying chemistry of the two-phase Brust method
within the NC ﬁled.

reduce the Au(I) precursor in organic phase? Taken these questions

transferring Au(III) precursor, they found that TOAB also transfers
the reducing agent NaBH4 from aqueous to organic phase (Fig. 4-

TOAB (that forms inverse TOAB micelles) in previous two-phase
Brust methods may be the main reason for the unsuccessful tuning

(I) precursors caused by the chaotic phase transfer of both Au(III)

controllable, they developed a novel two-phase synthetic strategy
by decoupling the roles of the TOAB (Fig. 4c): 1) using hydrophobic
selenolate ligand (SeR for short) as protecting-cum-phase transfer
agent instead of TOAB to transfer Au(III) precursor from aqueous to
organic phase via the formation of Au(I)-SeR complexes; and 2)
deploying a little amount of TOAB to transfer NaBH4 into organic
phase for controllable reduction of Au(I)-SeR complexes in organic
phase. In this way, the reduction kinetics can be regulated by the
amount of the TOAB, and four selenolate-protected Au NC species
with size-tunability could be synthesized by simply adjusting the
amount of the TOAB (Fig. 4d). Moreover, this method has been
demonstrated to be versatile in synthesizing metal NCs protected
by various ligands because numerous ligands can be used as long
as the ligands are hydrophobic and have strong interaction with
metal ions. This study promoted the deep understanding on the
roles of phase-transfer agents and the mechanism of the two-
phase Brust method, and provided some new insights on the
design of novel synthetic strategies for size-tunable metal NCs.

of the resulting metal NCs. However, owing to the usage of TOAB
as phase-transfer agent in the two-phase system, a niggling post-
puriﬁcation treatment for metal NCs is required to remove TOAB
molecules from the NC solution prior to characterization and appli-
cation. From this viewpoint, a two-phase synthesis without the
usage of phase-transfer agents would be highly demanded.
2.1.2. Solvent-directed two-phase synthesis of hydrophobic metal NCs
Based on the understanding of the modiﬁed two-phase Brust
method where the TOAB is applied to transfer NaBH4 from aqueous
to organic phase for controllable reduction, it is rationalized that
the reduction for metal NCs could be controlled as long as one
can regulate the encountering chance of hydrophilic NaBH4 with
hydrophobic M(I)-ligand complexes, which may eliminate the
usage of TOAB concurrently achieve the size tuning of metal NCs.
On the basis, Yuan et al. inventively devised a general ‘‘solvent-
directed phase transfer” method for the synthesis of atomically
precise metal NCs (e.g., Au, Ag, and Cu) protected by a monolayer
of hydrophobic ligands (e.g., selenolate, thiolate, and phosphine)
[80,134]. In this method (Fig. 5a), in addition to the employment
of hydrophobic ligands as protecting-cum-phase transfer agents
of metal precursors, the unique design of this method was to intro-
duce a miscible solvent (e.g., alcohol, which is miscible in both
aqueous and organic phases) into the two-phase system, which
not only assisted the phase transfer of M(I)-ligand complexes from
water to organic phase, but more importantly controlled the
encountering chances between hydrophilic NaBH4 and hydropho-
bic M(I)-ligand complexes. If more miscible solvent was intro-
duced, the encountering of hydrophilic NaBH4 with hydrophobic
M(I)-ligand complexes at the water-toluene interface could be
easier, and the reduction kinetics could be faster. In this way, the

2.2. Two-phase synthesis of hydrophilic metal NCs
Hydrophilic metal NCs are promising in various potential appli-
cations
such
as
environmental
protection
and
biomedicine
[41,60,142–145], and thus acquiring high-quality water-soluble
metal NCs is in high demand. Compared with hydrophobic metal
NCs, the synthesis of hydrophilic metal NCs in aqueous phase is
more complicated because the formation of water-soluble metal
NCs are affected by more factors (e.g., pH, reduction and etching
kinetics, functional groups of ligands, and interference of inorganic
ions) [108,146,147]. For example, in the aqueous synthesis of
hydrophilic metal NCs, an unavoidable problem is the Extremely
good water-solubility of both M(I)-ligand complexes and reducing
agent (e.g., NaBH4), which usually leads to incontrollable reduction
of M(I)-ligand complexes and subsequent formation of polydis-
perse metal NCs [148,149]. Therefore, the key for a successful
design of synthetic methods is the controlled release of reaction
reagents (e.g., reducing agent, and the M(I)-ligand complexes).
Two-phase based strategies have been developed to synthesize
hydrophilic metal NCs with the aim of improving the control
release of the reagents and thereby the reduction kinetics. In this
subsection, two examples with their own unique designs in the
two-phase synthesis of hydrophilic Au NCs will be discussed.

metal NCs

they found that 6 to 9 CTAB molecules could bind with the MHA

action (to form CTA+–COO pairs). This result suggests that one
Au25(MHA)18 NC could be sustained in organic phase as long as
at least 6 of its surface ligand MHA binding with CTAB. A concern
is that many factors (e.g., the hydrophilicity and functional groups
of the surface ligand, the types of M(I)-SR motifs, and the metal-to-
ligand ratios of NCs) of metal NCs will inﬂuence the minimum
number of CTAB required, so more comprehensive studies may
be needed to fully solve this interesting fundamental issue.

3. Two-phase processing of metal NCs
A recognizable difference between the two-phase synthesis and
two-phase processing is their starting materials. While in the two-
phase synthesis of metal NCs mentioned in the last section the
starting material is M(I)-ligand complexes, the starting material
used for the two-phase processing is metal NCs or large sized metal
NPs. Processing is a treatment to the metal NCs/NPs, by which
either metal NCs/NPs could be engineered to metal NC species with
newly generated attributes, or a separation is completed for metal
NCs from other impurities or other NC species. Here the newly gen-
erated attributes of metal NCs after processing may refer to their
core size, composition, luminescence, and chirality. A general prin-
ciple of processing is to disturb the environment (e.g., by introduc-
ing etchant or adjusting pH, temperature, and solvent) of metal
NCs/NPs to change the stability of metal NCs/NPs, which leads to
the evolution of their sizes/structures, resulting in the generation
of new attributes of metal NCs. Therefore, a two-phase system is
commonly used to process metal NCs/NPs due to the easy variation
of their environment.
3.1. Two-phase processing metal NCs for size tailoring
The two-phase processing strategy can be used to tailor the size
of metal NCs/NPs. In this section, only the cases that the two-phase
system is involved in the whole process will be discussed. A repre-
sentative example is provided by the Pradeep group [93–95]. They
built a two-phase system to process the raw NCs - Au25(SG)18 NCs
[94]. In the two-phase system (Fig. 8), Au25(SG)18 NCs in water was
processed via interfacial etching by hydrophobic octanethiol (OT
for short) molecules in toluene at room temperature (see the mid-
dle route in Fig. 8) and elevated temperature (55 ℃, the right route
in Fig. 8), resulting in the formation of hydrophilic luminescent Au
NCs in water phase and hydrophobic non-luminescent Au NCs in
organic phase, which were tentatively assigned to Au23(SG)18 NCs
and Au33(OT)22 NCs, respectively. Here it should be noted that at
room temperature the OT molecules are only served as etching
agent, but at elevated temperature the OT molecules involve in
the protection of Au NCs after the etching process, which may be
caused by the different stabilities of Aux(SG)y NCs and Aux(OT)y
NCs in different solution phases at different temperatures.
The interfacial etching is applicable to process various metal
NCs by different etchants. For example, the Pradeep group also
used the interfacial etching strategy to process MSA-protected Ag
NPs with core sizes of 5–10 nm (MSA denotes mercaptosuccinic
acid) for monodisperse Ag NCs [95]. The parent Ag NP precursors
in the aqueous phase were mixed with an organic solution of
excess MSA (here toluene, carbon tetrachloride, and diethyl ether
can be used as the organic solvent; the MSA is used as the etchant).
Processing of the Ag NPs could occur via etching by MSA molecules
at the water–organic interface under stirring condition, which
gradually converted Ag NPs to Ag7(MSA)7 and Ag8(MSA)8 NCs.
The authors also demonstrated that other ligands such as nucle-
obases can also be employed as etchant for processing MSA-
protected Ag NPs, and the Ag NCs protected by both nucleobase
and MSA with an estimated mass of 7 KDa could be obtained [93].

important in inﬂuencing their chemical properties and application
performance, and even the subtle change in the metallic composi-
tion (e.g., one atom exchange) of metal NCs will cause dramatic dif-
ference in this issue. This cognition has motivated researchers to
develop various means to tune the NC’s composition for varying

3.3. Two-phase processing metal NCs for luminescence generation
Luminescence may be the most important attribute of metal
NCs, which made them promising in many applications such as
bio-imaging, sensing, solar cell, and optical devices [69,70]. Hence
acquiring highly luminescent metal NCs is always a big goal in the
NC ﬁeld. It is documented that the luminescence of metal NCs is
dependent on their size, structure, oxidation states, etc. The two-
phase processing strategy could be an efﬁcient way to ﬁne modify
the size and structure of metal NCs for obtaining highly lumines-
cent metal NCs.
For example, Yuan et al. developed a reversible phase transfer
protocol based on electrostatic interaction by which polydisperse,
non-luminescent, and unstable metal NCs could be processed to
monodisperse, highly luminescent, and extremely stable metal
NCs (e.g., Ag, Au, Pt, and Cu NCs) [83,87]. Speciﬁcally, raw metal
NCs

aqueous phase to organic phase via electrostatic interaction
through forming CTA+–COO ion pairs (the COO– is derived from
the deprotonation of carboxyl group of the thiolate ligand). The
key of this method is to create a mild etching environment in
organic phase by phase transfer, and the NCs could suffer a mild
etching process by free thiolate molecules, which led to the forma-
tion of highly luminescent metal NCs with good monodispersity
and stability. An intriguing feature of this method is that the as-
formed luminescent metal NCs in organic phase could be retrans-
ferred to aqueous phase by interrupting the ionic bonds between
anionic metal NCs and cationic CTA+ ions with another anionic sur-
factant. Another advantage of the method is its versatility. As
demonstrated, a variety of luminescent metal NCs protected with
different types of ligands could be obtained via this processing
since the phase transfer is only dependent on the electrostatic
interaction between thiolate ligands and CTAB surfactant.

luminescent metal NCs, the two-phase processing strategy is also
capable to process large sized metal NPs for luminescent metal
NCs. For example, Lin et al. developed a novel interfacial etching

3.4. Two-phase processing for sorting and puriﬁcation of metal NCs
Another function of the two-phase processing is to purify or
selectively separate metal NCs. As known, after completing the
synthesis of metal NCs, a puriﬁcation treatment is usually required
to separate the as-synthesized metal NCs from inorganic species
(e.g., reducing agents and inorganic ions) and by-products, which
can be realized by the two-phase processing strategy. For example,
Martin et al. utilized a two-phase system to quickly extract
hydrophobic hexanethiolate-protected Au NCs from relatively
polar methanol to non-polar hexane, while left residues (e.g.,
NaBH4, Cl, and Na+) in the methanol phase [97]. The hydrophobic
hexanethiolate ligand on the NC surface prefers the non-polar hex-
ane solvent (polarity index is 0) to less polar methanol (polarity
index is ~ 5.1), which is the driving force for the extraction of
hexanethiolate-protected Au NCs from methanol to hexane. This
process efﬁciently puriﬁes NC samples, and additional post-
puriﬁcation treatment of metal NCs is unnecessary. Similar two-

processing
strategy
was
also
used
to

authors also found that metal NCs could be concentrated by the

extract one speciﬁc NC species from a mixture of NCs. For example,
enantiomeric metal NCs can be selectively separated from racemic

4. Two-phase functionalization of metal NCs
Functionalization is an important way to modify or alter the
physiochemical properties of metal NCs for broader applications.
Currently, the two-phase based functionalization mainly focuses
on engineering the surface chemistry of metal NCs (i.e., surface
ligands). For example, hydrophilic metal NCs can be functionalized
to hydrophobic ones by ligand exchange within the two-phase sys-
tem, or vice versa. In this section, we will discuss this topic in three
aspects by employing several representative examples: 1) func-

is to allow hydrophobic ligand molecules approach the hydrophilic
metal NCs and subsequently replace the hydrophilic surface ligand.
It is tough to solve this problem in one-phase system because of
the difﬁculty in ﬁnding a proper solvent to dissolve both the hydro-
philic metal NCs and hydrophobic ligands for ligand exchange.
Therefore, a two-phase system is preferably chosen since it could
include the hydrophilic metal NCs and hydrophobic ligand mole-
cules into one system for ligand exchange.

4.2. Functionalizing metal NCs from hydrophobic to hydrophilic
Owing to the explosive advancement in synthesizing hydropho-
bic metal NCs via one- and two-phase based strategies, abundant
hydrophobic metal NCs are available as template NCs for convert-
ing them to hydrophilic or amphiphilic ones, which has been
demonstrated to be efﬁcient in acquiring water-soluble metal NCs.
In 2004, Woehrle et al. developed a two-phase method to con-
vert Au NCs from hydrophobic to hydrophilic [153]. However, the
as-synthesized hydrophilic Au NCs are not atomically precise. In
2005, Shichibu et al. conducted a similar study and established a
two-phase system where a chloroform solution of hydrophobic
PPh3-protected Au11 NCs was mixed with an aqueous solution of
GSH with a GSH/Au NC ratio of 400:1 [89]. After stirring at 50 ℃
for 15 hrs, GSH (with stronger binding afﬁnity to Au) could fully
replace the surface ligand PPh3 of the Au NCs, and successfully
functionalized them to hydrophilic Au25(SG)18 NCs. Later, the
underlying mechanism on the functionalization of PPh3-protected
Au11 NCs by GSH was studied by Mckenzie et al. [154] In 2014, they
found that pure Au11(PPh3)8Cl3 NCs have superior stability and
cannot be functionalized by GSH, which is in stark difference from
the case of Au11(PPh3)7Cl3 NCs or the mixture of Au11(PPh3)7-8Cl3
NCs where they can be easily functionalized by GSH to form Au25(-
SG)18 NCs (Fig. 14a). In addition, the formation of Au25(SG)18 NCs
instead of other sized Au NCs may be due to that the Au25(SG)18
NCs have better stability and other sized Aun(SG)m NCs with less
stabilities cannot be survived at 50 ℃.
With the technical development of the two-phase functional-
ization, the surface of metal NCs could be completely modiﬁed
without the alternation of their metallic cores and structures. For
example, AbdulHalim et al. applied a two-phase strategy to intro-
duce ﬂuorine groups onto the surface of Ag44(MNBA)30 NCs (here

MNBA of Ag44(MNBA)30 NCs in water phase [86]. The authors also
demonstrated that other aryl ligands such as 4-nitrophiophenol
and 2-naphthalenethiol can also be used for the functionalization
of the Ag44 NCs within this system. Recently, Zan et al. designed
a general two-phase functionalization method to acquire water-

(Cap), N-acetyl-Lcysteine (NAC), MBA, GSH, and MSA (Fig. 14b-c)
[155].
5. Two-phase application of metal NCs
5.1. Two-phase application of metal NCs in catalysis

widely applied in various catalytic applications such as photocatal-

there is a common belief that the steric hindrance of the NC’s sur-
face ligands could inhibit the catalytic activity. Moreover, the cat-
alytic mechanism of metal NCs in aqueous environment is usually
ambiguous. Such concerns can be smartly disposed by a recently
developed MOF (metal–organic framework)-mediated phase trans-
fer of metal NCs from organic phase to water phase via the use of
host–guest chemistry.

5.2. Two-phase application of metal NCs in self-assembly
In nanoscience ﬁeld, many nanomaterials as building blocks can
be assembled into 1D, 2D and 3D conﬁgurations for enhancing or
customizing their properties and application performance because
of the collective and synergistic effects of building blocks in the
assembly. Metal NCs with atomically precise size and engineerable
surface chemistry could be considered as an ideal building blocks
for self-assembly of different sizes, morphologies, and orders. Cur-
rently, self-assembly of metal NCs can be realized by two-phase
assembly strategy via the interactions between NCs and the
cross-linkers (e.g., ions and molecules), which is different from
other
self-assemblies
that
mainly

the
inter-cluster

For example, Yao et al. designed an amphiphilic Au25(MHA)18@-
xCTA NC (x = 6–9) based on a phase-transfer-driven ion-pairing
reaction between hydrophobic CTA+ cations and the anionic COO
of the Au25(MHA)18 NCs (Fig. 16a-b) [82]. Such amphiphilic
Au25(MHA)18@xCTA NCs (x = 6–9) could be assembled into
sheet-like bilayers at the liquid  air interface (Fig. 16c-j). This
phenomenon resembles to the formation of lyotropic liquid crys-

is

imparting
the
amphiphilicity to metal NCs, which may meaningfully extend their
applicability in fundamental and applied research due to their
molecular-amphiphile-like self-assembly properties.
6. Conclusions
Two-phase based strategies have been developed to be a facile
means for synthesizing, processing, functionalizing, and applica-
tions of metal NCs. A number of two-phase synthetic methods have
been designed to produce both hydrophobic and hydrophilic metal
NCs with tunable sizes, various surface ligands, and other intrigu-
ing attributes such as chirality. Furthermore, two-phase strategies
can be applied to efﬁciently process metal NCs to ones with strong
luminescence, new size or composition, chirality, and higher pur-
ity. In addition, surface functionalization of metal NCs can also
be achieved within the two-phase system, which made possible
the switch of metal NCs between hydrophilicity and hydrophobic-
ity. Recently, two-phase strategies were delicately extended to
applications of metal NCs such as catalysis and self-assembly. In
this Review, the underlying mechanisms of those two-phase based
strategies are also discussed, which could pave the way in generat-
ing new metal NC species with novel physiochemical properties. In
the past several decades, arresting research progress has been
made in this regard.
Although exciting progress has been achieved in the two-phase
synthesis, processing, functionalization, and application of metal
NCs, additional collaborative efforts are still required from theoret-
ical and experimental chemists to address emerging challenges in
this area. For example, at present, atomically precise size control of
metal NCs has been readily realized via the two-phase synthesis.
By contrast, while it is known that the composition of metal NCs
play vital roles in affecting their physicochemical properties, cus-
tomizing the composition of alloy NCs at atomic level via two-
phase strategies for tailoring their physicochemical properties
and application performance is still a major challenge. In addition,
comparing to the two-phase synthesis of metal NCs, the advances

the underlying chemistry of the two-phase based strategies, and
further promote the development of metal NCs in fundamental
studies and applications. We hope this Review will excite more
materials scientists and chemical engineers to develop more effec-

them useful for environmental monitoring and protection, energy
conversion, catalysis, bio-medical, molecular recognition, chiral
and luminescent probes.

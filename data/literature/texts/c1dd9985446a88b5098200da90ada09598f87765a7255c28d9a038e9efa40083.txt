ABSTRACT: Periodontitis, a prevalent chronic inflammatory
disease worldwide, is triggered by periodontopathogenic bacteria,
resulting in the progressive destruction of periodontal tissue,
particularly the alveolar bone. To effectively address periodonti-
tis, this study proposed a nanoformulation known as CuS@MSN-
SCS. This formulation involves coating citrate-grafted copper
sulfide (CuS) nanoparticles with mesoporous silica (MSNs),
followed by surface modification using amino groups and sulfated
chitosan (SCS) through electrostatic interactions. The objective
of this formulation is to achieve efficient bacteria removal by
inducing ROS signaling pathways mediated by Cu2+ ions.
Additionally, it aims to promote alveolar bone regeneration
through Cu2+-induced pro-angiogenesis and SCS-mediated bone
regeneration. As anticipated, by regulating the surface charges, the negatively charged CuS nanoparticles capped with sodium
citrate were successfully coated with MSNs, and the subsequent introduction of amine groups using (3-aminopropyl)-
triethoxysilane was followed by the incorporation of SCS through electrostatic interactions, resulting in the formation of
CuS@MSN-SCS. The developed nanoformulation was verified to not only significantly exacerbate the oxidative stress of
Fusobacterium nucleatum, thereby suppressing bacteria growth and biofilm formation in vitro, but also effectively alleviate the
inflammatory response and promote alveolar bone regeneration without evident biotoxicity in an in vivo rat periodontitis
model. These findings contribute to the therapeutic effect on periodontitis. Overall, this study successfully developed a
nanoformulation for combating bacteria and facilitating alveolar bone regeneration, demonstrating the promising potential for
clinical treatment of periodontitis.
KEYWORDS: CuS nanoparticles, sulfated chitosan, antibacteria, bone regeneration, periodontitis
INTRODUCTION
Periodontitis, as a prevalent chronic inflammatory disease
worldwide, is characterized by bacterial infection and
progressive destruction of periodontium, including the alveolar
bone, gingiva, periodontal ligament, and root cementum.1−3
Bacteria directly contribute to the onset and progression of
periodontitis by forming a subgingival microbiota, which later
transforms into subgingival plaque biofilm.4,5 The subgingival
plaque biofilms induce an exaggerated proinflammatory
immune reaction in the gingival connective tissue, resulting
in paracrine signaling that leads to alveolar bone resorption.6
Progressive loss of alveolar bone can eventually result in tooth
looseness and dental loss, significantly impacting the quality of
patient’s life and imposing a substantial social and economic
burden.7−9 This indicates that control of bacteria biofilm and
alveolar bone regeneration is crucial for the treatment of
periodontitis. Currently, clinical therapeutic strategies for
periodontitis mainly consist of initial cause-related therapy,
such as home care review, scaling and root planing, as well as

ments, the effectiveness of these strategies in clearing bacteria
is often limited due to the abundance of bacteria present in
inflamed gingiva and deep periodontal pockets.3,11,12 More-
over, bone regeneration outcomes are variable and unpredict-
able, frequently lacking sufficient angiogenesis.13 For instance,
scaling and root planing is unable to completely eradicate
bacteria, leading to protracted periodontal inflammation.3,11,12
Although antibiotics can be employed as adjunctive therapy to
enhance bacterial clearance, they can induce bacterial drug
resistance and disrupt the balance of oral microbial
communities, resulting in unintended systemic side effects.14,15
Furthermore, regenerative periodontal surgery commonly
results in undesired angiogenesis for insufficient bone
regeneration and no improvement in tooth stability. As a
consequence, developing a strategy that effectively clears
bacteria, particularly in deep periodontal pockets and inflamed
gingiva, while promoting angiogenesis for enhanced bone
regeneration is fundamental for improving the clinical efficacy
of periodontitis treatment.
Nanoparticles possess unique antibacterial mechanisms
against various bacterial species due to their substantial surface
area and high charge density.5,16,17 They can effectively
penetrate deep into periodontal pockets and inflamed gingiva,
enabling deep sterilization.18,19 Copper sulfide (CuS) nano-
particles have received considerable attention in promoting
angiogenesis and osteogenesis for periodontitis therapy,20,21 as
they can release copper ions to induce the generation of
reactive oxygen species (ROS) via Fenton-like reactions,
leading to bacterial death.20,22,23 Additionally, they act as
essential catalysts for vascular endothelial growth factor
(VEGF) and inhibit active bone resorption, contributing to
angiogenesis and osteogenesis.21,24 Considering the potential
toxicity of CuS nanoparticles at high concentrations, various
approaches involving surface modification and incorporation of
biomolecules have been explored to improve their biocompat-
ibility.25,26 Specifically, mesoporous silica nanoparticles
(MSNs) offer excellent stability, good biocompatibility, a
large specific surface area, and convenient modification, thus
having been recognized as effective biocompatible carriers for
loading CuS nanoparticles and enhancing their overall
biocompatibility.26,27 Moreover, the release of silicon ions
from MSNs has the capacity to enhance osteogenic differ-
entiation and accelerate osteogenesis in periodontal tissues.28
Another noteworthy biomolecule is sulfated chitosan (SCS), a
polysaccharide derived from chitosan through sulfation at the
C-2 and C-6 positions with sulfonic groups (−SO3
−). SCS has
gained significant interest in enhancing osteogenesis due to its
structural similarity to heparin, which can enhance VEGF-
mediated angiogenesis and exhibit a synergistic effect in
inducing BMP-2-mediated osteogenic differentiation.29−31
Collectively, these factors present a promising strategy for
developing CuS-based nanoparticles that can effectively
eradicate bacterial infections and enhance bone regeneration
for periodontitis therapy.

alternative for periodontitis treatment (Figure 1A). The

model.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Successful Fabrication of CuS@MSN-SCS. CuS@MSN-
SCS nanoparticles with a core−shell structure were synthesized
using a soft-template method. CuS nanoparticles exhibit
electronegative signals because of the sodium citric ligands
capping the nanoparticles (Figure 2A).32 Generally, direct
modification of silica on the electronegative CuS nanoparticles
as a shell coating is challenging due to the presence of
hydrolysate of tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), which also
carries negative charges. To overcome this, hexadecyltrime-
thylammonium bromide (CTAB), a surfactant, was initially
used as a soft template to form micelles and a bilayer of
cationic CTAB coating around the CuS nanoparticles through
electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions.33 Consequently,
the surface of CuS nanoparticles acquired a positive charge,
and under basic conditions, TEOS hydrolyzed and condensed,

Following the removal of the CTAB template through ethanol

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and field
emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) were used
to analyze the morphology of nanoparticles, including CuS,
CuS@MSN, CuS@MSN-NH2, and CuS@MSN-SCS nano-
particles (Figure 2B,C). The nanoparticle size was observed to
increase following the sequential modification of mesoporous
silica, amino groups, and SCS (Figure 2D), and the presence of
a core−shell structure was significantly evident in CuS@MSN,
CuS@MSN-NH2, and CuS@MSN-SCS samples. This obser-
vation indicated that CuS nanoparticles have been successfully
coated with the silica shell and modified by SCS. Elemental
mapping images of Cu, S, O, and N confirmed these findings
(Figure 2E). However, compared to CuS and CuS@MSN/
CTAB (Figure S2), a slight decrease in nanoparticle dispersion
was noted, which could be attributed to the imbalance among
van der Waals forces, steric effects, hydrophobic interactions,
electrostatic repulsion, and hydrogen bonding.36
The infrared spectra of the nanoparticles were recorded
using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy to
investigate the surface chemistry of the nanoparticles. In Figure
3A, following CTAB removal (Figure S3), all sample FTIR
spectra exhibited characteristic peaks related to the symmetric
and asymmetric stretching vibrations of Si−O−Si at 1070
cm−1, which are indicative of silica in the MSN framework.37
This confirms the successful coating of MSN onto CuS. In the
CuS@MSN-NH2 sample, new peaks emerged at around 3450
cm−1 (stretching vibration of −NH2),38 providing evidence for
the grafting of -NH2 groups onto CuS@MSN. Subsequently,
after SCS modification, the presence of a SO bond derived
from SCS was observed around 1203 cm−1,29 concomitant
with a blue shift of the Si−O−Si and −NH2 peaks to 994 and
3310 cm−1, respectively. These findings confirm the incorpo-
ration of noncovalent bonding of SCS in CuS@MSN-SCS
nanoparticles.

CuS@MSN-SCS Aggravated Oxidative Stress of F.
nucleatum to Suppress Bacteria Growth and Biofilm
Formation. Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum), crucial
for biofilm formation and maturation, acts as a “microbial
bridge” in dental plaque by facilitating coaggregation with early
and late colonizers.48−51 This bacterium can directly influence
host responses and enhance the infectivity of other
periodontitis pathogens.51 Targeted elimination of F. nuclea-
tum has been shown to effectively reduce the coaggregation of
pathogenic bacteria and inhibit biofilm formation.3 Therefore,
this study investigated the antibacterial efficacy of CuS@MSN-
SCS against F. nucleatum. As depicted in Figure 4A (top row),
the growth of F. nucleatum was observed to be enhanced in the
presence of 50 μg/mL CuS@MSN-SCS, compared to the
control group (0 μg/mL). This enhancement is attributed to
the intricate interaction between bacteria and their environ-
ment, where the copper ions derived from CuS@MSN-SCS
can lead to accelerated bacterial growth below toxic levels, as a
stress response occurs at low levels of Cu2+.52 However, higher
concentrations of CuS@MSN-SCS significantly reduced
colony formation, exhibiting a positive correlation between
the inhibition ratio and the concentration in the range of 100
to 300 μg/mL. For instance, in the 250 μg/mL and 300 μg/
mL CuS@MSN-SCS groups, the inhibition ratio exceeded
95% (Figure 4B), highlighting the antibacterial efficacy of
CuS@MSN-SCS. Since plaque biofilm is the primary cause of
periodontitis,3 the antibiofilm potential of the samples was
further evaluated through crystal violet staining, where higher
violet color intensity indicates greater biofilm biomass.3
Consistent with the colony count results, the control group
and the 50 μg/mL CuS@MSN-SCS group exhibited a deep
purple color (Figure 4A, middle row). With the increased
concentration of CuS@MSN-SCS, the OD value declined and
the color became lighter. The 200−300 μg/mL group
exhibited an approximately 50% reduction in OD value
compared to the control group (Figure 4C), suggesting a
significant antibiofilm efficacy of CuS@MSN-SCS at higher
concentrations. As for the evaluation of the antibiofilm activity
using live/dead fluorescent staining (Figure 4A, bottom row),
a notable reduction was noted in biofilm thickness when
treated with higher concentrations of CuS@MSN-SCS (Figure
4D). Additionally, the proportion of dead bacteria increased
compared to alive bacteria. Furthermore, to investigate the
relationship between the antibacterial capacity of CuS@MSN-
SCS and ROS production, intracellular ROS levels were
assessed with a ROS assay kit (Figure 4E). The expression of
ROS in F. nucleatum was not significantly affected by the
presence of CuS@MSN-SCS at low concentrations (50−150
μg/mL). However, as the concentration of the samples
increased to 200 μg/mL, a significant elevation in ROS levels
was indicated with a subsequent increase in concentration.
This could be ascribed to the release of Cu2+ derived from
CuS@MSN-SCS (as demonstrated in Figure S6). Initially,
Cu2+ ions undergo reduction by H2O2 to generate Cu+, which
subsequently engages in the Fenton-like reaction with H2O2.
This reaction leads to the production of
•OH and Cu2+,
instigating oxidative stress responses.22,53,54 Disruption of the
cellular oxidative stress tends to induce bacterial death through
DNA damage, protein oxidation, and lipid peroxidation,55
ultimately resulting in bacterial apoptosis and suppression of
biofilm formation.

CuS@MSN-SCS Promoted Bone Regeneration in
Periodontitis Rats. Based on the results obtained for
antibacterial capacity and biocompatibility, the concentration
of 250 μg/mL of CuS@MSN-SCS was chosen for treating
periodontitis in rats. Rat periodontitis was induced using the
ligation method in combination with a sugar-rich diet (Figure
6A). A ligature silk was placed around the maxillary second
molar of the rats to induce inflammation, subsequently feeding
them a high-sugar diet for 14 days.58 Afterward, CuS@MSN-
SCS was locally applied for treatment 10 times, while
minocycline (MiNo), a widely used antibiotic in clinical
practice,3 served as the positive control group to compare
therapeutic effects. As depicted in Figure 6B, in comparison to
the blank group, the periodontitis group displayed significant
bone loss, confirming the successful establishment of the
periodontitis model. In contrast, both the CuS@MSN-SCS
and MiNo groups exhibited positive therapeutic effects, as
indicated by the regeneration of alveolar bone, whereas no
significant difference was observed between them. These
findings were further supported by the assessment of alveolar
bone loss (ABL) (Figure 6C), a characteristic marker of
periodontitis progression.59 Moreover, the body weight of the
rats showed a significant increase following CuS@MSN-SCS
treatment (Figure 6D), and no notable signs of inflammation
or lesions were observed in organ tissues (Figure S7),
indicating the nontoxic nature of CuS@MSN-SCS toward
normal tissues and organs. In the XPS analysis, trace amounts
of residual CTAB, known for its toxicity to eukaryotic cells,60
were detected at a very low level in CuS@MSN-SCS.
However, existing studies have shown that rats exhibit a
certain tolerance to CTAB, with administration in drinking
water at doses below 20 mg/kg/day for one year.61 This means
the animals, such as rats, demonstrate a specific resistance to
the toxicity of CTAB. This resilience, observed in animals such
as rats, explains why the residual CTAB in CuS@MSN-SCS
did not induce toxicity in rats, affecting major organs and body
weight.

Based on the in vivo experiment results, alveolar bone
regeneration and inflammatory alleviation were observed in the
CuS@MSN-SCS group, akin to the MiNo group, with the
notable exception of neovascularization in alveolar bone
regeneration. Although CuS@MSN-SCS did not demonstrate
a significant improvement in most aspects of treatment
effectiveness compared to the use of the MiNo, it effectively
addressed the drawbacks associated with MiNo as an
antibiotic. Issues such as bacterial resistance66 were mitigated
by CuS@MSN-SCS, as its antibacterial activity was achieved
through a notable increase in intracellular ROS levels.
Furthermore, CuS@MSN-SCS could release Cu2+ and SCS
to promote angiogenesis, along with the release of SiO4
4−(as
shown in Figure S6) and SCS to facilitate alveolar bone
regenerationachievements that surpass the capabilities of
antibiotics, such as MiNo. Therefore, CuS@MSN-SCS
possesses advancements that MiNo lacks.
The nanoformulation developed in this work, CuS@MSN-
SCS, emerges as a revolutionary biomaterial in the field of oral
antimicrobial development. Unlike many existing studies that
predominantly depend on the generation of ROS via metal
ions through the Fenton-like reaction for treatment, such as
oncotherapy,67−69 this study not only achieves antibacterial
effects through the Fenton-like reaction mediated by copper
ions but also utilizes the angiogenic ability of copper ions to
promote bone formation. Furthermore, not limited to CuS,
this innovative nanomaterial integrates antibacterial activity
with the potential for angiogenesis and alveolar bone
regeneration by coating CuS nanoparticles with MSNs and
subsequent surface modification using amino groups and SCS.
During the local treatment of periodontitis, the release of Cu2+,
SiO4
4−, and SCS contributes to the multifaceted therapeutic
effects of the formulation without significant toxicity. None-
theless, there are some drawbacks associated with this
approach. Notably, the impact of CuS nanoparticles’ size and
silica shell’s thickness on periodontitis treatment, encompass-
ing aspects such as antibacterial activity, angiogenesis, and
alveolar bone regeneration, requires further investigation.
Additionally, the quantity of SCS adsorbed onto the surface
of CuS@MSN-NH2, along with methods to regulate this
adsorption, necessitate exploration to optimize treatment
effectiveness.
CONCLUSIONS
In summary, a nanoformulation CuS@MSN-SCS was
developed as a promising treatment for eradicating bacteria
biofilm and promoting alveolar bone regeneration in
periodontitis. The synthesis of CuS@MSN-SCS involved
coating CuS nanoparticles with MSNs and subsequent surface
modification using amino groups and SCS. A comprehensive
physicochemical characterization confirmed the successful
synthesis of CuS@MSN-SCS. In vitro antibacterial experiments
verified the significant antibacterial activity of CuS@MSN-SCS
against F. nucleatum through activating intracellular ROS
production. Furthermore, in vivo implantation of CuS@MSN-
SCS showed its effectiveness in reducing inflammation,
accelerating neovascularization, and promoting alveolar bone
regeneration without causing notable biotoxicity to major
organs. These results highlight the substantial therapeutic
potential of CuS@MSN-SCS in the treatment of periodontitis,
positioning it as an efficient biomaterial with promising clinical
applications in the future.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Synthesis of CuS@MSN-SCS. Synthesis of SCS. SCS was
synthesized following previously reported methods with slight
adjustments.29 Briefly, a total of 5 mL of chlorosulfonic acid
(HClSO3, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, China) was dropwise
added to 50 mL of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF; Shanghai
Xianding Biotechnology, China) in an ice bath and stirred for 15
min. The resulting mixture was then transferred dropwise into a
solution containing 50 mL of formamide (Macklin, China), 2 mL of
formic acid (Macklin, China), and 1 g of chitosan (MW: 2 × 105,
Golden-Shell Pharmaceutical, China) and stirred at 50 °C for 2 h
under a nitrogen atmosphere. Subsequently, 300 mL of ethanol was
added to the reaction mixture to precipitate the products. The
precipitate was obtained via vacuum filtration and washed using
ethanol three times. The obtained precipitate was redissolved in
ultrapure water, and the pH was adjusted to neutral using 1 M NaOH
and 1 M HCl. The resulting solution was centrifuged to remove any
undissolved material, and the supernatant was concentrated using a
rotary evaporator. After dialysis against water for 3 days and then
lyophilization, the resultant product was obtained and stored at 4 °C
for future use.
Fabrication of CuS@MSN Core−Shell Nanoparticles. To
synthesize citrate-coated CuS nanoparticles, a mixture solution was
prepared by dissolving 0.2 mmol of copper(II) chloride dihydrate
(CuCl2·2H2O, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, China) and 0.136
mmol of trisodium citrate dihydrate (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent,
China) in 200 mL of ultrapure water. The solution was agitated at
room temperature until a light blue mixture without sediment was
obtained. Subsequently, 0.2 mmol of sodium sulfide nonahydrate
(Na2S·9H2O, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, China) was added to the
reaction mixture and stirred for 5 min at room temperature. The
resulting product was then heated at 90 °C under stirring at 800 rpm
for 15 min, resulting in the formation of a dark-green solution
containing citrate-coated CuS nanoparticles.
To fabricate CuS@MSN, a total of 0.7 g of CTAB (Aladdin,
China) was added to 65 mL of the CuS-contained solution and stirred
at 40 °C under 500 rpm for 4.5 h. Subsequently, the reaction mixture
was further treated with 3 mL of ethanol, 100 μL of a 30 mg/mL
NaOH solution, and 100 μL of TEOS (Aladdin, China), followed by
maintaining the temperature at 40 °C for 1.5 h. Finally, the mixture
was aged at 4 °C overnight and washed thoroughly with ethanol to
remove CTAB, resulting in the formation of CuS@MSN.
Fabrication of CuS@MSN-SCS. To modify amino groups on the
surface of CuS@MSN nanoparticles, APTES (Aladdin, China) was
used. Initially, CuS@MSN nanoparticles obtained from 130 mL of
CuS nanoparticles were redissolved in 50 mL of water. Then, 50 μL of
APTES was added to the solution, followed by stirring at 400 rpm for
24 h at 50 °C. Subsequently, the product was washed three times with
water to obtain APTES-modified CuS@MSN nanoparticles, hereafter
referred to as CuS@MSN-NH2. Following this, 1 mg of SCS was
added directly into 50 mL of the CuS@MSN-NH2 solution and
stirred at room temperature for 1 day. Lastly, CuS@MSN-SCS was
obtained after the product was washed three times with water.

Antibacterial Analysis of CuS@MSN-SCS. Bacterial Culture. F.
nucleatum (ATCC 25586) was used to assess the antibacterial
capacity of CuS@MSN-SCS. Trypticase soy broth (TSB) medium
(Solarbio, China) was employed as both the liquid and solid culture
medium for F. nucleatum. The bacterial strain was streaked onto TSB
agar plates and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 2 days.
Subsequently, a single bacterial colony was selected and inoculated
into fresh liquid culture medium using an inoculating loop, followed
by incubation at 37 °C for 1 day. The resulting bacterial suspension
was diluted with TSB medium until the OD600 value of the bacteria
solution exceeded that of pure TSB media by 0.03−0.05.

Antibiofilm Effect of CuS@MSN-SCS against F. nucleatum. To
promote the formation of F. nucleatum biofilm, 100 μL of bacterial
suspension was added into a 96-well plate and incubated for 3 days
without agitation. The culture medium was refreshed after 2 days.
Subsequently, the biofilm formed at the bottom of the wells was
gently rinsed three times with PBS to eliminate planktonic bacteria.
Then, a mixed solution consisting of 100 μL of refreshed culture
medium and 100 μL of PBS containing various concentrations of
CuS@MSN-SCS (0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 μg/mL) was
added to the wells, and the biofilm was cocultured with the CuS@
MSN-SCS solution for 24 h. After incubation, the solution was
aspirated, and the crystal violet staining method was used to assess the
biomass of the biofilm. Briefly, 100 μL of 0.1% crystal violet solution
(Meilunbio, China) was applied to each well to stain the biofilm for
10 min. Subsequently, each well was thoroughly rinsed with ultrapure
water and dried at room temperature. Then, 150 μL of ethanol was
added to the wells for redyeing at room temperature. After 20 min,
100 μL of the ethanol solution was transferred to a new 96-well plate,
and the optical density of the crystal violet solution was assessed at
590 nm with a microplate reader (Bio Tek, Synergy TM H1/H1M).
The measurements were performed in triplicate (n = 3).
To evaluate the antibiofilm effect, a live/dead fluorescent staining
assay was conducted. Briefly, the developed biofilms were washed
thrice with PBS. Next, at room temperature, the LIVE/DEAD
BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit L13152 (Invitrogen, Molecular
Probes, Inc.) was applied to each well, and the biofilms were stained
away from light for 15 min. Following this, the PBS was aspirated
from the wells, and a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM,
Leica TCS SP8, Germany) was used to observe the stained biofilms.
LAS AF software (Leica Microsystems CMS GmbH) was utilized to
capture the CLSM images of biofilms. Subsequently, the quantifica-
tion of CLSM images was performed in triplicate (n = 3) using ImageJ
software.
Evaluation of Intracellular ROS Formation. To evaluate the
generation of ROS within bacteria, the intracellular ROS probe
DCFH-DA (Reactive Oxygen Species Kit, Solarbio, China) was
employed. A bacterial suspension (200 μL) was cocultured with PBS
containing various concentrations (0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300
μg/mL) of CuS@MSN-SCS for 24 h. Subsequently, the bacterial
suspensions were thoroughly mixed and split equally into two parts:
one for bacterial count analysis using the plate counting method, and
the other for ROS detection. For the plate counting method, 100 μL
of the suspension mixture was extracted, diluted with PBS, and
subsequently plated onto TSB agar plates. The plates were incubated
at 37 °C for 48 h, followed by counting the number of bacterial
colonies (n = 3) in each group. For ROS detection, the bacterial
suspensions were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min. After removal of
the supernatant, F. nucleatum was resuspended and coincubated with
the DCFH-DA solution for 20 min following the manufacturer’s
instructions. Afterward, the suspensions were rinsed with PBS,
followed by measuring the fluorescence intensity using a microplate
reader, with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 525 nm. Finally, the
fluorescence intensity was calculated by dividing the total fluorescence
intensity by the bacterial count.

Therapeutic Effect of CuS@MSN-SCS for Periodontitis
Treatment In Vivo. 8-week-old male SD rats were obtained from
Jinan PengYue Experimental Animal Raising Farm (China). The SD
rats were housed at a consistent temperature of 25 ± 1 °C with a
relative humidity of 60−70%, under a 12-h light-dark cycle. The
Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The Affiliated
Hospital of Qingdao University approved all animal-related
experimental protocols (Laboratory animal use license number:
QYFY WZLL 28194). The SD rats were randomly divided into
four groups: (i) Blank group, healthy rats without periodontitis; (ii)
Periodontitis group (negative control), periodontitis rats treated with
0.9% saline solution; (iii) CuS@MSN-SCS group, periodontitis rats
treated with CuS@MSN-SCS; (iv) MiNo group (positive control),
periodontitis rats treated with MiNo (Periocline, Sunstar Inc., Osaka,
Japan). Pluronic F-127 (Sigma-Aldrich) at a concentration of 0.2 g/
mL was mixed with 0.9% saline solution and stirred overnight at 4 °C.
The resulting solution gelled at room temperature, serving as the
blank gel. In the CuS@MSN-SCS group, 250 μg/mL CuS@MSN-
SCS was added to the blank gel, creating the CuS@MSN-SCS gel.
To induce experimental periodontitis in rats via the ligature-
induced periodontitis model, 4−0 silk sutures were subgingivally
ligated around the cervical region of bilateral second molars (M2) of
the maxillary for 2 weeks. Subsequently, the silk sutures were
removed, and the ligation sites underwent various local treatments
every 2−4 days for a total of 10 treatments. 100 μL of blank gel, 100
μL of CuS@MSN-SCS gel, or 100 μL of MiNo were injected into the
buccal and palatal sides of periodontal pockets of M2. On the 30th
day, the rats were euthanized, and both the maxillae were surgically
removed and dissected for further experimental analysis. The same
operators performed all surgical procedures on anesthetized rats.
The specimens were analyzed using a Quantum GX2 micro-CT
imaging system (PerkinElmer) to observe bone regeneration. The
maxillary sample was securely fixed on the sample bed, and a 360°
rotation scanning was performed. CT image reconstruction was
performed using the Data Viewer software (Quantum GX2,
PerkinElmer). Based on the reconstructed micro-CT images,
measurements were taken using ImageJ software to determine the
distances from CEJ to ABC on the buccal side of each root of M2.
The rats’ weights were monitored during the treatment, and the
weight gain was calculated at the end of the animal experiment. After
maxillary specimens were collected, the maxillae were immediately
fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution (Biosharp, China) for 48 h
for histological analysis. Subsequently, decalcification with a 10%
ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid solution (Solarbio, China) was
performed at room temperature for 4 weeks. The decalcified maxillae
then underwent dehydration, followed by being embedded in paraffin
and sectioned parallel to the long axis of the crown of M2. H&E
(Solarbio, China) and Masson staining (Solarbio, China) were carried
out following the respective instructions. The immune response score
was evaluated by three individuals, referring to ISO 10993 guidelines,
based on the assessment of immune cells. The measurement of the
degraded collagen area was conducted via ImageJ software. The safety
of CuS@MSN-SCS in vivo was assessed by monitoring the changes in
body weight and major organs.
Immunohistochemical staining of TNF-α and CD31 was further
employed to analyze the inflammatory responses and vascularization
in the periodontitis model. The sections were incubated overnight
with rabbit anti-TNF-α (Servicebio, China) or rabbit anti-CD31
(Servicebio, China) primary antibody. HRP-labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit
IgG (Servicebio, China) was employed as a secondary antibody for 50
min. Using ImageJ software, relative quantification of immunohis-
tochemical staining images was performed by measuring average
optical density.
Statistical Analysis. The data was presented as mean ± standard
deviation. GraphPad Prism version 8.3.0 Software was utilized for
statistical analysis. Differences between groups were analyzed via one-
way ANOVA with Tukey’s test. Asterisks were used to indicate the
significance levels: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

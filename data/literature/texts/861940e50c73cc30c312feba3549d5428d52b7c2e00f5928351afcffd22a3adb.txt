Large-Ring Cyclodextrins as Chiral Selectors for Enantiomeric
Pharmaceuticals
Christian Sonnendecker, Sebastian Thgrmann, C8dric Przybylski, Franziska D. Zitzmann,
Nicole Heinke, Yannick Krauke, Kate Monks, Andrea A. Robitzki, Detlev Belder, and
Wolfgang Zimmermann*
Abstract: Large-ring cyclodextrins (CD) are cyclic glucans
composed of 9 or more a-1,4-linked glucose units. They are
minor
side
products
of
bacterial
glucanotransferases
(CGTases, EC 2.4.1.19) and have previously been available
only in very small amounts for studies of their properties in
supramolecular complex formation reactions. We engineered
a CGTase to synthesize mainly large-ring CD facilitating their
preparation in larger amounts. By reversed phase chromatog-
raphy, we obtained single CD samples composed of 10 to 12
glucose units (CD10, CD11, and CD12) with a purity of
> 90%. Their identity was confirmed by high resolution mass
spectrometry and fragmentation analysis. We demonstrated the
non-toxicity of CD10–CD12 for human cell lines by a cell
proliferation assay and impedimetric monitoring. We then
showed that CD10 and CD11 are efficient chiral selectors for
the capillary electrophoretic separation of the enantiomeric
pharmaceuticals fluvastatin, mefloquine, carvedilol, and pri-
maquine.
CD are a-1,4-linked cyclic glucans with a degree of
polymerization (DP) between 6 to > 100. Branched a-1,6-
CD have also been described.[1] In contrast to larger CD, CD6,
CD7, and CD8 (a-, b-, g-CD) have been studied extensively
and are produced commercially.[2] They form cone-shaped
structures with a hydrophobic cavity and an outer hydrophilic
area, enabling the reversible binding of hydrophobic mole-
cules in their cavity to form supramolecular host–guest
complexes. Thereby the stability and solubility of the guest
compounds can be increased, allowing the application of
these small CD in the food, pharma, and cosmetics indus-
tries.[3] CD composed of 9 to 12 glucose units show a larger
variety in their structures. In contrast to the smaller CD with
syn-relative conformations of the glucopyranoside units,
CD10 and larger CD show band flips and kinks and are
devoid of a single cavity.[4,5] CD9–CD13 form complexes with
ibuprofen[6] and digitoxin[7] and complexes of CD9, CD10,
and CD11 with dodecaborate anions have also been de-
scribed.[8] The potential of CD for the capillary electro-
phoretic (CE) separation of chiral compounds has already
been demonstrated.[9,10] CE has also been previously used to
determine inclusion complex formation constants of CD9–
CD13.[6] CD are formed by the enzymatic conversion of starch
by CGTases through intramolecular transglycosylation reac-
tions. At initial reaction conditions, CGTases produce CD of
a wide size spectrum.[11] Larger CD are rapidly degraded by
the enzyme in a reverse reaction to linear glucans which are
subsequently converted to smaller CD.[11–13] Mixtures of
mostly CD6, CD7, and CD8 are therefore the final products
of the CGTase reaction with starch while larger CD with a DP
of 9 and above can only be obtained in very small
quantities.[4,13] Owing to the lack of efficient synthesis and
separation techniques, only limited information has been
available on the properties of the larger CD as supramolec-
ular host compounds.
Herein, we describe the biocatalytic synthesis and prep-
aration of CD10–CD12 by employing a genetically engi-
neered CGTase for their specific production and a strategy for
their efficient purification by reversed phase chromatography.
The toxicity of CD10–CD12 for human cell lines was
investigated and their performance as chiral selectors in CE
for a series of enantiomeric pharmaceuticals was evaluated.
The use of the CGTase G825 variant Y183R engineered to
specifically produce larger CD allowed us to prepare large-
ring CD in sufficient amounts for further analysis.[12] Since the
CGTase synthesized CD8 as the smallest cyclic product,
a high proportion of larger CD in the reaction mixtures could
be obtained facilitating their subsequent purification.[12] The
very small amounts of large-ring CD produced by commer-
cially available CGTases has been a major bottleneck in the
study of these novel supramolecular host compounds. Ueda
and co-workers isolated CD9 and larger CD from a commer-
cial CD mixture which involved the degradation of the non-
cyclic glucans by yeast and the addition of complexing agents
to remove CD6–CD8, resulting in a yield of only 0.03% CD9

The CD mixture used for subsequent purification by prepa-
rative HPLC consisted of 27% CD10–CD12 (Figure 1a).
We then achieved the purification of CD10–CD12 by
reversed phase chromatography using a preparative C18H
250 X 20 mm column in a single step with a purity of 95%
(CD10) and 91% (CD11 and CD12) (: 0.7% S.D., n = 5)
(Figure 1b and Supporting Information Figure S1). By 10
repeated runs, 142 mg CD10, 155 mg CD11, and 149 mg
CD12 were obtained from 2 g of the CD mixture. Previously
described methods had required several different chromatog-
raphy steps with various eluent concentrations.[7,15,17] Small
amounts of large-ring CD could be obtained by ion exchange
chromatography,[15] and CD8–CD10 could be separated using
a silica-bonded [60]fullerene stationary phase.[18] The separa-
tion of CD9 in a single step was achieved using a normal phase
amino column however with high concentrations of acetoni-
trile as eluent.[16] In contrast, the C18 RP-HPLC phases were
capable of separating the large-ring CD with low concen-
trations of methanol as eluent.
We confirmed the identity and high purity of the isolated
large-ring CD by high resolution electrospray-mass spec-
trometry (ESI-MS) and ESI-MS/MS. Analysis of the purified
CD10, CD11, and CD12 samples by ESI-MS showed that the
most prominent ions observed at m/z 833.2539, 914.2802, and
995.3068 could be unambiguously ascribed to a double
sodiated form ([M + 2Na]2+) of CD10 (Supporting Informa-
tion, Figure S2), CD11 (Supporting Information, Figure S3),
and CD12 (Supporting Information, Figure S4), respectively.
The ion assignments were deducted both from high mass
accuracy (, 2.5 ppm), experimental and theoretical isotopic
pattern comparison, and by a systematic fragmentation step
(MS/MS). As example, upon MS/MS experiments, m/z
833.2522 ([M + 2Na]2+) lead to a charge reduction (sodium
loss), and then to a sequential and constant mass loss of
162.05 Da (1 +), that is, C/Z or C/Y fragments according to
Domon and Costello nomenclature,[19,20] reflecting a 1,4-
glycosidic bond cleavage from the cyclic structures (Support-
ing Information, Figure S5a).[20] Similar results were obtained
for CD11 and CD12 samples (Supporting Information, Fig-
ure S6 and S7). Because of the high mass accuracy and by the
fragmentation experiments, small amounts of branched large-
ring CD with glycosidic 1,6 links were also detected in the
samples as a minor product of the CGTase.

CD with two human cell lines by performing an XTT cell
proliferation test as well as impedimetric analyses to monitor
changes in cellular adhesion and cell contacts. Our results
showed no toxic effects of the purified CD10, CD11, and
CD12 samples and of a mixture of CD8–CD15 (Figure 2a).

cytotoxic effects, none of the CD exhibited toxic effects on the
HEK 293 and MCF-7 cell lines. It has been shown previously

We finally investigated the potential application of the
large-ring CD as chiral selectors for enantiomeric pharma-
ceuticals using CE as previously described for CD6–CD9.[10]
Results of the enantioselective separation of twelve pharma-
ceuticals by CD10–CD12 in comparison to CD6–CD8 are
shown in Figure 3a. We found that the enantiomers of several
of the malaria drugs tested could be baseline-separated using
the isolated large-ring CD. While none of the CD was suitable
for the separation of all of the pharmaceuticals tested, the
highest separation efficiency was obtained with CD10 and
CD11 allowing the complete separation of four of the twelve
pharmaceuticals, while among the small CD, only CD7 was
capable to completely separate salsolinol enantiomers. Sal-
butamol and ropivacaine showed no separation of enantio-
mers with any of the CD tested. Mefloquine, carvedilol,
primaquine, and fluvastatin could be completely separated by
the large-ring CD, while only partial separations could be
observed with CD6–CD8. Ibuprofen could be partially
separated by CD12 while very high resolution factors were
achieved for primaquine and mefloquine with CD10.[25]
Electropherograms of the separation of mefloquine enantio-
mers by the CD are shown in Figure 3b. The increasing
flexibility of the CD rings concomitantly with their size will
render even larger CD likely to be unsuitable for enantio-
meric discrimination. However, CD10–CD12 are unique in
showing a ring architecture different from CD6–CD8 while
still providing sufficient rigidity to form stable complexes and
to act as chiral selectors. A derivatization of the large-ring CD
for example, by methylation, acetylation or sulfonation as
shown previously using smaller CD could possibly improve
their separation efficiency even further.[26]
In summary, the strategy of optimizing the biocatalytic
synthesis of large-ring CD using an engineered CGTase and
the development of an efficient separation technique allowed
us to obtain, for the first time, large-ring CD samples in high
purity and in sufficient amounts for further analyses and
application studies. We confirmed their structure by high
resolution ESI-MS and described the presence of branched
large-ring CD as a minor side product of the CGTase
cyclization reaction. The CD were demonstrated to be non-
toxic to human cell lines and CD10 and CD11 were effective
for the enantiomeric separation of several enantiomeric
pharmaceuticals by CE, demonstrating their potential use in
microfluidic lab-on-a-chip devices incorporating mammalian
cells.

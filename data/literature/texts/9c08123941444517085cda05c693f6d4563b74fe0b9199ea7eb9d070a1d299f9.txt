remains unexplored. 
 Besides the reducing agent and solution pH, the solvent for 
the reaction may also affect the formation kinetics of Au NCs, 
as demonstrated in several recent studies. For example, Qian 
et al. [ 40 ] investigated the effect of solvent on the size distribution 
of Au NC precursors prior to size-focusing. They found that 
mix-sized Au NCs in the range of 38–102 Au atoms were pref-
erentially formed in acetone, while much smaller Au NCs in 
the range of 10–39 Au atoms were formed in methanol. More 
recently, Liu et al. [ 41 ] studied the solvent effect on the size of 
ﬁ nal Au NCs in a Brust-like method. They found that small-
sized Au(I)-thiolate complexes were formed in tetrahydrofuran, 
acetone, or ethyl acetate, and a subsequent reduction of such 
small complexes led to the formation of Au 25 (SR) 18 . By com-
parison, large-sized Au(I)-thiolate complexes were formed in 
methanol or acetonitrile, and upon the reduction, a large-sized 
Au 144 (SR) 60 was formed. This study suggests the feasibility of 
synthesizing metastable Au NCs via controlling the solvent con-
ditions, though no conclusion of how the solvent conditions 
affect the size/structure of Au(I)-thiolate complexes and the 
ﬁ nal Au NCs has been drawn. 
 Here, we report an efﬁ cient protocol to produce a new Au 
NC species, Au 29 (SR) 20 , by using a mixed solvent to tailor the 
formation kinetics of Au NCs in the CO-reduction method. The 
key strategy is to modify the polarity of the reaction medium 
by introducing a weakly polar solvent (methanol, dielectric con-
stant of 32.35 at 293 K) to water (dielectric constant of 80.37 
at 293 K), which could alter the reaction pathway by tailoring 
the size/structure of Au(I)-thiolate complexes, leading to the 
formation of Au 29 (SG) 20 . This synthetic protocol is also appli-
cable to other mixed solvent systems such as water–ethanol and 
water–acetone. 
 We used glutathione (GSH), a natural tripeptide, as the 
model thiolate ligand for the synthesis of Au 29 (SG) 20 . In a typical 
 Thiolate-protected gold nanoclusters or thiolated Au NCs are 
ultrasmall particles (<2 nm) stabilized by a particular thiolate 
ligand. [ 1–3 ] They consist of a few to several hundreds of Au 
atoms and are typically denoted as Au n (SR) m , where SR is the 
thiolate ligand, and  n and  m are speciﬁ c integers. Thiolated Au 
NCs in this sub-2-nm size regime possess discrete electronic 
states, and thus exhibit unique molecule-like properties, such 
as quantized charging [ 4,5 ] and strong luminescence. [ 6,7 ] The 
electronic states and physicochemical properties of thiolated Au 
NCs are highly dependent on their size. [ 8–10 ] Therefore, the syn-
thesis of Au NCs with well-deﬁ ned sizes is of interest to both 
basic [ 11,12 ] and applied [ 13,14 ] research in NC chemistry, such as 
resolving the NC structure [ 15–19 ] and building the size–property 
correlation of the NCs. [ 9,20,21 ] 
 Separation techniques such as liquid chromatography [ 22–24 ] 
and gel electrophoresis [ 9,25–27 ] have been used to isolate discrete-
sized Au NCs from polydisperse Au NCs. For example, Negishi 
et al. [ 9 ] used polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) to 
separate polydisperse Au NCs protected by glutathione (GSH) 
[denoted as Au n (SG) m ], and successfully obtained nine Au NC 
species, including Au 10–12 (SG) 10–12 , Au 15 (SG) 13 , Au 18 (SG) 14 , 
Au 22 (SG) 16 , Au 22 (SG) 17 , Au 25 (SG) 18 , Au 29 (SG) 20 , Au 33 (SG) 22 , and 
Au 39 (SG) 24 . However, only a small quantity of Au NC product 
(in microgram scale) could be obtained in this separation-
assisted approach. Direct synthesis of discrete-sized Au NCs 
is more efﬁ cient for the production of Au NCs in bulk quanti-
ties (up to gram scale). [ 28 ] Recently, thermodynamic-selection or 
size-focusing strategy has been developed to directly produce Au 
NCs with a well-deﬁ ned size in the reaction solution. Successful 
examples include the synthesis of Au 25 (SR) 18 , Au 38 (SR) 24 , and 
Au 144 (SR) 60 . [ 29–35 ] However, thiolated Au NCs with other sizes, 
such as Au 15 (SR) 13 , Au 18 (SR) 14 , and Au 29 (SR) 20 , could not be 
obtained from the size-focusing protocol as these NCs are less 
tolerant (or metastable) to the hash size-focusing or thiol-etching 
process. There is therefore a pressing need to develop milder 
synthetic strategies for such metastable NC species, which could 
further enrich the size spectrum of thiolated Au NCs.

 The polarity of the mixed solvent was varied by the volume 
fraction of methanol,  f m , in the solvent, which could be used to 
inﬂ uence the formation kinetics of Au NCs in the CO-reduction 
method. Methanol is a polar protic solvent and is miscible with 
water. It has a dielectric constant of 32.35 at 293 K, which is 
much lower than that of water (80.37 at 293 K) (Table S2, Sup-
porting Information). The introduction of a certain amount of 
methanol to water can decrease the apparent dielectric constant 
of the mixed solvent. According to the linear approximation 
assumption of the dielectric constant of a mixed solvent, [ 45 ] 
the apparent dielectric constant of the methanol–water system 
decreases linearly with the methanol fraction. For instance, the 
dielectric constant of a mixed solvent with 20%  f m is 70.77 [see 
Table S2 (Supporting Information) for the calculated dielectric 
constants of the mixed solvents used in this study]. A lower 
apparent dielectric constant suggests a lower polarity of the 
mixed solvent, which could screen out the charges of Au(I)-SG 
complexes and disrupt the hydration layer surrounding them

 Besides the polarity of the mixed solvent, we have also iden-
tiﬁ ed several other critical experimental conditions for the 
high-yield synthesis of Au 29 (SG) 20 . First, the optimized GSH-
to-Au ratio or  R [GSH]/[Au] for the synthesis of Au 29 (SG) 20 was 1.5 
(Figure S4a, red line), if the volume fraction of methanol  f m 
was kept at 20%. No Au NCs were formed at a higher  R [GSH]/
[Au] ratio (e.g., 2.0; Figure S4a, blue line, Supporting Informa-
tion). This observation could be understood from the strong 
resistance of Au(I)-SG complexes against the reduction by 
CO, which is known to be a weak reducing agent. In contrast, 
a lower  R [GSH]/[Au] ratio (e.g., 1.0) led to a faster but less con-
trolled reduction kinetics, leading to the formation of a mix-
ture of Au NCs in different sizes, as evidenced by its feature-
less absorption spectrum (Figure S4a, black line, Supporting 
Information). 
 Second, the optimized reaction time (when  R [GSH]/[Au] = 1.5 
and  f m = 20%) for the synthesis of Au 29 (SG) 20 was ≈16 h, as 
suggested by the time-course analyses of the absorption spectra 
of the reaction solution (Figure S4b, Supporting Information). 
After 1 h of reaction, a distinct absorption peak at ≈723 nm 
was observed for the Au NCs formed in the reaction solution. 
This absorption peak is very close to the characteristic absorp-
tions of Au 33 (SG) 22 reported by Negishi et al. [ 9 ] As the reaction 
proceeded, the absorption peak progressively shifted to a lower 
wavelength, and was ﬁ nally stabilized at ≈696 nm after 16 h. 
A further increase in the reaction time (e.g., to 20 h) did not 
change the absorption spectra of the resulting Au NCs, and 
16 h was therefore selected as the optimized reaction time for 
Au 29 (SG) 20 . The time-course analysis of the absorption spectra 
suggests a better stability of Au 29 (SG) 20 over Au 33 (SG) 22 , which 
could drive the slow size evolution of Au 33 (SG) 22 to Au 29 (SG) 20 
possibly via a thermodynamic selection or a size-focusing pro-
cess in a less harsh condition. 
 In summary, we presented here a scalable synthetic pro-
tocol for a new Au NC species, Au 29 (SG) 20 , which could not 
be obtained in previously devised thermodynamic-selection or 
size-focusing protocol. The key strategy in our protocol was to 
adjust the composition of the mixed solvent in the CO-reduc-
tion method, leading to a desired polarity of the solvent that can 
induce the preferential formation of Au 29 (SG) 20 . Polar protic 
solvents, such as methanol, ethanol, and acetone, were found to 
be capable of facilitating the selective synthesis of Au 29 (SG) 20 . 
This study is interesting not only because it provides an efﬁ -
cient and scalable synthetic protocol for Au 29 (SG) 20 but also 
because it lends insights into the effect of solvent polarity on 
the size-tailoring of Au NCs. The knowledge generated in this 
study may also be applied for the synthesis of other metastable 
Au NCs in other reaction systems.

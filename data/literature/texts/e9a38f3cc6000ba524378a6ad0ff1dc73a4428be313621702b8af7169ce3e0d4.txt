A B S T R A C T   
For most approved subcutaneously (SC) administered drug products in the US, the recommended injection sites 
(i.e., abdomen, thigh, and upper arm) are usually based on experience from phase 3 trials. Relative bioavail­
ability data directly comparing the pharmacokinetics (PK) of different SC injection sites are often not available 
and the underlying mechanisms that may affect SC absorption have not been systematically investigated. In this 
study, we surveyed clinical PK data (AUC, Cmax, and Tmax) for SC administered drug products including thera­
peutic proteins and peptides based on literature and FDA database. The PK data after abdominal injection was 
used as a reference to determine the relative bioavailability of SC injections to the arm and thigh. The survey 
retrieved 19 immunoglobulin G (IgGs), 18 peptides/small proteins (molecular weight < 16 kDa), and 8 non-IgG 
proteins that had available clinical PK data from multiple SC injection sites. Among these, 5 (26%) IgGs, 9 (50%) 
peptides/small proteins, and 3 (38%) non-IgG proteins, exhibited injection site-dependent PK (i.e. PK differed by 
injection sites). Correlation analyses revealed that the PK of peptides/small proteins undergoing rapid SC ab­
sorption (Tmax ≤2 h), elimination (CL/F ≥39 L/h) or low plasma protein binding were more sensitive to in­
jection sites. Similarly, non-IgG proteins (molecular weight ≥16 kDa) with high CL/F and low Tmax are 
associated with high risk of injection site-dependent SC absorption. IgGs with T1/2 < 15 days or Tmax < 5 days are 
more likely to show injection site-dependent SC absorption. Positive charge of the drug molecule (isoelectric 
point ≥8) may reduce SC absorption from all three injection sites but is not associated with high risk of injection 
site-dependent SC absorption. In summary, the results suggested that regional differences in pre-systemic 
catabolism and local SC blood flow potentially contribute injection site-dependent SC absorption of peptides/ 
small proteins while local lymphatic flow and FcRn binding likely contribute to site-dependent SC absorption of 
IgGs.   
1. Introduction 
There has been an increasing trend towards subcutaneous (SC) de­
livery of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) in recent years versus intrave­
nous (IV) administration [1]. In addition, most approved peptide drugs 
are administered via SC route. SC drug products can be self-administered 
by a patient or administered by another person at home, which is an 
advantage over IV administration especially for chronic use. Commonly 
used regions for SC injection include upper arm/deltoid, abdomen, and 
outer thighs. Unlike small molecule drugs, some SC administered ther­
apeutic proteins and peptides such as insulins [2], human growth hor­
mone [3], and monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) [4] exhibit a different 
extent and rate of absorption when administered from distinct injection 
sites (i.e. injection site-dependent absorption). 
For most approved SC administered drug products in the United 
States, relative bioavailability (BA) studies comparing pharmacokinetics 
(PK) among injection sites are usually not conducted. The approved 
injection sites are usually based on the injection sites used in Phase 3 
trials. Injection site rotation is often used in Phase 3 trials without PK 
data informing whether injection sites may affect subcutaneous 
bioavailability. In order to predict if a biologic product has SC injection 
site-dependent PK or not, a thorough understanding of underlying 
mechanisms of site-dependent SC absorption and identification of 
physicochemical properties and PK parameters of the biologic

The factors that influence the absorption of some biological drug 
products such as mAb following SC administration have been summa­
rized in several reviews [1,5–7]. The main cellular components of SC 
tissue, also named hypodermis, are adipose cells and, to a minor extent, 
fibroblasts, and macrophages [1]. Fibroblasts produce components of 
the extracellular matrix (ECM) such as collagen and glycosaminoglycans 
(GAGs). The ECM contains collagen, GAGs, and proteoglycans. Hyal­
uronic acid (HA) and chondroitin sulfate (CS) are the most common 
GAGs present in ECM. At physiological pH, HA, CS and proteoglycans 
are negatively charged while collagen is positively charged. The inter­
stitial space is overall negatively charged, resulting in faster transport of 
negatively charged biologics compared to positively charged ones. It is 
unknown whether there is a regional difference in electric charges in 
ECM that may affect SC absorption of biologics. 
Blood and lymph vessels are distributed throughout the hypodermis. 
Regional differences in SC blood and lymph flow have been reported, 
which may influence SC absorption of biologics [1,6]. For example, the 
average lymph flow rates from human anterior trunk, arm/shoulder, 
and thigh are 2.8, 2.0, and 4.2 cm/min, respectively [8]. In addition, SC 
adipose tissue blood flow (ATBF) in abdominal region is higher than that 
in femoral region, especially under postprandial state. Abdominal ATBF 
is maintained at >30% higher than femoral ATBF (4.0 versus 3.0 mL/ 
min/100 g adipose tissue) for at least 6 h post a meal [9]. 
The bioavailability of SC administered drug product can also be 
affected by presystemic catabolism (proteolysis or macrophage clear­
ance) that occurs at the injection site or/and during lymphatic transport. 
The proteins in SC interstitial space are qualitatively similar to those in 
human plasma although their concentrations are approximately 50% 
lower than that in plasma [10]. For example, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 
(DPP4), an enzyme responsible for glucagon-like peptide-1 catabolism, 
is detected in subcutaneous adipose tissue [11]. Therefore, peptides 
vulnerable to proteolysis in human plasma are expected to undergo 
presystemic catabolism in SC interstitial space and lymph fluid. SC 
bioavailability of parathyroid hormone, calcitonin, and insulin was 
improved by coadministration with aprotinin, a serine protease inhibi­
tor [12]. With more tertiary structure relative to peptides, mAbs and 
other therapeutic proteins are less vulnerable to proteolysis. However, 
with their large molecular weight (MW), macrophage uptake of protein 
drugs in hypodermis and lymphatics are expected. For example, 
phagocytosis-mediated catabolism of human growth hormone (hGH) 
and pegylated erythropoietin (PEG-EPO) was observed at subcutaneous 
administration sites and lymph of sheep and rats, respectively [13]. 
Neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) is expressed in endothelial cells and inter­
stitial macrophages [14]. Immunoglobulin G (IgGs), Fc- or albumin- 
fused proteins/peptides, and albumin bound peptides can be rescued 
by FcRn from lysosomal degradation in endothelial cells and macro­
phages. Studies in FcRn knockout mice showed 3-fold lower subcu­
taneous absolute bioavailability for an IgG1 mAb relative to wild-type 
mice (28.3% vs. 82.5%) [15], indicating an important role of FcRn 
binding in presystemic catabolism and SC absorption of IgGs. Also, Fc- or 
albumin-fusion and fatty acid- or cholesterol-conjugation (to improve 
albumin binding) have been successfully used to improve SC bioavail­
ability and prolong the elimination half-life of peptide drugs [6]. 
In addition to metabolic stability and FcRn binding, physiochemical 
factors such as MW, hydrophobicity, and electric charge may affect SC 
absorption of biologics [6]. Transport of a biologic in ECM is generally 
dictated by molecular size-based steric exclusion, nonspecific protein 
binding, and electrostatic interaction with the various ECM components 
[16]. From the interstitial space, there are two major pathways for the 
systemic absorption of a protein or peptide: transport through lymphatic 
vessels and diffusion across blood vessels. Molecules with MW <16 kDa 
are mainly absorbed by blood vessels while molecules with MW ≥16 
kDa are mainly absorbed by the lymphatic system [17]. In addition, 
FcRn-mediated transcytosis across the vascular endothelium plausibly 
contributes to SC absorption of IgGs and Fc- or albumin-fused proteins 
[1]. Besides physicochemical factors, formulation properties such as 
concentration of peptides and proteins, injection volume, excipients, 
and sustained-release formulation also can affect SC absorption of pro­
teins and peptides [13]. 
As discussed above, many factors may affect SC absorption of bi­
ologics but the regional differences in these factors contributing to in­
jection site-dependent absorption are not well known. The aims of the 
current study are: (1) to collect SC injection site-dependent clinical PK 
data from a survey of drug products including therapeutic proteins and 
peptides; (2) to identify the factors contributing to injection site- 
dependent absorption; and (3) to identify the characteristics of pro­
teins and peptides associated with increased risk of injection site- 
dependent absorption. Considering the significant roles of MW and 
FcRn binding in SC absorption of these drug products, we divided pep­
tides and proteins into three categories: (1) peptide and protein drugs 
with MW < 16 kDa, (2) non-IgG proteins and pegylated-peptides with 
MW ≥16 kDa, and (3) IgGs. 
2. Methods 
For SC drug products approved or reviewed by the FDA, the product 
labeling, clinical pharmacology reviews, and population PK (popPK) 
analysis results were surveyed to collect PK data. European public 
assessment 
reports 
database 
(https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/ 
medicines) was searched to identify SC drug products. The list of ther­
apeutic antibodies from the antibody society website (https://www.ant 
ibodysociety.org/resources/approved-antibodies/) was also used to 
identify SC administered antibodies. To identify drug candidates at late- 
stage development or drug candidates which were not submitted for 
regulatory review, a thorough literature search was conducted using 
PubMed and Google. For cases when the PK parameter ratios of different 
injection sites were available from both a dedicated clinical PK study 
and popPK analysis, the data from the dedicated PK study was used. For 
cases when data from two or more clinical PK studies were available, the 
data from the study with a larger sample size was used. When the PK 
data of different devices (e.g., prefilled syringe, autoinjector, and sy­
ringe) were available, the data of all devices were collected. 
Considering the MW cut-off of 16 kDa for blood flow- and lymph 
flow-mediated SC absorption, we used MW of 16 kDa to distinguish 
peptides/small proteins and non-IgG proteins. Of note, the peptides/ 
small proteins group of products contain peptides and proteins accord­
ing to the FDA’s regulation which interprets protein to mean alpha 
amino acid polymers with greater than 40 amino acids in size. Non-IgG 
proteins include enzymes, antibody fragments, fusion proteins, GM-CSF, 
erythropoietin and other therapeutic proteins. Fc-fused proteins were 
allocated to non-IgG proteins because their MWs were significantly 
different from that of IgGs. Pegylated peptides with MW ≥16 kDa were 
also assigned as non-IgG proteins. 
In our analysis, an arm/abdomen or thigh/abdomen ratio of geo­
metric means outside of 80–125% for AUC or Cmax or a ratio of median 
Tmax outside of 50–200% was defined as a difference in PK parameters. A 
product was determined to have injection site-dependent SC absorption 
if there was a difference in AUC, Cmax or Tmax. To identify factors which 
cause different SC absorption by injection sites, we conducted correla­
tion analyses between the AUC or Cmax ratios of two injection sites and 
PK parameters [apparent clearance (CL/F), time of Cmax (Tmax), elimi­
nation half-life (T1/2), absolute bioavailability (F), and fraction unbound 
in human plasma (fu,p)] or physicochemical parameters (MW, isoelectric 
point, number of amino acids, and number of disulfides). The PK pa­
rameters were mainly collected from drug labels. When the parameters 
were not available from drug labels, the values reported in literature 
were collected. MW, number of amino acids, and number of disulfides 
were collected from drug labels or DrugBank Online (https://go.drug 
bank.com/). Isoelectric point was collected from literature or calcu­
lated using Expasy (https://web.expasy.org/compute_pi/).

3. Results 
3.1. Drug products with clinical PK data from ≥2 SC injection sites 
The survey retrieved 46 peptides/small proteins (MW < 16 kDa), 40 
non-IgG proteins (MW ≥16 kDa), and 44 IgGs with clinical SC PK data 
available (Fig. 1). Furthermore, 18 peptides/small proteins, 8 non-IgG 
proteins, and 19 IgGs had clinical PK data available from ≥2 SC injec­
tion sites. Among these, 9 (50%) peptides/small proteins, 3 (38%) non- 
IgG proteins, and 5 (26%) IgGs, exhibited injection site-dependent ab­
sorption. Our survey results showed that for 85 among 130 (65%) SC 
administered therapeutic proteins and peptides, the recommended in­
jection sites were supported with phase 3 trials in the absence of injec­
tion site-based clinical relative bioavailability (BA) or popPK data. For 
38 among 45 remaining drug products, the PK data from different SC 
injection sites was collected in dedicated relative BA studies. However, 
for 3 peptides, 2 non-IgG proteins, and 2 IgGs, the thigh/abdomen or 
arm/abdomen ratios were determined by popPK analysis. 
3.2. Peptides/small proteins 
Among 18 peptides/small proteins with SC PK data from ≥2 injec­
tion sites, there are 7 insulin or insulin analogues, 5 glucagon-like 
peptide-1 (GLP-1) agonists, 1 glucagon analogue, and 1 amylin 
analogue for blood sugar control in diabetic patients (Table 1). The 
extensive clinical investigations on the PK of anti-diabetic peptides/ 
small proteins may have been triggered by the well-known injection site- 
dependent SC absorption of insulin [2]. However, clinical SC PK data 
from ≥2 injection sites is limited for other peptide/small protein drugs. 
The ranges of thigh or arm to abdomen ratios in AUC and Cmax were 
69–141% and 53–143%, respectively, for the 9 peptides exhibiting in­
jection site-dependent absorption (Table 1). The results showed that 
except for taspoglutide, AUC and/or Cmax of upper arm and thigh in­
jections were similar or lower compared to abdomen injection (Table 1). 
For the 9 peptides/small proteins without injection site-dependent ab­
sorption, the thigh/abdomen ratios in Cmax were consistently between 
80 and 90%. Except for regular insulin, all the other 17 peptides showed 
no site-dependent difference in Tmax. 
Correlation analyses with four PK parameters (CL/F, Tmax, T1/2, and 
F) revealed that peptides/small proteins undergoing rapid SC absorption 
(Tmax ≤2 h, except taspoglutide) or elimination (CL/F ≥39 L/h) were 
more likely to show a difference in drug exposure between thigh and 
abdomen injections (Figs. 2A-2D). The values of PK parameters used in 
correlation analyses are summarized in Table S1 (Supplementary ma­
terials). No obvious correlation with T1/2 or F was observed (Figs. 2E- 
2H). It is noteworthy that among the 9 peptides/small proteins without 
injection site-dependent SC absorption, insulin degludec, insulin dete­
mir, liraglutide and semaglutide are chemically conjugated with fatty 
acids (acylation), which significantly increases their binding to inter­
stitial proteins such as albumin (fu,p ≤0.02, Supplementary Table S1), 
Tmax (7–48 h) and T1/2 (55–89 h). 
Except for taspoglutide, all the other 17 peptides/small proteins 
showed arm/abdomen ratios in AUC within 80–125% and only regular 
insulin and insulin lispro showed arm/abdomen ratios in Cmax < 80% 
(Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1). For the 9 peptides/small proteins 
with injection site-dependent SC absorption, the rank in relative 
bioavailability is abdomen > arm > thigh. Consistently, correlation 
analyses showed that peptides/small proteins with Tmax ≤2 h (except 
for taspoglutide) or CL/F ≥39 L/h were more likely to show a difference 
in drug exposure between arm and abdomen injections (Supplementary 
Fig. S1). 
3.3. Non-IgG proteins 
The survey retrieved 8 non-IgG proteins that had available clinical 
PK data from multiple SC injection sites. For follitropin beta, only the 
relative bioavailability from vaginal and abdomen injections was 
available. For the other 7 non-IgG proteins, the ranges of thigh or arm to 
abdomen ratios in AUC and Cmax were 45–105% and 41–105% respec­
tively (Table 2). No injection site-dependent difference in Tmax was 
observed. No correlation analysis with PK parameters was conducted 
due to small sample size. In general, non-IgG proteins with injection site- 
dependent PK have shorter Tmax (3.5–15.5 h) and T1/2 (3.6–53 h) and 
higher CL/F (0.34–15.7 L/h) compared to non-IgG proteins without 
injection site-dependent PK (Supplementary Table S1). 
Fc-fusion (luspatercept and dulaglutide) and albumin-fusion (albi­
glutide) did not exhibit injection site-dependent SC absorption perhaps 
through FcRn-dependent rescue from lysosomal degradation in macro­
phages. For non-IgG proteins without Fc or albumin domain, previous 
study in thoracic lymph duct cannulated rat and dog models showed that 
SC catabolism in ECM and lymph nodes was the main cause for low 
bioavailability [33]. The low thigh/abdomen ratios in AUC and Cmax of 
somatropin and lipegfilgrastim suggest that there might be a regional 
difference in extracellular proteolytic enzymes and/or macrophage 
abundance (i.e. higher macrophage abundance in interstitial space and 
lymph of thigh region than that of arm and abdomen regions). It is 
interesting that lipegfilgrastim (pegylated filgrastim) showed a 32% 
lower bioavailability from thigh injection compared to abdomen injec­
tion. Although pegylation reduces macrophage uptake of non-IgG pro­
teins and increases their Tmax and T1/2 [34], it cannot rescue the non-IgG

4. Correlation analyses with physicochemical parameters for 
peptides/small proteins and non-IgGs 
Drug transport in human hypodermis is influenced by their physi­
cochemical parameters including molecular size, charge, higher struc­
ture, and hydrophobicity or nonspecific protein binding. Therefore, 
MW, number of amino acids, isoelectric point (pI), number of disulfides 
and fu,p were used for correlation analyses and the values of these pa­
rameters are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. A macromolecule 
with pI >7.4 is positively charged in hypodermis while a macromolecule 
with pI <7.4 is negatively charged. The number of disulfides represents 
the complexity of secondary and tertiary structure of proteins or pep­
tides. A protein with higher order structure is usually less vulnerable to 
proteolysis. As shown in Fig. 3A, a trend of negative linear correlation 
(R2 = 0.33) was observed between SC bioavailability and pI, indicating 
electrostatic interaction with SC ECM may decrease the bioavailability 
of positively charged peptides and proteins. However, no clear corre­
lation between site-dependent SC absorption and electric charge was 
observed for either peptides/small proteins or non-IgG proteins (Fig. 3A 
and B). Both positively charged (pI >7.4) and negatively charged (pI 
<7.4) peptides/proteins exhibited site-dependent SC absorption. The 
results suggest that for both peptides/small proteins and non-IgG pro­
teins, the contribution of electrostatic interaction to site-dependent SC 
absorption is limited. 
Correlation analysis with fu,p was conducted for peptides/small 
proteins only because the fu,p of non-IgG proteins and IgGs are usually 
not available. Nonspecific protein binding in ECM was not correlated 
with the extent of SC absorption of peptides/small proteins (Fig. 3C) but 
increased their Tmax (Fig. 3D). SC absorption rate of peptides/small 
proteins with low protein binding (fu,p > 40%) is more likely to be 
affected by SC injection sites. This may be explained that protein binding 
slows down the diffusion of peptides/small proteins into SC blood ves­
sels and makes the absorption rate less sensitive to regional difference in 
blood flow. 
For peptides/small proteins <16 kDa, no correlation between site- 
dependent SC absorption and MW was observed (Supplementary 
Figs. S2A and S2B). In contrast, for non-IgG proteins with MW ≥16 kDa, 
a lower MW was likely associated with a lower bioavailability from 
thigh injection compared to abdominal injection. This is not surprising 
because most high MW non-IgG proteins used in the correlation analysis 
are fused with Fc or albumin, or pegylated, which may reduce the risk of 
site-dependent SC absorption. Furthermore, no obvious relationship 
between site-dependent SC absorption and amino acid number or di­
sulfide number was observed (Supplementary Figs. S2C – S2F). 
4.1. IgGs 
Among 19 IgGs with multi-site PK data available, 5 IgGs showed 
injection site-dependent PK (Table 3 and Fig. 4). The ranges of thigh or 
arm to abdomen ratios in AUC and Cmax were 78–141% and 75–152%, 
respectively, for these 5 IgGs (Table 3). Only the thigh/abdomen Cmax 
ratio of benralizumab was out of 67–150%. Only the arm/abdomen Tmax 
ratio of alirocumab was out of the range of 50–200%. Of note, different 
device presentations were used in these SC PK studies. Most IgGs were 
SC administered using a syringe or prefilled syringe. In the relative 
bioavailability studies, 9 IgGs were administered with both auto­
injectors and prefilled syringes and 1 IgG was administered with auto­
injectors only. The ratios of PK parameters were derived from the same 
device presentation. 
As shown in Fig. 4, the thigh/abdomen ratios in AUC or Cmax of all 
the 19 IgGs were consistently >80%. The thigh/abdomen AUC ratios of 
3 IgGs and the thigh/abdomen Cmax ratios of 5 IgGs were > 125%. For 
IgGs exhibiting site-dependent PK, the bioavailability from thigh injec­
tion was higher than that from abdomen injection. For the 13 IgGs with 
PK data available from arm and abdomen injections, the arm/abdomen 
ratios in AUC or Cmax were consistently ≤125% (Supplementary Fig. S3). 
The arm/abdomen AUC ratios of 1 IgG and the arm/abdomen Cmax ra­
tios of 2 IgGs were < 80%. The rank in overall SC bioavailability from 
three injection sites [thigh > abdomen ≈arm (or abdomen slightly 
higher than arm)] was consistent with the rank in the average lymph 
flow rates at the three regions (4.2, 2.8, and 2.0 cm/min for thigh, 
anterior trunk, and upper arm, respectively [8]), indicating that regional 
difference in lymph flow may affect SC absorption of IgGs.

5. Discussion 
The majority of peptides/small proteins (e.g., incretins and insulins) 
are agonist hormones, which can be SC administered at very low doses 
[6]. In contrast, many of the therapeutic antibodies are antagonists, 
which require a relatively high-dose administration. Due to the low 
volume of SC injection (≤2 mL), many approved therapeutic antibodies 
were intravenously administered. Recently, with the advances in SC 
formulation development and combination with hyaluronidase which 
allows for a larger SC dosing volume, more SC administered therapeutic 
antibodies have been approved by the US FDA. However, our knowledge 
of the effect of injection sites on SC absorption of therapeutic proteins 
and peptides is still scarce, mainly limited to insulin and insulin ana­
logues. No comprehensive analysis of currently available injection site- 
based clinical PK data has been conducted. 
Our survey showed that for two-thirds of SC administered peptides 
and proteins, the recommended injection sites were supported with the 
injection sites used in Phase 3 trials in the absence of injection site-based 
clinical relative bioavailability (BA) or popPK data. Our survey of the 
injection site-based clinical PK data of 45 products showed that injection 
sites are more likely to influence SC absorption of peptides/small pro­
teins (50%), less likely to affect SC absorption of non-IgG proteins 
(38%), and least likely to affect IgGs (26%). This finding is consistent 
with the overall in vivo catabolic stability of peptides/small proteins, 
non-IgG proteins, and IgGs. The secondary and tertiary structures of 
proteins reduce pre-systemic catabolism or degradation of non-IgG 
proteins and IgGs. FcRn binding further decreases SC catabolism and 
macrophage clearance of IgGs.

sites, a dedicated relative bioavailability study is preferred over popPK

For peptides/small proteins, rapid SC absorption (Tmax ≤2 h) or

injection site-dependent SC absorption. Because peptide/small protein

association with high CL/F suggests that regional difference in pre- 
systemic catabolism or degradation may be one of the major mecha­
nisms of injection site-dependent SC absorption of peptides. However, 
due to the lack of in vitro catabolic stability assay in different tissue 
homogenates, no correlation analysis with catabolic stability can be 
conducted. 
Because peptides/small proteins with MW < 16 kDa are mainly 
absorbed through SC blood vessels, the association with short Tmax in­
dicates that regional difference in SC blood flow may be another major 
mechanism of injection site-dependent SC absorption of peptides/small 
proteins. After a meal, abdominal SC blood flow is 30% higher than 
femoral SC blood flow (4.0 versus 3.0 mL/min/100 g adipose tissue) and 
the different blood flow is maintained 6 h post the meal [9]. According 
to drug labels, fast-acting insulins (regular insulin, insulin lispro, insulin 
glulisine, insulin aspart) and insulin detemir are given within 15 min 
before a meal or within 20 min after starting a meal. Pramlintide is 
administered immediately prior to each major meal. Exenatide and 
lixisenatide are given within 60 min before a meal. All the peptides 
administered under postprandial state showed thigh/abdomen ratio in 
Cmax less than 1. Except lixisenatide, all the other peptides/small pro­
teins administered under postprandial state showed thigh/abdomen 
ratio in AUC less than 1. Peptides/small proteins undergoing quick SC 
absorption (Tmax ≤2 h, Supplementary Table S1) are more sensitive to 
the fluctuations in SC blood flow and all of them except insulin aspart 
showed thigh/abdomen ratio in Cmax less than 0.8. For insulin degludec, 
insulin detemir, liraglutide, and semaglutide, the conjugation with fatty 
acids increases their binding affinity to albumin (fu,p ≤0.02) and thus 
slows down SC absorption rate and reduces SC catabolism of these 
peptides/small proteins, rendering their SC absorption less sensitive to 
the regional difference in SC blood flow. Peptides/small proteins tightly 
bound to interstitial proteins such as albumin are expected to have a low 
risk of injection site-dependent SC absorption.

Furthermore, the injection site-dependent PK of taspoglutide suggests

Our survey demonstrated that 38% (3/8) of non-IgG proteins showed 
injection site-dependent SC absorption, which is slightly lower than the 
50% (9/18) for peptides/small proteins. However, the risk of injection 
site-dependent SC absorption for non-IgG proteins might be under­
estimated because 3 non-IgG proteins in Table 2 (luspatercept, dula­
glutide and albiglutide) are Fc- or albumin-fused proteins, which are 
associated with low risk of injection site-dependent SC absorption. In 
our survey, only 7 among 40 (18%) SC administered non-IgG proteins 
are Fc- or albumin-fused proteins but they represent 38% of products 
with clinical PK data from multiple injection sites (Table 2) which is an 
over-representation. For non-IgG proteins without Fc or albumin 
domain, 3 among 5 (60%) showed injection site-dependent SC absorp­
tion. For peptides/small proteins and IgGs, injection sites affect the rate 
of absorption (Cmax) more significantly than the extent of absorption 
(AUC), which may be explained by regional differences in SC blood flow 
and lymph flow, respectively. In contrast, based on the data of soma­
tropin and lipegfilgrastim (Table 2), injection sites affect the AUC and 
Cmax with a similar magnitude, indicating that regional difference in 
macrophage degradation may play a major role for these non-IgG pro­
teins. Regional differences in the contents of macrophage in human 
adipose tissues were reported in literature. The contents of macrophages 
varied up to 7-fold among human adipose tissues [58]. In human sub­
jects with body mass index <25 kg/m2, the contents of macrophages in 
abdominal and femoral SC adipose tissue were 1.4% and 1.6%, respec­
tively [59]. SC administration of follitropin beta to the vaginal region 
resulted in 45% increase in AUC and 27% increase in Cmax compared to 
SC administration to abdomen. Currently, there is no literature report on 
SC adipose tissue macrophage content in human vaginal region. It is 
unknown whether the contents of macrophages in abdominal and 
vaginal SC adipose tissue are different. Although the mechanism of in­
jection site-dependent absorption for follitropin beta is still unknown, 
we include this example in Table 2 to highlight the high incidence of 
injection site-dependent SC absorption for proteins without Fc or albu­
min domain. Considering the high rate (60%) and large magnitude 
(inter-injection site AUC ratio: 45–145%) of injection site-dependent SC 
absorption, attention should be paid to non-IgG proteins without Fc or 
albumin domain and more clinical data is warranted to confirm the 
findings. 
Our data suggested that regional differences in SC lymph flow 
contribute to injection site-dependent SC absorption of IgGs. The 
observation that IgGs with relatively fast SC absorption (Tmax ≤5 days) 
are more likely to show injection site-dependent SC absorption also 
supports the conclusion. However, the absence of injection site- 
dependent SC absorption for 14 IgGs suggests the existence of addi­
tional mechanisms for site-dependent absorption of IgGs. Correlation 
analysis with isoelectric point (Fig. 5) excluded the role of electrostatic 
interaction with ECM. Our analysis showed that IgGs with T1/2 < 15 
days (Fig. 4) are associated with an increased risk of injection site- 
dependent absorption. FcRn binding affinity is an important determi­
nant of the elimination half-life of IgGs [60], suggesting that an IgG with 
relatively low FcRn binding affinity may be more vulnerable to injection 
site-dependent absorption. This speculation is further supported by the 
observation that 3 among 5 proteins without Fc domain showed injec­
tion site-dependent SC absorption. We speculate that relatively low FcRn 
binding and regional difference in macrophage degradation may 
contribute to injection site-dependent absorption of IgGs. Unfortunately, 
the measurements of IgG binding affinity to FcRn are dependent on 
assay configuration and the dissociation constants (KD) for wild-type 
human IgG1 Fc domains binding to human FcRn varied over 100-fold 
in literature [61]. The large inter-lab variability in FcRn binding affin­
ity measurements precludes a meaningful correlation analysis between 
injection site-dependent absorption and FcRn binding parameters re­
ported in literature. 
We also preliminarily explored injection site-related clinical PK data 
of SC administered oligonucleotides. Among 13 FDA and EMA approved 
therapeutic oligonucleotides, 6 are SC administered and only vola­
nesorsen has injection site-related clinical PK data available. According 
to popPK analysis, the thigh/abdomen or arm/abdomen AUC ratio for 
volanesorsen was within 80–125% but the thigh/arm AUC ratio was 
129% [62]. Due to the very limited injection site-related clinical SC PK 
data, it is unknown whether injection sites are likely to affect the PK of 
SC administered oligonucleotides. 
Based on the present study, we propose potential factors which affect 
the SC absorption and lead to injection site-dependent PK of therapeutic 
proteins and peptides in four categories: (1) peptides/small proteins 
with MW < 16 kDa, (2) acylated peptides/small proteins (or albumin 
bound peptides/small proteins), (3) non-IgG proteins with MW ≥16 
kDa, and (4) IgGs, Fc- or albumin-fused peptides/proteins (Table 4). The 
injection site-dependent SC absorption of peptides/small proteins with 
MW < 16 KDa is likely due to regional differences in local blood flow 
and extracellular catabolism. For acylated peptides/small proteins, the 
risk of injection site-dependent SC absorption is likely affected by their 
binding affinity to interstitial proteins such as albumin. Many factors 
such as the fatty acid length, composition, and position of conjugation 
affect binding affinity to albumin [6]. Acylated peptides/small proteins 
tightly bound to albumin are absorbed mainly through lymphatic uptake 
and the overall risk of injection site-dependent SC absorption is low.

site-dependent PK. Based on the results from our correlation analyses, a 
series of characteristics are proposed to be associated with increased risk 
of injection site-dependent SC absorption (Table 4). The proposed 
mechanisms and risk characteristics are based on clinical PK data of a 
small sample size (N = 45). Further research in this area is warranted to 
verify the mechanisms and risk characteristics. It is noteworthy that 
proposed mechanisms and risk characteristics are not applicable to drug 
products in sustained-release formulations. 
The clinical relevance of SC injection site-dependent PK is dependent 
on the exposure-response relationship of individual product. Even 
though the injection site-dependent variations in PK may not necessarily 
affect the efficacy or safety of a SC administered biologic, the informa­
tion of injection site-dependent absorption can be used to optimize the 
design of relative bioavailability study for the product. For example, if a 
product is identified to have an increased risk of site-dependent PK 
based on the characteristics in Table 4, a relative bioavailability study 
designed with a single SC injection site may reduce the variability in PK. 
Furthermore, for peptides/small proteins with short Tmax or high CL/F, 
if it is not feasible to use a single injection site for a relative bioavail­
ability study, the study may be conducted under fasting conditions to 
reduce the variability in PK. 
In addition to the limited clinical PK data from multiple SC injection 
sites, the lack of reliable in vitro assay or in vitro data is another obstacle 
to overcome. Fully validated in vitro models for assessment of SC ab­
sorption (i.e. in vitro subcutaneous tissue binding assay, in vitro stability 
assay in subcutaneous tissue homogenate and lymph fluid, in vitro 
macrophage degradation assay, in vitro FcRn- dependent transcytosis 
assay) are still missing. In vitro FcRn binding affinity measurement 
shows a large inter-study variability. Advances in in vitro models for 
assessment of SC absorption will facilitate the prediction of injection 
site-dependent SC absorption of therapeutic proteins and peptides. A 
dialysis-based in vitro model was developed to predict SC absorption 
and bioavailability of insulin and monoclonal antibodies [64–66]. 
Further research in this area may be informative. 
6. Conclusions 
In summary, our findings from a comprehensive review of clinical PK 
data of SC administered therapeutic proteins and peptides suggest that 
at least 50% of peptides/small proteins and proteins without Fc- or al­
bumin domains with PK data from multiple SC injection sites show in­
jection site-dependent SC absorption. More than a quarter of IgGs with 
PK data from multiple SC injection sites show injection site-dependent 
SC absorption. Base on correlation analyses with PK parameters and 
physicochemical parameters, we have identified the factors potentially 
contributing to injection site-dependent SC absorption and proposed PK 
characteristics associated with an increased risk of injection site- 
dependent SC absorption. SC absorption of proteins and peptides is a 
complex process. In addition to the factors investigated in this study, 
many other intrinsic factors (i.e. body mass index, gender, age) and 
extrinsic factors (i.e. exercise, injection device) may potentially affect SC 
absorption from different injection sites. Further investigations are 
needed to understand underlying mechanisms of injection site- 
dependent SC absorption.

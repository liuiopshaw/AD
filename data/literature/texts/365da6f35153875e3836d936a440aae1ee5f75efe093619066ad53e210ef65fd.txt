Copper single-atom nanozyme with
intelligent capture and photo-enhanced
activity for controlling plant bacterial
diseases
Hao Jiang1,2,3, Yue Xing1, Zhifeng Ma2, Guangjin Fan2, Zhongwei Liu3, Song Yang1 &
Lin Cai
2
Nanozymes hold promise for controlling plant bacterial diseases, but con-
ventional ones suffer from low bacterial afﬁnity, inefﬁcient enzyme-like
activity, and thus poor antibacterial efﬁcacy. Here, we report a photo-
enhanced copper single-atom (CuSA)-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme with high
afﬁnity and efﬁcient peroxidase (POD)-like activity. CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2
exhibits higher efﬁcacies against bacterial speck and bacterial wilt diseases in
tomatoes, surpassing the commercial thiodiazole copper by 13.33% and
52.77%, respectively. Mechanistically, it catalyzes H2O2 to generate toxic
hydroxyl radicals (·OH) via POD-like activity; near-infrared irradiation boosts
this activity by lowering activation energy and accelerating mass transfer.
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that CuSA-loaded
ZnS@MoS2 captures bacteria via Metal-O-P bonds on cell surfaces, reducing
·OH short-range quenching to enhance efﬁcacy. This SA nanozyme design,
integrating intelligent capture and photo-enhanced activity, offers an insight
for plant bacterial disease control.
Plant bacterial diseases cause substantial annual crop yield losses,
posing a severe threat to global food security1–3. For instance, Pseu-
domonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 (Pst. DC3000) and Ralstonia
solanacearum (R. solanacearum) are major pathogens limiting pro-
duction of Solanaceae crops. The bacterial speck disease caused by Pst.
DC3000 reduces the quality of tomatoes, resulting in a 75% reduction
in their output, signiﬁcantly impacting the global economy4. Similarly,
R. solanacearum causes bacterial wilt disease, leading to yield reduc-
tions of 0–91% in tomato, 33–90% in potato, 10–30% in tobacco, and
80–100% in banana5. While agrochemicals are pivotal for controlling
plant bacterial diseases6, their escalating overuse and inherent inefﬁ-
ciency have driven a steady rise in bacterial resistance7. This resistance
has further complicated disease control, creating an urgent need for
efﬁcient alternative antibacterial strategies.
Nanozymes,
nanomaterials
with
enzyme-like
activity,
offer
advantages including low cost, high stability, scalable production, and
multifunctionality8–10. Peroxidase (POD)-like nanozymes are particu-
larly promising: they catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 into hydroxyl
radicals (·OH), the most effective reactive oxygen species (ROS) for
eradicating pathogenic bacteria11–14. This catalytic activity is well-
matched to the microenvironment of plant infection sites, where
pathogens induce the production of ROS (including H2O2) and organic
acids15,16. Notably, ~52% of sunlight consists of near-infrared (NIR) light;
this enables NIR-enhanced nanozymes to kill bacteria more effectively

by boosting their POD-like activity17,18. POD-like nanozyme with NIR
enhancement, therefore represent a promising approach for control-
ling plant bacterial diseases. Structural modiﬁcations can further
optimize antibacterial efﬁcacy, such as loading exogenous metal
atoms into a support matrix19. Single-atom (SA) nanozymes with pre-
cisely deﬁned electronic and geometric structures are especially
effective at mimicking the catalytic centers of natural enzymes20,21. For
example, CeO2-supported Cu SA nanozymes demonstrated enhanced
POD-like activity and reduced ·OH antioxidant capacity activity,
effectively combating Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and
Escherichia coli19. Nevertheless, the rapid quenching effect of ROS and
the low afﬁnity of SA nanozymes to the bacterial surface severely limit
their applications22. Two-dimensional (2D) materials address these
issues via multivalent binding: WS2, MoS2, and VOxC nanosheets form
strong Metal-O-P bonds with bacteria and possess membrane-
conforming ﬂexibility, thereby increasing the contact area and bind-
ing strength22–24. Complementary strategies involve ROS-generating
metal ions (e.g., Zn2+) that increase membrane permeability25. Inte-
grating these advantages, a multifunctional Cu SA nanozyme com-
bining NIR enhancement, optimized bacterial afﬁnity via 2D support,
and ROS-mediated membrane disruption could enable intelligent
pathogen capture and kill in plant protection systems.
In this study, we develop a photo-enhanced CuSA-loaded
ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme with intelligent capture and killing bifunction
through an atom loaded strategy. We evaluated this nanozyme for the
control of tomato speck disease (caused by Pst. DC3000) and tomato
bacterial wilt (caused by R. solanacearum), and investigated its anti-
bacterial mechanism. Combined experimental and density functional
theory (DFT) calculations demonstrate that CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2
nanozyme exhibits high POD-like activity, efﬁciently catalyzing H2O2 to
generate ·OH. This process is further enhanced under NIR irradiation.
Additionally, CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme can capture bacteria
cells by interactions with the bacteria surface, avoiding the rapid
quenching effect of ·OH. Beneﬁting from these advantages, CuSA-
loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme achieves high efﬁcacy in controlling
plant bacterial diseases. These ﬁndings deepen the understanding of
the antibacterial mechanism of SA nanozymes, guide the design of
speciﬁc and efﬁcient bactericidal nanozymes for plant disease control,
and mark the pioneering application of SA nanozymes in the man-
agement of agricultural bacterial diseases.
Results and discussion
Synthesis and characterization
Firstly, different amounts of Cu were added to synthesize the CuSA-
loaded ZnS@MoS2 using a conventional hydrothermal method
(Fig. 1a).To identify the optimal Cu addition, we evaluated the POD-like
catalytic activity of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 and its effects on tomato
seed germination. At a Cu addition of 0.25 mmol, CuSA-loaded
ZnS@MoS2 exhibited the highest POD-like activity and no toxicity to
tomato seeds (Supplementary Fig. 1). Therefore, the addition of Cu
used in all subsequent experiments was 0.25 mmol. As shown in Fig. 1b,
CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 had an irregular overall morphology, with
granular nanoparticles (NPs) distributed on the surface of lamellar
structures. Supplementary Fig. 2 conﬁrmed that these granular and
lamellar components corresponded to ZnS and MoS2, respectively.
The zeta potential and average particle diameter of CuSA-loaded
ZnS@MoS2 were −23.86 mV and 513.09 nm, respectively (Supple-
mentary Fig. 3). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of CuSA-loaded
ZnS@MoS2 revealed characteristic peaks for ZnS (PDF#89-2156), MoS2
(PDF#06-0097), and S (PDF#13-0141) (Supplementary Fig. 4).The high-
resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), selected area
electron diffraction (SAED) pattern, and Fast Fourier transformation
(FFT) pattern indexing further conﬁrmed the presence of three sub-
stances: ZnS (crystal plane: 102, interplanar spacing: 0.31 nm), S
(crystal plane: 222, interplanar spacing: 0.33 nm), and MoS2 (crystal
plane: 002, interplanar spacing: 0.61 nm) (Supplementary Fig. 5).
These lattice parameters matched the standard XRD data for ZnS,
MoS₂, and S, conﬁrming their crystalline phases. According to the
previous report26, we speculated that thioacetamide ﬁrst reduced Cu2+
to Cu+, then sulfurized MoO3 to MoS3. The subsequent reaction
between MoS3 and Cu+ produced MoS2, Cu2+, and S, which was con-
ﬁrmed by XRD pattern results. Meanwhile, the absence of nanocrystal
and large clusters characteristic diffraction peaks for Cu suggested
that Cu could exist in a single atomic form. To verify this, the high-
angle annular dark ﬁeld scanning transmission electron microscopy
(HAADF-STEM) was employed to compare the atomic images of CuSA-
loaded ZnS@MoS2 and ZnS@MoS2. However, it was difﬁcult to clearly
observe the presence of single Cu atoms in the HAADF-STEM images of
CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2. (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 6). HAADF-
STEM elemental mapping showed uniform distribution of Cu, Zn, O,
Mo, and S across CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 (Fig. 1d). Due to HAADF-
STEM’s Z-contrast imaging was inherently limited when imaging high-Z
(heavy) metals alongside low-Z (light) elements, the weak scattering
intensity of low-Z elements made them undetectable in the presenceof
high-Z counterparts27–29. This likely explained the attenuated O signal
in the elemental mapping. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum
results showed that the peaks at 452, 613, 899, 1049, 1126, 1388, 1612,
and 3425 cm−1 were corresponding to the Metal-O30, Metal-S31, C-H32,
C-O33, C-N34, N-C-H35, C = O36, N-H/O-H, respectively (Supplementary
Fig. 7). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) conﬁrmed the ele-
mental composition of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2, consistent with
HAADF-STEM mapping (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Considering the cri-
tical role of element valence states, high-resolution XPS analysis of Mo
3d, Cu 2p, S 2p, Zn 2p, and O 1s was performed. The Mo 3d spectrum
showed that two characteristic peaks at 229.3 and 228.6 eV were cor-
responding to the Mo4+ (Supplementary Fig. 8b)37. The characteristic
peaks at 232 and 233.2 eV were corresponding to the Mo6+ 38. The
characteristic peak at 226.1 eV was attributed to the presence of sulfur
2s39. Furthermore, the characteristic peaks observed in the Cu 2p at
932.4 and 952.3 eV were ascribed to Cu+ 40,41, while the peak detected at
946.3 eV were assigned to weak satellite peak (Supplementary
Fig. 8c)42. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8d, three characteristic
peaks at 162.6, 168.9, and 161.4 eV were assigned to Mo-S, S in thio-
sulfate and sulfate, and Cu/Zn-S, respectively43. This suggested that the
material could present a Cu-S-Zn structure. The Zn 2p was divided into
the characteristic peaks at 1021.7, 1044.7, and 1038.2 eV, attributing to
Zn 2p3/2 and Zn 2p1/2, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8e)44–46. The O
1s spectrum exhibited three characteristic peaks at 531.7, 530.5, and
533.0 eV corresponding to the binding energy of Metal-O, lattice-O,
and surface-adsorbed water, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8f)47,48.
Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES)
quantiﬁed the elemental contents: Cu (11.4 wt%), Zn (27.5 wt%), and Mo
(11.9 wt%) (Supplementary Fig. 9). Collectively, these results conﬁrmed
the successful synthesis of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 with a Cu-S-Zn
structure.

the pivotal process, delving into their intricate and nuanced actions

In summary, CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme efﬁciently con-
verted low-toxic H2O2 into highly toxic ·OH. Under NIR irradiation, its
POD-like activity was further enhanced by photothermal heating,
which lowered the reaction activation energy. This dual capability
(catalytic ·OH generation and NIR-enhanced activity) enabled localized
production of potent ROS, thereby boosting bactericidal efﬁcacy.
In vitro antibacterial activity and mechanism
Encouraged by the catalytic activity and ROS generation mechanism of
CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme, we performed in vitro anti-
bacterial assays. The representative strains Pst. DC3000 and R. sola-
nacearum, two devastating Gram-negative bacteria infecting the leaves
and roots of Solanaceae, were chosen as the study subjects. For
comparison, we used the commercial bactericide thiodiazole copper
(a widely used agricultural antibacterial agent). As shown in Fig. 4a, c
and Supplementary Figs. 18–20, CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 inhibited
Pst. DC3000 and R. solanacearum in a dose-dependent manner, out-
performing thiodiazole copper at all tested concentrations. Consistent
with this, the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) of CuSA-
loaded ZnS@MoS2 against both pathogens were lower than that of
thiodiazole copper, conﬁrming its higher antibacterial activity (Sup-
plementary Table 3). CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 also showed better
inhibition than CuO NPs, ZnO NPs, and MoS2 nanosheets (Supple-
mentary Figs. 21 and 22). Furthermore, it outperformed CuSA@MoS2
and ZnS@MoS2 (Supplementary Figs. 23–26), indicating a synergistic
antibacterial effect between CuSA and ZnS in the nanozyme. To opti-
mize the POD-like catalytic condition for antibacterial assays, we ﬁrst
determined the optimal H2O2 concentrations for Pst. DC3000
(100 μM) and R. solanacearum (10 μM) by testing H2O2’s intrinsic
antibacterial effect (Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28). Under these
optimal H2O2 conditions, CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 increased the
inhibition rate of Pst. DC3000 and R. solanacearum by 69.71% and
87.63%, respectively, compared to the H2O2-only control (Fig. 4b and
Supplementary Figs. 29 and 30). To verify that ·OH was the primary
contributor to antibacterial activity, we performed ·OH scavenging
assays using tert-butanol (TBA). Under H2O2 conditions, the addition
of TBA signiﬁcantly reduced the antibacterial activity of CuSA-loaded
ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme, suggesting that the ·OH generation during
catalysis was the primary contributor of antibacterial activity (Sup-
plementary Figs. 31 and 32). Then, the ·OH generation capabilities of
CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme at 2.5 and 12.5 μg/mL were mea-
sured to be 9.44 and 16.16 U/mL, respectively (Supplementary
Fig. 33a). To rule out Cu ion leaching as a contributing factor, we
measured Cu ion release from CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 after 2 h and
tested the antibacterial activity of this leached Cu concentration. No
signiﬁcant inhibition was observed, conﬁrming the antibacterial
mechanism was not dependent on Cu ion release (Supplementary
Fig. 34). Given the NIR-enhanced POD activity of CuSA-loaded
ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme, it had great potential in plant leaf bacterial
disease control18. The NIR irradiation not only improved the anti-
bacterial ability of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme against Pst.
DC3000, but also further boosted activity under H2O2 conditions
(Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 29). These phenomena could be
attributed to the fact that NIR irradiation reduced the activation

Biosafety
Nanomaterials were promising for improving plant growth, crop
yields, and disease resistance59–61; however, their inevitable release into
agricultural environments during application raised biosafety con-
cerns. Therefore, the biosafety of nanomaterials to plants should be
evaluated before practical applications. We preliminarily evaluated the
leaching behavior of Cu2+ in CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme
(200 μg/mL) at different pH levels (pH 5 and pH 7) and in different
solutions (water and PBS) over different time intervals (1 d, 3 d, 5 d, and
8 d) (Supplementary Fig. 43). The results demonstrated that CuSA-
loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme exhibited the highest concentration of
Cu2+ release at pH 5, reaching a maximum of 8.62 μg/mL. At both pH 7
and in water, the release of Cu2+ remained relatively stable. In contrast,
the amount of Cu2+ released in PBS was below the detection limit of
ICP-OES, indicating that the release of Cu2+ in PBS was minimal. We
next assessed the CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme’s safety to
Solanaceae plants (tomato, tobacco) and human cells. Exposure to
CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 had no signiﬁcant effect on plant height, root
length, shoot biomass, or seed germination rate (Supplementary
Figs. 44–46). CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme also demonstrated
no cytotoxic effects on human gut cell lines (Caco-2 cells) across a
range of concentrations (0, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL), indicating
its
biosafety
(Supplementary
Fig.
47).
However,
CuSA-loaded
ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme at 400 μg/mL exhibited low toxicity towards
Caco-2 cells. These results indicated that CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2
nanozyme had potential biosafety for Solanaceae plants and human
cells. Furthermore, the acute toxicity of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2
nanozyme on non-target organisms, including zebraﬁsh and earth-
worms, was evaluated. The results indicated that the nanozyme at 20
and 200 µg/mL exhibited no signiﬁcant difference in survival rates of
zebraﬁsh and earthworms (Supplementary Fig. 48), conﬁrming the
safety of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme for non-target organ-
isms. We also evaluated the effects of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nano-
zyme on root endosphere, rhizosphere soil microbes, and leaf
endosphere microbes, as well
as the
effects
of
CuSA-loaded
ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme + NIR on leaf endosphere microbes. The
results indicated that the antibacterial efﬁcacy and photothermal
effects of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme had a minimalimpact on
microbes in plants and in the environment, preliminarily conﬁrming
the safety of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme (Supplementary
Figs. 49–51). To further conﬁrm that the application concentration of
CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme was non-toxic to tomato plants,
we assessed the malondialdehyde (MDA) content, SOD activity, POD
activity, and CAT activity in tomato plant leaves after foliar application
at 200 μg/mL and roots after root application at 20 μg/mL. The results
indicated that CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme did not induce
oxidative damage to the tomato plants (Supplementary Fig. 52).
Similarly, we found that foliar application of 200 μg/mL nanozyme +
100 μM H2O2 and root application of 20 μg/mL nanozyme + 10 μM
H2O2 did not induce oxidative damage in tomato plants. This indicated
that the ·OH of catalytic production by nanozymes from H2O2 did not
pose any harm to the plants (Supplementary Fig. 53). Notably, we
observed that the foliar or root application of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2
nanozyme could induce plant immunity by upregulating the expres-
sion of NPR1, PR1b1, and PR5 genes (Supplementary Figs. 54 and 55).
We also evaluated the changes in the contents of Cu, Zn, and Mo
elements in both plants and soil after the foliar application and root
application of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme. Foliar application
of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme facilitated the uptake of Zn and
Mo elements by the roots, as well as the uptake of Cu, Zn, and Mo
elements by the shoots (Supplementary Fig. 56). However, there were
no signiﬁcant changes in the concentrations of Cu, Zn, and Mo ele-
ments in the soil. Root application of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nano-
zyme facilitated the uptake of Cu and Zn by the roots and Cu and Mo
by the shoots, while also increasing the soil content of Zn and Mo
(Supplementary Fig. 57).
The degradation of nanomaterials in the environment was
crucial for assessing their biosafety. Thus, we evaluated the degra-
dation of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme in soil and the infec-
tious environment around bacteria according to the previous
reports62,63. The results indicated that over time, Cu in the nanozyme
primarily existed as residual fraction and organic-bound fraction; Zn
predominantly occurred as Fe-Mn oxides-bound fraction and
organic-bound fraction; while Mo was mainly found in the form of
organic-bound fraction (Supplementary Fig. 58). Furthermore, malic
acid, H2O2, and horseradish peroxidase (HRP) + H2O2 could promote
the oxidation of CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme (Supplemen-
tary Figs. 59–61). However, the presence of Cu+, Mo4+/Mo6+, and S2−
within the CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme increased complexity
to the system, which was conﬁrmed through XPS and previous
reports regarding H2O2 and HRP + H2O2 interactions63,64. Therefore,
soil and infectious environment around bacteria could gradually
degrade the CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme, suggesting that
the nanozyme possessed potential biosafety and long-term efﬁcacy
in controlling bacterial diseases.

In summary, we develop a CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme
with highly efﬁcient POD-like and NIR-enhanced activities for the
control of tomato bacterial speck and tomato bacterial wilt diseases.
CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme shows higher activities than other
reported POD-like nanozyme. Experimental studies and theoretical
calculations demonstrate that CuSA-loaded ZnS@MoS2 nanozyme can
achieve the “capture and kill” bifunctionality by binding to the cell
wall/outer membrane. Additionally, NIR irradiation can enhance the

mechanisms, providing a strategy for designing highly efﬁcient and
speciﬁc antibacterial nanozymes.

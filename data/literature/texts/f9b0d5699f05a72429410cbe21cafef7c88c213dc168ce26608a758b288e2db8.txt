A B S T R A C T
Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease caused by bacterial infection, leading to the destruction of 
periodontal tissues and impairment of oral function. The high-glucose microenvironment within periodontal 
pockets promotes the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria, worsening periodontal infections. Glucose oxidase 
(GOx)-like nanozymes can utilize elevated glucose levels locally to produce acid and hydrogen peroxide, aiding 
in infection control. Nevertheless, the catalytic efficiency of these nanozymes is low. Enhancing their activity 
may offer a promising approach for periodontitis treatment. Herein, we developed a NIR-driven hydrogel 
(AgZ@Au/PLEL) incorporating Au nanoparticles (Au NPs)-modified zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) 
loaded with Ag2S nanoparticles, aimed at treating periodontitis through antibacterial action and promotion of 
alveolar bone regeneration. The thermosensitivity and injectability of PLEL hydrogel ensured effective appli­
cation in complex periodontal structures. The Au NPs exhibit GOx-like activity, catalyzing the decomposition of 
glucose in the periodontal pocket to produce acid and hydrogen peroxide. Upon exposure to near-infrared (NIR) 
laser irradiation, the catalytic activity of Au NPs is enhanced, resulting in increased hydrogen peroxide pro­
duction that synergizes with photothermal therapy (PTT) for antibacterial effects. Meanwhile, the increased acid 
promotes ZIF-8 degradation, releasing zinc ions that stimulate alveolar bone regeneration. In vitro, laser- 
irradiated hydrogel exhibited potent bactericidal activity against periodontitis pathogens and promoted bone 
marrow stem cell differentiation. In vivo studies revealed that the NIR-driven nanosystem significantly enhanced 
treatment efficacy for periodontitis. Therefore, the NIR-driven GOx-like nanozyme-modified injectable ther­
moresponsive hydrogel offers a promising strategy for managing periodontitis.
1. Introduction
Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory condition caused by patho­
genic bacteria [1]. As the disease progresses, it progressively damages 
periodontal tissues, including the alveolar bone, periodontal ligaments, 
and gingiva. This ultimately leads to tooth loosening and loss, which 
significantly impairs the patient’s pronunciation and mastication [2,3]. 
Furthermore, periodontitis has been associated with systemic diseases, 
such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and Alzheimer’s disease [4,5]. 
Current treatment strategies for periodontitis primarily focus on elimi­
nating infection and restoring periodontal tissues. Clinical interventions 
for infection control include mechanical debridement and antibiotic 
administration [6,7], and tissue repair is primarily achieved through 
guided tissue regeneration (GTR) [8]. Nonetheless, mechanical 
debridement is often ineffective in removing plaque from complex 
anatomical sites, such as narrow and deep periodontal pockets and root 
furcations [9]. The effectiveness of antibiotics is further compromised 
by the rise of bacterial resistance [10]. Additionally, GTR has limited 
efficacy in promoting alveolar bone regeneration [4,11]. Consequently, 
there is a need for novel therapeutic approaches, such as antimicrobial 
photothermal therapy (PTT) [12], nanozyme-based treatments [13], 
and tissue engineering strategies [14], to eliminate pathogens and pro­
mote alveolar bone regeneration.

can promote the growth of pathogenic bacteria, such as porphyromonas

generate therapeutic products may represent a viable strategy for peri­
odontitis treatment. Glucose oxidase (GOx) catalyzes the oxidation of 
glucose to produce hydrogen peroxide and gluconic acid [13]. Mean­
while, generated hydrogen peroxide can exert antibacterial effects to a 
certain extent [17], and gluconic acid production reduces pH, altering 
the local microenvironment. GOx-like nanozymes (e.g., Au NPs, black 
phosphorus quantum dots) have been reported to mimic GOx catalytic 
activity, achieving similar biochemical effects [18]. However, the 
enzyme-mimicking activity of these nanozymes is relatively low due to 
insufficient surface active sites, nanozyme aggregation, and size-related 
factors [19–24]. Therefore, researchers are exploring various approachs 
to address this limitation, including altering external conditions (such as 
temperature and pH), controlling the synthesis conditions of nano­
particles, and improving nanozymes dispersion [20,21,24]. Elevated 
temperatures have been reported to enhance the kinetics of the GOx-like 
nanozyme-catalyzed 
process, 
significantly 
increasing 
hydrogen 
peroxide yield [19,25]. Therefore, incorporating heat-generating 
methods, such as photothermal therapy (PTT), can enhance the cata­
lytic activity, enabling GOx-catalyzed reactions to achieve therapeutic 
effects.
PTT converts light into heat, causing protein denaturation and 
structural damage to bacterial cells [26]. Unlike conventional antibac­
terial treatments, such as antibiotics or disinfectants, PTT provides a 
localized approach that reduces resistance risk and minimizes systemic 
side effects. Due to its controllability, broad-spectrum efficacy, and 
rapid action, PTT has been widely used in antibacterial applications 
[27]. Our group previously synthesized near-infrared (NIR) responsive 
Ag2S nanoparticles as photothermal agents for PTT [28,29]. Ag2S was 
chosen for strong NIR absorption, biocompatibility, and desirable pho­
tothermal conversion, making it highly suitable for in vivo applications 
[14,15]. Moreover, NIR light (700–1000 nm) allows deeper tissue 
penetration and reduced phototoxicity, enhancing its in vivo 
effectiveness.
Metals and their derivatives have shown promise in bone regenera­
tion applications [30]. Metal ions such as zinc, calcium, and cobalt 
facilitate bone repair within specific concentration ranges [31]. Zinc 
promotes osteoblast differentiation and mineralization [32,33], calcium 
supports bone matrix formation [34], and cobalt enhances vasculari­
zation [35], all essential for bone regeneration. Zeolitic imidazole 
framework-8 (ZIF-8), a type of metal–organic framework (MOF), can 
release zinc ions under acidic conditions [36]. This property has been 
explored for its potential application in bone repair [37–39]. A key 
challenge remains in artificially triggering and regulating zinc ion 
release for optimal osteogenic effects. Recent advances include stimuli- 
responsive materials, such as pH-sensitive or enzyme-degradable coat­
ings, enabling more controlled and targeted release.
Consequently, we developed an NIR-driven nanosystem that in­
tegrates antibacterial properties with alveolar bone repair capabilities 
for periodontitis treatment (Fig. 1). This nanosystem employs ZIF-8 as 
the foundational matrix to encapsulate Ag2S nanoparticles, yielding 
Ag2S@ZIF-8 nanoparticles (AgZ). Through ion exchange and in situ 
reduction approaches, Au nanoparticles (Au NPs) were precisely 
anchored onto the surface of AgZ, generating Ag2S@ZIF-8@Au 
(AgZ@Au) nanoparticles. AgZ@Au nanoparticles were then incorpo­
rated into an injectable thermoresponsive hydrogel composed of poly(D, 
L-lactide)-poly(ethylene glycol)-poly(D,L-lactide) (PDLLA-PEG-PDLLA, 
PLEL), yielding the AgZ@Au/PLEL hydrogel. Without NIR irradiation,

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Materials

drate (HAuCl4⋅3H2O) were purchased from Adamas (China). Sodium 
borohydride (NaBH4) and zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2⋅6H2O) 
were bought from Beijing Chemicals (China). D,L-Lactide was bought 
from Daigang chemicals (China). PEG (Mn = 1500) and stannous 
octoate (Sn(Oct)2, 95 %) were obtained from Astatech (China). Sodium 
sulfide nonahydrate (Na2S⋅9H2O) and silver nitrate (AgNO3) were pur­
chased from Chengdu Kelong Chemical Reagent (China). 3-mercapto­
propionic acid (3-MPA) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). 
Cell culture plates were purchased from Nest Biotechnology Co., Ltd 
(China) and transwell plates were purchased from Corning (USA). Fetal 
bovine serum (FBS), α-MEM and RIMP-1640 medium were obtained 
from Thermo (USA). BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase color development 
kit and hydrogen peroxide assay kit were bought from Beyotime 
Biotechnology (China). Glucose detection kit was purchased from 
Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was bought from MCE

2.2. Synthesis and characterization of Ag2S NPs, Ag2S@ZIF-8 (AgZ) and 
Ag2S@ZIF-8@Au (AgZ@Au)
A total of 400 µL of 3-MPA (1 M) was added to 20 mL of deionized 
(DI) water, and the pH was adjusted to 5.0 by the dropwise addition of 
NaOH (100 mM) solution. Following this, 1.6 mL of AgNO3 solution (50 
mM) was introduced, and the reaction was maintained at 50 ◦C for 1.5 h. 
Subsequently, 0.4 mL of Na2S⋅9H2O solution (100 mM) was added, and 
the reaction continued for an additional 8 h [28,29]. The final product 
was collected by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 1 h and dried under 
vacuum for 24 h.
10 mg of Ag2S NPs and 50 mg of Zn(NO3)2⋅6H2O were dissolved in 3 
mL of DI water. A solution of 2-methylimidazole (970 mg) was prepared 
by dissolving in 2 mL of DI water. The latter was then added dropwise to 
the former under stirring at 600 rpm for 10 min. The suspension was 
centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min to collect the solid product, which 
was washed three times with methanol and dried in vacuum for 24 h to 
obtain Ag2S@ZIF-8 (AgZ).
10 mg of AgZ were dissolved in 10 mL of methanol, followed by the 
addition of 100 µL of HAuCl4 (50 mM). The reaction mixture was stirred 
at 600 rpm for 24 h. The resulting solution was centrifuged at 12,000 
rpm for 10 min, and the precipitate was resuspended in 5 mL of meth­
anol. Then, 2.5 mg of sodium borohydride was added, and the reaction 
was allowed to proceed until the solution turned wine-red. Ag2S@ZIF- 
8@Au was obtained via washing with methanol, centrifuging (12000 
rpm, 10 min), and vacuum-dried overnight.
The morphology of the obtained products was examined using a 
transmission electron microscope (TEM) and high-resolution TEM 
(Talos F200S, FEI, USA). Elemental mapping of AgZ@Au was performed 
using scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and corre­
sponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, SUPER X, FEI, 
USA). The particle size and zeta potential were determined by dynamic 
light scattering (DLS) using an Anton Paar Litesizer 500 (Anton Paar, 
Austria). The elemental composition was analyzed with X-ray photo­
electron spectroscopy (XPS) using a Thermo Fisher K-ALPHA system 
(Thermo Fisher, USA). The crystal structures of AgZ and AgZ@Au were 
characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Rigaku Ultima IV 
diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan). UV–vis spectra were obtained with a 
Hitachi U-2900 spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan).
2.3. Synthesis and characterization of PLEL, Ag2S/PLEL, AgZ/PLEL and 
AgZ@Au/PLEL hydrogel
The synthesis of PDLLA-PEG-PDLLA (PLEL) was conducted following 
our previously established method [40–43]. Briefly, PEG (Mn = 1500) 
was heated under vacuum at 100 ◦C to remove residual moisture. D, L- 
lactide and Sn(Oct)2 were then added in a specified ratio, and the re­
action was carried out under a nitrogen atmosphere at 140 ◦C for 10 h. 
The resulting crude product was dissolved in water and stirred at room 
temperature. The solution was subsequently heated to 80 ◦C, allowing 
the product to precipitate. This purification process was repeated once, 
and the final product was lyophilized to constant weight to obtain the 
PDLLA-PEG-PDLLA (PLEL) triblock copolymer. The obtained polymer 
was then dissolved in PBS (pH 7.4) and stirred overnight at 4 ◦C to form 
the PLEL hydrogel. Ag2S NPs, AgZ, and AgZ@Au were dissolved in DI 
water to prepare solutions at specified concentrations. These solutions 
were then combined with PLEL hydrogel at a defined ratio and stirred 
overnight at 4 ◦C to yield the corresponding hydrogels.
After that, the sol–gel phase transition of the hydrogels was assessed 
using the test tube inversion method. The samples were initially placed 
at 4 ◦C for 10 min. Following this, the tubes were inverted by 180◦, and 
the flow of the hydrogel was observed within 1 min. If no flow was 
observed, the sample was deemed to be in the gel state; Otherwise, it was 
classified as a solution. Photographs were taken to document the 
appearance changes at various temperatures.
The rheological properties of the hydrogels were assessed using a 
rotational rheometer (HAAKE Rheostress 6000, Thermo Scientific, 
USA). Both the blank PLEL (20 wt%) and AgZ@Au/PLEL hydrogel (20 
wt%), pre-cooled to 4 ◦C, were placed onto a circular plate with a 
diameter of 20 mm, maintaining a 1 mm gap between the fixture and the 
plate. The storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G’’) were measured 
over a temperature range from 4 ◦C to 60 ◦C at a controlled heating rate 
of 1 ◦C /min. Measurements were conducted under constant frequency 
(1.0 Hz) and constant stress (4.0 dyn/cm2).
2.4. Zn2+ release in vitro
The in vitro release behavior of AgZ@Au/PLEL hydrogel (20 wt%) 
under various conditions was evaluated. A 2 mL sample of hydrogel 
containing 100 µg/mL of AgZ@Au was introduced into a 50 mL glass 
vial and allowed to gel at 37 ◦C for 30 min. Subsequently, 15 mL of pre- 
warmed PBS solution (pH 7.4 or pH 6.5) was gently added, and the 
mixture was shaken lightly at 100 rpm at 37 ◦C. At designated time 
points (t = 0.5 h, 1 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 72 h, 168 h, 336 h, 504 h), 2 mL of 
the release medium was sampled and replaced with an equal volume of 
fresh PBS. Additionally, for one group in PBS at pH 7.4, samples were 
collected after exposure to 808 nm laser irradiation (2.0 W/cm2, 5 min). 
All collected release media were stored at 4 ◦C, and the amount of Zn2+
released was quantified using inductively coupled plasma-optical 
emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) (Agilent 5100, Agilent, USA).
2.5. Evaluation of photothermal performance
A thermal imaging camera (Fluke Ti32, Fluke, USA) was employed to 
monitor the temperature variations of AgZ and AgZ@Au solutions (0 ~ 
500 µg/mL) under 808 nm laser irradiation (2.0 W/cm2, 5 min). Tem­
perature changes of a 100 µg/mL AgZ@Au solution were also recorded

2.7. Antibacterial performance in vitro
Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis, ATCC33277) and Fuso­
bacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum, ATCC10953) were cultured at 37 ◦C 
under anaerobic conditions (80 % N2, 10 % H2, and 10 % CO2). The 
growth medium consisted of brain heart infusion (BHI, Oxoid, UK) 
supplemented with yeast extract (5 mg/mL), menadione (1 mg/mL), and 
hemin (5 mg/mL).
2.7.1. Growth curves plotting
The antibacterial activity of various treatment groups (PBS, PBS +
NIR, AgZ/PLEL, AgZ/PLEL + NIR, AgZ@Au/PLEL + NIR, AgZ@Au/ 
PLEL + Glu, AgZ@Au/PLEL + Glu + NIR) was assessed by generating 
growth curves for P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum. The assay was conducted 
using 24-well transwell plates, with 100 µL of hydrogel containing 
nanoparticles (100 µg/mL) added to each transwell chamber, while 1 mL 
of bacterial suspension (P. gingivalis at OD600 = 0.13, F. nucleatum at 
OD600 = 0.14) was introduced into the wells. The samples were then 
either subjected to 808 nm laser irradiation (2 W/cm2, 5 min) or incu­
bated in the dark. Optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured at 
predefined intervals (0, 6 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h) to monitor bacterial 
growth, and growth curves were plotted to evaluate the antibacterial 
inhibition of each treatment group.
2.7.2. CFU counting
Bactericidal activity of the various treatment groups was evaluated 
via colony-forming unit (CFU) counting using the plate-coating method, 
alongside OD600 measurements. The experiment was performed in 24- 
well transwell plates, with each well containing 1 mL of bacterial sus­
pension (P. gingivalis at OD600 = 0.40, F. nucleatum at OD600 = 0.25). 
Samples were exposed to 808 nm laser irradiation (2 W/cm2, 5 min) or 
incubated in the dark. After 6 h of incubation at 37 ◦C, OD600 values 
were recorded. Each bacterial suspension was diluted 105-fold, and 100 
µL of the diluted solution was plated on blood agar. P. gingivalis plates 
were incubated for 120 h and F. nucleatum plates for 48 h, after which 
CFU counts were conducted to determine bacterial viability.
2.7.3. Live/dead staining
After treatment, 100 µL of the bacterial suspension was collected and 
resuspended in an equal volume of 0.9 % NaCl solution. Samples were 
stained using the Live & Dead Bacterial Staining Kit, and 5 µL of the 
stained suspension was placed on a microscope slide, and covered with 
an 18 mm square coverslip. Fluorescent microscopy (DMi8, Leica, Ger­
many) was employed to capture images.
2.7.4. Bacteria morphology observation
After treatment, the bacterial suspensions were centrifuged at 6000 
rpm for 10 min. The resulting pellets were fixed with 2.5 % glutaral­
dehyde and subsequently dehydrated through a graded ethanol series 
(30 %, 50 %, 70 %, 80 %, 90 %, and 100 %, v/v). Dehydrated samples 
were dispersed onto clean silicon wafers, air-dried, and coated with 
gold. Bacterial morphology was observed using a scanning electron 
microscope (SEM, JSM-IT700HR, JEOL, Japan).
2.8. Cytocompatibility assessment
Rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (rBMSCs) were isolated 
from the femurs of 4-week-old Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats and cultured in 
α-MEM supplemented with 15 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin/ 
streptomycin. Mouse fibroblasts (L929) were cultured in RPMI-1640 
medium containing 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin. All 
cells were incubated at 37 ◦C in a humidified atmosphere of 5 % CO2.
2.8.1. Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay
The assay was performed in 24-well transwell plates, with 50 µL of 
hydrogel (Ag2S, AgZ, or AgZ@Au at 100 µg/mL) placed in the transwell 
chambers, and L929 or rBMSC cells (5000 cells/well) seeded in the 
lower wells. The culture medium for L929 and rBMSC cells was replaced 
with a mixture of CCK-8 reagent and fresh medium (1:10, v/v) after 1 
and 3 days of incubation, respectively. The plates were then incubated in 
the dark for 2 h, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.
2.8.2. Assessment of cellular morphology
Following treatment, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI, and the 
cytoskeleton was stained with TRITC-phalloidin fluorescence probe. 
Cellular morphology was visualized using a confocal microscope 
(FV3000, Olympus, Japan).
2.9. In vitro osteogenic differentiation
rBMSCs at passages 2 ~ 4 were utilized to evaluate the osteogenic 
differentiation potential of the hydrogel in vitro.
2.9.1. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining
This assay was performed using transwell plates, with the medium 
replaced every 3 days. On days 3 and 7, ALP staining was performed 
using the BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase color development kit, 
following the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained cells were observed 
and imaged using an inverted microscope (Leica, Germany).
2.9.2. Alizarin red s (ARS) staining
rBMSCs (1000 cells/well) were cultured in wells beneath the trans­
well chambers, with the medium replaced every 3 days. On days 14 and 
21, cells were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde and stained with ARS. 
After being washed three times with DI water, cells were observed and 
imaged using an inverted microscope.
2.10. In vivo periodontitis treatment
This animal study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of 
West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University. Twenty-four 
male SD rats (approximately 200 g each) were randomly assigned to 
six groups: healthy control (HL), Control, AgZ/PLEL + NIR, AgZ@Au/ 
PLEL + NIR, AgZ@Au/PLEL + Glu, and AgZ@Au/PLEL + NIR + Glu (n 
= 4 per group). Periodontitis was induced using a ligature and bacterial 
injection method. Specifically, a 4–0 silk suture was placed around the 
second maxillary molar of each rat, followed by injection of 20 µL of 
P. gingivalis suspension (OD600 = 0.4) into the gingival sulcus every 24 h 
for 2 weeks.

injected into the periodontal pockets. The rats were subjected to 808 nm 
laser irradiation (2 W/cm2) for 5 min or left untreated. Temperature 
variations and photothermal images were recorded using a thermal 
imaging camera. This treatment regimen was repeated at 7-day intervals 
for a total of 2 weeks. Gingival sulcus fluid was collected with sterile 
cotton swabs, and microbial colony counts were determined on coated

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Fabrication and characterization of Ag2S NPs, AgZ and AgZ@Au
In our previous work, NIR-responsive PTT reagents (Ag2S NPs) using 
3-mercaptopropionic acid (3-MPA) as protecting agent were prepared 
for component optimization through a green and safe chemical copre­
cipitation method [28,29]. The Ag2S NPs exhibited homogeneous dis­
persity with an average size of ~17.0 nm, as revealed by transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 2a). The Ag2S NPs were subsequently 
coated with an acid-responsive zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) 
to construct a nanosystem. With an optimized mass ratio of Zn2+/2- 
methylimidazole (1:19.4), the ZIF-8 coated Ag2S NPs were spherical-like 
with a particle size of ~ 240 nm (Fig. 2b) and named AgZ. Subsequently, 
Au3+ replaced Zn2+ in ZIF-8 through ion exchange, followed by in situ 
reduction to generate Au nanoparticles (Au NPs), resulting in the NIR- 
driven nanosystem (AgZ@Au) (Fig. 1). The morphology of AgZ@Au 
was observed by TEM, displaying spherical-like particles with average 
diameter of ~170 nm (Fig. 2c). The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) 
image (Fig. S1) revealed that Au NPs (~5 nm) were homogeneously 
anchored to the surface of AgZ, with the 0.23 nm lattice spacing 
belonging to Au (111) planes. The SAED pattern (Fig. 2d) confirmed the 
high crystallinity of the Au NPs. High-angle annular dark-field scanning 
transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and elemental map­
ping of AgZ@Au (Fig. 2e) showed the homogenous distribution of Ag, S, 
Zn, Au within nanosystem, confirming the successful construction of the 
ZIF-8 entity, Ag2S NPs, and Au NPs, respectively. Moreover, the mass 
fractions of Zn, Ag, and Au in AgZ@Au were determined to be 18.4 %, 
13.0 %, and 9.8 %, respectively, using ICP-OES.
The zeta potential and particle size of AgZ@Au were further 
analyzed. As shown in Fig. 2f, g, the zeta potential of AgZ@Au was 
measured as −10.95 mV, with a hydrated diameter of ~ 247 nm. The x- 
ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis confirmed that the Au 
element (Fig. 2h) and two distinct peaks in Au4f spectra (Fig. 2i and 
Fig. S2) were only observed in AgZ@Au nanosystem and were absent in 
AgZ and Ag2S NPs. Au peaks appeared at 84.10 eV (4f7/2) and 87.73 eV 
(4f5/2) (Fig. 2h, i). X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. 2j) revealed 
that the crystal structures of AgZ@Au and AgZ were similar to ZIF-8, 
indicating the preservation of the fundamental structure of ZIF-8. 
Compared to AgZ, AgZ@Au displayed distinctive diffraction peaks for 
the (111), (220), and (311) crystal planes of Au NPs (Fig. 2k) [45,46].

(LSPR) effect of Au NPs [47,48].
3.2. Evaluation of photothermal performance
Photothermal images of AgZ@Au under laser irradiation were 
captured using a thermal imager (Fig. 3a). Fig. 3b and c showed that the 
temperature of AgZ@Au increased with higher concentration and laser 
intensity. The temperature of AgZ@Au (100 µg/mL) rose by 30.4 ◦C 
after 300 s of exposure to an 808 nm laser at 2 W/cm2, followed by a 
concentration-dependent way. Similarly, AgZ also displayed a compa­
rable concentration-dependent photothermal response (Fig. S3a), pri­
marily attributed to the incorporated Ag2S (Fig. S4), as demonstrated in 
our previous studies [28,29]. Notbaly, AgZ@Au exhibited superior 
photothermal performance compared to AgZ. This enhancement is pri­
marily attributed to the localized surface plasmonic resonance (LSPR) of 
surface-modified Au NPs [47,48], which generated additional heat 
under the same laser intensity, leading to a higher temperature increase. 
Meanwhile, the intrinsic photothermal effect of Au NPs is minimal 
(Fig. S5), underscoring the necessity of Ag2S incorporation. Further­
more, even after five irradiation/cooling cycles, the quantitative tem­
perature profile of AgZ@Au remained consistent, demonstrating the 
sufficient photothermal stability of the material (Fig. 3d). Based on 
cooling phase data from a photothermal cycle (Fig. 3e, f, Fig. S3b and c) 
and using Roper’s method [44], the photothermal conversion effi­
ciencies of AgZ@Au and AgZ were determined to be 33.3 % and 28.7 %, 
respectively. The enhanced photothermal effect of AgZ@Au may be 
attributed to the modification of Au NPs.

attributed to the Au NPs in AgZ@Au, which act as GOx-like nanozymes, 
converting glucose into hydrogen peroxide and gluconic acid. To mimic

3.4. Characterization of hydrogel
PLEL is an amphiphilic block copolymer composed of a hydrophilic 
PEG segment and a hydrophobic polylactic acid segment, which spon­
taneously forms micelles with a core–shell structure in aqueous solution 
(Fig. 1). The applicability of PLEL as a carrier for periodontitis treatment 
depends on its ability to undergo sol–gel transformation at physiological 
temperature. The in vitro gelation of AgZ@Au/PLEL was evaluated via 
the tube inversion method (Fig. 4a). The system exhibited a flowable sol 
state at 4 ◦C. As the temperature increased, PLEL micelles enlarged, 
leading to aggregation and bridging, forming a semisolid hydrogel at 
37 ◦C, and reverting to a fluid state at 55 ◦C. Both the unmodified 
hydrogel and the hydrogel with 100 µg/mL AgZ@Au displayed this 
reversible phase transition. The phase transition at 37 ◦C highlights the 
hydrogel’s suitability for in vivo injection, whereas the transition at 
55 ◦C suggested that laser exposure can alter its morphology, demon­
strating its temperature sensitivity and photo-responsiveness.
To further elucidate the dynamic mechanical behavior of the PLEL 
hydrogel under temperature variation, dynamic rheological experi­
ments were performed (Fig. 4b). Consistent with macroscopic charac­
terization, AgZ@Au/PLEL hydrogel exhibited thermosensitive behavior 
similar to that of blank PLEL. Specifically, when the temperature 
exceeded ~37 ◦C, both the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G’’) 
increased rapidly, the storage modulus (G’) exceeded the loss modulus 
(G’’), indicating a gel state. At temperatures above ~42 ◦C, the loss 
modulus (G’’) surpassed the storage modulus (G’), showing a transition 
to a sol state.

3.5. In vitro antibacterial effect
AgZ@Au is expected to exhibit significant antibacterial potential, 
attributed to its excellent photothermal conversion and photothermally 
enhanced catalytic performance. The antibacterial efficacy of AgZ@Au 
was tested on P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum. The inhibitory effects on 
their growth were evaluated by plotting bacterial growth curves under 
different treatments. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, without laser irradiation, 
the growth curve for the AgZ/PLEL group exhibited a gradual increase, 
indicating a relatively slow proliferation of P. gingivalis. Conversely, the 
number of bacteria of AgZ@Au/PLEL with the addition of β-D-glucose 
decreased by 33.1 % after 72 h, attributed to the generated hydrogen 
peroxide from glucose. With laser irradiation, the AgZ@Au/PLEL + NIR 
group’s number of bacteria consistently decreased by 35.4 %, while the 
AgZ@Au/PLEL + Glu + NIR group exhibited a significant decrease of 
almost 100 %. This indicated that the photothermally enhanced cata­
lytic efficiency of AgZ@Au provided an effective antibacterial effect in 
the presence of glucose. Fig. 5b and Fig. S7 showed spread plate images 
of pathogenic bacteria under various treatments. As illustrated in Fig. 5b 
and e, many colonies remained in the AgZ/PLEL group even after laser 
irradiation, while laser irradiation of AgZ@Au/PLEL significantly 
reduced the CFU. This can be attributed to the stronger photothermal 
properties of AgZ@Au, which can generate more heat to promote anti­
bacterial activity. Meanwhile, a significant reduction of colonies was 
also observed in the AgZ@Au/PLEL + Glu group, where the hydrogen 
peroxide generated from glucose catalyzed by the GOx-like nanozyme 
exerted antibacterial effects. However, this effect was insufficient for 
complete bacterial eradication, highlighting the necessity for further 
enhancement. The group further reduced visible colonies after adding 
both glucose and laser irradiation, with 99.72 % and 99.93 % antibac­
terial rates against P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum, respectively. The OD600 
measurements showed in Fig. S8 aligned with the plate-counting results, 
showing the lowest values for the AgZ@Au/PLEL + Glu + NIR group 
(P. gingivalis: 0.24; F. nucleatum: 0.21). Its superiority arises from the 
coordination between the nanozyme catalytic reaction and laser irra­
diation, which significantly elevated hydrogen peroxide levels in this 
process. The live/dead assay results further ensured that AgZ@Au/PLEL 
plus laser management with the addition of glucose caused more bac­

3.6. In vitro cytocompatibility evaluation
In addition to the remarkable antibacterial capabilities, ensuring the 
biocompatibility of the material is crucial for promoting periodontitis 
recovery. The biocompatibility of PLEL hydrogel loaded with different 
components was assessed using a CCK-8 assay. Fig. 6a and Fig. S9
showed that AgZ/PLEL and AgZ@Au/PLEL maintained over 80 % L929 
cell viability at concentrations of 0–100 µg/mL. Therefore, 100 µg/mL 
was selected for AgZ@Au in subsequent in vitro cell experiments. 
Additionally, all three hydrogels demonstrated biocompatibility with 
rBMSCs (Fig. 6b).
The impact of the AgZ@Au/PLEL hydrogel on rBMSC morphology 
was assessed. Fig. 6c showed that rBMSCs in all groups maintained 
normal morphology, with elongated spindle-shaped or polygonal forms. 
These results confirm the biocompatibility of the rationally designed and 
synthesized AgZ@Au/PLEL for future biomedical applications.

3.7. Osteogenic differentiation in vitro
The ability of hydrogels to induce osteogenic differentiation of 
rBMSCs in vitro was assessed by alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and 
alizarin red S (ARS) staining. ALP serves as a marker of early osteogenic 
differentiation. Fig. 7a showed that minimal blue staining in the PLEL 
and Ag2S/PLEL groups, whereas the AgZ/PLEL group exhibited signifi­

indicating elevated ALP expression. Glucose was converted to gluconic 
acid via AgZ@Au, facilitating ZIF-8 decomposition and zinc ion release.

confirmed in our hydrogel release kinetics studies. The AgZ@Au/PLEL 
+ Glu + NIR group exhibited the most intense blue staining and the 
highest ALP expression, surpassing the additive effects of the individual 
treatments. In this group, the NIR-induced temperature rise boosted the 
GOx-like catalytic activity of AgZ@Au, generating more acid and pro­

3.8. Therapeutic efficacy of periodontitis in vivo
Considering the excellent antibacterial ability, osteogenic capacity, 
and good biocompatibility of AgZ@Au in vitro, its anti-periodontitis 
effect was investigated in vivo. The whole in vivo experiment process 
was shown in Fig. 8a, including the establishment of periodontitis model 
and the treatment process. The model was established using a ligature 
and bacterial injection. After that, hydrogels were injected into the 
periodontal pockets and subjected to 808 nm laser irradiation (Fig. 8b).
A thermal imager was used to monitor temperature changes during 
laser irradiation, and photothermal curves were plotted (Fig. 8c and 8e). 
The AgZ@Au/PLEL group showed a 37.3 ◦C increase after 5 min, sur­
passing the AgZ/PLEL and control groups. This trend was consistent 
with in vitro photothermal performance evaluation results. Meanwhile, 
the plate coating method was used to analyze microbial counts in 
gingival crevicular fluid. Fig. S10 showed that the AgZ@Au/PLEL + Glu 
+ NIR group had fewer bacterial colonies compared to all other groups 
(except the healthy control), suggesting the AgZ@Au/PLEL hydrogel is 
also effective against bacteria in vivo.
Since alveolar bone loss occurs as bacterial infection and inflam­
mation progress [2,3], micro-CT was performed to evaluate bone loss 
(Fig. 8d). Compared with the healthy control (HL) group, the vertical 
distance between the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) and the alveolar 
bone crest (ABC) was markedly increased in the control group (an in­
crease of 0.43 mm). In contrast, AgZ@Au/PLEL + Glu + NIR group 
significantly decreased the CEJ-ABC by 0.27 mm (Fig. 8f), closely 
resembling the HL group. Additionally, bone volume (BV), tissue volume 
(TV) and BV/TV ratio were measured for the palatal alveolar bone. As 
shown in Fig. 8g and h, the BV/TV ratio in the AgZ@Au/PLEL + Glu +
NIR group was second only to the HL group and significantly higher than 
in other experimental groups. These results indicated that the AgZ@Au/ 
PLEL hydrogel promoted alveolar bone regeneration within the peri­
odontal pockets, leading to restoration of bone mass.
Histopathological analysis was performed on maxillary bone sam­
ples. HE staining images (Fig. 9a) showed significant alveolar bone 
destruction due to periodontitis, characterized by slender bone 
morphology and a worm-eaten appearance under the microscope, 
indicating active bone resorption. After treatment, the bone morphology 
in the AgZ@Au/PLEL + Glu + NIR group showed significant improve­
ment, closely resembling the HL group.
Notably, reversal lines were observed in the bone tissue, indicating 
cessation of resorption and new bone formation above these lines. 
Moreover, Masson’s trichrome staining results (Fig. 9b) revealed that 
disorganized collagen fibers between the alveolar bone and tooth root in 
the control group, suggesting damage to Sharpey’s fibers. In contrast, 
the AgZ@Au/PLEL + Glu + NIR group exhibited dense collagen fiber 
alignment, similar to normal tissue.
Additionally, periodontitis often involves excessive production of 
cytokines, such as IL-1β and IL-6. As shown in Fig. 9c and Fig. S11, the 
control group showed extensive brown staining around the alveolar 
bone, indicating high levels of IL-1β and IL-6. In contrast, the AgZ@Au/ 
PLEL + Glu + NIR group exhibited significantly lower expression, 
demonstrating the hydrogel’s effectiveness in controlling infection and 
reducing inflammation. In vivo toxicity of the hydrogel was assessed by 
HE staining of major organs in rats. Fig. S12 showed no pathological 
abnormalities, indicating that the material is biocompatible and safe.
4. Conclusions
In this study, Ag2S nanoparticles were employed as a core, coated 
with ZIF-8, and precisely anchored with Au NPs to form AgZ@Au. This 
nanosystem was incorporated into an injectable thermosensitive PLEL 
hydrogel, yielding the AgZ@Au/PLEL hydrogel. The hydrogel indicated 
excellent biocompatibility, photothermal properties, antibacterial effi­
cacy, and the ability to promote alveolar bone regeneration. Au NPs 
exhibited GOx-like activity, converting glucose into hydrogen peroxide 
and gluconic acid. Upon NIR laser irradiation, this catalytic reaction was 
enhanced to achieve therapeutic outcomes, with the generated 
hydrogen peroxide synergizing with PTT to eliminate pathogens. The 
acid-responsive release of zinc ions from ZIF-8 further facilitated BMSCs 
differentiation. These findings suggested that the AgZ@Au/PLEL 
hydrogel is a promising candidate for periodontitis treatment, offering 
an innovative NIR-driven GOx-like therapeutic strategy.

ABSTRACT: The serious threat of antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections has
brought an urgent need for the development of new antibacterial nanomaterials.
We encapsulate glutathione (GSH)-protected gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) in
zeolitic imidazolate frameworks-8 (ZIF-8) and present their potential in
antibacterial capabilities. Under white light irradiation, AuNCs-embedded ZIF-
8 nanocomposites show assembly-enhanced emission and reactive oxygen species
(ROS) generation. AuNCs@ZIF-8 exhibit almost complete inactivation of
bacterial growth within 60 min of light irradiation. Scanning electron microscopic
results show that AuNCs@ZIF-8 nanocomposites are captured by bacterial cells,
and the leakage of alkaline phosphatase and nucleotides from bacteria
demonstrate that the photoinduced ROS can easily destroy the bacterial surface
and totally kill the bacteria. Herein, our antibacterial nanocomposites have
photoenhanced bactericidal capability and show promising applications for sterilization.
■INTRODUCTION
In recent years, bacterial infections have emerged as a serious
threat to human health and brought a major global economic
loss.1 Especially with the emergence of the new coronavirus, it
has become a priority to ﬁght oﬀviral and bacterial harm. Over
the past decade, antibiotics were regarded as the most eﬀective
antibacterial medicine, selectively inhibiting or killing bacteria
by preventing DNA replication or repair and protein
synthesis.2 However, the widespread use of antibiotics in
general medicine also has an important impact on the
emergence of drug-resistant strains in patients.3 Among
many common pathogenic strains, an antibiotic-resistant
gene has already been disseminated.4 Consequently, the
preparation of a new antibacterial material with high eﬃciency
and safety as an alternative class of antibiotics has become a
hot topic. The applications of antibacterial materials have
attracted wide attention because of the signiﬁcance in many
ﬁelds, for instance, medicine treatment, food packaging,
hygiene, water treatment, and biological medicine.5 With the
development of nanotechnologies, researchers in relevant ﬁelds
are making eﬀorts to discover new antibacterial nano-
composites, including cationic polymers, antibacterial peptides,
noble metals, photothermal agents, and reactive oxygen species
(ROS)-generated agents.6−8
It is worth nothing that ROS-generated nanomaterial agents
can be regarded as potential antibacterial agents because of the
rapid oxidation of various biomacromolecules in cells.9
Although a low level of ROS has a signiﬁcant eﬀect on
supporting the cellular life cycle, excessive ROS could generate
oxidative stress, giving rise to oxidative damage to the cellular
constituents (e.g., nucleotides).10 Through this property, ROS
acts as strong oxidants to destroy targeted harmful bacteria and
contributes to sterilization. Recently, the release of ROS from
antibacterial materials has been realized in photocatalytic
disinfection11 and antibacterial photodynamic therapy.12
Gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) are deﬁned as nanostructures
with <2 nm in diameter consisting of several to a hundred gold
atoms.13 Because of their strong quantum size conﬁnement
and discrete energy levels, AuNCs exhibit strong luminescence
and good biocompatibility, resulting in widespread use in
bioimaging and biosensing.14,15 Meanwhile, AuNCs also
display antibacterial capability after being metabolized by
bacterial cells.16,17 More importantly, AuNCs can act as
inorganic photosensitizers because of their long triplet-state
lifetime. ROS can be generated by the energy transfer from
AuNCs to molecular oxygen with light irradiation, which is
especially eﬀective for photodynamic therapy.18,19 Never-
theless, optical and chemical properties of AuNCs are highly
sensitive to their capping ligands and surrounding circum-
stances. Consequently, it is necessary to develop AuNCs-based
nanocomposites to achieve high ﬂuorescence intensity and

meanwhile improve their ROS generation. Metal−organic
frameworks (MOFs), as the name implies, are crystalline
porous solids composed of a three-dimensional structure of
inorganic metal ions and organic linkers. Zeolitic imidazolate
frameworks-8 (ZIF-8) are a kind of characteristic porous
MOFs consisting of transition-metal Zn and imidazolate
linkers.19,20 By virtue of their large capacity and high porosity,
ZIF-8 are wildly applied as carriers of nanoenzymes,
antibacterial agents and ﬂuorescent probes in biomedical
areas; hence, they can fabricate favorable composite materi-
als.21,22 When AuNCs were entrapped in ZIF-8, quantum
yields and ﬂuorescence lifetimes of AuNCs were greatly
improved because of the aggregation-induced emission.23,24
However, there is no report on antibacterial application of
AuNCs-incorporated ZIF-8 nanocomposites because of their
photoinduced ROS generation. Therefore, it is important to
study ROS release of AuNCs as photosensitizers and explore
their ROS-induced photodynamic eﬀect when AuNCs are
encapsulated in ZIF-8, evidently contributing to photoinduced
antibacterial applications.
In this work, herein, we design a novel antibacterial agent by
incorporating AuNCs into a ZIF-8 matrix (AuNCs@ZIF-8)
through simple coordination-assisted self-assembly for safe,
rapid, and eﬀective bacterial disinfection. AuNCs@ZIF-8 can
be readily irradiated by a white light source to produce ROS,
where the ROS production amount is highly improved when
many AuNCs are incorporated into ZIF-8. After AuNCs@ZIF-
8 nanocomposites are captured by bacterial cells, photo-
induced ROS can easily attach to the bacterial surface and
totally destroy the bacteria (Scheme 1). AuNCs@ZIF-8 are
regarded as ROS storage that have an extinctive impact on
bactericidal activities.
■RESULT AND DISCUSSION
Synthesis of AuNCs@ZIF-8 Nanocomposites. AuNCs
were incorporated in a ZIF-8 matrix to form AuNCs@ZIF-8
nanocomposites through simple coordination-assisted self-
assembly. Zn2+ ions of ZIF-8 have a coordinative interaction
with the carboxyl group in glutathione (GSH)-protected
AuNCs. The morphologies of AuNCs@ZIF-8 and AuNCs
were ﬁrst characterized by transmission electron microscopy
(TEM) (Figure 1a and Figure S1). The prepared AuNCs@
ZIF-8 have monodispersibility and a uniform size of 75 ± 9
nm. In comparison to ZIF-8 (Figure 1a, insert), no obvious
diﬀerence was observed in the shape and size of AuNCs@ZIF-
8, indicating that AuNCs were conﬁned in the framework of
ZIF-8 without aﬀecting its morphology. The powder X-ray
diﬀraction (XRD) results further demonstrated that the
incorporation of AuNCs into a ZIF-8 matrix did not change
the ZIF-8 skeleton, as the XRD pattern of AuNCs@ZIF-8 was
almost identical to that of pure ZIF-8 (Figure 1b).23 TEM and
XRD results demonstrated that the incorporation of AuNCs
cannot change the ZIF-8 morphology and skeleton.
Furthermore, energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), photo-
luminescence, and X-ray photoelectron spectrum (XPS)
analyses conﬁrmed that the AuNCs were incorporated into
the ZIF-8 framework successfully. EDS results showed that the
elements Zn, C, N, and O were the main components of the
system and the elements Au and S veriﬁed the incorporation of
AuNCs in ZIF-8 (Figure 1c and Figure S2). Moreover,
compared to pure AuNCs, AuNCs@ZIF-8 nanocomposites
have obvious ﬂuorescence enhancement. After incorporation,
the ﬂuorescence intensity of the AuNCs in the nanocomposites
was ∼10 times as high as that of the free ones (Figure 1d). The
ﬂuorescence intensity of the AuNCs was enhanced in the
matrix conﬁnement because of the aggregation-induced
emission of AuNCs, reducing the collisions with solvents and
restraining the vibration and rotation of excited AuNCs.25
Meanwhile, the ﬂuorescence of AuNCs slightly turned from
orange red to yellow, which was in accord with the slight
negative shift of the ﬂuorescence spectra (Figure 1d insert).
Additionally, XPS analysis provided information on the
elemental composition and chemical states of elements in
the composite materials. The XPS fully scanned spectrum
showed the presence of Zn 2p, O 1s, N 1s, C 1s, S 2p, and Au
4f signals in the range of 0−1200 eV (Figure 1e). Compared to
the previous report of AuNCs (84.1 eV), the XPS spectrum of
Au 4f7/2 (83.6 eV) in AuNCs@ZIF-8 exhibited a slight
negative shift, which might be due to the electron transfer
between AuNCs and ZIF-8 (Figure 1f).26 The XPS results
further conﬁrmed the existence of AuNCs in ZIF-8 nano-
composites.
Photoinduced ROS Generation. 2,7-Dichlorodihydro-
ﬂuorescein (DCFH) was used as a probe to detect ROS
production, where nonﬂuorescence DCFH is rapidly oxidized
by ROS to become dichloroﬂuorescein (DCF), which emitted
ﬂuorescence at 525 nm of wavelength. The ﬂuorescence
intensity of DCFH-containing solution increased upon
irradiation with a Xe lamp for 10 min, suggesting the increase
of ROS generation in the solution (Figure 2). When without
irradiation (in dark), there is almost no ﬂuorescence signal for
DCFH containing phosphate buﬀered saline (PBS), AuNCs,
and ZIF-8 during the 10 min detection (the three lines almost
overlapped), and AuNCs@ZIF-8 have a little bit of
ﬂuorescence because of the ﬂuorescent emission of incorpo-
rated AuNCs. After light illumination, both free ZIF-8 and free
AuNCs showed an increase of ﬂuorescence intensity that was
attributed to their ROS generation. The formation of ROS
from ZIF-8 might be from the activation of oxygen molecules
by photoinduced Zn+ intermediate catalytic centers within
porous ZIF-8 through electron transfer.27 Additionally, ROS
generation of AuNCs could be facilitated by photoinduced
energy transfer to oxygen molecules from triplet excited states
of AuNCs.18,28 In comparison to free AuNCs and ZIF-8,
AuNCs@ZIF-8 nanocomposites showed the fastest increase of
ﬂuorescence intensity, even higher than the summation of free

ROS. The results were in agreement with their enhanced
phosphorescence emission. In the literature, a similar
phenomenon was found where AuNCs embedded in a

component to achieve the synergistic eﬀect of higher ROS

Photoinduced Disinfection Performance. Photoin-
duced antibacterial activities of AuNCs@ZIF-8 were detected
using a spread plate method. In view of their excellent ROS
generation capability, AuNCs@ZIF-8 were analyzed with
additional antibacterial tests and compared to AuNCs and
ZIF-8. Figure 3a,b showed that AuNCs@ZIF-8 almost
completely inhibited the growth of Gram-negative Escherichia
coli (E. coli) after 60 min of light illumination (approximately
0% cell viability), whereas AuNCs@ZIF-8 without light
showed 39.4% cell viability, suggesting AuNCs@ZIF-8 could
eﬃciently inhibit bacterial growth with the assistance of light
irradiation. The results could be explained by the abundant
ROS generation of AuNCs@ZIF-8 induced by light. Addi-
tionally, the partial cell death induced by AuNCs@ZIF-8 in
dark might be due to the intrinsic antibacterial property of
AuNCs and ZIF-8. Metal ions Zn2+ from ZIF-8 were
responsible for inhibiting and killing the bacteria.29 Reducing
the size of the nanoparticles down to the nanoclusters range
could enhance antimicrobial activity.30 AuNCs or ZIF-8 with
light irradiation showed 55.5% and 33.1% cell viability, which
was slightly lower than the AuNCs and ZIF-8 without light,
suggesting that the ROS generation from free AuNCs or free
ZIF-8 by light was too low to induce total cell death. As shown
in Figure 3c,d, after 60 min of light irradiation, AuNCs@ZIF-8
also exhibited superior antibacterial activity (approximately 0%
cell viability) to Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S.
aureus) compared to control samples (9.8% for AuNCs and
4.3% for ZIF-8), suggesting a certain amount of ROS was
required to achieve complete sterilization. Treatment with
AuNCs@ZIF-8, ZIF-8, and AuNCs without light irradiation,
the cell viability was 22.1%, 32.4%, and 51.4%, respectively.
The results demonstrated that AuNCs@ZIF-8 nanocomposites
exhibited a distinguished photoenhanced antibacterial eﬀect,
and combining two antibacterial materials into a single system
can achieve higher antibacterial activities.

Mechanism of Disinfection. For further investigation of
the disinfection mechanism, the morphology and cell wall
integrity of bacterial cells were observed by scanning electron
microscopy (SEM); E. coli control without treatment was rod-
shaped with a complete cell wall and smooth surface (Figure
5a). After treatment with AuNCs@ZIF-8 in the dark, the
bacterial cells were surrounded by the nanocomposites and
their cell walls were almost unchanged (Figure 5b). After
treatment with AuNCs@ZIF-8 under 20 min of light
irradiation, the cell walls became partially wrinkled and
incomplete (Figure 5c). In addition, Figure 5d shows that S.
aureus cells without treatment were smooth and sphere-
shaped; meanwhile, the cells treated with AuNCs@ZIF-8 in
the dark also looked plump (Figure 5e). After experiencing
AuNCs@ZIF-8 and light illumination, the cells were out of
shape and the cell walls were damaged and uneven (Figure 5f).

With light illumination, it was obviously observed that many
AuNCs@ZIF-8 nanocomposites attached to the bacterial cell
wall, and some of them even diﬀused into the cell wall and
destroyed their integrity (Figure 5c,f). The adsorption of
nanoparticles onto the cell wall was due to the electric
attraction between positively charged AuNCs@ZIF-8 (+27.4
mV) and negatively charged bacterial cells, which was
signiﬁcant for antibacterial agents to exert their bactericidal
eﬀect. The nanocomposites could destroy the cell wall more
eﬃciently in virtue of the strong interaction.
For evaluation of the cell wall permeability and leakage of
genetic materials, alkaline phosphatase (AKP) activity and
leakage of nucleotides were analyzed in the bacterial cells
treated with AuNCs@ZIF-8 nanocomposites and light
illumination. AKP exists between the cell wall and cell
membrane, which is a superior index for cell wall permeability.
The optical density (OD) value at 520 nm (OD 520) can be
measured to detect AKP activity.32 As shown in Figure 6a, in
contrast to the control group, treatment with AuNCs@ZIF-8
and light irradiation caused an increase by 70 and 700 times in
the amount of AKP leakage in E. coli and S. aureus bacterial
cells, respectively (***p < 0.001). The extracellular AKP
activity was in line with the SEM images, demonstrating the
cell wall has been damaged after treatment.
In addition, the release of nucleotides from bacterial cells
was detected to study cell membrane integrity and leakage of
genetic materials. The nucleotides have strong UV absorptions
at 260 nm.33 As shown in Figure 6b, in contrast to the control
group, treatment of AuNCs@ZIF-8 and light irradiation
caused a signiﬁcant diﬀerence in the amount of nucleotides
leakage for E. coli (***p < 0.001) and S. aureus (**p < 0.01).
The results suggested that the AuNCs@ZIF-8 exhibited
obvious antibacterial properties, triggering the changes in
membrane permeability and nucleic acid leakage.
■CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we designed a photoinduced ROS-generated
antibacterial agent for bacterial disinfection. ZIF-8 was used as
a carrier for encapsulating AuNCs because of their high
porosity and good biocompatibility. AuNCs embedded in ZIF-
8 showed assembly-enhanced photoinduced ROS production.
After 60 min of light irradiation, AuNCs@ZIF-8 nano-
composites initiated severe oxidative stress to totally kill the
bacterial cells, which attributed to their capture by bacterial
cells and subsequent destruction of the bacterial cell wall. This
article provides a valuable and novel strategy to develop ROS-
generated antimicrobial materials with photoenhanced bacter-
icidal capability.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Materials. Chloroauric acid and sodium hydroxide were
purchased from Sigma. 2-Methylimidazole was purchased from
Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Co. Ltd. Zinc nitrate hexahy-
drate (AR) was brought from Guangdong Xiqiao Chemical
Co. Ltd. Glutathione reduced was purchased from Sangon
Biotech. DCFH was purchased from Shanghai Macklin
Biochemical Co. Ltd. Gram-negative E. coli and Gram-positive
S. aureus were purchased from American ATCC Co. Luria
Broth (LB) was purchased from Qingdao Haibo Biotechnol-
ogy Co. Ltd.
Characterization. pH values of the solutions were
measured by using a Mettler Toledo FE20/EL20 Laboratory
pH meter. TEM images were obtained with a JEOL JEM-
1200EX electron microscope operating at 100 kV with the
dispersions that were dropped onto carbon-coated copper
grids and then dried in air. High-resolution transmission
electron microscope images were obtained with a JEOL JEM-
1200EX electron microscope operating at 100 kV. The
ﬂuorescence spectra were obtained on a Shimadzu RF-
5301PC spectroﬂuorophotometer. The zeta potential of
AuNCs@ZIF-8 was measured with a Brookhaven ZetaPlus
apparatus. The UV−vis absorption spectrum was recorded on
a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer. The powder XRD
patterns were recorded on a Rigaku D-Max 2550 diﬀrac-
tometer equipped with a graphite monochromator using Cu
Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) at a scanning speed of 4 min−1. XPS
was performed on a Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc ESCALAB
250XI spectroscopy. EDS and electron-mapping measurements
attached to an SEM were used to analyze the composition. A
300 W Xe lamp coupled with an AM 1.5 ﬁlter (400 < λ < 780
nm) was employed as a light source with an optical power
density ﬁxed at 100 mW cm −2 (1 sun) to simulate sunlight
irradiation.
Synthesis of AuNCs. AuNCs were synthesized in a water
bath according to a previous report at 70 °C.34 Concisely, the
aqueous solution of HAuCl4·4H2O (40 mM, 1 mL) was mixed
with GSH (100 mM, 0.6 mL) and ultrapure water (18.4 mL).
Then the solution was heated to 70 °C under stirring for 24 h
and then cooled to 0 °C. The AuNCs were puriﬁed using a
dialysis bag. The puriﬁed AuNCs solution was adjusted to 20
mL and stored at 4 °C for the following experiments.
Synthesis of AuNCs@ZIF-8 Nanocomposites. AuNCs@
ZIF-8 was synthesized according to a previously reported
method with a little revision.23 One milliliter of AuNCs was
precipitated with Zn(NO3)2 aqueous solution (80 μL, 40 mg
mL−1). After removal of the supernatant, the precipitated
AuNCs were speedily injected into the mixture of Zn(NO3)2
methanol solution (2 mL, 35 mM) and 2-methylimidazole (2-
Melm) methanol solution (2 mL, 160 mM) under magnetic
stirring (500 rpm). After 12 h, the resulting precipitate was
centrifuged, washed, and vacuum-dried at 60 °C to obtain a
light yellow powder. In the following assays, the ﬁnal
concentration of AuNCs@ZIF-8 in the system was 500 μg
mL−1 and the dosage of free AuNCs (or free ZIF-8) was the
same between AuNCs@ZIF-8 and AuNCs groups (or ZIF-8
group).

Bacterial Cell Culture. E. coli and S. aureus were selected
as model bacteria. They were cultured in a LB medium at 37
°C with shaking at 250 rpm for 20 h. After centrifugation
(3000 rpm, 10 min), the cultured bacteria were dispersed in
PBS at a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU mL−1 for the following
experiments.
Antibacterial Activity of AuNCs@ZIF-8. Typically, 10 μL
of the bacterial solution (1 × 108 CFU mL−1) and 200 μL of
the AuNCs@ZIF-8 nanocomposites, AuNCs, and ZIF-8 were
respectively injected into 790 μL of the PBS. Then each
solution was irradiated under a Xe lamp for a total of 60 min.
As the reaction progressed, 100 μL of the mixture was taken
out at 20 min intervals, which were determined by a standard
plate count method to count the cell viability of bacteria.
SEM Analysis. The E. coli and S. aureus bacterial
suspensions mixed with AuNCs@ZIF-8 nanocomposites in
PBS were irradiated under a Xe lamp for 20 min. After
centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min and washing with PBS,
the precipitate was ﬁxed with 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde for 4
h at 4 °C, dehydrated by an ascending series of ethanol washes
(30%, 50%, 80%, and 100%). The prepared samples were gold-
sputtered followed by SEM observation.
Determination of AKP Activity. The activity of AKP, an
extracellular enzyme, serves as an index for the permeability of
the bacterial cell wall. After illumination for 60 min, the
mixture of bacteria (10 μL, 1 × 108 CFU mL−1) and 200 μL of
AuNCs@ZIF-8 or PBS (control group) were centrifuged for
removal of the precipitate. The supernatant was analyzed with
an AKP assay kit (cat. no. a059-1; Nanjing Jiancheng
Bioengineering Institute) and the OD at 520 nm was measured
to detect AKP activity.
Determination of Released Nucleotides. The 10 μL of
bacterial suspensions (1 × 108 CFU mL−1) mixed with 200 μL
of AuNCs@ZIF-8 or PBS (control group) were irradiated
under a Xe lamp for 60 min. The mixture was centrifuged
(12000 rpm, 10 min) and the supernatant was ﬁltered with a
ﬁlter membrane. Finally, the OD of the resulting solution was
measured at 260 nm (OD 260) using a microplate reader.

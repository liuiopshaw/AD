Abstract
Persistent bioﬁlm infections pose a critical health threat with their relentless pres-
ence and ampliﬁed antibiotic resistance. Traditional antibacterial photodynamic
therapy can inhibit bacteria extracellularly but struggles to control bioﬁlm forma-
tion and virulence. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop photosensitizers, such
as ultra-small gold nanoclusters (AuNCs), that can penetrate bioﬁlms and internal-
ize into bacteria. However, AuNCs still face the challenge of insufﬁcient reactive
oxygen species (ROS) production and limited near-infrared light absorption. This
study develops a model of indocyanine green (ICG)-sensitized AuNCs with atomic-
precision size effect. This approach achieved near-infrared light absorption while
inhibiting radiation transitions, thereby regulating the generation of ROS. Notably,
different-sized AuNCs (Au10NCs, Au15NCs, Au25NCs) yielded varied ROS types,
resulting from different energy level distributions and electron transfer rates. ICG-
Au15NCs achieved a treatment efﬁcacy of 99.94% against Staphylococcus aureus
infections in vitro and signiﬁcantly accelerated wound healing in vivo. Moreover,
this study highlights the unique role of ICG-AuNCs in suppressing quorum sens-
ing, virulence, and ABC transporters compared to their larger counterparts. This
strategy demonstrates that atomic-precision size effect of AuNCs paves the way for
innovative approaches in antibacterial photodynamic therapy for infection control.

INTRODUCTION
Bacterial infections pose a signiﬁcant global public health
challenge, with an estimated annual mortality of 700,000
worldwide, projected to escalate to 10 million by 2050,
alongside potential economic losses of $100 trillion.[1–3]
A primary cause of the persistence of these infections
is the formation of bacterial bioﬁlms. These bioﬁlms,
comprising bacteria encased in self-produced extracellular
polymeric substances, form a formidable physical barrier to
antibiotics.[4] Bioﬁlms can signiﬁcantly amplify their resis-
tance to traditional antibiotics by up to 1000-fold in compar-
ison with planktonic bacteria.[5] Additionally, the virulence
factors produced by bacteria can inﬂict lasting host dam-
age, including tissue destruction, chronic inﬂammation, and
impaired wound healing, culminating in prolonged illness
and potentially severe long-term complications.[6] Antibac-
terial photodynamic therapy (aPDT), which employs photo-
sensitizers to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon
light exposure, aims to cause irreversible damage to bacte-
rial organisms.[7] Despite its potential, current approaches
using FDA-approved organic compounds like porphyrins
and methylene blue, alongside novel inorganic photosensi-
tizers, fail to effectively penetrate deep into bioﬁlms.[8–15]
These photosensitizers generally produce ROS only extra-
cellularly at bioﬁlms surfaces, resulting in non-selective
oxidative damage to cell membranes, leakage of intracellular
contents, release of virulence factors, and exacerbated host
damage.[16,17] Moreover, these strategies are insufﬁcient in
mitigating the host damage induced by bacterial virulence
factors.[18,19]
Gold nanoclusters (AuNCs), with a diameter of less than
2 nm, represent an innovative category of atomically precise
photosensitive materials.[20] These clusters have garnered
signiﬁcant research interest in biological imaging, photo-
catalysis, and antibacterial therapy, due to their unique
properties.[21–23] Consisting of a deﬁned number of gold
atoms and surrounded by surface ligands, their minuscule
size enables deep penetration into bioﬁlms and facilitates bac-
terial internalization.[24] Despite their potential, research on
AuNCs-based aPDT faces challenges such as limited absorp-
tion to the visible spectrum, inadequate ROS production, and
a restricted variety of ROS types.[25,26] To overcome these
obstacles, researchers have explored additional methods to
augment the efﬁcacy of AuNCs-based aPDT. For instance,
Yuan et al. developed a dual-functional photosensitizer com-
bining photothermal and photodynamic properties through
a coupling strategy to enhance therapeutic effectiveness.[27]
Similarly, Jiang et al. engineered composite materials by
integrating DNA cleaving enzymes with nanoclusters, thus
improving antibacterial activity by facilitating dispersion
within bioﬁlms.[28] Additionally, Xie et al. achieved a syn-
ergistic antibacterial effect, greater than the sum of its parts,
by combining MXenes with AuNCs.[29] Compared to the
aforementioned combination therapies, aPDT can avoid the
complexity and potential synergistic side effects.[12,20] How-
ever, enhancing the inherent ROS production of AuNCs
continues to be a formidable challenge. Additionally, the
ability to leverage the atomic precision of AuNCs to tailor
antibacterial targeting mechanisms remains to be elucidated.
In response to the challenges associated with bacterial
bioﬁlm formation highlighted in the preceding analysis,
this study aims to develop a novel approach using nano-
photosensitizers based on AuNCs. This approach involved
leveraging the atomic-precision size effect and sensitiza-
tion modiﬁcations of AuNCs. Notably, we employed the
FDA-approved photosensitizer indocyanine green (ICG) to
sensitize AuNCs of varying sizes, offering a promising
solution. The covalent interactions between AuNCs and
ICG achieved near-infrared (NIR) absorption while inhibit-
ing radiative transitions. By adjusting the energy levels of
AuNCs and ICG, we altered the photogenerated electron
dynamics to achieve efﬁcient electron–hole separation and
electron transfer, which in turn enhanced ROS production and
diversiﬁed the types of ROS generated. More importantly,
ICG-AuNCs could penetrate bioﬁlms and enter bacteria
more effectively, addressing the limitations of ROSs’ range
of action and short half-life. Additionally, transcriptomic
sequencing analysis revealed that treatment with ICG-AuNCs
reduced bioﬁlm formation and bacterial toxin secretion,
thereby enhancing overall healing outcomes—an advantage
not observed with their larger counterparts. This research
endeavors to advance innovative therapeutic strategies for
combating bacterial bioﬁlms and improving patient outcomes
in the realm of antibacterial therapy (Scheme 1). Further
exploration of modiﬁcation studies in AuNCs for aPDT
holds promise for advancing therapeutic interventions in
antibacterial treatments.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
2.1
Synthesis and characterization of
ICG-AuNCs
Three sizes of glutathione-protected AuNCs (Au10NCs,
Au15NCs, and Au25NCs) were examined to understand the
inﬂuence of nanoparticle size on sensitized-modiﬁed AuNCs
in aPDT. These sizes were chosen based on their distinct char-
acteristics as reported in prior studies. Then, the synthesis of
ICG-conjugated AuNCs was conducted at room temperature
in a mixed aqueous and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution.
Among them, ICG-Au15NCs exhibited superior antibacterial
performance and were therefore selected for detailed investi-
gation using a density functional theory (DFT) computational
model, illustrated in Figure 1A. Optimal ratios of ICG to Au
were determined through absorption spectroscopy, correlat-
ing peak intensities with concentration variations depicted in
Figure S1A. Consistency in the quantity of Au atoms and
ICG across all nanoparticle sizes was maintained to ensure
controlled experimental conditions. Ultraviolet–visible (UV–
vis) absorption spectroscopy, detailed in Figure 1B, indicated
a shift in the ICG absorption peak from 795 to 710 nm
post-conjugation, aligning with established literature.[30]
Additionally, absorption spectra of the three pure AuNCs
are presented in Figure S1B, exhibit characteristic peaks.
Furthermore, emission spectroscopy results (Figure 1C and
Figure S1C–E) revealed complete quenching of ICG’s NIR
emission and the luminescence of AuNCs upon the formation
of ICG-AuNCs. This demonstrates strong distance-dependent
photoinduced electron transfer.
The Fourier Transform Infrared spectra (FTIR) of ICG-
Au15NCs are displayed in Figure 1D. Speciﬁcally, the
absence of an absorption band at ν(S─H) = 2523 cm−1

of structural and electronic factors, particularly in the types of
ROS and photoinduced electron transfer. Research is needed
to elucidate the physical mechanisms driving the enhanced
ROS generation observed with ICG-AuNCs. In the following
sections, we will discuss these issues in detail.

2.2
Size-dependent ROS enhancement
performance of ICG-AuNCs
To thoroughly explore the size-dependent ROS enhancement
effect of ICG-AuNCs, we utilized Electron paramagnetic res-
onance (EPR) spectroscopy to analyze the types of ROS.
ICG, as a type II photosensitizer, generates only 1O2 under
808 nm NIR irradiation (Figure 2A–C). The behavior of
ICG-AuNPs mirrored that of ICG, adhering to the type II pho-
todynamic pathway, characterized solely by energy transfer
between the photosensitizer and O2. In stark contrast, ICG-
AuNCs displayed enhanced production of ⋅OH and ⋅O2−via
the type I photochemical pathway, indicating the occurrence
of electron transfer in addition to energy transfer between the
photosensitizer and O2. Notably, ICG-Au15NCs were found
to signiﬁcantly generate 1O2, ⋅OH, and ⋅O2−, as evidenced
in Figure 2A–C. Additionally, the EPR spectra of AuNCs
with different sizes were used as control groups, as shown
in Figure S4A–I. This conﬁrms the pronounced impact of the
atomic-precision size effect of AuNCs on ROS production
and highlights the critical roles of both energy and electron
transfer mechanisms within the ICG-AuNCs system.
As depicted in Figure 1B,C, following the formation
of ICG-AuNCs, a noticeable blue shift in the absorption
position occurs, and luminescence quenching is no longer
observed. This phenomenon is generally attributed to the for-
mation of charge transfer complexes, the utilization of excited
state energy in electron transfer, and effective energy level
alignment between the donor and acceptor molecules.[34–36]
To further elucidate the direction of electron transfer, the
energy level distribution of AuNCs was estimated using
cyclic voltammetry, as shown in Figure 2D. Upon exci-
tation, electrons in ICG transition from the highest occu-

2.3
Antibacterial performance of
ICG-AuNCs in vitro
In vitro antibacterial experiments aimed to validate the
antibacterial efﬁcacy of ICG-AuNCs, with free ICG and
larger-sized ICG-AuNPs serving as controls. For this pur-
pose, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, ATCC 29213) and
Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC 47076), representing Gram-
positive bacteria and Gram-negative bacteria respectively,
were selected. The results, depicted in Figure 3A–B and
Figure S5, showed that under NIR irradiation, treatments with
ICG and ICG-AuNPs partially reduced the colony-forming
units (CFUs) of both bacterial bioﬁlms, with reductions of
less than 1-log and 2-log, respectively. In contrast, the three
different sizes of ICG-AuNCs displayed markedly enhanced
antibacterial efﬁciencies, showcasing a distinct atomic-
precision size effect. Among them, ICG-Au15NCs proved to
be the most effective, signiﬁcantly reducing bacterial bioﬁlms
with over 3-log decreases in CFUs for both S. aureus and
E. coli, achieving antibacterial efﬁciencies of 99.94% and
99.90%, respectively. Meanwhile, ICG-Au10NCs and ICG-
Au25NCs displayed efﬁciencies of 98.52% and 98.81% for S.
aureus, and 98.01% for E. coli each, as recorded in Figure 3B
and Figure S5. This correlates closely with the ROS quantum
yield of ICG-AuNCs. Given that an antibacterial efﬁciency of
less than 99.90% may not meet clinical standards due to the
risk of rapid proliferation and potential reinfection of surviv-
ing bacteria, ICG-Au15NCs, with their superior antibacterial
performance, stand out as highly effective for controlling
bacterial infections.[17,39]
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to
further investigate the antibacterial process by examining
the morphological changes in bacterial bioﬁlms. Figure 3C
shows the morphologies of S. aureus bioﬁlms following var-
ious treatments, while SEM images of E. coli bioﬁlms are
presented in Figure S6. In the ICG-Au15NCs group, the
bacteria exhibited the most signiﬁcant structural integrity dis-
ruption, with many cells showing severe damage or complete
dissolution. In contrast, the membrane disruption in the ICG-
Au10NCs and ICG-Au25NCs groups was less pronounced
(Figure S7A). This membrane disruption leads to the release
of cellular contents, including proteins and nucleic acids.
Detailed results for S. aureus are shown in Figure 3D and
Figure S7B–C, and for E. coli in Figure S8A,B. Notably,
ICG-Au15NCs exhibited the highest leakage of nucleic acids
and proteins, likely due to the elevated levels of ROS pro-
duced, demonstrating the profound impact of nanoparticle
sizing on antibacterial efﬁcacy.
The spatial interaction between nanomaterials and biosys-
tems, particularly its impact on bacteria, is crucial for antibac-
terial efﬁcacy.[40] Given that the lifespans of ROS range
from nanoseconds to milliseconds, their direct impact on
the internal environment of bacteria is inherently limited.[41]
To better understand these interactions, TEM and TEM-
mapping were utilized to analyze the distribution and relative
intensity of Au of ICG-AuNCs and ICG-AuNPs, examin-
ing their spatial interaction with bacteria. Figure 3E shows
that ICG-AuNPs predominantly accumulate on the bacterial
surface without penetration, whereas ICG-Au15NCs signiﬁ-
cantly enrich Au inside bacteria cells, with a relative intensity
four times that of ICG-AuNPs, as detailed in Figure 3F.
Further, TEM-mapping of ICG-Au10NCs and ICG-Au25NCs
(Figure S9) shows better intracellular Au enrichment than
ICG-AuNPs, yet without signiﬁcant size-dependent varia-
tions within these nanoclusters. These results underscore an
internalization efﬁciency: ultra-small ICG-AuNCs are able to
enter bacterial cells, unlike their larger counterparts (ICG-
AuNPs). However, internalization efﬁciency does not vary
signiﬁcantly with size among the nanoclusters, corroborat-
ing previous ﬁndings.[24] This nuanced difference in the
locations and quantities of ROS production may impact
bacterial metabolism differently. MTT assays (Figure S10)
reveal that S. aureus’s metabolic activity is most signiﬁcantly
suppressed after treatment with ICG-Au15NCs. Hence, the
atomic-precision size effect of ICG-AuNCs pertains to efﬁ-
cient internalization, which is not inﬂuenced by size within
the nanoclusters scale, yet varying ROS quantum yield from
different sizes causes distinct oxidative impacts, affecting
bacterial metabolism in diverse ways.

2.4
Antibacterial mechanism of
ICG-AuNCs
ICG-Au15NCs demonstrated the most potent antibacterial
properties among three sizes of ICG-AuNCs and were there-
fore chosen to compare antibacterial mechanisms against
ICG-AuNPs. We performed transcriptomic analysis using
RNA-sequencing on S. aureus ATCC 29213 bioﬁlms treated
with both ICG-Au15NCs and ICG-AuNPs. Principal com-
ponent analysis (PCA), illustrated in Figure S12A, revealed
signiﬁcant differences among the control, ICG-AuNPs, and
ICG-Au15NCs groups. After treatment, 417 genes showed
statistically signiﬁcant changes with ICG-AuNPs, and 705
genes with ICG-Au15NCs (Figure S12B). The Venn dia-
gram in Figure S12C identiﬁes the intersections and unique
segments of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). An
empirical Bayesian approach highlighted 272 upregulated
and 493 downregulated genes with |log2(fold change)| ≥1
and p-value < 0.05, as depicted in the volcano plot (Figure
S13). A comprehensive heatmap provided in Figure S14 visu-
alizes these genes, with those highly expressed marked in red
and those signiﬁcantly suppressed in green.
TEM and SEM imaging, as shown in Figures 3E and 3C
respectively, revealed that ICG-Au15NCs induce ROS pro-
duction within bacterial cells, contrasting with the predomi-
nantly extracellular activity of ICG-AuNPs. Given the critical
role of ribosomes in bacterial cells, we speciﬁcally analyzed
the expression of ribosome-related genes. Ribosomes are fun-
damental components of bacterial cells and key targets for
many antibacterial mechanisms due to their essential func-
tion in protein synthesis.[43] As depicted in Figure S15, there
was a signiﬁcant downregulation of these genes in bacteria
treated with ICG-Au15NCs, highlighting their intracellular
action and inﬂuence on protein synthesis. In contrast, treat-
ment with ICG-AuNPs did not show notable changes in these
genes, further conﬁrming the distinct mechanisms and effects
of the two treatments.
Genes collaborate to drive biological functions, and Kyoto
Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway anal-
ysis is instrumental in elucidating these functions.[44] As
shown in Figure 4A, compared to the ICG-AuNPs group,
pathways related to quorum sensing, S. aureus infection,
ABC transporters, and bioﬁlm formation were notably down-
regulated in the ICG-Au15NCs treatment group. Furthermore,
gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) identiﬁes gene sets that
exhibit signiﬁcant expression changes within speciﬁc biolog-
ical pathways, independent of predeﬁned gene sets.[45] The
GSEA results, illustrated in Figure 4B, indicate that follow-
ing treatment with ICG-Au15NCs, compared to ICG-AuNPs,
gene sets related to quorum sensing, S. aureus infection,
and ABC transporters underwent signiﬁcant changes. The
normalized enrichment scores (NES) measure the degree of
enrichment of a speciﬁc gene set within a ranked list of genes.
In this context, the NES values of −1.48, −1.44, and −1.26
indicate a downregulation of gene expression across these
three pathways. This suggests that the “atomic-precision size
effect” of ICG-Au15NCs can effectively disrupt the forma-
tion of bacterial bioﬁlms and further impact broader bacterial
biological behavior.
Bacteria communicate within bioﬁlms via quorum sens-
ing molecules. Disruption of their synthesis, secretion, and
reception can signiﬁcantly impact bioﬁlm development. As
depicted in Figure 4C, the quorum sensing pathway of S.
aureus was notably affected, with 15 of 51 genes signif-
icantly downregulated. KEGG Orthologs analysis, shown
in Figure S16, indicated a substantial decrease in genes
phnA and phnB, involved in signaling molecule synthesis.[46]
Interestingly, phnA and phnB are also crucial for bioﬁlm for-
mation, further supporting that their downregulation could
hinder bioﬁlm development. Furthermore, expression of
SecY2, essential for bacterial protein secretion, was markedly
reduced, diminishing quorum sensing capabilities, toxic-
ity, and immunogenicity (Figure S16).[47] The reception of
signaling molecules was also impaired, evidenced by the
reduced expression of the Opp family (Opp/Ami, Prg Z/Opp,
Tra C/Opp).[48] Overall, the protein–protein interaction (PPI)
network demonstrates that treatment with ICG-Au15NCs
inhibited the entire pathway of inter-bacterial signaling
(Figure S17), an effect not observed with ICG-AuNPs. This
indicates that the ICG-Au15NCs can effectively disrupt the
formation of bacterial bioﬁlms at a molecular level.
The S. aureus infection pathway, which outlines the bac-
terial adhesion, invasion, toxin, in vivo and protein release
leading to host cell damage and inﬂammation, is crucial
for understanding pathogenicity. As shown in Figure 4D,
the downregulation of genes within this pathway indicates
that ICG-Au15NCs attenuate the pathogenicity of the bacte-
ria. This pathway includes bacterial surface proteins, toxins,
cell wall constituents, host cell receptors, and signaling
molecules. Figure S18 identiﬁed a decrease in expression of
the isdA and Sbi genes on the surface of S. aureus. Accord-
ing to the PPI network depicted in Figure S17, isdA, a
critical adhesin, binds to host proteins, facilitating adhesion
and resistance to lactoferrin. Its reduced expression likely
diminishes both adhesion and pathogenicity.[49] Additionally,
genes such as sbi, aur, and scc, which encode proteins that
disrupt host immunity, were also downregulated (Figure 4D
and Figure S18). Intriguingly, genes associated with S. aureus
cytolysins (Hlg/Luc, lukF-PV, hlgB, lukS-PV, hlgC) and
superantigen enterotoxin (seu, sem, sei, sen) showed reduced
expression, further mitigating bacterium’s capacity to induce
pathogenic effects and inﬂammatory responses.[50] In sum-
mary, compared to ICG-AuNPs, ICG-Au15NCs signiﬁcantly
suppress the S. aureus infection pathway, effectively reduc-
ing the bacterium’s virulence while enhancing host resistance
and healing capacity.
ABC transporters in S. aureus are pivotal for bacterial
growth, metabolism, bioﬁlm formation, and the secretion of
virulence factors.[51] Treatment with ICG-Au15NCs led to the
downregulation of genes in the phn gene cluster (phnE, phnC)
and ugpC, which are involved in metabolizing and transport
of phosphorylated compounds in S. aureus.[52] (Figure 4E
and Figure S19). Additionally, the expression of crucial phos-
phate transport genes pstB, pstA, and pstC was also reduced
(Figure 4E). GO analysis further conﬁrmed the downregu-
lation in the biosynthesis and metabolism of extracellular
matrix components and amino acids, including histidine,
tryptophan, and α-amino acids (Figure S20). This suggests
that ICG-Au15NCs may disrupt phosphate transportation and
metabolism via ABC transporters, consequently reducing
nutrient availability, which could inﬂuence bioﬁlm formation
and the secretion of virulence factors. This ﬁnding provides

The unique antibacterial action of ICG-Au15NCs may stem
from their water-soluble, ultra-small size, enhancing their
internalization into bacterial cells compared to the larger
ICG-AuNPs. Given SecY2’s critical role in bacterial toxic-
ity, quorum sensing, and bioﬁlm formation, further molecular
dynamics simulations identiﬁed SecY2 as a biological binding
site, as shown in Figure 4F. These simulations demonstrate
that ICG-Au15NCs can tightly bind to SecY2 through hydro-
gen bonds and salt bridges, suggesting that the ROS generated
by ICG-Au15NCs at ultra-micro distances can directly impact
biomolecules like SecY2 and broader bacterial biochemical
processes. Future research will delve deeper into the direct
interactions between AuNCs and biomolecules, potentially
unveiling more about their mode of action.

offer unparalleled advantages over ICG-AuNPs in both efﬁ-
cient antibacterial action and reducing bacterial pathogenic-
ity. In contrast, the larger ICG-AuNPs primarily exert
effects extracellularly, damaging the bacterial cell mem-
brane through ROS production and inducing oxidative stress.
Although ICG-AuNPs are capable of killing bacteria, they
fall short in multi-level regulation of bacterial virulence,
quorum sensing, and bioﬁlm formation. Thus, leverag-
ing the atomic-precision size effect of AuNCs to enhance
aPDT capabilities and reduce bacterial toxicity presents a
compelling and effective antibacterial strategy.

to be upregulated during the early stages of blood ves-

with various concentrations of ICG-AuNCs.
Overall, the in vivo results clearly demonstrate that ICG-

but also reduce the expression of virulence factors. This leads
to decreased tissue inﬂammation, reduced collagen degra-
dation, and promoting angiogenesis, ultimately facilitating
effective healing of infected wounds.

CONCLUSION
In this study, we successfully developed a novel multi-
sized NIR-activated ICG-AuNCs model and validated its
efﬁcacy in combating bioﬁlm formation and bacterial vir-
ulence factors both in vitro and in vivo. The inﬂuence of
atomic-precision size effect on sensitization modiﬁcations
was initially conﬁrmed. The ICG-Au15NCs composite, with
matched energy levels and high electron–hole separation efﬁ-
ciency, signiﬁcantly enhanced the generation and diversity of
ROS, which are critical for intensifying antibacterial action.
ICG-AuNCs exhibited the ability to penetrate and internal-
ize into bacterial bioﬁlms and cell membranes, a capability
that larger ICG-AuNPs lack. This led to remarkable regula-
tion of bacterial metabolism, effectively suppressing quorum
sensing, S. aureus infection pathways, and ABC transporters
activity, resulting in reduced bioﬁlm formation and decreased
toxicity. Overall, the AuNCs may herald a new era for
photosensitizers, offering innovative strategies for bioﬁlm
clearance through their ultra-small size, addressing the issue
from multiple dimensions. These strategies could be applied
to other bacterial infectious diseases, potentially transforming
approaches to microbial infection control.

The gut microbiota came into focus within the last years regarding being associated with or even under-
lying neuropsychiatric diseases. The existence of the gut-brain-axis makes it highly plausible that bacte-
rial metabolites or toxins that escape the intestinal environment or approach the vagal connections
towards the brain, exert devastating effects on the central nervous system. In Alzheimer’s disease
(AD), growing evidence for dysbiotic changes in the gut microbiota is obtained, even though the question
for cause or consequence remains open. Nevertheless, using modulation of microbiota to address inﬂam-
matory processes seems an attractive therapeutic approach as certain microbial products such as short
chain fatty acids have been proven to exert beneﬁcial cognitive effects. In this review, we summarize,
contemporary knowledge on neuroinﬂammation and inﬂammatory processes within the brain and even
more detailed in the gut in AD, try to conclude whom to target regarding human microbial commensals
and report on current interventional trials.

1. Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by progressive loss of
neuronal structures (synapses or even cells) which is accompanied
by a devastating loss of cognitive functions and changes in person-
ality (for a recent overview see: [1]). Since decades, only symp-
tomatic treatments were clinically available, such as donepezil or
rivastigmine. These drugs postpone - by their effect on neurotrans-
mission - functional decline for some months, potentially allowing
a delayed reception order for a nursing home (e.g. [2]). More
recently, passive immunization medications targeting Ab peptides
have been approved by the FDA under the premise that clinical
efﬁcacy will be proven in future and ongoing studies [3,4]. There-
fore, new innovative drugs, usable for a broad spectrum of patients
and economically manageable, are still needed. Within the last few
years, two new scientiﬁc evidences emerged in the somehow
exhausted ﬁeld of AD research: neuroinﬂammation with the focus
on microglia on one hand (e.g. [5]) and the microbiome on the
other hand (for example, [6]). This resulted in a discussion regard-
ing the gut-brain-axis as a new source for therapy strategies.
Within this review, we will highlight 1) what is known about
inﬂammatory processes in the brain and more detailed the gut in
AD; 2) summarize current knowledge on what might be called
an ‘‘AD (gut) microbiome”; 3) present therapies targeting this
altered
gut
microbiota
that
might
alleviate
or
modify
neuroinﬂammation.
1.1. Inﬂammatory processes involved in AD
The contribution of the immune system has been for long and
intensely discussed in AD research; however, still it appears as a
double-edged sword providing both, deleterious and beneﬁcial
aspects. Reports about immune mediators in peripheral or central
liquid biopsies such as plasma are inconsistent (see for example
reviewed in [7]). Elevation of the pro-inﬂammatory marker Tumor
necrosis factor alpha (TNFa), for example, has been mostly associ-
ated with cognitive decline in AD (summarized by [8]) but also
reports exist that describe unchanged or even decreased levels in
blood or brain-derived biomaterial (e.g. [9-11]). Even if TNFa is
one of the best-studied immune mediators, these inconsistencies
impair its usage as a biomarker. They might rely on individual dif-
ferences but also on the difﬁculty to identify and compare espe-
cially early stages of disease. While along these early stages,
inﬂammatory processes seem to be potentially protective and pos-
sibly to be affected by therapeutic modulation, manifested disease
seems to come along with a chronic silent immune reaction,
resembling immunoaging
(Fig.
1). Thus, distinct therapeutic
approaches will be needed considering early versus late stage of
disease but also, probably, regarding CNS-targeting effects versus
systemic effects.
1.2. Inﬂammation in the CNS along AD pathogenesis
Within the CNS, predominantly microglia and astrocytes exert
immunological functions. Excellent reviews highlighted within
the last years how inﬂammation and especially distinct microglial
activity states contribute to AD evolvement and progression (e.g.
[12,13]). We therefore refer to these publications and summarize
only some of the key ﬁndings here to present a sketch of the situ-
ation in the CNS.
1.2.1. Microglia:
These cells are thought to derive from the yolk sac during devel-
opment in different waves of hematopoiesis [14,15]. Microglia play
an important role in functional maintenance within the brain as
e.g. supporting neurons trophically or phagocyting debris. How-
ever, different cell types with different functions have to be distin-
guished. Classically, two types were described - M1 and M2. While
M1-type cells can be induced by lipopolysaccharides and inter-
feron c and induce a pro-inﬂammatory scenario, M2-type cells
on the contrary are activated by IL-4 or IL-13 and induce anti-
inﬂammatory reactions (for example [16-18]). Thus, it needs to
specify M1/M2-characteristic markers of microglia to draw a phys-
iological relevant picture of stages within pathogenesis such as
PGC-1-related coactivator for M1 or ARG1, FIZZ1, and YM1 for
M2 (see [13]). While M2 microglia might have the potential to
ameliorate Ab-induced damage by inducing its phagocytosis
[19,20], M1 polarization seems to attenuate phagocytosis [21]. In
the hippocampus of PS1M146L/APP751SL model mice, an age-
dependent switch from M2 towards M1 has been observed [22].
However, nowadays the M1/M2 classiﬁcation can only be used as
a simpliﬁed model as proteomics and transcriptomic approaches
revealed further heterogeneity of microglial populations – not only
along the disease stages but also in spatial terms [23] and in
response to different disease hallmarks, i.e. Ab and Tau species
[24]. In sum, there might be different ways to interfere with micro-
glial conditions in therapeutic considerations: modifying the phe-
notypic change between pro- and anti-inﬂammatory populations
or attenuating the chronic late stage of microglial activation (re-
viewed e.g. in [12]).

1.2.2. Astrocytes:
There is an emerging picture of maybe comparable heterogene-
ity of astrocytes in AD: a cellular molecular map revealed, for
example, in 7 to 10 month old 5xFAD model mice major differences
in astrocyte expression state as compared to wild type controls
[25]. Six transcriptional sub-clusters could be identiﬁed which var-
ied in their respective amount during different disease stages. In
the 5xFAD mice, an additional subtype termed disease-associated
astrocytes (DAA) was found, characterized by a Gfap-high state.
Interestingly, 18 from the 239 DAA signature genes overlapped
with
the
signature
genes
previously
described
for
disease-
associated microglia (e.g. Cathepsins B, D, and L; [26]). A meta-
analysis on mouse data [27] summarized that there is – similar
to the situation with microglia – a highly differing picture regard-
ing acute and chronic disease stages. Within acute neurodegener-
ation
models,
e.g.,
mainly
a

regarding
protein

indicated overexpression of astrocyte-secreted extracellular matrix
proteins. If and to what extent these ﬁndings can be transferred to
human patients still needs to be demonstrated (for recent reviews
on astrocytes in AD, see for example [28] or [29]).
The increasingly understood differentiation of inﬂammatory
CNS cell types, however, results in the demand to diagnose pre-
cisely the current stage of a patient, concerning inﬂammation.
Thus, peripheral robust markers delivered from other organ sys-
tems such as the gut – might be helpful, assuming that inﬂamma-
tion there might reﬂect or even precede inﬂammatory processes of
the brain.
1.3. Inﬂammatory conditions in the intestine in AD
While a plethora of publications describes the inﬂammatory
state in the brain or blood of rodent disease models but also in
AD patients, only little is known about a potential inﬂammation
in the gastrointestinal system itself and the impact of such on neu-
rodegeneration. First evidences might be gained from a potential
correlation of inﬂammatory gastrointestinal diseases and the risk
to
develop
a
neurodegenerative
disease:
two
recent
well-
powered studies report, for example, on an increased risk for AD
in Inﬂammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) patients. By comparing IBD
patients (1,742) and healthy controls (17,420) in a Taiwanese pop-
ulation longitudinally, the risk of developing dementia was found
to be of 5.5% for patients, while only 1.4% of healthy controls
became demented [30]. Moreover, age of onset was lower in the
patient cohort. In an even larger Korean study (24,830 IBD and
99,320 non-IBD individuals, [31]) the risk was also found elevated
(adjusted hazard ratio for AD 1.14; 1.56 for PD). A promising mar-
ker for inﬂammatory events in the gut might be provided by cal-
protectin (CP) since fecal CP is elevated in IBD [32-34]. If
Calprotectin can serve for reporting on intestinal inﬂammation in
AD has still to be proven, but a small study in patients [35] at least
showed that most of the 22 investigated patients had concentra-
tions outside normal.
Dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) is used in rodents to elicit colitis
and by using this, the impact of gut inﬂammatory events could be
recapitulated in certain AD mouse models. Exemplarily, within the
knock-in mouse model AppNL-G-F an exacerbated plaque deposition
and a decreased microglial CD68 immune reactivity was found
[36]. Similar observations were gained in a pharmacological PD
mouse model [37] and even in wild type mice (e.g. neuronal loss,
[38]). In 3xTg AD model mice, where the treatment with DSS facil-
itated pathology in gut as well as in brain, vagotomy blunted the
observed effects and ameliorated Ab and Tau deposits [39]. Strik-
ingly, this surgical intervention was also able to restore learning
and memory capacity. This might hint at the vagus nerve (VN) as
one of the major routes in gut-brain-axis communication in AD.
A study using Paenalcaligenes hominis, a bacterium with increased
abundance in aging humans and mice, demonstrated that celiac
vagotomy could not prevent colitis in SPF mice; however, it pre-
vented cognitive impairment. Interestingly, here, also extracellular
vesicles of the bacterium were able to evoke deleterious effects,
while LPS derived from it remained without effect.
However, also leakiness of the respective barriers and especially
the gut-blood barrier has been described in AD and might explain
how bacterial metabolites may exert effect on the CNS. In SAMP8
mice, for example, a reduced expression of the tight junction pro-
teins occludin and ZO-1 has been found in comparison to the con-
trol strain in intestinal tissue [40]. This mouse line is characterized
by an accelerated aging phenotype and therefore used as a spo-
radic type model for AD. An increase in leakiness of the intestinal
barrier was also reported for AppNL-G-F mice [41], 5xFAD mice
[42] or Tg2576 mice [43]. In a drastic model of disintegrated bar-
rier (cecal ligation and puncture, [44]) this could be conﬁrmed:
the bacterial sepsis increased hippocampal Ab plaque load and
neuroinﬂammation in APP/PS1-21 mice. Occurrence of a leaky
gut barrier seems also to play a role in human patients as a positive
correlation of amyloid PET signal intensities with endothelial dys-
function markers was found [45].
Direct measures of inﬂammatory signatures in the gastroin-
testinal compartment are still scarce. In mouse models increased
inﬂammation has been described such as GFAP expression eleva-
tion or reduced IL-17 production in mesenteric lymph nodes or
Peyer’s patches [41,46,47]. As the increased accumulation of gut-
derived immune cells in meninges in a mouse DSS induced colitis
model was observed [38], this might give a good explanation how
inﬂammation in the gut might proceed towards the brain. Direct
measures of inﬂammatory markers in gut of AD patients are – so
far not available – while deposition of Ab has been described in
small studies [43,48]. However, for PD, increased CD88 and
NFkBp65 in sigmoid colon biopsies was demonstrated (n = 5–6,
[37]).
A debate still to conduct is, if inﬂammation triggers pathology
or if it is a secondary effect to it. For example, NLRP3 (NOD-,
LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3) inﬂammasome was
found elevated in 5xFAD mice in colon as compared to wild type
controls [42]. The effect was already observed in mice designated
as young. However, they were aged 5 months and the comparably
aggressive 5xFAD mouse model at this age is already affected by
pathology [49]. Transfer of AD patients-derived microbiota to
APP/PS1 mice, for example, induced NLRP3 in the intestine [50].
Thus, the observed inﬂammatory effect might be due to an already
affected microbial community and not an independent driver. In
this regard, it is of note that the microbiota changes over the time
span of pathogenesis – at least in AD model mice: for example, an
early
increase
in
Akkermansia
was
found
in
APP/PS1
mice
(2 months, [51]), while at a higher age, Blautia increased (9 month
of age). This stage-dependency has been also observed for 5xFAD
[52] or APP/PS1 mice [53] and might be relying on an acute bacte-
riotoxic effect of increasingly elevated Ab peptide levels at the
beginning of disease which then turns into a stabilized, Ab-
coping community [54].
2. The AD microbiome
The number of investigations on the microbiome in preclinical
animal models or AD patients is still restricted. These investiga-
tions are also methodologically limited as most are based on
sequencing approaches and focus on bacterial organisms. This
means, that mostly the mycobiome and viral components are
neglected and not much information is gathered about functional

2.1. Findings from studies in humans and AD mouse models
The intestinal systems of mouse and man share many similari-
ties but are also characterized by species-dependent differences.
One prominent example is the cecum – a structure used in mice
for fermentation of plant-derived materials and for vitamin syn-
thesis. In humans its function is not clariﬁed and it is comparably
small [55]. In both, human and mouse, the microbiome is predom-
inantly constituted by the phyla Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes (e.g.
[56,57]); however, Ley at el. concluded from a deeper taxonomic
analysis that about 85% of bacterial genera found in mouse are
not present in human beings [56]. As most AD preclinical models
are based on mice, transferability of ﬁndings therefore might be
difﬁcult in general (for a critical review see [58]). By comparing
available studies in man and mouse, only ﬁve bacterial groups
could consistently be identiﬁed to be changed within more than
one study: Blautia, Odoribacter, Ruminococcus, Alistipes, and S24-7
(summarized in [59]). Additionally, Akkermansia might include
notable species as it is increased in Parkinson’s disease as well as
in AD but reduced in aging, which presents the highest risk factor
for AD (see for example reviewed in [60]). In the following, some
characteristics of these bacterial groups are described with a focus
on their anti- or pro-inﬂammatory potential.
2.1.1. Blautia
The genus Blautia has been found increased in an AD mouse
model as well as in a human cohort study in differing biological
samples (blood-derived bacterial membrane vesicles or fecal sam-
ples [61,62]). Blautia belongs to the Lachnospiraceae family (phy-
lum Firmicutes). The anaerobic bacteria seem to participate in
anti-inﬂammatory, probiotic reactions as they are, for example,
increased in healthy controls in comparison to patients with Uvei-
tis, an inﬂammatory eye disease [63]. In addition, Crohn’s disease
patients reveal reduced abundance [64]. To date, 20 species have
been described for Blautia, formerly also misclassiﬁed to the genera
Clostridium or Ruminococcus [65]; an overview on e.g. genus-typical
characteristics can be found in: [66]. Blautia is commonly found in
mammalian feces and intestine and provides a wide range of inter-
esting secondary metabolites: siderophores, involved in iron scav-
enging or pigments providing stress protection can be found in
individual strains as well as bactericide substances such as bacte-
riocin [66]. Siderophores released by the microorganisms might
change the metal homeostasis in the gut eliciting subsequent sys-
temic effects. There are evidences linking metal dysregulation and
AD (reviewed in [67,68]). For example, post-mortem analyses of
amyloid plaques in the brain revealed accumulation of iron, cop-
per, and zinc [69]. The metal dysbalance, for example through iron
accumulation, might lead to microglia activation, leading to neu-
roinﬂammation and neurodegeneration via ROS production. Addi-
tionally, Blautia is a provider of short chain fatty acids (for a
detailed analysis of expression of enzymes involved in short chain
fatty acid (SCFA) biosynthesis in Blautia in chicken cecum, see
[70]). To evaluate the protective nature of Blautia as a probiotic
ﬁnally is difﬁcult as most investigations are conducted on the
genus level – also the ones regarding AD. For example, there are
also reports on elevated Blautia OTUs in inﬂammatory diseases or
cancer [71-73] and it might be a question of distinct species or
even strains and physiological conditions in the very ecological
niche that lastly decide on the outcome for the host. This is an issue
that also holds true for the in the following described genera.
2.1.2. Odoribacter
Odoribacter, belonging to the Porphyromonadaceae (Bacteroide-
tes), was also increased in two mouse models for AD: the
senescence-accelerated model SAMP8 [74], which shares some fea-
tures with genetical models such as the APP/PS1 strain [75]. In
addition, PD patients with mild cognitive impairment comprised
higher Odoribacter amount than healthy controls or patients with-
out cognitive impairment [76]. In 2008, the genus Odoribacter was
separated from Bacteroides [77]. It contains up to now three spe-
cies: Odoribacter splanchnicus, Odoribacter laneus, both found in
human feces [78 79], and Odoribacter denticanis, isolated from
canine [77]. Odoribacter, and especially O. splanchnicus, has been
correlated with anti-inﬂammatory, protective properties: e.g.,
exposure led to reduced IL-8 production in HT-29 cells [80] and
anti-colorectal cancer activity, probably mediated by malic acid
[81]. Moreover, it has been shown to limit colonic inﬂammation
in ulcerative colitis [82]. Interestingly, a negative correlation has
been observed for brain weight and Odoribacter in a rat anorexia
nervosa model [83] and for hippocampal blood brain barrier integ-
rity (e.g., claudin5 or occludin expression, [84]) in rats after psy-
chological stress. For O. laneus, data are lacking that allow
estimation of its probable effect on the host and neurodegenerative
processes.
2.1.3. Ruminococcus
The genus Ruminococcus (Ruminococcaceae, Firmicutes) was
ﬁrstly described in 1949, isolated from bovine rumen [85]. There
are six species assigned to this genus, while ﬁve more need to be
re-classiﬁed from Lachnospiraceae (for a review on Ruminococci
see [86]). In AD mouse models, this genus has been found to be
reduced [53,75]. R. torques was described, for example, to exacer-
bate symptoms of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis in a mouse model
[87]. However, R. albus has been found to act neuroprotective
against oxidative stress in both, human neuroblastoma cell line
SH-SY5Y as well as in a rat model [88]. The ambivalent role of
the genus and even single strains can be exemplarily demonstrated
for R. gnavus: it has been found enriched in inﬂammatory diseases
such as IBD [89] or spondyloarthritis [90] in humans, while it ame-
liorated atopic dermatitis by enhancing Treg amount and activity
[91]. Moreover, in 2019, Henke and colleagues described that this
bacterium produces an inﬂammatory polysaccharide that is able
to promote TNFa secretion from dendritic cells [92]. Only two
years later, it was shown that some isolates of R. gnavus, however,
promote a tolerogenic immune response via a capsular polysaccha-
ride [93].
2.1.4. Alistipes
Thirteen species belong so far to the genus Alistipes (Rickenel-
laceae, Bacteroidetes, [94]). A. ﬁnegoldii, for example, has been
assigned anti-inﬂammatory properties as it seems to attenuate col-
itis [95] but other reports concluded a rather pro-inﬂammatory
function [96]. Alistipes was additionally positively correlated with
ROS and negatively correlated with SOD as well as GSH [97]. Inter-
estingly, a correlation of Alistipes with mental health has been
drawn: for example, stress exposure of animal models induced
the abundance of this bacterium [98,99] as did one of the risk fac-
tors for autism in rat offspring [100]. Furthermore, Alistipes is able
to reduce serotonin availability by expression of tryptophanase
activity [101] and to contribute to GABA levels (in chicken, [70]).
In senescence-accelerated SAMP8 mice as well as in human AD
patients [61,74,102], unclassiﬁed Alistipes were elevated – an
observation that also occurred in PD patients [103]. However, in
a Chinese population levels were reduced in MCI patients as com-
pared to healthy controls [104].

2.1.5. S24-7
The bacterial family S24-7, for which also the name Muribacu-
laceae has been proposed (phylum Bacteroidetes), are highly abun-
dant in the mouse gut but also the intestine of other animals. Due
to lack of cultivation until recently, only few functional analyses
are provided so far from the inferred 685 species [105]. They con-
tribute e.g. to release of genomic DNA within the gut mucus layer
and comprised lower pro-inﬂammatory potential as compared to
the whole gut ﬂora genomic DNA in Raw267 cells [106]. In the
immune-priming phase of collagen-induced arthritis in mice,
abundance of S24-7 was reduced [107] as this was also the case
in AD mouse models [61,108].
2.1.6. Akkermansia
The Akkermansia genus (phylum Verrucomicrobia) yet contains
only two species – Akkermansia muciniphila that is found in human
and murine gut and A. glycaniphila, a bacterium occurring in the
gut of a python (four other Akkermansia candidate species exist,
[109]). This should limit evaluation of potential effects to a certain
degree when conducting sequencing approaches. Nevertheless,
here also strain variability interferes and only few strains and their
genomes are available. In general, Akkermansia muciniphila seems
to be accepted as a probiotic [110] and a beneﬁcial regulator of
the immune response [111]. Administration of the Chinese strain
A. muciniphila GP01 to APP/PS1 mice not only affected the gut itself
(intestinal barrier integrity, [112]) but also decreased Ab deposi-
tion and led to improved performance in the Y-maze test. While
in PD, several reports exist about increased Akkermansia levels
[113], a clear conclusion for AD cannot be drawn as investigations
remain controversial [59]. However, in other additional diseased
conditions, Akkermansia has been shown beneﬁcial or reduced in
the state of inﬂammation (e.g., chronic colitis, [114]; psoriasis
[115]; diabetes [116]).
In sum, this gives a rather mixed picture of microbial changes in
the gut ecosystem in AD with a high number of uncertainties
regarding both, direction of change but also resulting conse-
quences for the host. Due to this, a therapeutic approach targeting
single microbial organisms can yet not be deduced. Additionally, it
cannot be distinguished if the change in microbiota is an underly-
ing cause or occurs as a secondary effect due to pathological
changes of the host, which will be discussed in more detail within
the next chapter.
2.2. Dysbiosis or secondary effect?
The number of reports describing differences in gut microbiota
compositions in AD patients in comparison to the corresponding
healthy control groups is increasing. However, studies trying to
elucidate a causal relationship between AD and gut dysbiosis,
especially in humans, are still scarce, mostly descriptive and
observed changes are expected to contribute to the initiation or
progression of the disease; however, effects could also be sec-
ondary to the disease or therapy. Several factors can directly or
indirectly contribute to the composition and species abundance
of the gut microbiota. One important point is the inﬂuence of the
administered pharmacological drugs, which has been largely
ignored so far. For the treatment of the symptoms of AD with tem-
porary beneﬁts, four drugs - donepezil, rivastigmine, and galan-
tamine - which are acetylcholine esterase inhibitors and the N-
methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist memantine are
currently approved by the FDA (full review: [117]). Since 2021
aducanumab, a human monoclonal antibody that is said to selec-
tively target aggregated Ab [118] got the approval in the US. For
all the mentioned medications, digestive side effects such as con-
stipation, diarrhea, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and vomiting
are reported amongst others (for more see [119,120,121]). Due to
the fact that cholinergic and NMDA receptors are expressed in
the gut, the potential impact of the drugs on physiological function
of this organ can be explained [122]. These gastrointestinal dys-
function in patients could subsequently cause disturbance and
instability in gut microbiota leading to an imbalance of the highly
complex ecosystem in the gastrointestinal tract. For example, it
was already demonstrated that gut microbial composition inﬂu-
ences stool consistency through alteration in gut transit time
[123]. Another side effect of the pharmacological drug therapies
are changes in the eating behavior of patients. Differences in the
intake of food due to decreased appetite, dysphagia, nausea, or diet
refusal, for example, can also be the underlying reason for changes
in the microbiota [119]. However, abnormal eating behavior does
not necessarily have to be due to the medication but it is already
known that people with dementia frequently develop eating prob-
lems and dietary changes are observed, especially in the later
stages of the disease [124]. The changes in eating and dietary
habits lead to weight loss, malnutrition, and dehydration [125].
Reasons could be secondary to cognitive impairment or due to
metabolic or neurochemical abnormalities. For example, olfactory
dysfunction is described to occur in patients with dementia, where
the ability to identify and discriminate odors is constrained [126].
This can also inﬂuence appetite and eating behavior. Diet is one of
the key modiﬁable factors inﬂuencing the composition of the gut
microbiota (reviewed in [127]), indicating that the differences in
microbiota composition in studies investigating AD patients com-
pared to control groups could indeed be a secondary effect.
Another factor which has been mostly ignored so far is that the
reported changes in gut microbiota compositions in AD patients
could also be secondary to the disease since Ab, a key hallmark
in AD pathology, has been shown to have antibiotic effects
[128,129]. Ab peptides have been shown to function as antimicro-
bial peptides (AMP). This class of peptides has numerous similar
characteristics including b-sheet structure, the ability to form oli-
gomers and ﬁbrils, and the ability to act anti-microbially [130].
The function of Ab as an AMP was demonstrated regarding fungal
and bacterial infections in mouse, nematode, and cell culture mod-
els of AD [54,128]. Considering the presence of Ab depositions in
the intestine of individual AD patients [48], signiﬁcant changes in
gut microbiota could arise simply due to the antibiotic effect of
local peptides. First evidence that acute in vivo exposure to Ab elic-
its a shift in the gut microbiome, affecting the abundance of
numerous individual operational taxonomic units, was provided
[54].
In sum, there are many plausible explanations why the previous
studies investigating parameters that inﬂuence the structure and
function of the gut microbiome in AD encounter confounding fac-
tors. The secondary effects of the disease or therapy, such as phar-
macological treatments, eating behavior or the antibiotic effect of
Ab could explain why investigations comparing the gut micro-
biome of patients affected by AD with age-matched healthy con-
trols has not resulted in conclusive ﬁndings yet. Most studies
diagnosing dysbiosis are statistically underpowered, counting only
small human sample sizes with no clear delimitation of the groups
[131]. Therefore, it remains a challenge to fully understand
whether a given microbiome has any causative inﬂuence on AD,
or whether identiﬁed associations are secondary effects. Parame-
ters such as medications or eating habits should be closely moni-
tored in future studies to make sure that the test subjects have
the same background. Ideally, tests investigating causative effect
of dysbiosis on AD can only be provided by prospective studies
where microbiota composition is regularly assessed for all partici-
pants before the onset of the disease. Otherwise, early manipula-
tion of gut commensals as a remedy to possibly reverse the

2.3. Impact of microbiota on AD progression
Recently, it has been postulated that bacterial inhabitants in the
gut contribute to the development and progression of AD by possi-
bly promoting the formation of senile plaques, the accumulation of
neuroﬁbrillary tangles, and neuroinﬂammation [132]. Various
studies show the potential connection between the microbiota
and the onset and progression of AD, although the speciﬁc mecha-
nisms remain unknown. The gut microbiome is assumed to have a
crucial function in regulating inﬂammation, a process that once
was assumed to be a consequence of AD pathology but with new
evidence occurring linking it as possible contributor to the disease.
The gut and brain are closely connected through the gut-brain axis
(Fig. 2). Disturbances in bidirectional communication could con-
tribute to neuroinﬂammation and the development of CNS diseases
in which the gut might play a central role. Experiments utilizing
either germfree rodents or treated with antibiotics or probiotics,
respectively, show the potential interaction between the gastroin-
testinal system and the CNS [133]. The microbiota-gut-brain axis
includes, endocrine, immune, metabolic, and neural pathways,
which play a critical role in upkeeping brain homeostasis [134].
Active mediators of the bidirectional communication between
gut and brain are microbial products. The resident intestinal bacte-
ria can interact with the host directly or through secretion of
metabolites. These molecules, which are intermediates or end
products of microbial catabolism can originate from bacterial
metabolism of food substrates, modulations of host molecules, or
directly from bacteria.
Age-related changes in the microbiota could be connected to
frailty in the elderly population with reduced stability of the gut
microbiome and less resistance to environmental disturbances
such as stress [142]. This might impair the homeostasis of the eco-
logical system making it more susceptible to invasion by oppor-
tunistic species or adverse shifts. For example, since the ﬁrst
stages of AD increases in the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio can
be observed [143].
2.3.1. Loss of beneﬁcial microbial metabolites
Alterations in the microbiota composition might induce the
decrease of beneﬁcial microbes and microbial metabolites. For exam-
ple, 95% of plant derived compounds such as phenols, polyphenols,
and tannins (PPT) are subjected to metabolism by the microﬂora in
the colon [144]. Flavan-3-ols, one of the most abundant and bioactive
dietary polyphenol groups, have been reported to exert several
health-promoting effects by acting as, for example, antioxidants by
scavenging free radicals and chelation of transition metals, along
with the mediation and inhibition of enzymes and also show anti-
inﬂammatory properties (review: [145]). Furthermore, ﬂavonoids
are capable to cross the blood brain barrier (BBB) [146,147] and show
protective action through improvement of memory-associated neu-
ronal signaling and changes in neutral sphingomyelin-speciﬁc phos-
pholipase C activity [148]. Wang et al. demonstrated that phenolic
acids accumulated in the brain, effectively impair the self-assembly
of Ab-peptides into neurotoxic Ab-aggregates with high molecular
weight and amyloid ﬁbrils in vitro [149].
Other important metabolites are SCFAs such as butyric acid,
acetic acid, and propionic acid, which are produced primarily by
microbiota through processing of undigested ﬁbers and proteins.
They have broad effects on host immune system development
and function (summarized in [150]). SCFAs exhibit health beneﬁ-
cial properties in the gut by maintenance of intestinal barrier
integrity and mucus production [151,152].

Additionally, SCFAs can pass the BBB via monocarboxylate

by upregulating tight junction protein expression and providing
alternative substrates for brain energy metabolism (see [153]).
Besides that, SCFAs can enhance microglial function, essential for
the elimination of brain debris such as protein aggregates [154].
It was reported that a SCFA mixture as well as individual SCFAs
decreased the release of IL-1b, TNF-a, MCP-1, and cytotoxins by

2.3.2. Harmful microbial compounds
Additionally, besides decrease in beneﬁcial metabolites, alter-
ations in the relative abundance of bacterial species may increase
harmful substances such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS), bacterial
amyloids, and trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO). Under healthy
conditions, the intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) provide a crucial
barrier by the expression of intercellular tight junction proteins
to prevent the entering of invasive microorganisms and poten-
tially harmful microbial toxins into circulation, (summarized in
[159]).
Furthermore,
Toll-like
receptors
(TLR),
expressed
on
macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), and intestinal epithelial cells
monitor pathogens in the gut by recognizing damage- and
pathogen-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs and PAMPs)
and subsequently initiating the downstream signal transduction
[160]. However, damage to the intestinal mucosal barrier as a
concomitant of physiological aging or due to invasion of oppor-
tunistic pathogens, along with chronic inﬂammation allows lumi-
nal contents to escape into circulation. The pro-inﬂammatory
endotoxin LPS, a membrane component of gram-negative bacte-
ria such as Escherichia coli and Bacteroides fragilis stimulates
immune cells, resulting in the release of pro-inﬂammatory factors
into the systemic circulation, which causes an inﬂammatory
response in the gut that further increases gut and BBB permeabil-
ity [161]. Therefore, the ability to penetrate through the two
major biophysiological barriers increases, making it possible to
access brain compartments. Compellingly, increased LPS abun-
dance has been identiﬁed in neocortex and hippocampus of AD
patients’ brains [162]. There, LPS can trigger neuroinﬂammation
by activating TLRs in microglia. Hoogland et al., demonstrated
microglial activation upon stimulation with LPS in wild type
C57BL/6J mice [163]. In another study, LPS treatment in NMRI
mice caused increase in cytokines including IL-1b, IL-6, IL-10,
and TNF-a in the serum and brain and induced astrocyte activa-
tion [164]. Additionally, in vitro studies indicated that bacterial
endotoxin can elicit enhanced Ab accumulation [165]. Further-
more, LPS exposure to human primary brain cells was shown
to be an effective inducer of the pro-inﬂammatory transcription
factor NF-kB (p50/p65) complex, which is a common trigger in
pathogenic pathways involved in inﬂammation-mediated neu-
rodegeneration [166]. Taken together, these observations suggest
a critical role of LPS in inﬂuencing systemic and CNS inﬂamma-
tion related to AD.
Besides endotoxins, several intestinal microbes such as Escheri-
chia coli (E. coli) and Salmonella spp produce amyloids (summarized
in [167]) which can get access to the brain and contribute to
inﬂammatory processes. For example, E. coli extracellular amy-
loids, called curli ﬁbers, which consist of the major subunit CsgA,
are a common secretory component that serve for surface attach-
ment and adhesion, bioﬁlm forming, and defence against host
defenses (summarized in [168]).
So far, the inﬂuence of curli was not investigated in AD. How-
ever, Chen et al. showed that in aged Fischer 344 rats oral exposure
to E. coli producing curli led to enhanced production of alpha-
synuclein in the gut and simultaneously in the brain [169]. Besides
that, enhanced cerebral inﬂammation with microgliosis, astroglio-
sis, and up-regulation of TLR2, IL 6, and TNFa was observed. Inter-
estingly, like the Ab42 peptide, the extracellular 17.7 kDa CsgA
amyloid precursor consists of a PAMP that is recognized by the
human immune system TLR2 [170].
2.3.3. Routes towards the brain
Reports describe possible uptake of e.g., bacterial amyloids by
specialized epithelial cells of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue
[171,172]. Further transportation is mediated via interaction
between ﬁbers of the enteric nervous system and parasympathetic
neurons of the VN, from where they may reach the CNS through
retrograde axonal transport or transportation across the blood
brain barrier mediated by the receptor for advanced glycosylation
products (RAGE). This gives a possible explanation for the route of
amyloids from the gut to the privileged and protected brain.
One of the fastest and most direct way for the microbiota to
inﬂuence the brain is through the VN, the main component of
the parasympathetic nervous system, comprised of afferent and
efferent nerve ﬁbers. The VN is able to perceive gut information
regarding the microbiota and transmit it to the CNS, where it is
integrated to cause an adapted or improper response [173,174].
Some bacterial strains can produce neurotransmitters or modulate
neurotransmitter catabolism, which can act directly on VN end-
ings.

Overall, several potential mechanisms exist which may explain
how changes in the microbiota contribute to the progression of
dysbiosis, especially in the context of inﬂammation. For example,
a meta-analysis regarding cytokines showed signiﬁcantly higher
concentrations of IL-6, IL-1b, IL-12, IL-18, TNFa, and transforming

could explain entry of noxious substances into the circulation, con-
tributing to the progression of a leaky BBB, which allows entrance
into the brain. In the brain, they might cause neuroinﬂammation
and Ab accumulation.
3. Therapeutic approaches in AD targeting the microbiome and
their implication on inﬂammation
With no current effective therapeutic treatments to cure AD, the
microbiota and its manipulation have been acknowledge as a new,
promising approach. The currently described therapeutic strategies
targeting the microbiome include the use of antibiotics, pre-, pro-

3.1. Antibiotics
Antibiotics have been used for decades to eliminate or inhibit
bacterial colonization of the human body [179]. Consequently,
broad-spectrum antibiotics can remarkably modify the composi-
tion of the microbiome in the gut. There are reports both, describ-
ing how antibiotics positively as well as negatively affect AD. It has
e.g., been shown that long-term broad-spectrum combinatorial
antibiotic treatment of male APP/PS1 transgenic mice induces dis-
tinct alterations in gut microbial diversity with a decrease in amy-
loid plaque deposition and increased levels of soluble Ab. In the
respective study, antibiotic treatment also affected neuroinﬂam-
matory responses by leading to a reduction in plaque-associated
gliosis as well as changes in microglial morphology [180]. In a clin-
ical study, the elimination of the pathogenic strain Helicobacter
pylori by eradication in 28 patients with AD demonstrated
improvement in cognitive and functional status at the 2-year clin-
ical endpoint [181]. Beyond its anti-infectious effect, the antibiotic
rifampicin also exerted signiﬁcant neuroprotective effect by its
anti-amyloid, anti-tau, anti-inﬂammatory, and cholinergic effects
in various in vitro and in vivo studies (for a full review see [182]).
However, the observed improvement in animal models could not
be proven in clinical trials [183].
While tested antibiotics proved to diminish inﬂammation and
ameliorate cognitive deﬁcits animal models of AD, mostly contro-
versial results have been obtained from clinical trials. A multicen-
ter, blinded, randomized trial in 2013 with 406 patients with mild
to moderate AD exhibit no beneﬁcial effects on cognition or func-
tion in AD after 12 months of treatment with doxycycline and
rifampin [183].
Additionally, the reported ﬁndings are solely correlative and do
not elucidate concise mechanism on how antibiotic treatment
could have beneﬁcial effects. Whether due to loss of pro-
inﬂammatory factors deriving from the microbiome, or through
colonization of a new state community with disease-preventing
factors remains unknown. A report even provided evidence that
antibiotics–treatment might evoke a beneﬁcial effect on AD
pathology based on their anti-diabetic potential [184]. The effects
included improvement of the nesting score in antibiotics-treated
mice reﬂecting on well-being and on integrity of the hippocampus
as well as decreased plaque load in this brain area. This was
observed in parallel with a reduction of blood sugar and raise of
serum glucagon in the antibiotics-treated animals along with a
decline in the receptor for advanced glycation end products
(RAGE), the inward transporter of Ab peptides.
In conclusion, manipulation of the microbiome using antibi-
otics is applicable in animal models for investigations of this
emerging ﬁeld but not a realistic therapy approach. The method
cannot be applied on patients since recolonization after adminis-
tration is uncontrolled and therefore serious consequences can-

3.2. Fecal material transplant (FMT)
Fecal material transplant (FMT) is the administration of fecal
matter from a donor into the gut of a recipient with the aim to
directly alter the recipient’s gut microbiome. There is up so far only
limited number of respective reports showing the manipulation of
the microbiome in AD mouse models by this technique. Valeri and
colleagues investigated the impact of one-time fecal material
transplant from wild type mice of different ages into the AD mouse
model 5xFAD [185]. Since aging is one of the major risk factors for
AD pathogenesis, material derived from the caecum of young, age-
matched wild type and from middle-aged, 1 year old wild type
mice
was
transferred
into
5xFAD
mice
after
antibiotics-
treatment. The study reported that FMT allowed transfer of some
aspects of the aged microbiome such as increased cultivatable fecal
Enterobacteriaceae and Lactobacillaceae representative bacteria and
decrease in the amount of Firmicutes. Systemic consequences were
provided by an elevated level of the LPS-binding protein (LBP) in
the serum of mice treated with cecal material from old donors,
indicating increased gut permeability with leakage of LPS into
the blood stream. Moreover, a higher plaque load was observed
in the prefrontal cortex and the dentate gyrus in mice receiving
FMT from old conspeciﬁcs. The study demonstrated that single-
time FMT was enough to transfer an aged phenotype in the recip-
ient animals, showing the efﬁcacy of the method but also the need
to wisely chose appropriate donors. Another report described the
impact of FMT from AD patients into APP/PS1 double transgenic
mice [50]. The results showed alteration in intestinal expression
of NLRP3 inﬂammasome in the gut and inﬂammatory factors in
peripheral blood after FMT. Additionally, in mice receiving fecal
matter from AD patients cognitive impairment was more severe
as assessed in the Morris water maze test. This could be a conse-
quence of the observed activation of microglia in the hippocampus
and the enhanced expression level of inﬂammatory factors such as
IL-18, IL-1b or TNFa. Both studies show the efﬁcacy of FMT, since
manipulation of the microbiota of the recipient mice led to changes
directly in the gut but also peripheral system up to alterations in
the brain. At the same time, this method could be useful as innova-
tive approach to transfer fecal matter from a healthy donor into the
gut of an affected recipient for the purpose of directly changing the
recipient’s microbial composition to provide a health beneﬁt. How-
ever, it is unclear how long this will persist and not be overwritten
by nutrition, habits or genotype. A report, for example, described a
stability of the transferred fecal material in the recipient for six
weeks [186]. Positively inﬂuencing the intestinal microbiota
through FMT is not a recent concept: records show its use
2000 years ago in China for the treatment of food poisoning and
diarrhea and in western medicine, the application ﬁrst appeared
in 1958 for the treatment of pseudomembranous enterocolitis
[187]. The efﬁcacy of introducing stool from the healthy donor into
the infected patients with Clostridium difﬁcile infection is indis-
putable [188]. The microbiota of the donor stool competes with
the C. difﬁcile bacteria in the recipient intestine and therefore
restores the gut barrier defenses by stimulating the mucosal
immune system and converting primary bile acids into secondary
bile acids that inhibit the germination and growth of C. difﬁcile
spores (reviewed in [188]). The idea that FMT leads to donor-like
normalization of the microbial community which could provide a
novel perspective on abnormal systemic factors in AD was sug-
gested in few studies. For example, in the study by Shen et al.,
the effect of transferring microbiota from AD patients into APP/
PS1 mice could be restored by transplantation of healthy human
microbiota [50]. In a further study, conducted on the murine trans-
genic AD model ADPLAPT carrying six mutations within transgenes
encoding human amyloid precursor protein (APP), presenilin-1
(PSEN1), and tau daily transfer of the fecal microbiota from WT
mice for 4 months ameliorated cognitive impairment along with
the amyloid b plaques and neuroﬁbrillary tangles formation, and
glial reactivity [189]. Additionally, the FMT in the transgenic recip-
ient mice reversed abnormalities in the expression of genes related
to intestinal macrophage activity in the colon and inﬂammatory
monocytes in the blood. Taken together, these results from preclin-
ical studies suggest that FMT could be a treatment option in AD.
However, the limited number of reports with partly small group
sizes already indicates that FMT has to be investigated more exten-
sively. Open questions include, determination of the characteristics
of a healthy microbiome and therefore selection of healthy donors,
timing and duration of treatment, and ensuring the safety of the
treated patient especially regarding long-term impacts.
The major advantage of FMT in comparison to other types of
manipulative treatment, e.g., through antibiotics, prebiotics, and
probiotics, is that this methods supplies the complete spectrum
of microbiota of a healthy individual and could potentially even
treat dysbiotic conditions of the disease which are not character-
ized yet [187].
3.3. Pro- and prebiotics
The use of pro- and prebiotics might possibly be another option
for modulation of the gut microbiota aiming to prevent or amelio-
rate AD symptoms. Compared to FMT, the application of speciﬁc
strains of bacteria to receive a speciﬁc clinical effect emerged only
50 years ago [190]. The ﬁrst probiotic-like treatment was produced
in 1965 [191]. Probiotics include bacteria that are assumed to con-
fer health beneﬁts to the host while prebiotics are substances that
serve as food for these bacteria.
There are studies reporting on the effectiveness of probiotics for
acute infectious or antibiotic-associated diarrhea, Clostridium difﬁ-
cile–associated diarrhea, hepatic encephalopathy, ulcerative colitis,
irritable bowel syndrome, and functional gastrointestinal disorders
(reviewed in [192]). There are different proposed mechanisms of
probiotics virtue described: probiotic bacteria might modulate
enhanced intestinal and systemic immune responses [193 194],
or show a direct effect on pathogenic bacteria by hindering the
adhesion of those to the intestinal epithelium [195] or by suppres-
sion of growth by binding to gram-negative bacteria [196]. Mainly,
beneﬁcial results can be found in studies of oral bacteriotherapy
with Lactobacilli and Biﬁdobacteria in AD (Table 1).
So far, most studies utilized either lactic acid-producing bacteria
Lactobacilli and Biﬁdobacteria alone or in combination [6,200,201]. In
different mouse models even short-time treatment exhibited some
positive effects. In the ddY mice, a strain showing good reproductive
performance and superior growth [197], which had been adminis-
tered Ab25-35 intracerebroventricularly, eleven days of probiotic
treatment were sufﬁcient to prevent cognitive dysfunction as
demonstrated in the Y maze and passive avoidance test [197]. Fur-
thermore, transcriptional proﬁling demonstrated the excessive
immune response triggered by Ab injection could be modulated by
B. breve A1 resulting in ameliorating effect of Ab toxicity [197].
In another mouse line with the human Ab sequence knocked
into the mouse APP gene locus (App NL-G-F) no changes on memory
improvement were observed after eight weeks of treatment with a
mixture of 8 different lactic acid-producing bacteria [41]. However,
minimal changes in the brain regarding plaque deposition, cyto-
kine level amounts, or gliosis were demonstrated via immunohis-
tochemistry for Ab, GFAP, and Iba-1. Additionally, decreased
intestinal inﬂammation such as reduction in the amount of serum
eicosanoid levels of PGE2, 6-keto PGF1a, and PGF2a and attenua-
tion of gut permeability by modulating the gut bacteria using pro-
biotics were observed.
However, probiotic supplementation exerting beneﬁcial effects
was demonstrated in a triple-transgenic mouse model of AD after

A further strategy was introduced using a Lactobacillus lactis

cassette encoding the human p62 protein [199]. This ubiquitin-
binding scaffold protein colocalizes with protein aggregates that
are ubiquitinated. Degradation of the protein is carried out by
autophagy and could be able to connect ubiquitinated proteins to
the autophagic machinery [204]. 3xTg-AD mice that were fed with

the gut to the brain [199]. Mice showed improved memory function

regarding inﬂammation and oxidation. While the beneﬁcial impact
of probiotics has been demonstrated in some animal models,
human studies are even more controversial. Using the same bacte-

amount of gut bacteria associated with enhanced inﬂammation.
Furthermore, improvement in tests regarding mental ﬂexibility
and stress, and elevated levels of serum BDNF were shown in
healthy elders over 65 years [200]. On the other hand, the same
treatment period led to no cognitive and biochemical changes in
AD patients [201]. The discrepancies could be related to the chosen
targets since a healthy group is compared to AD patients. However,
changes were reported in AD patients treated with multispecies

fecal zonulin, increase in anti-inﬂammatory Faecalibacterium praus-

kynurenine but cognitive improvement was not investigated [202].

long-term treatment of AD and oral drug intake is clearly a
preferred method due to patient acceptance and seldom side

3.4. Others (nutrition, light)
Additional possibilities for bacteria-targeted interventions in
the intestine to address inﬂammatory processes which may pro-
vide a therapeutic approach in AD are conceivable.
One option is provided by adjusting the intake of nutrition to
induce a shift in the microbial composition. For example, a wheat
amylase trypsin inhibitors (ATI)-depleted diet was suggested in a
study. Metabolic changes such as increases in oxygen consumption
in 5xFAD consuming ATIs were assessed compared to the group fed
with low ATI amount [136]. Furthermore, the crop component ATI,
which was provided with a normal gluten-containing diet, resulted
in alteration in intestinal architecture and reduced cognitive abili-
ties in fear conditioning test and nest building test in the AD model
mice. The authors thus concluded that an ATI-depleted diet might
serve as an alternative therapeutic approach.
Another study investigated the impact of probiotics fermenta-
tion technology (PFT), a keﬁr grain product, in adult albino mice
[207]. PFT keﬁr grain composed of Lactobacillus keﬁri demonstrated
enhancement of insulin signaling, shown by the upregulated
expression of insulin receptors and activation of PI3K/Akt signal-
ing. Moreover, increased hippocampal acetylcholine levels, and
reduced Ab1–42 levels were evidenced effecting the improvement
in cognitive function.
Positive effects were also observed in APP/PS1 mice treated
with Jatrorrhizine (JAT), a primary component of a traditional Chi-
nese herbal. JAT improved hippocampal contextual memory,
decreased the Ab plaques load in the cortex and hippocampus
and changed the amount of certain gut microbiota such as the
most predominant phyla Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Lactobacil-
lus levels as along with the F/B ratio [208].
Additionally, photobiomodulation (PBM) therapy was sug-
gested as a treatment approach [209]. Normally, PBM uses red or
near-infrared light to arouse healing, or relieving pain and inﬂam-
mation, and induce nerve repair, utilized in diverse range of brain
disorders [210]. In an Aß-induced AD mouse model, PBM at three
different wavelengths (especially 630 nm and 730 nm) improved
cognitive capabilities in the Morris water maze as well as a reduc-
tion in amyloidosis and tau phosphorylation in the hippocampus.
In addition, the 16S rRNA sequencing of fecal contents showed
alteration in the diversity and abundance of the microbiota with
a reduction in Helicobacter, Bacteroidales, and Rikenella amounts.
In summary, several approaches are described that might be use-
ful for therapy of AD. However, more studies are needed on these
suggestions since these are all one-time studies with the urgent
need for investigations in humans besides the described animal
models.
4. Conclusion
In recent years, the role of neuroinﬂammation and the micro-
biome in AD research came into focus. Microbial changes are
described in the gut ecosystem in AD. However, a high number
of uncertainties regarding both, direction of change but also result-
ing consequences for the host impede progress in this area of
research. The contribution of peripheral inﬂammation and inﬂam-
matory processes in the gut of diseased patients besides hyperre-
active microglia and astrocytes in the brain are still to be
debated. The question whether the changes in microbiota are
underlying causes or occur as a secondary effect due to pathologi-
cal changes of the host remains open. Nevertheless, it is highly
plausible that loss of beneﬁcial microbial metabolites and the
increase in harmful microbial compounds could explain entry of
noxious substances into the circulation, or approach the vagal con-
nections towards the brain contributing to the increased perme-
ability of the BBB, which enables their entry into the brain. In the
brain they might induce neuroinﬂammation and Ab accumulation.
Therefore, modulation of the microbiota to combat inﬂammatory
processes for example through FMT or pre- or probiotics seems
to be an innovative therapeutic approach. Additional research on
the therapeutic possibilities is needed to translate ﬁrst ﬁndings
from animal models into clinical practice.

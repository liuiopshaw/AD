A B S T R A C T   
An increasing number of epidemiological studies have shown that there is a significant inverse relationship 
between the onset of Alzheimer’s disease/Parkinson’s disease (AD/PD) and cancer, but the mechanism is still 
unclear. Considering that intestinal flora can connect them, we tried to explain this phenomenon from the in­
testinal flora. This review briefly introduced the relationship among AD/PD, cancer, and intestinal flora, studied 
metabolites or components of the intestinal flora and the role of intestinal barriers and intestinal hormones in 
AD/PD and cancer. After screening, a part of the flora capable of participating in the occurrence processes of the 
three diseases at the same time was obtained, the abundance changes of the special flora in AD/PD and various 
types of cancers were summarized, and they were classified according to the flora function and abundance, 
which in turn innovatively and reasonably explained the fact that AD/PD and cancer showed certain antagonism 
in epidemiological statistics from the perspective of intestinal flora. This review also proposed that viewing the 
risk relationship between diseases from the perspective of intestinal flora may provide new research ideas for the 
treatment of fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) and related diseases.   
1. Introduction 
The brain is the highest part of the nervous system and the material 
basis of higher nervous activity such as consciousness, spirit, language, 
learning, memory, and intelligence. However, with the increase in the 
average age of humans, the incidence of various brain-related nervous 
system diseases has increased significantly, and AD and PD are the most 
common. AD is a neurodegenerative disease (ND) that occurs in the 
elderly and presenile, with the characteristic pathological changes of

also a standard ND of the nervous system, clinically defined as a 
movement disorder characterized by movement symptoms related to 
dopaminergic degeneration of the substantia nigra [2]. The exact eti­
ology is still unclear. Genetic factors, environmental factors, aging, and 
oxidative stress may be involved in the degeneration and death of PD 
dopaminergic neurons. Similar to AD, PD has no complete cure but

Cancer generally refers to all malignant tumors and has the biolog­
ical characteristics of abnormal cell differentiation and proliferation, 
uncontrolled growth, infiltration, and metastasis. Its occurrence is a 
multifactor, multistep, and complex process divided into three pro­
cesses: carcinogenesis, cancer promotion, and evolution. At present, the 
standard clinical treatments for cancer include surgery, chemotherapy, 
radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. 
Intestinal microorganisms, also known as intestinal flora, are located 
in the intestinal tract of animals in a large number and variety, and 
accompany individuals throughout their lives. An increasing number of 
studies have shown that the steady-state of intestinal flora is closely 
related to individual health and is also associated with cognitive 
impairment, AD, PD, various intestinal cancers, glandular cancers, and 
distal cancers. The in-depth study of intestinal flora has provided new 
ideas for the diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of diseases and pro­
vides theoretical support for FMT. 
Epidemiological studies show that there is a significant antagonistic 
relationship between AD/PD and cancer. The probability of suffering 
from cancer after suffering from AD/PD will decrease, and vice versa. 
Currently, there is no authoritative explanation for this phenomenon. 
This review linked intestinal flora with AD/PD and cancer and explained 
this phenomenon. Common neurodegenerative diseases also include 
Huntington’s disease (HD) and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), but 
the former is mainly related to genetic factors, and the onset age is often 
earlier than cancer, whereas the latter has no risk relationship with 
cancer [3]. Therefore, we did not discuss them in this review. 
2. Interconnection among AD/PD, cancer, and Intestinal flora 
2.1. AD/PD and Intestinal flora 
AD and PD can significantly affect patients’ quality of life, and due to 
their irreversibility, they have always been the targets of researchers. 
The role of intestinal flora in AD/PD has provided us with new ideas for 
treating two diseases. Some researchers used a newly developed trans­
genic AD mouse model and found that there was no significant differ­
ence in the composition of intestinal flora between them before the onset 
of disease in the early stage and WT mice, but this difference appeared 
when the mice started to suffer from disease at six months, suggesting 
that it was possible that the pathological conditions of AD caused 
changes in intestinal flora [4]. This indicates that the change in brain 
state will also affect the composition of the intestinal flora. Researchers 
transplanted the feces of normal mice into AD mice simultaneously, and 
behavioral analysis showed that the short-term memory ability and 
cognitive level of AD mice were improved. They also suggested that this 
change might be related to FMT reversing the decrease in Bacteroidetes 
and Alloprevotella and the increase in Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, 
Akkermansia, and Desulfovibrio [5]. Studies have shown that Mucispir­
illum and Ruminiclostridium are enriched in AD model mice, and 
increasing the relative abundance of Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium 
may improve cognitive levels [6]. At the same time, studies have shown 
that different diets will change the intestinal flora and then impact the 
incidence of PD [7]. At the same time, compared with healthy people, 
there are significant differences in the composition of intestinal flora in 
PD patients, showing an increase in Akkermansia, Bifidobacterium, and 
Lactobacillus and a decrease in Prevotella [8–11]. These studies show that 
there is a close relationship between AD/PD and intestinal flora. At the 
same time, some studies or clinical trials use FMT to treat or improve 
AD/PD, which also shows the close relationship between AD/PD and 
intestinal flora. 
2.2. Cancer and Intestinal flora 
Many studies have shown that the composition of intestinal micro­
organisms is closely related to the prevention or treatment of cancer. For 
example, the intestinal flora plays a role in the therapeutic response of 
patients with melanoma to programmed cell death protein 1(PD-1), and 
its diversity is positively correlated with the therapeutic response. In 
terms of flora composition, patients with more Clostridium bacteria were 
more likely to respond to PD-1 treatment, whereas patients with more 
Bacteroides bacteria were less likely to respond to PD-1 therapy. After the 
feces of patients are transplanted, the tumor mice in the response group 
have a higher immune level, and the tumor volume is significantly 
reduced compared with that in the control group [12]. At the same time, 
intestinal microbial dysregulation excessively stimulates CD8+ T cells, 
and promotes chronic inflammation and early T cell failure, leading to 
susceptibility to colonic tumors and, consequently, reducing antitumor 
immunity [13]. Some substances, such as fucoidan, can restore intestinal 
wall damage in mice and prevent the occurrence of breast cancer by 
increasing the diversity of the intestinal flora composition and 
increasing the Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes phylum ratio [14]. Vitamin D 
supplementation could regulate the steady state of intestinal flora in 
colorectal cancer (CRC) mice and regulate the integrity of the Akker­
mansia muciniphila-mediated intestinal barrier, thereby playing a role in 
the reversal of CRC [15]. Sulforaphane can normalize the intestinal flora 
imbalance of bladder cancer mice with a significant increase in Bacter­
oides fragilis and Clostridium cluster I [16]. The pathways of microor­
ganisms affecting cancer can be summarized into three points: (1) 
changing the balance between the proliferation and death of host cells; 
(2) directing immune system function; and (3) affecting the metabolism 
of the host. There is no doubt that cancer and intestinal flora also have a 
close relationship [17]. 
2.3. AD/PD and cancer 
An increasing number of epidemiological studies have shown a 
specific correlation between AD/PD and the onset of cancer. The ADNI 
cohort demonstrated a significant negative correlation between multiple 
cancers and subsequent AD development [18]. Transcriptional 
meta-analysis revealed many genes with opposite expression patterns in 
AD and lung cancer, and pointed out that mitochondrial metabolism in 
AD and lung cancer was reversely regulated, suggesting that they might 
be involved in the reverse coinfection of the two diseases [19]. Men­

demonstrated 
the 
protective 
effects 
of 
cancer-related gene variants on AD. At the same time, single types of 
cancer, including lung cancer, breast cancer, and leukemia, were sta­
tistically associated with a low risk of AD, and signaling pathways 
related to tumorigenesis, including mTOR, MAPK, insulin resistance, 
autophagy, inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic pathways, 
were also shown to be associated with neurodegenerative diseases [20]. 
An increase in PIN1 expression is related to a delay in the onset age of 
sporadic AD, whereas a decrease in PIN1 expression is associated with a 
reduced risk of various cancers [21]. Moreover, studies have shown that 
individuals with low expression of PIN1 in cancer have a higher prob­
ability of secondary diagnosis of AD than individuals with high 
expression of PIN1. Ten types of cancer were included in this study, of 
which prostate, ovarian, and lung cancers showed the greatest negative 
correlation with AD [22]. A study of millions of participants showed that 
people with AD had a 50% reduced risk of cancer and that people with 
cancer had a 35% reduced risk of AD [23]. Studies have shown that 
patients diagnosed with PD have a reduced risk of colorectal cancer 
(CRC) [24]. Knocking down the PD-related gene ATP-13-A2 can reduce 
the risk of colon cancer tumors by regulating autophagy [25]. PD and 
cancer share a pathway related to mitochondrial protein mutations [26]. 
Skin cancer and general cancer may have a protective effect on PD [27]. 
Other studies have shown that the overall cancer risk of PD patients is 
reduced, but the risk of individual types of cancer (melanoma and breast 
cancer) is increased [28,29]. In fact, compared with AD, the relationship 
between PD and cancer has changed from the negative correlation 
generally believed in the early years to a positive correlation between 
PD and certain types of cancer, as believed by many researchers.

3. Effects of metabolites or components of intestinal flora on 
AD/PD or cancer 
3.1. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) 
SCFAs are essential metabolites of intestinal microorganisms and 
include, acetate, propionate, and butyrate [30]. As signaling molecules, 
these metabolites can affect a series of host activities and play critical 
roles in host metabolism, immune system [31], and cell proliferation. 
SCFAs can directly affect the sympathetic system [32], thereby 
increasing the release of mucosal serotonin and affecting learning and 
memory functions [33,34], and maintaining the integrity of the intes­
tinal barrier to reduce the risk of colon cancer [35,36]. The onset age of 
PD is directly proportional to the SCFA level, suggesting that SCFAs may 
have a protective effect on PD [37]. As one of the SCFAs, butyrate can 
reduce AD by affecting the p-glycoprotein pathway [38]. At the same 
time, butyrate has been confirmed to have a robust anticancer ability in 
various cancer cell cultures and mouse models [39]. It can effectively 
promote cancer cell apoptosis and reduce cell proliferation capacity and 
activity through different signaling pathways [40–42]. In general, 
SCFAs can be viewed as an important "life-sustaining substance" that 
helps maintain the normal functioning of tissues and organs to ensure 
overall health. 
3.2. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) 
LPS, one of the main components of the cell wall of gram-negative 
bacteria, is involved in the pathogenesis of AD. Some experiments 
have proven that LPS can lead to the enhancement of neuro­
inflammation and the accumulation of neuronal Aβ [43] and can dam­
age the blood-brain barrier so that it can enter the brain [44], hinder the 
outflow of Aβ from cells [45], and induce hyperphosphorylation of tau 
protein [46]. At the same time, LPS, as an endogenous proinflammatory 
endotoxin, is often used to model the characterization of PD and plays a 
significant role in the pathogenesis of PD [47,48]. In the development of 
cervical cancer, LPS can downregulate the expression of Fra-1 [49], thus 
reducing the inhibitory effect of Fra-1 on the proliferation of cervical 
cancer cells [50]. LPS may lead to the deterioration of cancer by pro­
moting tumor cell metastasis and tumor angiogenesis [51], which is 
closely related to LPS activating the TLR4 signal [52]. In summary, the 
proinflammatory toxicity of LPS has adverse effects n AD/PD and 
cancer. 
3.3. Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) 
H2S is produced from cysteine by intestinal epithelial cells and in­
testinal flora (main source). It was generally considered a toxic gas in the 
past, but with the development of research, researchers have discovered 
its multiple functions [53]. First, H2S is an important signaling molecule 
in organisms that can improve the progression of AD by enhancing Nrf2 
[54], reduce the production of Aβ by inhibiting β secretase 1 (BACE1) 
[55], and protect neurons by maintaining mitochondrial function [56]. 
In addition to the positive effect on AD, some research groups have 
proven that H2S also has a neuroprotective effect on the PD model [57], 
and the principle is to maintain mitochondrial function [58]. However, 
in the same way, H2S at a safe and nontoxic concentration can protect 
not only healthy cells, such as neurons, but also cancerous cells from 
apoptosis [56]. In a variety of cancers, including ovarian cancer, oral 
cancer, thyroid cancer, colon cancer, breast cancer, etc., the upregula­
tion of H2S-producing enzyme CBS has been detected [59–62], which 
can enhance the proliferation ability of cancer cells and make cancer 
cells more sensitive to it [56,63]. In summary, the protective role of H2S 
is "friend or foe", which helps protect neurons and improve the condition 
for AD/PD, but can worsen the cancer condition. 
3.4. Tryptophan (TRP) metabolites 
TRP is an essential aromatic amino acid that is a common ingredient 
in foods eaten with protein. It can be utilized by the intestinal flora in the 
intestinal tract and metabolized into ligand molecules of aryl hydro­
carbon receptor (AHR), including indole, indole propionic acid, indole 
acetic acid, skatole, and tryptamine [64–66]. AHR, a cytoplasmic 
ligand-activated transcription factor, can be activated by tryptophan 
metabolites as ligands, thus participating in the effects on many dis­
eases, including inflammation, oxidative stress injury, cancer, and 
aging-related diseases [67]. In nervous system-related disorders, 
including AD and PD, tryptophan metabolites can cross the blood-brain 
barrier and activate AHR to regulate astrocytes and reduce central 
nervous system inflammation [68]. AHR is also present in the intestine 
and has been shown to be involved in the regulation of homeostasis of 
the intestinal environment and the integrity of the intestinal mucosal 
barrier [69,70]. However, in studies related to multiple cancers, AHR 
and its ligand activators have shown procancer activity, and ligand 
antagonists of AHR have the potential to become anticancer drugs 
[71–75]. This indicates that AD/PD and cancer were affected in the 
opposite way during the activation of AHR by tryptophan metabolites. 
3.5. Others 
Other common metabolites of intestinal flora include secondary bile 
acid and trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO). Deoxycholic acid (DOCA) is 
a secondary bile acid produced by the metabolism of primary bile acids 
by the intestinal flora and has been shown to cause inflammatory re­
actions[76]. Increased concentrations of secondary bile acids, such as 
deoxycholic acid and lithocholic acid, may be related to the progression 
of AD, PD, and cancer [77–79]. Studies have shown that serum TMAO 
levels in AD/CRC patients are higher than those in healthy people, and 
its concentrations may be positively correlated with AD/cancer pro­
gression [80–83]. However, PD patients with low levels of TMAO are 
more likely to convert to dementia [84]. The effect of TMAO on disease 
is complex. 
4. Role of the intestinal tract in AD/PD or cancer 
4.1. Intestinal barrier 
The intestinal barrier is one of the essential barriers of the human 
body, and is closely related to the study of intestinal microorganisms and 
the steady-state balance of organisms. The interaction between intesti­
nal microorganisms and the human body is achieved to a large extent by 
affecting intestinal permeability [85]. Studies have shown that changes 
in intestinal permeability are closely related to various diseases, 
including inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, and metabolic diseases 
[86,87]. We reviewed the views of many researchers, who generally 
believe that the integrity of the intestinal barrier is closely related to 
neuroinflammation, and that an increase in intestinal permeability may 
increase the risk of AD [88,89]. Moreover, the integrity of the intestinal 
barrier protects against PD [90,91]. For cancer, increased intestinal 
permeability leads to the translocation of bacteria and bacterial toxins, 
such as LPS, and the resulting inflammatory response accelerates cancer 
[92,93]. In several experimental studies exploring the relationship be­
tween intestinal flora and AD/PD and cancer, researchers often regard 
the change in the intestinal barrier as a critical evaluation index [4,14, 
94–96]. Therefore, the integrity of the intestinal wall is fundamental to 
the resistance of AD/PD and cancer.

4.2. Intestinal hormone 
4.2.1. Cholecystokinin 
Cholecystokinin (CCK) is an intestinal peptide hormone that is also 
abundant in the peripheral and nervous systems [97]. Studies have 
shown that CCK signals are regulated by intestinal microorganisms, 
which have certain anti-inflammatory and immune regulatory effects 
[98]. CCK has been shown to be associated with the development of a 
variety of cancers, including gastrointestinal cancer [99]. However, in 
the nervous system, CKK plays the opposite role. Studies have shown 
that it is an integral part of maintaining memory and cognition, and the 
high expression of CCK is related to the low probability of AD [100]. In 
addition, the CCK levels of PD mice and AD patients are also down­
regulated, which indicates the potential role of CCK in neuroprotection 
[101,102]. This means that the levels of CCK in AD/PD or cancer in­
dividuals may antagonize each other’s pathogenesis. Unfortunately, 
there are no studies that can link changes in CCK to a particular strain. 
4.2.2. Glucagon-like peptide 
Glucagon-like peptide (GLP-1), an incretin hormone, is present in the 
nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS) and the olfactory bulb [103]. The 
reduction in the phyla Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes in the intestine can 
stimulate the secretion of GLP-1. Probiotics, such as Bifidobacterium, can 
increase GLP-1 levels in the intestinal tract and plasma of rodents [104]. 
Researchers have summarized that GLP-1 can improve almost all the 
pathologies of AD mice and protect dopaminergic neurons in a PD model 
[105,106]. GLP-1 receptor agonists can induce apoptosis of endometrial 
carcinoma [107]. Generally, GLP-1 is more beneficial to individuals. 
4.2.3. Corticotropin-releasing factor 
Corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF) is a 41-amino-acid-residue 
peptide that is abundantly expressed in neurons in the paraventricular 
nucleus of hypothalamus (PVN) region of the hypothalamus [108]. 
Lactobacillus can inhibit the expression of CRF in the hypothalamus 
[109], and Clostridium butyricum can block the increase in CRF levels in 
patients before surgery [110], all of which indicated that CRF was 
closely related to intestinal flora. Studies have shown that elevated CRF 
levels induce phosphorylation of hippocampal Tau protein and that its 
receptor antagonists are beneficial for AD mice [111,112]. At the same 
time, CRF can promote the growth and metastasis of the MCF7 breast 
cancer cell line [113], and protect cancer cells from destruction by NK 
cells [114]. 
4.2.4. Ghrelin 
Ghrelin is a polypeptide hormone derived from the stomach but is 
also expressed in other peripheral organs [115]. Serum ghrelin levels are 
negatively correlated with the commensal Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus 
and Streptococcus [116,117]. Levels of Firmicutes and ghrelin showed a 
negative correlation in both humans and rodents [118,119]. Some re­
searchers have summarized the therapeutic significance of ghrelin in 
AD/PD and believe that ghrelin can improve the pathology of AD/PD 
[120–122]. In contrast, ghrelin is expressed in a variety of cancers and

5. Intestinal flora related to AD/PD and cancer and its action 
mechanism 
Generally, different bacteria participate in various diseases, but some 
bacteria can play a role in many diseases. This review describes some 
flora that can affect AD/PD and cancer at the same time. 
Bifidobacterium has been shown to reduce the levels of proin­
flammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-1β) and increase the 
production of the protective cytokines TGF-β and IL-10 in vitro and in 
vivo [125], thus improving intestinal permeability [126]. Bifidobacte­
rium may induce T cells through dendritic cell (DC) [127], antigen 
presentation, and antigen-specific T cell stimulation, thereby exerting an 
inhibitory effect on cancer. Multiple species of Bifidobacterium have been 
shown to improve AD [128,129], but only in vitro evidence of a positive 
impact of Bifidobacterium on PD [130]. 
Lactobacillus has bactericidal and antibacterial abilities and can 
inhibit enterotoxin bacteria, thereby reducing their secretion of IL-6, IL- 
8, IL-1β, TNF-α, and TNF -β caused by them. The factors have neurotoxic 
effects [131,132], and their downregulation can further reduce in­
flammatory reactions [133]; they can also effectively inhibit Heli­
cobacter pylori from preventing the occurrence of gastric cancer [134]. 
Lactobacillus can effectively inhibit the development of cervical cancer 
by regulating HPV oncogenes and activating immunity [135] and can 
also inhibit the proliferation of the colon cancer cell line HT-29. (1) 
Lactobacillus can activate and enhance the antitumor effect of immune 
cells, such as thymus-derived cells (T cells) and immune factors pro­
duced by natural killer (NK) cells, DCs, macrophages, and immune cells; 
(2) a large number of unmethylated dinucleotide repeats in the Lacto­
bacillus nucleus can activate the innate immune response by binding to 
specific receptors on human cell surfaces; and (3) Lactobacillus, which is 
suitable for reproduction and growth in an anaerobic environment, can 
selectively accumulate in the hypoxic region of solid cancer [136]. At 
the same time, many species of Lactobacillus have been proven to 
improve AD [129], and only in vitro evidence shows that Lactobacillus 
has a positive effect on PD [130]. 
Ruminococcaceae induces immune tolerance through Treg cells, thus 
promoting the development of cancer [137], and its abundance is 
related to the level of acetylated H3 in CD4+T cells, whereas the level of 
acetylated H3 is related to the Treg/Th17 balance [138]. It is able to 
produce secondary bile acids that affect the mitotic process, leading to 
DNA damage and ROS production, thus increasing the risk of colon 
cancer [139,140]. However, it is inversely proportional to LPS levels 
[141] and helps maintain the integrity of the intestinal barrier [142], 
which reduces the risk of AD/PD. At the same time, a high abundance of 
Ruminococcaceae may improve the state of AD patients [143]. 
Blautia products butyrate and propionate inhibit histone deacetylase 
in host tumor cells and have a universal anticancer effect [144]. Blautia 
may be related to the pathological aggravation of AD, namely, lower 
levels of Aβ 42/Aβ 40 and higher levels of P-tau and P-tau/Aβ 42 in the 
cerebrospinal fluid [145]. 
Prevotellaceae is related to an increase in SCFA, thiamine, and folic 
acid synthesis and a decrease in mucin synthesis, which leads to the 
expansion of intestinal permeability. Secreted hydrogen sulfide protects 
dopaminergic neurons [146], but it may accelerate cancer progression 
[56]. 
Prevotella can induce the production of Th17 cells in the mouse colon 
[147], whereas the Th17 cytokines IL-17A, IL-17F, and IL-22 induce the 
intestinal epithelium to produce tight junction proteins and antibacterial 
peptides, thus supporting the integrity of the intestinal barrier [148]. 
However, many of Prevotella’s subordinates accelerate the progression of 
cancer. For example, Prevotella intermedia may mediate P53 gene mu­
tation through peptidyl arginine deaminase (PAD) enzymes [149], and it 
is associated with an increased risk of CRC [150]. Prevotella buccalis 
infection was significantly associated with an increased risk of cervical 
cancer [151]. 
According to the existing evidence, Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus 
positively affect AD/PD and cancer. Ruminococcaceae, Prevotellaceae, 
and Prevotella significantly improve on AD/PD but harm cancer. Blautia 
has universal anticancer ability, but it may aggravate the pathology of 
AD (Fig. 1).

we should analyze its specific influence on conditions in combination 
with its relative abundance. Tables 1 and 2 summarize many of the

7. FMT 
FMT has achieved better therapeutic effects than conventional 
treatments in treating certain diseases (such as C. difficile sensitization 
and enteritis) [167,168], but this is the greatest achievement of FMT. 
Although it has applications in the treatment of other diseases, such as 
Huang et al. [169], who used FMT to treat a PD patient, and the leg 
tremor almost disappeared after one week, but it recurred after two 
months. This suggests that FMT has a certain effect in the treatment of 
PD, but there are limitations in efficacy and persistence. Xue et al. [170] 
demonstrated through clinical experiments that colonic FMT had a 
certain relief effect for PD patients, but it still had negative effects, such 
as diarrhea, abdominal pain, and flatulence. The treatment of AD with 
FMT is still in the experimental animal stage, whereas cancer treatment 
is mainly reflected in its auxiliary effect on immunotherapy [171,172]. 
Refer to Table 3 for specific content. 
In summary, although research on intestinal flora is not limited to 
intestinal diseases, extended FMT remains in treating intestinal disor­
ders, and its application in other conditions is sporadic. In addition, FMT 
is still accompanied by problems such as limited efficacy, easy recur­
rence, and safety risk related to the unclear mechanism and effect of 
intestinal flora and the instability of fecal donors. We can say that the 
application of FMT is still in the initial stage of "to know-how, but do not 
know why.". 
To solve the disadvantages of FMT, researchers have made great 
efforts. Some people propose that carcinogenic bacteria will spread after 
FMT, so FMT donors can be screened or replaced by engineered human 
strains to improve the safety of FMT [176]. However, as engineered 
human strains have not shown better effects, they cannot wholly replace 
FMT [177]. The impact of FMT depends on the composition and di­
versity of the fecal flora of the donor [178]. Therefore, some people 
proposed the concept of superdonors, that is, to conduct clinical 
screening of donors and select donors with better intestinal flora 
composition (less pathogenic bacteria and more beneficial bacteria) as 
FMT donors. Antibiotic pretreatment is also one of the schemes to 
improve the clinical effects of FMT, based on the principle of controlling 
the harmful bacteria in donor feces with antibiotics, thereby improving 
the implantation efficiency of beneficial microorganisms [179]. 
In this review, based on the antagonism between AD/PD and cancer 
risk, we found that they are complementary in the composition of in­
testinal flora. On this basis, if flora sequencing technology is combined 
with antibiotic treatment, it may provide a new research idea for 
improving the safety and effectiveness of FMT. 
8. Discussion 
The statistical risk relationship between AD/PD and cancer has al­
ways been a problem bothering researchers. Relevant studies are not 
uncommon, but most have limitations and lack broad persuasion for 
diseases with complex mechanisms such as AD/PD and cancer. The in­
testinal flora is complex and diverse. This review mainly introduces six 
representative species (Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Ruminococcaceae, 
Blautia, Prevotellaceae, Prevotella), whose metabolites and components 
are multifarious. This review presents SCFAs, LPS, H2S, and tryptophan 
together with secondary bile acids and TMAO. These bacteria and me­
tabolites have either good or bad effects on the disease, including in­
testinal permeability, intestinal hormones, immunity, genetic materials, 
inflammation, oxidative stress, neuroprotection, and metabolism. 
Therefore, the effect of intestinal flora on the condition is extensive and 
profound. In this review, the effect of intestinal flora on conditions is 
innovatively taken as a starting point, and the abundance difference of 
some functional intestinal flora in AD/PD and cancer is combined to 
reasonably explain the antagonistic relationship between AD/PD and 
cancer. 
On this basis, we believe that the changes in the composition of the 
intestinal flora, as a potential cause of a large number of common dis­
eases, can be linked to the risk relationship of different conditions. An in- 
depth study of the mechanisms and effects of intestinal flora in the 
occurrence of diseases may explain the risk relationship between 
seemingly unrelated ailments. 
FMT has achieved better therapeutic effects than conventional 
treatments in treating certain diseases (such as C. difficile sensitization 
and enteritis) [167,168]. However, its application is still greatly limited 
due to the unclear mechanism, limited efficacy, and safety [118]. In this 
review, we explained the disease risk relationship as the breakthrough 
point and found unexpected complementarity between AD/PD and 
cancer flora. If the mechanism is explored in-depth, it may provide a 
potential research idea for developing FMT, AD/PD, and cancer 
treatment.

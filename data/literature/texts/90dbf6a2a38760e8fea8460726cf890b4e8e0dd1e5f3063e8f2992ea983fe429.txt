Alzheimer’s disease is a gradually advancing neurodegenerative disease. According to the report 
by ``World Health Organization (WHO)'', there are over 55 million individuals currently living 
with Alzheimer’s disease and other dementia globally, and the number of suﬀerers is increasing 
every day. In absence of eﬀective cures and preventive measures, this number is predicted to triple 
by 2050. The disease’s origin is still unclear, and also no such treatment is available for eradicating 
the disease. Based on the crucial factors that are connected to the disease’s progression, the 
authors developed several types of mathematical models. We review such mathematical models 
that are utilized to better understand the pathophysiology of Alzheimer’s disease. Section-wise, we 
categorize the mathematical models in terms of diﬀerent components that might be responsible for 
Alzheimer’s disease. We explain the mathematical models with their descriptions and respective 
conclusions. In addition to mathematical models, we concentrate on biological aspects of the 
disease and possible therapeutic targets. We explore the disease’s biological basis primarily to 
understand how proteins, glial cells, cytokines, genes, calcium signaling and oxidative stress 
contribute to the disease. We go through several treatment targets that might stop the progression 
of the disease or at least slow it down. We present a table that summarizes the mathematical 
models in terms of their formalisms, highlighting key components and important remarks.
1. Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a complex neurodegenerative illness that poses a signiﬁcant global health challenge in the 21st century. 
Millions of individuals worldwide, including large populations in countries like the United States, India and China, are aﬀected by 
AD. The U.S. population could see a surge in the number of individuals aged 65 and older with AD, potentially reaching 13.8 
million by the year 2060 [1,4]. As a result, AD has become an important area of research and is capturing the interest of scientists 
and healthcare professionals. The disease was first ident­fied by Dr. Alois Alzheimer in the year 1906, and according to his name, 
the disease became known as Alzheimer’s disease. In this neurodegenerative disease, mainly the elements of the central nervous 
system stop working, leading to progressive memory loss, cognitive decline and impaired reasoning. AD symptoms include confusion, 
diﬃculty with language, impaired reasoning, mood swings and disorientation. In AD, neurons suﬀer progressive damage, leading to 
impaired synaptic communication and eventual cell death. This disruption aﬀects cognitive function and memory.
Despite years of research, the exact causes of the progression of AD remain unknown. However, scientists have made signiﬁcant 
progress in understanding the complex interaction of genetic, environmental and lifestyle factors that contribute to the development

Mathematical models can be used to investigate the disease’s progression and understand its underlying causes. Mathematical

Started with discovering the biomarker components in the twentieth century, AD research has come far away. Besides A𝛽plaque 
and NFTs, many other components in the brain, along with factors like age, lifestyle, environment and genetics, have been incorporated 
into the responsible factors. This review aims to present an analysis of the current state of AD research insights from both mathematical 
modeling and crucial i­fluencing factors. By reviewing the existing research papers, publications and biological review articles, we 
explore the nature of AD, its molecular pathology, risk factors, potential biomarkers and emerging therapeutic interventions in this 
paper. For this intention, we analyze each paper that is referenced in this article, including the model descriptions and its conclusions.
With the increasing global prevalence of AD, a comprehensive literature review on this becomes crucial. There is still a shortage 
of useful treatments and cures. This review serves as an idea to develop new approaches in theoretical or mathematical frameworks 
and provide a new research direction. It conducts a comprehensive and critical study of the current status of research findings and 
assists in identifying gaps or inconsistencies in research. This thorough review not only gives the biological or mathematical overview 
of the disease but also oﬀers an idea of novel approaches which can be used to diagnose or treatment of AD.

biological background of AD, that is, we investigate how proteins, glial cells, cytokines, genes, calcium signaling and oxidative stress 
contribute to the disease. Various mathematical models that incorporate essential factors such as A𝛽proteins, 𝜏proteins, glial cells, 
etc., have been illustrated segment-wise in Section 3. In Section 4, we discuss the articles that help us to understand the therapeutic 
interventions for eradicating AD. In the end, we wrap up our discussion in Section 5.
2. Contributory biological factors of AD
There are many biological components that dedicate to AD. To understand this complex disease or to form a biologically relevant 
mathematical model for AD, we have to know about the crucial biological factors related to AD. Some of such biological anticipated 
factors have been r­flected in Fig. 1. Here, we discuss about the contributions of proteins, glial cells, cytokines, genes, calcium 
signaling and oxidative stress in the disease.
Proteins: Proteins have a crucial role in the advancement and evolution of AD. There are two main proteins that are associated 
with AD, namely A𝛽protein and 𝜏protein. A𝛽protein accumulation in brain tissue has become a well-studied phenomenon in the 
progression of AD. In a viewpoint of the article [175], the authors assessed research investigating the mechanisms responsible for 
the buildup of A𝛽peptides and the formation of A𝛽oligomers. A bigger protein, named amyloid precursor protein (APP), is broken 
down in presence of secretase into smaller fragments called A𝛽protein.
A𝛽plaques are basically the extracellular deposits of A𝛽peptides. It is believed that these A𝛽plaques has a contribution in the 
disturbance of communication between neurons, which results in neuronal malfunction and ultimately, cell death [135]. Although 
the exact processes by which A𝛽protein damages neurons are not fully understood. It is thought that the buildup of these plaques 
may create oxidative stress and i­flammatory reactions, both of which can be harmful to neurons.
A𝛽oligomers are small clusters of A𝛽molecules that are thought to be toxic to brain cells and interfere with their normal 
functioning. They can disrupt synaptic communication, promote i­flammation and cause neuronal damage. Studies say that both 
A𝛽oligomers and plaques are pathogenic and contribute to AD development [162]. Some research also suggests that soluble A𝛽
oligomers, rather than plaques, are more directly linked to the neurotoxic eﬀects and cognitive deficits seen in AD [104].
The stability of oligomers and their role in the context of fibril formation is crucial for understanding the disease. Oligomers 
are typically stable and do not necessarily polymerize into fibrils directly. Fibrillar oligomers are early forms of fibrils and are on 
the pathway towards the formation of mature fibrils. Fibrillar oligomers are small, semistable, 𝛽-sheet rich oligomers and they have 
a tendency to transition into fibrils [160]. Other side, no­-fibrillar oligomers are usually stable and they are often considered off­
pathway in the aggregation process. According to the study of Vander Zanden et al. [160], no­-fibrillar oligomers are the most toxic, 
followed by fibrils, with monomers being the least toxic. The latest study revealed that no­-fibrillar oligomers can also convert into 
ﬁbrillar oligomers, although the conversion rate is very slow [79,117]. A schematic diagram has been given to show this fibrillar and 
no­-fibrillar pathway of A𝛽aggregation process in Fig. 2.

AD, 𝜏undergoes abnormal phosphorylation, and it misfolds and aggregates into a twisted tangle inside the neurons, known as NFTs

Recent research shows that in AD, there is an involvement of a protein called cellular prion (PrP𝐶) protein, which is mainly 
responsible for prion diseases. PrP𝐶protein can form complexes with A𝛽protein, which leads to AD [85]. Though the involvement 
of the protein in AD is not fully understood, many mathematical models have been formed based on this concept.
Glial cells: Studies demonstrate a signiﬁcant connection between glial cells and the progression of AD. Glial cells are neuron­
supporting cells. Within the central nervous system, four distinct categories of glial cells exist: astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia 
and ependymal cells. We have already discussed that A𝛽protein accumulation in the brain has become an important phenomenon 
in the progression of AD. A𝛽plaques activate glial cells, which then eliminate A𝛽[91]. Microglia and astrocytes are the two glial 
cells that are supposed to be mostly involved in AD. A question arises regarding the potential neuroprotective or neurotoxic roles of 
microglial cells and astrocytes in AD. Based on genetic and cell biology data, microglia contain defense mechanisms that prevent the 
harmful accumulation of A𝛽and the onset of AD. But activated microglia can have harmful actions in AD [26,68].

Microglia have protective roles in the early stages of AD, their prolonged or excessive activation can become harmful. Microglia 
can clear A𝛽aggregates through phagocytosis in the early stages of AD and shows the protective nature. In AD, chronic microglial 
activation can lead to an ongoing i­flammatory response. This persistent i­flammation results in the release of cytokines and ROS 
that can harm neurons, causing cell death and contributing to disease progression.
Microglia help in A𝛽plaque formation [148] and contribute to the progression of tauopathy [11]. On the other hand, microglia 
can be activated by both A𝛽and 𝜏proteins aggregations [41,62]. Moreover, the activation of astrocytes and the production of A𝛽
are interdependent [57,173]. Astrocytes are closely linked to the buildup of abnormal 𝜏protein forms in tauopathies [137]. But 
the relationship between them is not fully understood. Oligodendrocytes have a role in initiating and spreading human 𝜏protein 
within the corpus callosum of wild-type (WT) mice, as indicated by Ferrer et al. [55] in their study. In AD, activated glial cells can 
compromise the BBB, allowing harmful molecules to enter and exacerbate neuroi­flammation [32]. Consequently, glial cells make a 
substantial contribution to the advancement of AD.
Cytokines: Microglial cells serve as the delegates of the immune system within the brain. In AD, the activation of microglia and 
astrocytes triggers the release of various cytokines, which can either support neuronal health or exacerbate neurodegeneration [164]. 
Activated microglia have two types: pro-inflammatory microglia and anti-inflammatory microglia [154]. Small proteins called cy­
tokines are essential for regulating the immune system and cells growth and activity. Although anti-inflammatory microglia promote 
anti-inflammation and neuroprotection, pro-inflammatory microglia cause i­flammation and neurotoxicity [35]. Cytokines like tu­
mor necrosis factor-𝛼(TNF-𝛼), interleukin-1𝛽(IL-1𝛽) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), can promote i­flammation, leading to oxidative stress 
and further neuronal damage. Some cytokines, like interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-𝛽(TGF-𝛽), have anti­
inflammatory properties and may help mitigate the damage caused by i­flammation in AD. The connection between i­flammatory 
cytokines and the risk of AD may help us better understand the pathogenesis of AD. Domingues et al. [50] discussed the function 
of cytokines and chemokines related to AD. A detailed description is given in a tabular form in that paper about the impact of 
cytokines and chemokines on A𝛽deposition, A𝛽production and clearance and 𝜏protein production and clearance. The pathway 
diagram (Fig. 3) demonstrates the central role of activated glial cells in mediating neuroi­flammation through cytokine production, 
ultimately leading to synaptic dysfunction and neurodegeneration in AD.

Calcium signaling: Calcium signaling assumes a crucial role in facilitating neurotransmitter release, governing synaptic plasticity 
and enabling eﬀective neuronal communication. Hence, calcium signaling disruption in synapses can impede synaptic transmission

but interconnected nature of these calcium-mediated mechanisms, emphasizing how initial Ca2+ dysregulation can propagate through

Oxidative stress: Oxidative stress is also a signiﬁcant factor in the progression of AD. Oxidative stress arises when there exists 
a disparity between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within cells and the protective antioxidant mechanisms of the 
body. ROS has the potential to induce cellular dysfunction and even trigger cell death by harming lipids, proteins and deoxyribonucleic 
acid (DNA) within brain cells, ultimately contributing to the development of AD [60]. The overproduction of ROS can also damage 
proteins and DNA in the mitochondria, impairing the ability of neurones to produce energy. Aggregation of A𝛽and 𝜏protein is a 
compensatory reaction to underlying oxidative stress [119]. Oxidative stress i­fluences the ability of A𝛽peptide to aggregate into 
ﬁbrils, which then lead to plaque formation [29]. It disrupts neural communication and promotes neuroi­flammation. Other hand, 
oxidative stress contributes to the hyperphosphorylation of 𝜏proteins, which can lead to the formation of NFTs, a hallmark of AD. 
The authors focused on this factor in forming mathematical models for the progression of AD. Fig. 5 illustrates the interconnected 
pathways to express how oxidative stress initiates a cascade of cellular damage, promotes A𝛽aggregation and 𝜏phosphorylation, 
and ultimately leads to neurodegeneration in AD.

3.1. Modeling AD in terms of A𝛽protein aggregation mechanism
In this subsection, we discuss the mathematical models, in which A𝛽protein and its ploymer units like A𝛽protein monomer, A𝛽
protein dimer, A𝛽protein oligomers, etc. are involved in the model. The mathematical models containing the A𝛽protein as well as 
other components are reviewed here, with details provided below.
3.1.1. A𝛽protein and its aggregation
The aggregation process of A𝛽protein plays a very vital role in the progression of AD and many works focused on the aggregation 
mechanism of A𝛽protein [2,38,39,90,123,145]. In AD, A𝛽-40 or A𝛽-42 polymers eventually fold into amyloid fibrils and clump into 
amyloid plaques in the brain. The conversion from soluble monomer to insoluble fibril has an impact on AD. To understand this 
procedure, a mathematical model consisting of diﬀerential and algebraic equations describing this kinetic pathway of A𝛽protein 
aggregation is derived [123]. This type of kinetic model is also helpful in understanding the mechanism of toxicity of A𝛽protein. 
Pallitto & Murphy [123] studied a mathematical model by using this method. Kim et al. [90] used urea to reduce the production of 
the aggregation of A𝛽protein and also examined how urea i­fluences size distribution, aggregation kinetics and the morphology of 
aggregates.

3.2.4. Age and gender in AD
C.S. Drapaca [52] proposed a temporal mathematical model of AD and used fractional order diﬀerential equations to describe 
the temporal evolution. The model contains the following population: surviving neurons, dead neurons, activated microglia in a pro­
inflammatory and anti-inflammatory state, quiescent astrocytes, proliferative reactive astrocytes and aggregation-prone A𝛽fibrils. 
The method is generalized by Puri & Li [134]. The variable fractional order elucidates the concept of fading memory resulting from 
the neuro-protection loss caused by AD. The authors presented numerical evidence indicating that the reduction in the population of 
surviving neurons is more conspicuous in postmenopausal female patients when compared to males at the equivalent stage of AD.
As we know, AD biomarkers include both the A𝛽and 𝜏pathology. Authors [63] used an inclusive approach to estimate prevalence 
incorporating both symptomatic AD individuals and those at elevated risk, including A𝛽-positive (A𝛽-deposition) persons without 
considering 𝜏pathology or other neurocognitive disorders. In this study, the global burden of AD is characterized by providing data 
on country, gender and age. Using this data, we have plotted a bar chart to show the global mean prevalence with respect to age for

3.6. Neuronal dynamics
AD interferes with functions that are essential to neurons and their networks. Neurons are the fundamental building blocks of the 
nervous system. The hallmark pathological causes of AD, including the accumulation of A𝛽plaques and NFTs have a direct impact on 
neuronal structure and function. The progressive loss of neurons in memory-associated regions of the brain causes cognitive decline, 
which is one of the factors of AD. The interplay between neurons and the molecular processes involved in AD pathology is essential 
to understand. Hence, in this subsection, we discuss the models based on neuronal networks.
Bertsch et al. [15,17] introduced mathematical models that describe the initiation and development of AD by utilizing transport 
and diﬀusion equations as the foundation of their approach. In the work [15], brain neurons are treated as a continuous medium and 
organized according to their level of dysfunction. The authors assumed two mechanisms: the diﬀusion and aggregate formation of 
soluble A𝛽protein and prion-like transmission of the disease between neurons. The authors constructed a mathematical model that 
utilized a set of Smoluchowski equations to depict amyloid concentration. Additionally, they incorporated a kinetic-type transport 
equation to d­fine the distribution function of neuronal dysfunction. The numerical results are supported qualitatively by clinical 
images of AD distribution throughout the brain. The authors [17] investigated the existence and uniqueness of solutions to an initial 
boundary value problem related to AD. Beyond the inclusion of reaction-diffusion equations, the model also incorporates a transport 
equation that characterizes neuron malfunctioning. To establish the existence of these solutions, the authors employed a combination 
of mathematical tools, including the method of characteristics for the transport equation, a priori estimation techniques for solutions 
of the reaction-diffusion equations, a variation of the classical contraction theorem and the utilization of the Wasserstein metric for 
assessing probability measure properties.
Jafari et al. [82] put forward an innovative model centered on Cellular Automata (CA) for investigating the advancement of 
AD. The model represents the synapses of individual neurons as square cells positioned around a central cell. A crucial parameter 
within this framework is the A𝛽protein, which is computed using diﬀerential rate equations taken from the model by Puri & Li 
[134]. Notably, the authors introduced a novel deﬁnition of the AD rate applicable to an M × L-neuron network. Numerically, it is 
demonstrated that changes in a few parameters have a signiﬁcant impact on the progression of AD. Moreover, their research unveiled 
that AD rate exhibits a greater sensitivity to changes in astroglia levels compared to microglia.
Patow et al. [128] has studied the eﬀect of A𝛽and 𝜏on neuronal activity. A whole-brain dynamic model has been used to find 
the optimal parameters which describes the eﬀects of A𝛽and 𝜏on the excitation-inhibition balance of the local nodes. The authors 
have found the critical roles for 𝜏and A𝛽in complicated neuronal dynamics. Moreover, it is suggested that models of large-scale 
brain function in AD may be eﬀectively d­fined using regional distributions.
The spread of 𝜏and A𝛽throughout a human connectome is modeled by Alexandersen et al. [6] and they look into how the 
disease aﬀects neuronal dynamics. Early-stage hyperactivation, late-stage hypoactivation and frequency slowing related to AD are all 
explained. It is demonstrated that hyperactivation and frequency slowing are mostly caused by local neurotoxicity (triggered by 𝜏
and A𝛽) rather than topological relationships across various areas.
Stefanovski et al. [151] showed that how computational multi-scale brain model connects the phenomena of many scales and, as 
a result, helps to find possible disease pathways that might lead to better diagnosis and treatment. Simulations were used to replicate 
known empirical changes in electroencephalogram (EEG) between AD patients and healthy controls (HCs). In comparison to the mild 
cognitive impairment (MCI) and HC groups, the virtual AD group had slower frequencies in both simulated local field potentials and 
EEG.
Demirtas et al. [48] explored the resting-state functional connectivity (FC) across the entire brain in individuals with preclinical 
Alzheimer’s disease (PAD), mild cognitive impairment and mild dementia due to AD. The i­fluence of APOE4 carriership and its 
association with variations in core AD CSF biomarkers are also examined.

through non-neuronal tissues. Using this model, the authors assess the dynamic network containing several cross-talks across diﬀerent

The Smoluchowski equation characterizes the evolving densities of diﬀusing particles with a propensity for pairwise coagulation. 
In their research, the authors [56] investigated the homogenization process of a set of Smoluchowski’s discrete diffusion-coagulation 
equations, which model the aggregation and diﬀusion behavior of A𝛽protein peptides. By systematically removing an i­finite number 
of tiny holes of size 𝜖(neurons), from the immobile region Ω representing brain tissue, the authors have introduced a periodically 
perforated region denoted as Ω𝜖. This mod­fied domain can accommodate a non-uniform Neumann boundary condition, charac­
terizing the generation of A𝛽protein at the neuronal membranes. Through their examination, the authors have demonstrated that 
as 𝜖approaches zero, the solution of this micro-scale model gradually converges, in a two-scale manner, towards the solution of a 
macro-scale model that exhibits asymptotic consistency with the original one.
In a recent work, Cabrera-Alvarez et al. [25] developed a mechanistic multi-scale model of AD progression, linking A𝛽and 
hyperphosphorylated 𝜏. The model showed that changes in neural inhibition, primarily due to A𝛽, drive the electrophysiological 
changes in AD. It also revealed that early deposit locations of A𝛽and hyperphosphorylated 𝜏determine the disease’s spatiotemporal 
progression.
The paper [172] combines Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) with symbolic regression to discover a reaction-diffusion 
model for AD, focusing on 𝜏protein misfolding and spreading. PINNs are a type of machine learning model that integrates our prior 
physics-based knowledge into the training process of neural network. Using longitudinal 𝜏PET data from Alzheimer’s patients and 
healthy controls, the study ident­fies distinct misfolding models for both groups. The Alzheimer’s group shows a faster tau misfolding 
process compared to the control group. Authors anticipate that their study will serve as a foundation for more comprehensive analyses, 
leading to the development of image-based technologies for early diagnosis and, ideally, early treatment of AD and other misfolding­
protein-based neurodegenerative disorders.

3.9. Modeling through data and neuroimaging
Using modern neuroimaging methods combined with data-driven models provides pathways for early diagnosis and progression 
of AD. In this subsection, we delve into the fusion of data science and neuroimaging in the study of AD.
A spatiotemporal analysis was performed on EEG data obtained in the groups of AD patients. The Karhunen-Loeve analysis 
approach was used on four groups of subjects [88]. In severe AD patients, the analysis revealed considerable bilateral temporal 
functional diﬀerences and notable intra-hemispheric correlation with inter-hemispheric connection problems.
Ito et al. [80,81] constructed a model utilizing data derived from the literature to portray the longitudinal progression of 
Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive (ADAS-cog) scores, speciﬁcally the change from baseline, in individuals diagnosed 
with mild to moderate AD. The described model [80] is

(42)

untreated disease progresses. The functions 𝑃𝐷𝑝𝑏𝑜(𝑡) and 𝑃𝐷𝑑𝑟𝑢𝑔(𝑡) describe the placebo eﬀect and the eﬀect of the drug, respectively. 
Additionally, 𝜖characterizes the residual error in the model. This model encompasses the pace at which AD advances, the observable

curvilinear rate of AD advancement.
A stochastic, multi-state framework is employed, incorporating United Nations global population projections and data gleaned 
from epidemiological investigations, to forecast the global burden of AD and evaluate the potential eﬀects of therapies that delay 
the development of AD. Modest improvements in therapeutic and preventive measures that result in a slight delay in the onset and 
development of AD may signiﬁcantly decrease the global burden of AD [22].
Khanal et al. [87] proposed a framework for constructing a biophysical model that is capable of predicting and simulating realistic 
longitudinal MRIs of AD patients. The three main components of the framework are realistic MRI production, brain deformation 
and atrophy generation. The equations in the model, which is based on biomechanical principles, are mostly comparable to Stoke­
equations in fluid mechanics with a non-zero mass source portion. This model prescribes intricate atrophy patterns in order to imitate 
longitudinal MRIs. This approach may be used to investigate the interplay between the mechanical reactions of various brain tissues 
to neurodegeneration and test ideas on the distribution of brain atrophy. Fuzzy inference system is created with subtractive clustering 
technique to classify MRI images of patients with MCI or/and normal controls [92]. As input characteristics for the fuzzy inference 
system, means and standard deviations of MRI intensities over the most informative regions of interest (ROIs) are employed.
Several attempts have underscored the signiﬁcance of calcium-dependent cellular processes in the biochemical characterization 
of AD, suggesting that disturbances in calcium homeostasis may have a role in AD pathophysiology. In their study, Tiveci et al. [157] 
primarily explored hypotheses regarding physiological and biochemical changes in both healthy individuals and those with AD. They 
further established correlations between these changes and measurable physiological parameters derived from functional neuroimag­
ing data, speciﬁcally the temporal patterns of the blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) signal. When calcium dynamics are 
integrated into the model, it demonstrates that both the BOLD signal and the pr­files of metabolite concentrations exhibit temporal 
patterns consistent with the experimental data available in the literature.
Whittington et al. [165] studied the spatial distribution of A𝛽protein. To investigate diﬀerent theories of A𝛽protein spread in 
AD, the authors have proposed a mathematical model using A𝛽protein in vivo PET imaging data. This study yields the conclusion 
that the in vivo spatiotemporal distribution of A𝛽protein in AD can be mathematically represented through a logistic growth model. 
This model accounts for the spatial heterogeneity of the carrying capacity in the brain, maintains a constant exponential growth rate 
and features a consistent time for reaching half-maximal A𝛽protein concentration.
Extensive research has been conducted to investigate A𝛽protein distribution, deposits within the cerebral cortex in AD [23]. 
A computer-enhanced image analysis is done to examine this distribution. This method has the potential to facilitate future quanti­
tative investigations into the varying distribution of A𝛽in AD and certain dementia cases.

AD progression, aligning with the findings of DeVos et al. [49].
4. Therapeutic trials
AD ranks among the intricate neurodegenerative diseases. While there is currently no cure for AD, researchers have ident­fied 
several therapeutic targets that show promise in potentially slowing down the advancement of AD. The discovery of drugs that can 
delay the beginning of AD can include molecular, genetic and pharmacological techniques. Accumulation of A𝛽protein can be a 
major oﬀender in AD pathogenesis. A comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing protein aggregation 
and its impact on neuronal cell death holds the promise of innovative therapeutic strategies. An appealing therapeutic approach is 
to block the formation of A𝛽oligomers and fibrils, which may inhibit fibrillation and fibrilization-dependent neurotoxicity, are the 
drugs that regulate the activity of the enzymes 𝛾-secretase or 𝛽-secretase, notably inhibitors of 𝛽-secretase (BACE-1) [124]. However, 
in clinical trials, immunotherapy has proven to be signiﬁcantly more successful compared to 𝛽/𝛾-secretase interventions [175].
Mathematical models have been formed by researchers containing inhibitor drugs to treat AD. The research presented in the 
referenced paper [101] introduces three distinct drug models: the monomer-inhibitor drug model, the polymer-inhibitor drug model 
and the combined drug model. The study employs numerical simulations to substantiate the results. These simulations yield valuable 
insights, revealing that the monomer-inhibitor drug exhibits promise in mitigating the early stages of AD. Conversely, the polymer­
inhibitor drug demonstrates enhanced eﬃcacy in addressing more advanced AD cases. The monomer-inhibitor drug exhibits the

For many years, it has been assumed that A𝛽and 𝜏proteins act separately, with no A𝛽-𝜏interaction. However, new research

5. Discussion and conclusion
Mathematical models are instrumental in enhancing our comprehension of AD and can assist in the development of new therapeutic 
approaches. Depending on the specific research aim and objectives, several types of mathematical models can be formed to understand 
AD dynamics. However, it is important to highlight that AD is a complicated and multifaceted disease, and mathematical models are 
only one of many tools used to investigate it. The motivation to write this literature review on AD is the need to comprehensively 
understand the complexities of this neurodegenerative disease from both mathematical and biological points of view.
In this article, by reviewing the existing papers on AD, we went through the mathematical models section-wise according to their 
elements. Now here, we provide a table (see Table 3) that primarily focuses on the formalisms of several mathematical models, 
detailing their components and key comments.

5.1. Pros and cons of diﬀerent mathematical approaches
Mathematical models in the study of AD can take diﬀerent approaches, each with its own pros and cons:
ODEs are mainly used to know how a system changes over time based on its current state. This type of mathematical model allows 
us to model the temporal dynamics of etiological components contributing to AD. As a result, it can capture the progression of AD 
over time and also predict the future state of the disease. Here, for understanding the aggregation process of A𝛽and 𝜏proteins, 
and the interaction process between various biological factors, temporal models have been employed. On the other hand, temporal 
models may oversimplify the complex process, that is, it is not able to capture the multifactorial nature of AD. Complexity can be 
added in such models by incorporating time-varying parameters.

Spatiotemporal models are also suitable to model the progression of such non-infectious diseases because of the spreading nature 
of the components (proteins or cells, etc.) over time in the brain. A spatiotemporal mathematical model captures the propagation and 
dynamics of the disease in both space and time. Such models can provide insights into spatial patterns related to AD. Sometimes, it 
might be diﬃcult to handle such models due to the involvement of both time and space. The validation of the model highly depends 
on the spatial and temporal data, which may increase diﬃculties in capturing the pattern.
By incorporating randomness and variability, stochastic models are also helpful in capturing the inherent uncertainties involved in 
AD dynamics. Many authors incorporated stochasticity into their models by considering the eﬀect of randomness [77], the stochastic 
noises [171] and the extrinsic noise in the cellular microenvironment [58]. The outcome of the process cannot be predicted with 
certainty but rather follows a probabilistic pattern.
Network models aid researchers in understanding disease development by portraying elements such as genes and proteins as nodes 
and their interactions as edges. Moreover, researchers may discover novel therapeutic targets by analyzing the interaction between 
drugs and biological entities in a network model. Such models can capture the complex interaction and interconnection between 
brain networks. Network models might be a little more complex than others to understand the pathology of AD due to identifying 
the interactions in the brain network and finding experimental data.
Many authors also formulated agent-based models which are more realistic and d­fine rules that apply to individual biological 
cell. Agent-based models can capture the characteristics of an individual agent like neurons, proteins, etc, and their local interactions 
that may represent the complex and heterogeneous nature of AD. However, identifying parameters for particular agents might be 
diﬃcult due to insuﬃcient availability of data. Some work on the agent-based model has been discussed already in this review.
Several authors formed their models based on data and neuroimaging. Neuroimaging models can identify both structural and 
functional changes in the brain that can help to diagnose AD. Such neuroimaging data can be used in models, but getting high-quality 
neuroimaging data can be diﬃcult and costly. However, there are very few works where the real experimental or clinical results are 
compared to the results of mathematical models.
Each approach has its advantages and disadvantages. Combining biologically-equivalent models and using experimental data can 
provide a more comprehensive understanding of this complex disease.
5.2. Future direction
In this review, in the Therapeutic trials Section 4, we mentioned many treatment strategies to eradicate or slow down the pro­
gression of AD. For modeling such disease scenarios by incorporating treatments, models involving impulsive diﬀerential equations 
can be useful [34]. Moreover, optimal control methods can also be used to minimize disease progression. A few nonlocal models are 
formed for AD progression [122], hence forming nonlocal models can be a new direction to understanding the AD progression. There 
are fewer works where the results have been validated using experimental and clinical data [65,66,122,165]. Specific AD laboratory 
data can be incorporated into the appropriate models for improved outcomes. Machine learning and art­ficial intelligence techniques 
can be deployed to analyze large-scale data sets [43]. Understanding the interplay between neurodegeneration and immune responses 
may reveal new therapeutic targets. Recent works on the yeast prion model focus on understanding protein misfolding, aggregation 
and amyloid propagation, providing a platform to study neurodegenerative diseases like AD and PD [103,113].
For comprehensive and fruitful AD research, it is needed to have cooperation between researchers from many fields, including 
mathematics, neurology and clinical medicine. We believe that this review will help the researchers understand this complex disease, 
form mathematical models, and identify new therapeutic approaches.

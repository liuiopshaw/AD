Zwitterionic Photosensitizer-Assembled Nanocluster
Produces Eﬃcient Photogenerated Radicals via
Autoionization for Superior Antibacterial Photodynamic
Therapy
Ping He, Mingxuan Jia, Linfang Yang, Haolin Zhang, Ruizhe Chen, Weiyun Yao,
Yonghui Pan, Quli Fan, Wenbo Hu,* and Wei Huang
Photodynamic therapy (PDT) holds signiﬁcant promise for antibacterial
treatment, with its potential markedly ampliﬁed when using Type I
photosensitizers (PSs). However, developing Type I PSs remains a signiﬁcant
challenge due to a lack of reliable design strategy. Herein, a Type I PS
nanocluster is developed via self-assembly of zwitterionic small molecule
(C3TH) for superior antibacterial PDT in vivo. Mechanism studies
demonstrate that unique cross-arranged C3TH within nanocluster not only
shortens intermolecular distance but also inhibits intermolecular
electronic-vibrational coupling, thus facilitating intermolecular photoinduced
electron transfer to form PS radical cation and anion via autoionization
reaction. Subsequently, these highly oxidizing or reducing PS radicals engage
in cascade photoredox to generate eﬃcient ·OH and O2‾·. As a result, C3TH
nanoclusters achieve a 97.6% antibacterial eﬃcacy against MRSA at an
ultralow dose, surpassing the eﬃcacy of the commercial antibiotic
Vancomycin by more than 8.8-fold. These ﬁndings deepen the understanding
of Type I PDT, providing a novel strategy for developing Type I PSs.

the problem, driving the emergence of new superbugs and per-
petuating a vicious cycle.[3,4] Antibacterial photodynamic therapy

design strategy. A critical limitation lies in the ineﬃcient PeT be-

Type I ROS.[20] Recent studies point out the importance of inter-
molecular PeT between PSs and electron-donating/withdrawing
substrates in enhancing Type I ROS production. Speciﬁcally, in-

water to generate Type I ROS.[22–25] Despite these advances, ex-
isting Type I PSs generally require multiple components, such
as substrates or donor/acceptor species for intermolecular PeT
and amphiphilic matrices for water solubility, posing signiﬁcant
challenges for clinical translation. Eﬀorts to simplify Type I PS
design have demonstrated the feasibility of achieving intermolec-
ular PeT between homogeneous PSs,[26] but its application in
aPDT remains unexplored. Based on these ﬁndings, the devel-

compared to C3T, along with a 42% reduction in 1O2 genera-

a 97.6% antibacterial rate against Gram-positive bacteria even at
an ultralow dose of 8 μg mL−1, surpassing the eﬃcacy of the com-

2. Results
2.1. Design and Characterization of Photosensitizer Nanocluster
via Self-Assembly
3PS*, typically achieved by singlet-to-triplet intersystem crossing
(ISC), is recognized as essential for ROS generation. Building
on our previous work that zwitterionic PS has substantial 3PS*
production via ISC,[27] we designed a novel zwitterionic C3TH
via the hydrolysis of its cationic precursor C3T. C3TH features
a side-chain quaternary ammonium group along with a benzoic
acid-terminated conjugation. Such a zwitterionic structure could
twist the conjugated system via electrostatic interaction, promot-
ing substantial 3PS* generation via ISC. In addition, this design
endows C3TH the excellent water solubility for bioapplications.
Details of the synthetic route and characterization are available
in the supporting information (Figures S1–S3, Supporting Infor-
mation).

2.3. Electrochemistry and Photosensitization Channel
To explore the reason for the superior Type I ROS production,
the redox potentials (vs Ag/Ag+) of C3T and C3TH were mea-
sured using cyclic voltammetry (Figures 2a, S12, Supporting
Information). The reduction potentials (vs NHE) of C3T and
C3TH were determined to be -1.04 and -1.05 V (Figure 2b), re-
spectively, while their oxidation potentials (vs NHE) were 0.86
and 1.37 V. Their lower reduction potentials, compared to those
of O2, H+ /·OH (0.31 V vs NHE) and O2/O2‾· (-0.33 V vs
NHE), suggest that both C3T and C3TH can transfer elec-
trons to 3O2, facilitating O2‾· and ·OH generation. This ﬁnd-
ing aligns with experimental results showing robust ·OH/O2‾·
production (Figures 1i and S13, Supporting Information).

2.4. Excited State Dynamics for Type I ROS Production
The combination of femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA)
and spectroelectrochemical analysis demonstrates the photogen-
erated PS+· or PS‾· for C3TH nanocluster that is responsible for
its Type I ROS production. For C3TH, the decay of excited-state
absorption (ESA) signals ≈520 nm is accompanied by the rise of
the ESA signal ≈580 and 1250 nm corresponding to the forma-
tion of new species (Figure 3a). In contrast, the decay of the ESA
≈560 nm for C3T is associated with the emergence of new species
exhibiting ESA signals near 430 nm and 900 nm (Figure 3b). To
identify these new species, spectroelectrochemical analysis was
employed, which shows the UV–vis-NIR absorption spectra be-
fore and after applying diﬀerent biases. C3TH reveals a rising
broadband absorption above ≈400 nm with the prolonged time
of application of oxidizing potential (Figure 3c), corresponding to
the PS+· (C3TH+·). This assigns the ESA signal near 580 nm in
the fs-TA spectrum partially to C3TH+·. Unfortunately, C3TH‾·
was undetectable in our spectroelectrochemical setup, likely due
to the limitations of our detection range. In contrast, C3T shows
characteristic absorptions of both PS+· and PS‾· (Figure 3d), with
overlapping signals ≈430 nm, which assigns the ESA signal ≈430
nm in the fs-TA spectrum to both C3T+· and C3T‾·.
Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy con-
ﬁrms the photogenerated electron for both C3TH and C3T
(Figures 3e and S15, Supporting Information), indicating the for-
mation of C3TH‾· in C3TH, even though it was undetectable
in spectroelectrochemical experiments. Notably, C3TH shows
higher photocurrent response compared to C3T (Figure 3f),
demonstrating its more eﬃcient photogenerated PS+· and PS‾·.
The origin of PS+· and PS‾· for C3TH are attributed to 3PS*, as
supported by quantum calculations and fs-TA results. These cal-
culations reveal enhanced ISC in C3TH relative to C3T, due to a
more energetically favored ISC channel and larger spin-orbit cou-
pling (Figure S16, Supporting Information). Experimental data
further corroborate these ﬁndings. The narrower fs-TA spectral
band centered at ≈580 nm, compared to the broader spectroelec-
trochemical spectral lineshape above 400 nm for C3TH, indicates
the contribution of additional species beyond the PS+· to the ESA
band ≈470 nm. The fs-TA kinetics reveal a delayed formation
and decay of new species with ESA ≈1250 nm for C3TH com-
pared to that ≈580 nm (Figure S17a, Supporting Information).
In contrast, new species for C3T form and decay concurrently
(Figure S17b, Supporting Information). Moreover, growing evi-
dence demonstrates broad-band characteristic absorption of or-
ganic radicals,[34–36] such as PS+· or PS‾·, whereas non-radical
material such as 3PS* usually exhibits a narrow absorption line-
shape. These phenomena support the following conclusion: i) the
ESA ≈580 nm for C3TH in fs-TA spectrum predominantly orig-
inates from 3PS* at initial delay time, resulting in the narrower
fs-TA spectral band centered at ≈580 nm; and ii) the subsequent
transform of 3PS* into PS+· or PS‾· corresponds to ESA band
round 580 and 1250 nm, respectively, thus explaining the delayed
dynamics of new species for C3TH. This means that ESA from
3PS* and PS+· overlap ≈580 nm, whereas the ESA ≈1250 nm
is predominantly attributed to PS‾·. Collectively, these ﬁndings
suggest that C3TH exhibits a stronger generation of 3PS* through
more eﬃcient ISC, which subsequently enhances the production
of PS+· and PS‾·, thereby contributing to its superior Type I ROS
production.
2.5. Proposed Mechanisms for Type I ROS Production
The formation of PS+· or PS‾· is most likely driven by autoion-
ization reactions among homogeneous PS due to the absence of
other potential substances in our PS systems (Figure 3g). The
thermodynamic favorability of autoionization reactions can be as-
sessed by calculating the vertical ionization potential (VIP) and
the vertical electron aﬃnity (VEA) of the PSs,[37] as the sum of
these values must be negative for the reaction to proceed. It is
evident that autoionization between 3PS states for both C3T and
C3TH is theoretically feasible, as the sum is negative (Table S2,
Supporting Information). However, autoionization reactions be-
tween 3PS* and 1PS* are not feasible, as the sum of their VIP
and VEA is positive. Moreover, the smaller average intermolec-
ular distance (3.2 vs 3.3 Å) within C3TH nanocluster compared
to the C3T nanocluster facilitates more eﬃcient intermolecular
PeT in the C3TH nanocluster (Figure S6, Supporting Informa-
tion). Therefore, 3PS* of both C3T and C3TH can be reduced in
principle through the autoionization reaction (1) by neighboring
3PS* ones as follows:
3PS∗+ 3PS∗→PS+ ∙+PS−∙
(1)
These results suggest an autoionization mechanism in C3TH
nanoclusters to eﬀectively generate Type I ROS (Figure 3g). Upon
photoirradiation, the ground-state C3TH is excited to the singlet
excited state (1PS*), which undergoes ISC to populate substantial
3PS*. Subsequently, eﬃcient intermolecular PeT between nearby
3PS* molecules, i.e., autoionization reaction, proceed to generate
PS+· and PS‾·. PS+· catalyzes the oxidation of H2O to generate
O2 in situ, while PS‾· transfers electrons to endogenous or in situ
generated O2, promoting the formation of O2‾· and ·OH. More-
over, PS+· may directly oxidize H2O to produce ·OH. All these
results lead to the eﬃcient production of ·OH.

2.8. Biosafety and Biocompatibility
The in vitro and in vivo results demonstrate the excellent bio-
compatibility and biosafety of C3TH. The negligible cytotoxicity
of C3TH withing mouse embryonic ﬁbroblasts (NIH 3T3) even
at concentrations up to 80 μg mL−1 reveals its excellent biocom-
patibility (Figure S21, Supporting Information). Throughout the
treatment period, there were no signiﬁcant changes in the body
weights of the mice in any group (Figure S22, Supporting In-
formation), and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of ma-
jor organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) revealed no
signiﬁcant morphological abnormalities or inﬂammatory lesions
(Figure S23, Supporting Information). The biosafety of C3TH
was further evaluated through routine blood tests and assess-
ments of liver and kidney function. Routine blood tests indicated
that white blood cell (WBC) counts were signiﬁcantly elevated
in the experimental groups (PBS, PBS+L, C3TH, and Van) com-
pared to the blank control group, suggesting that the mice exhib-
ited some inﬂammatory responses. However, WBC levels in the
C3TH+L group were closer to those of the blank control group,
indicating a subsiding inﬂammatory response (Figure S24, Sup-
porting Information). Finally, liver function markers (ALT, AST,
ALP) and kidney function markers (CREA, UA, BUN) remained
within the normal range in all groups, with no signiﬁcant dif-
ferences compared to the blank control group (Figure S25, Sup-
porting Information). These results conﬁrm that C3TH does not
induce hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity.
3. Conclusion
In summary, we have developed a C3TH nanocluster formed via
the self-assembly of homogeneous molecules, which produces ef-
ﬁcient Type I ROS for superior aPDT. The zwitterionic structure
of C3TH induces a twisted conﬁguration that oﬀers two key ad-
vantages: i) it enhances 3PS* production via facile ISC; and ii) pro-
motes unique cross-arrangement within nanocluster, facilitating
intermolecular PeT. The combination of abundant 3PS* and ef-
ﬁcient intermolecular PeT supports autoionization reactions be-
tween neighboring 3PS* to eﬀectively generate PS+· or PS‾·. Sub-
sequently, PS+· catalyzes the oxidation of H2O to produce O2 in
situ, while PS‾· transfers electrons to either endogenous or in
situ generated O2, resulting in the eﬃcient production of O2‾·
and ·OH with reduced reliance on endogenous O2. Utilizing this
superior Type I ROS production, our therapeutic dose is half that
of the clinically used vancomycin, yet its eﬃcacy exceeds that of
Van by more than 8.8-fold. This study deepens the understand-
ing on PeT processes between homogeneous PSs, marking a sig-
niﬁcant advancement in Type I mechanism. These ﬁndings are
expected to inspire new design strategies of Type I PSs with en-
hanced antibacterial properties.
4. Experimental Section
Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulation:
All the MD simulations in this
work were performed by GROMACS-2020.6 package.[41] All atom types
and their structural parameters were taken from the general amber force
ﬁeld (GAFF).[42] To mimic amorphous C3T and C3TH clusters, the large-
scale MD simulations were ﬁrst performed to obtain the PS cluster con-
formations in an aqueous solution. The MD simulations were suﬃciently
long for C3T and C3TH to reach stable amorphous states. Energy min-
imizationwas performed, followed by 5 ns equilibrium simulations with
position restrains in 3D for PSs under NPT (T = 298.15 K, P = 1 bar)
ensemble with temperature annealed from 298 to 373 K and cooling to
298.15 K in the ﬁrst 5 ns. Finally, 50 ns production MD simulations were
performed with 2 diﬀerent concentrations (10 and 30 PSs molecules in
10 nm3 water environment) under the NPT (T = 298.15 K and P = 1 bar)
ensemble. The cutoﬀdistance for VDW interactions was 1.0 nm. All bond
lengths were constrained via the LINCS algorithm. The formation process
results of C3T and C3TH clusters were illustrated by the representative
snapshots extracted from MD trajectories. Simulation results were visual-
ized by VMD 1.9.4 program.[43]
Huang–Rhys Factor Calculations:
From the molecular dynamics (MD)
simulation results, a cluster model that included solvent molecules and
was suﬃciently large for subsequent quantum chemical calculations was
extracted. Ground- and excited-state optimizations were performed us-
ing two-layer ONIOM calculations. The Huang–Rhys factors for C3T and
C3TH within the clusters were calculated using MOMAP program.[44–46]
Spectroelectrochemical Measurements:
Spectroelectrochemical mea-
surements were performed using a custom-built system comprising three
main components: an electrochemical workstation, a light source, and an
optical ﬁber spectrometer. The electrochemical workstation, an AUTOLAB
electrochemical analyzer, employed a standard three-electrode conﬁgura-
tion at room temperature. A platinum grid served as the counter elec-
trode, an Ag/AgCl electrode was used as the reference electrode, and a
platinum stick functioned as the working electrode, all housed within a 1
mm narrow-slit cuvette. The electrolyte solution consisted of 0.01 M PBS
buﬀer. The sample (cuvette) holder had an entry and exit port for light, en-
suring that light can pass through the electrolyte solution. Light from the
halogen lamp (HL-2000-LL, Ocean Insight) was focused through lenses
and directed into the sample cell through the entry port. After passing
through the sample cell, light exits from the exit port and enters an op-
tical ﬁber spectrometer (PG2000-Pro back-thinned spectrometer, ideaop-
tics, China). The spectrometer and electrochemical controller were syn-
chronized using the homebuilt software. During the experiment, the elec-
trochemical controller applies a potential or current while the spectrome-
ter simultaneously records spectral data.
Bacterial Culture:
Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus was ob-
tained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). MRSA (USA
300) were cultured in LB liquid medium at 4 °C, then centrifuged at 5000
rpm for 3 min to collect the bacteria. The turbidity of the bacterial suspen-
sion at 600 nm was measured using an absorption spectrophotometer,
and the concentration was adjusted to 108 CFU mL−1 for subsequent ex-
periments.

containing varying concentrations of C3TH, resulting in ﬁnal concentra-

Modeling of Infected Wounds in Mice:
Balb/c female mice (6–8 weeks
old) were purchased from Jiangsu Jicui Yakang Biological Company, and
used in strict accordance with the guidelines of the Administration of Lab-
oratory Animals of China and approved by the animal ethics committee of
Northwestern Polytechnical University (202101013). After anesthesia, the
backs of the mice were shaved, and an 8 mm diameter wound was cre-
ated using a perforator. To establish the infection model, 10 μL of MRSA
bacterial solution (107 CFU mL−1) was applied to the wound.
In vivo Antibacterial Experiments:
All mice were randomly divided into
5 groups, PBS, PBS+L, C3TH, C3TH+L, and Van. 12 h post-infection,
10 μL of PBS, 8 μL of C3TH (1 mg mL−1), or 8 μL of Van (1 mg mL−1) was
administered dropwise to the wound. For the light-treated groups (PBS+L
and C3TH+L), the wounds were irradiated with white light (330 longpass
ﬁlter, 132 mW cm−2) for 10 min every other day. The wound healing pro-
cess was monitored through photographs, and the infected area and body
weight of the mice were measured over the 7-day treatment period.
Statistical Analysis:
Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad
Prism 10.1.2 and Origin 2021 software. All data are expressed as the mean
± standard deviation (SD). Between-group analyses comparing the exper-
iments were analyzed using one- or two-way ANOVAs. Probability (P) val-
ues < 0.05 were considered statistically signiﬁcant. (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01,
***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001).

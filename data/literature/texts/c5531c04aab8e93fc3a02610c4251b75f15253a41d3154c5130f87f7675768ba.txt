ABSTRACT: Metal nanoclusters (NCs), composed of a metal
core and protecting ligands, show promising potentials as
enzyme mimics for producing fuels, pharmaceuticals, and
valuable chemicals, etc. Herein, we explore the critical role of
ligands in modulating the peroxidase mimic activity and
stability of Au NCs. A series of Au15(SR)13 NCs with various
thiolate ligands [SR = N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC), 3-mercapto-
propionic acid (MPA), or 3-mercapto-2-methylpropanoic acid
(MMPA)] are utilized as model catalysts. It is found that
Au15(NAC)13 shows higher structural stability than
Au15(MMPA)13 and Au15(MPA)13 against external stimuli
(e.g., pH, oxidants, and temperature) because of the intramolecular hydrogen bonds. More importantly, detailed enzymatic
kinetics data show that the catalytic activity of Au15(NAC)13 is about 4.3 and 2.7 times higher than the catalytic activity of
Au15(MMPA)13 and Au15(MPA)13, respectively. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that the Au atoms on the
motif of Au NCs should be the active centers, whereas the superior peroxidase mimic activity of Au15(NAC)13 should originate
from the emptier orbitals of Au atoms because of the electron-withdrawing effect of acetyl amino group in NAC. This work
demonstrates the ligand-engineered electronic structure and functionality of atomically precise metal NCs, which afford
molecular and atomic level insights for artificial enzyme design.
KEYWORDS: gold nanoclusters, ligand engineering, peroxidase mimic catalysis, catalytic activity, stability
A
tomically precise metal nanoclusters (NCs) have
emerged as a class of potential catalysts because of
their abundant active sites, precise formulas, molecular
purity, and well-defined hierarchical structure.1−6 It is known
that these metal-NCs-based catalysts have been used in
photocatalysis and electrocatalysis for hydrogen evolution,7−9
CO2 reduction,10−12 degradation of organic pollutants,13 etc.
Nonetheless, less attention is paid toward engineering metal
NCs as enzyme mimics for application in biocatalysis, which is
one of the main branches in catalysis for manufacturing
pharmaceuticals and valuable chemicals.14−19 In fact, metal
NCs that generally comprise a metal core and surface ligands
show promising potentials as enzyme mimics because of their
tailorability and enzymelike hierarchical structure (Scheme 1
a).20,5,21−26 Changes in the metal core27−31 or the surface
ligands32−34,25 in the external shell of metal NCs may
significantly affect their functionalities and catalytic perform-
ance. Therefore, metal NCs can provide an ideal platform for
engineering artificial enzymes at the molecular and atomic
level.
Recent efforts have been devoted to exploring the feasibility
of atomically precise Au NCs as enzyme mimics. For instance,
Au40(S-Adm)22/MOFs (S-Adm = adamantanethiolate) can
function like horseradish peroxidase,35 while Au25(MPA)18
NCs (MPA = 3-mercaptopropionic acid) serves as glutathione
peroxidase mimics,36 whose catalytic activity and selectivity
can be tuned by regulating the active center through single
metal atom substitution. Despite progress, the study of metal-
NC-based enzyme mimics is still in its infancy, with the ligand
effect on the modulation of enzyme mimic functionality of
metal NCs remaining elusive. The protecting ligands, which
are indispensable portions of Au NCs, are also important

understand the effects of the protecting ligands on modulating

In this study, we aim to unravel the critical roles of
protecting ligands in modulating the enzyme mimic catalytic
activity and stability of Au NCs. We chose Au15(SR)13,
protected by different thiolate ligands (SR refers to thiolate
ligand), as a model catalyst (prepared by the CO-reduction
method, Scheme 1b).41 As illustrated in Scheme 1b, the
thiolate ligands used in this work are N-acetyl-L-cysteine
(NAC), 3-mercapto-2-methylpropanoic acid (MMPA), and 3-
mercaptopropionic acid (MPA). The molecular structures of
NAC and MMPA are derived from that of MPA, in which a
hydrophilic acetyl amino group or a hydrophobic methyl group
is substituted on the α-carbon, respectively. The tailoring of Au
NCs with NAC, MMPA, and MPA can endow them with
different tertiary hierarchical structures, thereby resulting in
different enzyme mimic activity and stability. The results show
that, compared with Au15(MMPA)13 and Au15(MPA)13,
Au15(NAC)13 exhibits improved peroxidase mimic catalytic
ability and structural stability against external stimuli (e.g., pH
and temperature). Therefore, the engineering of Au NCs with
different protecting ligands could be an efficient strategy to
modulate the catalytic activity and stability of their enzyme
mimics and confer molecular and atomic level insights for
artificial enzyme design.

performance of Au NCs can be affected by the structure of

Au15(MMPA)13, and Au15(MPA)13 against pH changes, strong
oxidants (H2O2), and temperature. The effect of pH on the
structural stability of Au NCs was investigated by monitoring

Au15(MPA)13 can only remain stable from pH 6 to pH 10,

possess high thermal stability. In contrast, the UV−vis
absorption spectra of Au15(MPA)13 (Figure S8c) change
dramatically upon heating, thereby indicating that the

results

DFT calculations were performed to unravel the origin of
the different structural stabilities of these Au NCs. The binding
energies calculated by DFT show that Au15(NAC)13 has the
lowest binding energy (−14.05 eV), which is ∼0.97 and ∼1.1
eV lower than the binding energies of Au15(MMPA)13 (−13.08
eV) and Au15(MPA)13 (−12.95 eV) (Figure 3a). This data
suggests that Au15(NAC)13 is more thermodynamically stable
than Au15(MMPA)13 and Au15(MPA)13, which is consistent
with the experimental results. Furthermore, DFT calculations
reveal that the bond lengths of Au−Au bonds in the Au4 core
and the Au−Au and Au−S bonds in Au15(NAC)13,
Au15(MMPA)13, and Au15(MPA)13 are almost identical
(Figure 3b and Table S2), with little effect from the protecting
ligands. However, the different molecular structures of NAC,
MMPA, and MPA do affect the surface environments of
Au15(NAC)13, Au15(MMPA)13, and Au15(MPA)13, which are
different. The acetyl amino group in the side chain of NAC is
beneficial for the formation of intramolecular hydrogen bonds
in the molecular structure of Au15(NAC)13, which is supported
by DFT calculations (Figure 3c). In contrast, fewer intra-
molecular
hydrogen

in
Au15(MMPA)13 (Figure 3d) and Au15(MPA)13 (Figure 3e).
Therefore, the different structural stabilities among
Au15(NAC)13, Au15(MMPA)13, and Au15(MPA)13 should
originate from the differences of the side chains on the
protecting ligands.
Peroxidase-Like Activity. We chose a typical enzymatic
reaction facilitated by peroxidases as a model reaction to
decipher the roles of NAC, MMPA, and MPA in modulating
the catalytic ability of Au NCs as enzyme mimics. The
enzymatic reaction involves the decomposition of H2O2 and
the oxidation of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) (Figure
4a). The characteristic adsorption of the oxidation product
3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine diamine (oxTMB) at 652 nm
can be quantitatively measured using a UV−vis absorption
spectrometer. The peroxidase mimic catalytic reactions were
conducted as follows: 2 mg/L of catalyst [Au15(NAC)13,
Au15(MMPA)13, or Au15(MPA)13], 1 mM H2O2, and 1.5 mM
TMB were incubated in an acetate buffer solution (5 mM, pH
= 6) for 1 h. UV−vis absorption spectra were collected to
assess
the catalytic
performance
of Au15(NAC)13,
Au15(MMPA)13, and Au15(MPA)13. In general, the higher the
absorbance at 652 nm, the better the catalytic activity. As
shown in Figure 4b, the absorbance of Au15(NAC)13 + H2O2 +
TMB at 652 nm is much higher than that of H2O2 + TMB and
Au15(NAC)13 + TMB, which indicates that Au15(NAC)13
could greatly boost the oxidation rate of TMB in the presence
of H2O2. As for Au15(MMPA)13 and Au15(MPA)13, although
they could also promote the oxidation of TMB in the presence
of H2O2, their catalytic abilities are much lower than the
catalytic abilities of Au15(NAC)13 (Figure 4c,d).
ESI-MS was performed to confirm the integrity of
Au15(NAC)13, Au15(MMPA)13, and Au15(MPA)13 after cata-
lytic reactions. As shown in Figure S14, the characteristic peaks
of Au15(NAC)13 appeared at m/z = ∼1011.2, 1264.2, and
1685.9, which corresponded to different charges (5−, 4−, and
3−), respectively, thereby demonstrating the intact molecular
structure of Au15(NAC)13 after catalysis. Similarly, the
structural integrities of Au15(MMPA)13 and Au15(MPA)13
were also validated (Figures S15 and S16). All the evidence
indicates
that
Au15(NAC)13,
Au15(MMPA)13,
and
Au15(MPA)13 can remain intact after catalysis.

possibility of H2O2 dissociation into two hydroxyl radicals with
the assistance of Au15(NAC)13, Au15(MMPA)13, or
Au15(MPA)13. The energy required for the dissociation of
H2O2 is 2.70 eV without catalysts. In comparison, H2O2 can be
easily dissociated into two hydroxyl radicals in the presence of
Au15(NAC)13 with an energy barrier of −1.03 eV. The
hydroxyl radicals can further adsorb on the Au atoms in the
protecting shell of Au15(NAC)13, accompanied by the
formation
of

Au15(MMPA)13 and Au15(MPA)13 can also facilitate the
dissociation of H2O2, thereby resulting in the generation of
Au15(MMPA)13-2OH and Au15(MPA)13-2OH. However, their
dissociation rates are lower than that of Au15(NAC)13 because
of the higher energy barrier (Au15(MMPA)13 = 0.03 eV,
Au15(MPA)13 = −0.28 eV) (Figure 5b), which finally leads to
poorer catalytic activity. In addition, we analyzed the electronic
structure of motif Au atoms in Au15(NAC)13, Au15(MMPA)13,
and Au15(MPA)13 by calculating the Bader charge. The Bader
charge of motif Au atoms in Au15(NAC)13 is −0.90, which is
lower than those of Au15(MPA)13 (−0.81) because of the
electron withdrawing of the acetyl amino group. In
comparison, the methyl group in the MMPA tends to donate
electrons, which results in a higher Bader charge (−0.79). The
electron withdrawing of NAC is also supported by the XPS
analysis of Au-SR complexes (SR = NAC, MPA, and MMPA).
The XPS spectra of Au-NAC, Au-MPA, and Au-MMPA
complexes are shown in Figure S21. As illustrated in Figure
S21 the Au 4f7/2 binding energy of the Au-NAC complex is
about 84.10 eV, while the binding energies of Au-MPA and
Au-MMPA complexes are about 84.02 and 83.99 eV,
respectively. Thus, the different electronic structures of motif
Au atoms in Au15(NAC)13, Au15(MMPA)13, and Au15(MPA)13
caused by the different functional groups on the protecting
ligands mainly contribute to their different catalytic activity.
CONCLUSIONS
In summary, Au15(SR)13 NCs protected by different ligands,
such as NAC, MMPA, MPA, were successfully synthesized
through the CO reduction method. The as-synthesized
Au15(NAC)13 shows higher structural stability than
Au15(MMPA)13 and Au15(MPA)13 because of intramolecular
hydrogen bonds. More importantly, Au15(NAC)13 exhibits
higher peroxidase-like activity and catalytic kinetics, in
comparison with Au15(MMPA)13 and Au15(MPA)13, which is
ascribed to the emptier orbitals of Au atoms on the motif of Au
NCs and lowered the dissociation energy of H2O2. The ligand-
engineering strategy demonstrated in this study offers
molecular and atomic level insights in designing artificial
enzymes for energy, environmental, and pharmaceutical
applications.
EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Reagents. Tetrachloroauric(III) acid (HAuCl4·3H2O, ≥99.99%
metals basis), N-acetyl-L-cysteine (≥99%) (NAC), 3-mercaptopro-
pionic acid (≥99%) (MPA), 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine dihydro-
chloride hydrate (≥98%) (TMB), and sodium hydroxide (≥99.8%)
were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 3-Mercapto-2-methylpropanoic
acid (MMPA) was obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. The
40% acrylamide/bis solution (19:1), ammonium persulfate (APS),
resolving gel buffer (1.5 M Tris-HCl buffer, pH = 8.8), stacking gel
buffer (0.5 M Tris-HCl buffer, pH = 6.8), and N,N,N′,N′-tetramethyl
ethylenediamine were received from Bio-Rad. Carbon monoxide
(CO, 99.9%) was purchased from Singapore Oxygen Air Liquide Pte
Ltd. (SOXAL). Ultrapure water was obtained from the Millipore
Milli-Q UF Plus system.
Synthesis of Au NCs Protected by Different Ligands. Au
NCs protected by N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) were synthesized via a
reported CO reduction method.41 Briefly, 12.5 mL of 20 mM HAuCl4
and 10 mL of 50 mM NAC were mixed in 227.5 mL of ultrapure
water under vigorous stirring. Afterward, the pH value of the reaction
solution was adjusted to 8.0 by 1 M NaOH aqueous solution to
control the growth kinetics of Au NCs. Then, CO was pumped into
the mixture for 2 min to trigger the reduction process. Thereafter, the
reaction solution was aged at room temperature for 72 h under
vigorous stirring at 500 r/min. Finally, Au NCs protected by NAC
(described as NAC-Au NCs) were concentrated through a filter
membrane with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 3 kDa,
followed by purification through native polyacrylamide gel electro-
phoresis (PAGE, 30%). PAGE was carried out on the Bio-Rad Mini-
PROTEAN Tetra Cell or PROTEAN II xi Cell system to separate the
as-synthesized Au NCs. The resolving gel was freshly prepared by
mixing 30 mL of 40% acrylamide/bis solution (19:1), 10 mL of
resolving gel buffer, 200 μL of APS, and 20 μL of TEMED. In terms
of the stacking gel, 26 mL of ultrapure water, 10 mL of stacking gel
buffer, 4 mL of 40% acrylamide/bis solution (19:1), 200 μL of APS,
and 40 μL of TEMED were fully mixed to prepare the stacking gel.
The samples were dissolved in glycerol (Vsample/Vglycerol = 1:1) and
then loaded and eluted at 180 V for 24 h at 4 °C. The yellow band
was cut, mashed, and immersed in ultrapure water for 24 h to ensure
that the Au NCs were fully diffused out. Afterward, the resulting
solution was first filtered through a hydrophilic membrane with a pore
size of 0.2 μm, followed by ultrafiltration through a filter membrane of
3 kDa MWCO. Au NCs protected by 3-mercaptopropionic acid
(MPA) and 3-mercapto-2-methylpropanoic acid (MMPA) were
synthesized by the same protocol as NAC-Au NCs. The only
difference was that the solution pH was adjusted to 10 for MPA and
11 for MMPA. The same purification procedures, including PAGE
and ultrafiltration, were implemented to obtain ultrapure products of
MPA- and MMPA-protected Au NCs.
Characterizations. UV−vis absorption spectra were recorded on
a UV−vis spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimazu). Electrospray
ionization mass spectra (ESI-MS) were collected on a Bruker
MicroTOF-Q system by using negative mode. The sample was
injected into the chamber at a rate of 20 μL/min. The temperature of
the heat source was kept at 120 °C. IsCID energy, ion energy, and
collision energy were set as 1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 eV, respectively.
Furthermore, the prepulse storage was set as 25.0 μs. Inductively
coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Thermo
scientific iCAP 6000 series) was employed to analyze the

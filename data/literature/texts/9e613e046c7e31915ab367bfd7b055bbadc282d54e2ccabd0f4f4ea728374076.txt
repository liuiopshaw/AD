A B S T R A C T
Herein, a heterostructure based on MnO2 and g-C3N4 was constructed on the surface of metallic Ti implants, in
which MnO2 favored the transfer and separation of free charges to enhance the photoconversion eﬃciency of g-
C3N4 by 21.11%. Consequently, the yield of ROS was promoted signiﬁcantly, which denatured protein and
damaged DNA to kill bacteria eﬃciently. In addition, glutathione (GSH, L-γ-glutamyl-L-cysteinyl-glycine) de-
fending oxidative stress in bacteria, was oxidized by MnO2 in the hybrid coating once the bacterial membrane
was disrupted by ROS. Hence, after visible light irradiation for 20 min, MnO2/g-C3N4 coating exhibited superior
disinfection eﬃcacy of 99.96% and 99.26% against S. aureus and E. coli severally. This work provided a practical
sterilization strategy about MnO2/g-C3N4 systems through the synergistic eﬀects of enhanced photodynamic
antibacterial therapy and oxidization eﬀect of MnO2 with great biosafety, in which MnO2 enhanced the pho-
tocatalyst property of g-C3N4 to generate more ROS and deplete GSH to improve antibacterial eﬃciency. It will
bring more insight into rapid and highly eﬀective disinfection and antibacterial strategy without using tradi-
tional high-temperature, ultraviolet ray and antibiotics that cause side-eﬀects.

1. Introduction
Most of healthcare-associated infections are relevant with device-
related infections [1], with millions of people disabled or died owing to
pathogenic microorganism’s infection each year [2,3]. Microorganism
including Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, usually ﬂoats on
the surface of the medical device like plankton, or anchors itself in the
surface of the medical device [4,5], entering into the body through
wounds when medical devices contact patients. Once people get in-
fected, they require longer hospitalization due to a poor prognosis [6].
Currently, the sterilization of medical devices can be divided into
four categories according to the antibacterial mechanism: high-tem-
perature disinfection, oxidation sterilization, radiation bacteria-in-
activation and antibiotic bacteria-killing [7]. Conventional high tem-
perature antimicrobial techniques include dry heat sterilization and
steam sterilization [7,8], but medical devices may suﬀer deformation
risk under high temperature or moisture. In addition, this way cannot
be employed for medical implants completely. Oxidation sterilization
generally utilizes hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), ethylene oxide (EO),
ozone (O3) and vaporized peracetic acid (VPA) through denaturing
proteins and disrupting cell wall permeability [9,10]. And radiation
sterilization is carried out by Gamma radiation, electron beam and ul-
traviolet light to kill bacteria by breaking down bacterial DNA [9,11].
However, these kinds of techniques are very harmful for normal cells
and tissues. Although antibiotic bacteria-killing is the clinically used
way for bacterial infection including implants infections in vivo [12],
the abuse of antibiotics has been inducing bacteria-resistance even the
occurrence of superbugs [13]. Despite of the broad-spectrum anti-
bacterial properties, some antibacterial agents also exhibit cytotoxicity
or tissue toxicity to humans [14,15]. Hence, it is quite urgent to develop
highly eﬀective disinfection strategies with suﬃcient biosafety.
Photodynamic antibacterial therapy (PDT) is a novel and practical
method for curing bacterial infections [16]. Graphite carbon nitride (g-
C3N4) has been reported to be a novel metal-free photocatalyst with
2.7 eV broad bandgap [17,18], which can decompose water to produce
hydrogen and oxygen under visible light with highly chemical stability
and low cost. However, the disadvantages, such as the rapid re-
combination of photogenerated electron-hole pairs and the low utili-
zation of visible light restrict the wide application of this material [19].
Combining g-C3N4 with other photocatalysts is a good way to improve
its photocatalytic property.
MnO2 is a low-cost, resource-rich biocompatible semiconductor
[20], which has a narrow bandgap to endow MnO2/g-C3N4 hetero-
structure with high utilization of visible light [21]. More importantly,
glutathione
(GSH,
L-γ-glutamyl-L-cysteinyl-glycine),
an
important
component scavenging free radicals and toxins in bacteria [22], can be
oxidized by MnO2 to strengthen the eﬀects of PDT [23]. In this work,
MnO2 was grown in-situ on g-C3N4 through a simple redox method to
form a MnO2/g-C3N4 heterostructure on the surface of Ti implants, thus
accelerating the transfer of charges and inhibiting the recombination
between electrons and holes to enhance the photoconversion eﬃciency
(PCE) of g-C3N4 by 21.11%. At the same time, GSH was depleted by
MnO2, so that the disinfection eﬃciency of MnO2/g-C3N4 was improved
signiﬁcantly. Consequently, visible light irradiation could trigger this
surface system to kill bacteria eﬀectively within a short time. As pre-
viously mentioned, conventional disinfection techniques have various
disadvantages such as long time-consuming, high cost and biotoxicity.
Nonetheless, the prepared MnO2/g-C3N4 coating in this work can
sterilize medical devices with celerity, simpleness and biosafety. Our
work presents a practical sterilization strategy about MnO2/g-C3N4
systems through the synergistic eﬀects of enhanced photodynamic an-
tibacterial therapy and oxidization eﬀect of MnO2, in which MnO2 can
enhance the photocatalyst property of g-C3N4 to generate more ROS
and deplete GSH to improve antibacterial eﬃciency of MnO2/g-C3N4
coating.
2. Experiment section
2.1. Preparation of g-C3N4 coating
g-C3N4 was synthesized through thermal vapor liquid-polymeriza-
tion method [24]. Brieﬂy, the mechanically polished Ti plates (6 mm
diameter, 2 mm thick) were hydrothermally treated in 4 M NaOH so-
lution at 80 °C for 90 min before g-C3N4 modiﬁcation. 1.8 g ground
melamine powders were evenly spread on the bottom of a 22 ml cru-
cible, and the alkali-heat treated Ti plates were placed on a g-C3N4
wrapped metal wire frame at a height of about 1 cm from the bottom of
the crucible, with the treated side of Ti plates facing the bottom. The
uniform g-C3N4 coating on Ti plates labeled as g-C3N4-Ti, was obtained
by placing the crucible at a heating rate of 10 °C min to 520 °C and
maintained at 520 °C for 30 min, then naturally cooled to room tem-
perature.
2.2. Preparation of MnO2/g-C3N4 coating
1 g MnSO4·H2O was dissolved in 45 ml of deionized (DI) water to
prepare a uniform aqueous solution of MnSO4. Next, the g-C3N4-Ti
plates were placed in the solution and stirred electromagnetically for
12 h to facilitate the coupling of Mn2+ with nitrogen atoms on g-C3N4,
contributing to the subsequent growth of MnO2/g-C3N4 composite.
Finally, the above solution was heated in a water bath at 40 °C with the
addition of 0.6233 g KMnO4 and magnetically stirred for 12 h.
Subsequently, the ﬁnal samples were taken out and rinsed several times
with DI water to remove free MnO2 and Mn2+, and dried in air to
obtain a hybrid coating of MnO2 and g-C3N4 on Ti plates, named as
MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti. In order to study the photocatalytic property of
MnO2/g-C3N4 composite, the dosages of MnSO4·H2O and KMnO4 were
reduced or increased by 5 times based on the aforementioned pre-
paration method to obtain 0.2MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti and 5MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti
respectively.
2.3. Characterization
The surface morphology of samples was observed through a scan-
ning electron microscopy (SEM; JEOL-820 and JSM6510LV) and a ﬁeld-
emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM; JSM7100F, JEOL, JP)
equipped with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). And the micro-
structure of samples was characterized by a transmission electron mi-
croscope (TEM; Tecnai G20, FEI, USA). The elemental compositions and
chemical states of the prepared coatings were examined by X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc 250Xi), and
the phase structure of samples was examined by X-ray diﬀraction (XRD;
D8A25, Bruker, Germany) with diﬀraction angle (2θ) ranging from 5° to
80° and a step size of 0.01°. The UV-3600 (Shimadu, Japan) was em-
ployed to examine the UV–vis-NIR absorption of samples thus revealing
the optical property of samples.

2.6. Photoelectrochemical measurements
The photoelectrochemical property of samples was measured in a
standard three electrode system and recorded on an electrochemical
analyzer (CH1660E). The electrolyte is 0.5 M Na2SO4 aqueous solution
and 300 W Xenon lamp acted as the irradiation source. The photo-

the AC voltage was 5 mV. For LSV measurement, the scan rate was
0.05 V s−1. And LS-55 spectrometer was employed to detect the room-
temperature photoluminescence (PL) spectra of samples.
2.7. Ellman’s assay
Firstly, each sample was immersed in 150 μL of 0.8 mM GSH bi-
carbonate buﬀer solution (50 mM, pH = 8.7) in the well of a 96-well
plate for 5 min. After that, the well was illuminated by Xenon lamp for
20 min. An infrared thermal imager (FLIR, E50) was used to monitor
the temperature. Next, the solution of the well was transferred into the
well of a 48-well plate with 450 μL of 50 mM Tris-HCl solution
(pH = 8.0) and 100 μL of 10 mM dithionitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) bi-
carbonate buﬀer solution (50 mM, pH = 8.7), and was blended in a
rocking bed for 5 min. Afterwards, 200 μL of the blended solution was
taken to the well of a 96-well plate, and the optical density (OD) at
410 nm of the solution was measured by microplate reader (SpectraMax
I3MD USA) [27,28].

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Characterization of MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti
Fig. 1A shows the preparation procedure of g-C3N4 and MnO2/g-
C3N4 coatings on the surface of alkali-heat treated Ti plates, which
presents network structures (Fig. S1), beneﬁcial to gather thermal
vapor as the seed layer of g-C3N4. As shown in Fig. 1B and C, the g-C3N4
coating on Ti is composed of smooth sheets laminated into a ﬁlm, on
which the sheets are stacked to form towering arrays. After the growth
of MnO2 on g-C3N4-Ti, the surface gets rough caused by ﬂower-like
MnO2 nanosheets deposited on g-C3N4-Ti (Fig. 1D and E), bringing
more active sites for photocatalysis. And the MnO2/g-C3N4 coating is
deposited on the surface of Ti plates with an average thickness of
27.89 μm (Fig. 1F). From the energy–dispersive X–ray spectroscopy
(EDS) elemental mapping of MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti (Fig. 1G) for Mn, O, C,
and N, it can be seen that all of these elements are distributed uniformly
in MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti, conﬁrming that MnO2 is dispersed equably on the
surface of g-C3N4-Ti. TEM analysis is used for further study on the
microstructure of g-C3N4 and MnO2/g-C3N4 (Fig. S2A and B). MnO2/g-
C3N4 powders scraped from the surface of Ti plates is not as smooth as
individual g-C3N4 powders owing to MnO2 nanosheets deposited on the
surface. That can be veriﬁed from the elemental mapping (Fig. S2C–E)
of the red marked area in Fig. S2B, indicating the uniform deposition of
MnO2 on g-C3N4. The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image of MnO2/g-
C3N4 (Fig. 1H) corresponding to the blue marked area of Fig. S2B re-
veals that the lattice spacing of 0.326 nm and 0.308 nm corresponds to
the g-C3N4 (0 0 2) plane and the MnO2 (3 1 0) plane severally.
Fig. 2A shows the XRD patterns of the synthesised g-C3N4, MnO2
and MnO2/g-C3N4 powders scraped from the surface of Ti plates, re-
vealing the crystal structures and phases of as-prepared samples.
Compared with standard JCPDS cards, as-prepared g-C3N4 is assessed to
be hexagonal phase (JCPDS 87-1526), whose peaks appear at 13.1° (1 0
0) and 27.4° (0 0 2). The MnO2 peaks appear at 12.8° (1 1 0), 18.1° (2 0
0), 28.8° (3 1 0) and 37.5° (2 1 1), which is in good agreement with the
tetragonal phase of α-MnO2 (JCPDS 44-0141). After the growth of
MnO2 on g-C3N4, the peaks of g-C3N4 are still able to be observed in the
XRD pattern of MnO2/g-C3N4, indicating that the addition of MnO2 has
no inﬂuence on the crystal structure of g-C3N4. However, the peaks of
MnO2 grown on g-C3N4 are weak, which may be resulted from the small
amount and uniform dispersion of MnO2 on MnO2/g-C3N4 composite.
Moreover, the XRD pattern of g-C3N4/Ti is shown in Fig. S3, revealing
that Ti2O was generated according to JCPDS 11-0218. Nonetheless, the
thickness of MnO2/g-C3N4 coating (Fig. 1F) is too large that permit light
transmission to excite the photocatalyst property of Ti2O [29,30].
Hence, the substrates has no impact on the photocatalyst performance
of MnO2/g-C3N4 coating.
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is used to explore the ele-
mental compositions and chemical states of the as-prepared samples.
From the XPS spectrum of MnO2/g-C3N4 (Fig. 2B), the C, N, O, and Mn
elements coexist. As shown in Fig. 2C, C1 s spectra in both MnO2/g-
C3N4 and g-C3N4 have two distinct peaks at 287.70 and 284.58 eV,
assigned to the sp2 CeC bond and the sp2 hybrid carbon on the N-
containing aromatic ring NeC]N respectively [31,32]. Nevertheless,
the C1s spectrum of MnO2/g-C3N4 has one more peak of 285.95 eV than
that of g-C3N4, attributed to the CeO bond formed between g-C3N4 and
MnO2 [32]. In Fig. 2D, N1s spectra have peaks around 398.2, 399.4,
400.7 and 404.1 eV, corresponding to the sp2 hybrid nitrogen in the
triazine ring CeN]C, the tertiary nitrogen C^N, the free amino group
CeNeH and the charge eﬀect localization in the heterocycle severally
[32]. The three peaks of the O 1s spectra (Fig. 2E) at 529.41, 531.748
and 533.167 eV are attributed to the MneOeMn bond in MnO2 and the
oxygen atoms in the absorbed water respectively [32]. The peaks at
653.875 and 642.418 eV of Mn 2p spectra (Fig. 2F) correspond to Mn
2p3/2 and Mn 2p1/2, respectively [32]. The peak position corresponding
to each bond is slightly diﬀerent in g-C3N4 and MnO2/g-C3N4, which
may be caused by the combination of the diﬀerent electron densities
between MnO2 and g-C3N4.
The UV–vis absorption spectroscopy of samples is shown in Fig. S4,
showing the optical property of scraped g-C3N4, MnO2 and MnO2/g-
C3N4 powders. The absorption band of g-C3N4 is seen to be from UV to
463 nm. After the integration of MnO2 on g-C3N4, MnO2/g-C3N4 com-
posite holds a much broader absorption spectra and stronger absorption
intensity to utilize visible light more eﬃciently, compared with pure g-
C3N4. This may result from the formation of heterojunction at the tight
interface between the MnO2 and g-C3N4.
3.2. Photocatalytic properties
In order to prove that MnO2/g-C3N4 has superior photocatalytic
performance than g-C3N4, the photoelectric properties of the coatings
were measured. In the test of photocurrent performance, the higher
photocurrent/dark current ratios indicate less recombination of pho-
togenerated electrons and holes, and longer living free charge carriers
[33]. Considering the impact of the weight ratio of MnO2 to g-C3N4 on
the photocatalyst performance, two samples were prepared extra
through reducing or increasing the dosages of MnSO4·H2O and KMnO4
by 5 times based on the original preparation method, obtaining
0.2MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti and 5MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti respectively. In Fig. 3A,
MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti has non-zero dark current and the highest photo-
current/dark current ratios among samples, verifying that MnO2/g-
C3N4-Ti has most electron transferring and superior photocatalyst
property. As irradiated, photon energy can excite electron transfer then
increases number of photogenerated electron-hole pairs [34]. Never-
theless, 0.2MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti has less MnO2 than MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti,
namely less charge trapping sites. With the content of MnO2 increasing,
the trapping sites of 5MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti become the recombination
centers, resulting in the poor photocatalytic performance. For non-zero
dark current, we infer that it is owing to the additional pseudocapaci-
tance induced by the heterostructure [35,36]. The EIS measurement is
also related with the photocatalytic property. The EIS Nyquist plot
composes an arc, the radius of which represents the resistance of charge
transfer in photocatalyst materials [37]. As shown in Fig. 3B, MnO2/g-
C3N4-Ti group has the smallest arc under light, representing the highest
separation eﬃciency of electron-hole pairs and smallest resistance of
charge transfer. In addition, the linear-sweep voltammetry (LSV) is
employed to evaluate the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) of samples,
which also reﬂects the electron-transfer process [38]. From the LSV
curves shown in Fig. S5, it can be seen that the MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti group
under light has the highest ORR current. That is to say, MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti
can separate electron-hole pairs and generate charge carriers more
easily than g-C3N4-Ti under visible light [39]. Based on LSV curves, the
photoconversion eﬃciency (PCE) of g-C3N4-Ti and MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti is
calculated according to the equation [40,41]:

3.3. Antibacterial study and the mechanism
From the spread plates shown in Fig. S7, samples in dark have
negligible eﬀects on bacterial viability. In contrast, except for pure Ti,
both g-C3N4-Ti and MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti under 20 min illumination have
obvious inhibition on the growth of bacteria. It can be ascribed to the
light response of MnO2 and g-C3N4. g-C3N4-Ti shows the antibacterial
eﬃcacy of 57.66% (Fig. 5A) and 42.66% (Fig. 5B) against S. aureus and
E. coli respectively. MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti shows the highest antibacterial
eﬃcacy of 99.96% and 99.26% against S. aureus and E. coli. From the
SEM images of E. coli (Fig. 5C) and S. aureus (Fig. S8), serious de-
struction of bacterial membrane occurs under light, and bacterial cells
with smooth surface and regular shape can be observed in all dark
groups and pure Ti under light. Bacterial death is inferred to be relevant
with the disruption of cells membrane. In order to prove the hypothesis,
the bicinchoninic acid disodium (BCA) protein assays were performed,
because protein will leak out once membrane is destroyed. As shown in
Fig. 5D and E, the group with higher antibacterial eﬃcacy has more
protein leakage. In the previous reports, ROS is known to seriously
disrupt bacterial cells membrane [51,52]. Hence, the excellent anti-
bacterial property of MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti may arise from the generation of
numerous ROS in the MnO2/g-C3N4 heterostructure.
In Fig. S9, the tiny diﬀerence of temperature is observed between
each sample under light for 20 min. In order to explore the factor of
diminishing GSH, Ellman’s assay was performed. By controlling the
power of the Xenon lamp and dryer, experiments were carried out at
25 °C and 50 °C respectively. And samples were treated with irradiation
or dark treatment at each temperature. From the results shown in
Fig. 6A, with the experimental temperature increasing or irradiation
extension, the color of the solution becomes shallower, indicating the
increased loss of GSH. As shown in Fig. 6B, as temperature increasing,
the loss of GSH is augmented in each group [53]. Even if in the dark and
at 25 °C, MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti reduces GSH by 62.90% due to the oxidation
of GSH to GSSG through thiol-disulﬁde exchange facilitated by MnO2
[23]. Except for Ti groups, the GSH loss of the samples under light is
higher than the group in the dark arising from ROS oxidative eﬀect.
Hence, we propose an antibacterial mechanism of MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti by
enhanced photodynamic antibacterial therapy (PDT) with the reduction
of GSH. As shown in Fig. 6C, when the MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti is irradiated
with simulated sunlight, bacteria surrounding the instrument will be
attacked by ROS generated from MnO2/g-C3N4. Subsequently, the
membrane of bacteria will be destroyed. Meanwhile, GSH in bacteria
will be oxidized by MnO2 in the coating, enhancing the eﬃciency of
PDT.
3.4. Toxicology
From the results shown in Figs. 7A and S10A, when samples are not
irradiated, g-C3N4-Ti groups have higher viability of cells compared
with the control group (pure Ti), which is in good accordance with the
previous reports [54]. Although the cell viability of MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti
(109.70% for 24 h, 137.94% for 72 h) is slightly decreased when
compared with g-C3N4-Ti, it is still higher than that of pure Ti, in-
dicating the well biocompatibility of MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti. Such conclusion
can also be drawn from the cell morphologies observed by FE-SEM
(Fig. 7B and D). Cytoplasmic protrusions are almost found in each
sample, contributing to cells movements [55]. The increasement of
them in the length and quantity would improve the cell viability [56].
The longer and more protrusions are achieved within samples by a
sequence of g-C3N4-Ti > MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti > Pure Ti, and this is in
great agreement with the results of MTT. To further study the cyto-
toxicity of samples, the viability of cells after irradiation was assessed.
As shown in Fig. S10B, during the ﬁrst 24 h of cultivation, both the g-
C3N4-Ti (79.20%) and the MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti (92.92%) show slight de-
crease of cell viability compared to pure Ti, mainly ascribed to ROS
generated by g-C3N4-Ti and the MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti. The Mn2+ ions re-
leased by the reaction between GSH and MnO2 can enhance cell
growth, reducing cell damage brought by ROS [57]. This superiority of
MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti is also reﬂected in the assessment of 72 h cultivation

4. Conclusion
In this work, MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti was successfully prepared by thermal
vapor liquid-polymerization and redox method, and its disinfection
property was explored for the ﬁrst time. MnO2 and g-C3N4 are in in-
timate contact to form a Z-scheme heterojunction, thus exciting elec-
tron transfer from the VB of MnO2 to the CB of g-C3N4. That facilitates
the separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs, suppresses the
recombination of free charges, and improves the eﬀective utilization of
visible light to generate more ROS. Otherwise, MnO2/g-C3N4-Ti coating
can enhance the antibacterial eﬀect of PDT compared with g-C3N4-Ti
and Ti due to GSH oxidation in bacteria by MnO2. The prepared MnO2/
g-C3N4-Ti composite is simple in operation, low in cost and superior in
biocompatibility. Therefore, it is a potential candidate for sterilization
of medical devices.

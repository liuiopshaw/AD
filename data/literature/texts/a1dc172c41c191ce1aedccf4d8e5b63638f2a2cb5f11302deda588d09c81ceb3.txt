ABSTRACT: As a new type of nanomaterial, DNA-templated silver
nanoclusters (DNA-AgNCs) have been widely studied because of their
ﬂuorescence and antibacterial properties. In this study, we combined the
DNA-AgNCs with aptamers of bacteria to achieve a novel approach for the
visual detection and eﬀective elimination of bacteria. The aptamers of
Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) were linked to G-rich sequences to achieve
ﬂuorescence enhancement when approaching the DNA-AgNCs. The
capture of aptamers not only realized the visual monitoring of bacteria
but also promoted the antibacterial eﬀects. Additionally, a ﬂuorescent
nanoﬁlm with excellent selectivity and antibacterial activity in the detection
and elimination of S. aureus was developed based on the DNA-AgNCs.
These aptamer-functionalized DNA-AgNCs show signiﬁcant potential for
many applications in food packaging and biomedical engineering.
KEYWORDS: aptamers, DNA-templated silver nanoclusters, antibacterial, ﬂuorescent, nanoﬁlm
1. INTRODUCTION
Silver nanoclusters (AgNCs) have shown a broad application
prospect as next-generation materials in many ﬁelds, including
optics, catalysis, environmental detection, biomedical diag-
nostics, and various therapies.1−4 These nanoclusters usually
contain from a few to several hundred metal atoms with sizes
comparable to the Fermi wavelength of an electron.5 In this
size range, the band structure becomes discontinuous and is
broken down into discrete energy levels, somewhat similar to
the energy levels of molecules, which are obviously diﬀerent
from single metal atoms and plasmonic-metal nanoparticles.6
Therefore, AgNCs possess molecular-like properties, for
example, HOMO−LUMO transitions, chirality, luminescence,
and catalytic reactivity.7 However, the instability of AgNCs
severely limits their application, and a stabilizing scaﬀold is
required to overcome this limitation. Surface ligands can not
only prevent the aggregation of metal nanoclusters but also
endow them with new functionalities.8,9 Compared with other
scaﬀolds, nucleic acid is widely used as a material for the
assembly of metal nanostructures due to the programmability
and speciﬁcity of the Watson−Crick base pairing.10 DNA-
AgNCs stand out for their extremely wide range of reported
emission colors, which range from blue to infrared light.11,12
Additionally, the ﬂuorescence of AgNCs varies depending on
the changes in the DNA sequence and the transformation is
often reversible.13 In particular, it has been reported that DNA-
AgNCs are illuminated through approaching guanine-rich
DNA sequences, indicating the electronic transfer from
guanine to the cluster.14 Furthermore, numerous studies have
reported the color switching or lighting methods to identify
and quantify certain targets.15,16 Because of its high sensitivity,
good speciﬁcity, and strong stability, this nucleic acid-based
ﬂuorescent signal recognition method has been increasingly
applied in various analytical sensors for the detection of metal
ions, toxins, bacteria, biomarkers, etc.17
As a novel nanomaterial, both the ﬂuorescent and
antibacterial properties of DNA-AgNCs are worthy of
attention.18 Compared with other silver materials, nanosilver
provides greater eﬃcacy in defending against bacteria because
of its large speciﬁc surface area.19 Currently, the antibacterial
mechanism is usually considered as the synergistic eﬀect of
AgNCs and Ag+, Ag+-mediated killing, and AgNC contact
killing.20−22 Ag+ can interact with −SH of a protein to form a
stable S−S bond, resulting in protein deactivation. This
involves transmembrane ATP generation and mediated ion
transport across cell membranes.23,24 AgNCs, however, can
anchor to the bacterial cell wall, inducing leakage of the cellular
contents and, consequently, bacterial death.22 For this reason,
the antibacterial eﬀect of AgNCs is stronger on Gram-negative

reported, the antibacterial performance of DNA-AgNCs is
dependent on the DNA sequence.18 In particular, AgNCs
scaﬀolded with branched DNA exhibited an enhanced
antibacterial performance.25 Increasing the contact area
between nanoparticles and bacteria is one of the most eﬀective
ways to promote antibacterial activity.26 Aptamers have high
aﬃnity and speciﬁcity and are thus widely used in molecular
recognition.27 Furthermore, aptamers can signiﬁcantly enhance
the antibacterial activity of nanoparticles against target

widen their applications, a feasible way is incorporating AgNCs
into nanoﬁbers. Electrospinning is a relatively simple and cost-

acid (PLA) is widely used for electrospinning because of its
large speciﬁc surface area, good biodegradability, and good
biocompatibility.31−33
In this study, we synthesized DNA-AgNCs linked to the
aptamers for the successful visual detection and eﬀective
elimination of bacteria. The DNA aptamers act as a “bridge,”
cleverly connecting the optical and antibacterial properties of
DNA-AgNCs. In addition, a biodegradable DNA-AgNC
antibacterial nanoﬁlm was prepared via electrospinning
technology. The novel nanoﬁlm has promising potential for
applications in active packaging and biomedical engineering.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Materials and Chemicals. Analytical-grade silver nitrate
(AgNO3, 99.8%) and sodium borohydride (NaBH4, 97%) were
purchased from Aladdin Reagents (Shanghai, China). All oligonu-
cleotides were synthesized and HPLC-puriﬁed by Ruibo Biotech
(Beijing, China). The detailed base sequences used in this paper are
listed in Table S1 (Supporting Information). Human bronchial
epithelial cells (Beas-2B) were obtained from Shanghai Institutes for
Biological Sciences (SIBS). Trichloromethane and dimethyl for-
mamide were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO,
USA). All reagents were of analytical grade, and solutions were
prepared using ultrapure water (speciﬁc resistivity of 18 MΩ cm).
2.2. Synthesis of DNA-Stabilized AgNCs. AgNCs were
synthesized using the protocol developed by Yeh et al.14 Brieﬂy,
Nx-serial sequences (x: 0−15, as listed in Table S1) were ﬁrst mixed
with the AgNO3 solution and incubated at room temperature for 30
min. Thereafter, NaBH4 was added to the previous mixture, and it was
shaken vigorously for 1 min. The ﬁnal concentrations were 15 μM in
DNA, 90 μM in AgNO3, and 90 μM in NaBH4 in a 20 mM, pH 6.6
sodium phosphate buﬀer. The aqueous solution of NaBH4 was
prepared by dissolving the NaBH4 powder in water and adding the
required volume to the DNA/Ag+ mixture within 30 s, followed by
vigorous shaking for 5 s. The reaction was kept in the dark at room
temperature for 12 h before use. Finally, 15 μM Apt-G sequences
were separately added to the AgNCs solution, which was then
incubated at room temperature for 2 h to form Nx-AgNCs/Apt-G.
2.3. Antibacterial Activity Test. The antibacterial performance
of the synthesized AgNCs was evaluated by detecting the minimum
inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the material. MIC is described as
the lowest concentration of an antimicrobial agent that can prevent
the visible growth of bacteria after overnight incubation.2 Escherichia
coli (E. coli) and S. aureus were used in this study as the bacterial
models. The antimicrobial assay was performed as follows: ﬁrst, the
bacteria were cultured in Luria−Bertani (LB) medium and shaken for
24 h at 220 rpm and 37 °C. Thereafter, various concentrations of
DNA-AgNCs were incubated with 10 μL of 1 × 108 CFU/mL of
bacterial cells in a ﬁnal volume of 600 μL for 2 h. They were then
shaken at 220 rpm and at 37 °C for 12 h to obtain ﬁnal DNA-AgNC
concentrations of 0, 1.25, 2.5, 3.75, 5, 6.25, and 7.5 μM. Subsequently,
bacterial growth was determined by measuring the optical density
(OD) at 600 nm. This experimental procedure was repeated in
triplicate.
2.4. Cell Viability Assay. The eﬀect of N4-AgNCs/Apt-G on the
viability of Beas-2B cells was evaluated via a cell-counting kit 8 (CCK-
8) assay. Several groups of Beas-2B cells (1 × 104 cells, total volume
of 100 μL per well) were incubated in 96 well plates at 37 °C for 24 h
in a 5% CO2/95% air incubator. After the removal of the former
medium, various concentrations of N4-AgNCs/Apt-G were added to
the cells in a serum-free medium and cultured for 12 h. Then, 10 μL
of CCK8 solution was added, and they were incubated for another 2
h. Finally, the absorbance at 490 nm was recorded using a microplate
reader (ELx808, BioTek, USA). All experiments were conducted in
triplicate.
2.5. Fabrication via Electrospinning. Generally, 10 g of PLA
was dissolved in 50 mL of cosolvent (trichloromethane and dimethyl
formamide = 9:1, v/v). The electrospinning solution was loaded into
a 10 mL syringe. It was driven by a syringe pump (NE-300, New Era
Pump Systems Inc., USA) to obtain a solution ﬂow rate of 1 mL/h.
Electrospinning was conducted by applying a voltage varying from 14
kV with a power supply (ES50P-5W/DAM, Gamma, USA). A
grounded collecting plate covered by a piece of aluminum foil was
used as the collector for ﬁber deposition. The distance between the
needle tip and collector was between 10 and 14 cm. The resulting
nanoﬁlm was dried at 25 °C in a vacuum oven for 8 h.
2.6. Morphology Observation of Bacteria. For SEM imaging
of bacteria, 108 CFU/mL of bacteria were treated with N4-AgNCs/
Apt-G for 12 h, after which the suspended bacteria were washed and

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
As shown in Scheme 1, the ﬂuorescence of the DNA-AgNCs
was enhanced by S. aureus aptamer-linked G-rich sequences.
Thereafter, the obtained AgNCs were then electrospinned to
PLA to prepare the ﬂuorescent PLA−AgNC nanoﬁlm. When
the aptamer recognized and bonded with target S. aureus, G-
rich sequences are forced to separate from the DNA-AgNCs,
leading to strong ﬂuorescence quenching. In this way, the
visual monitoring of S. aureus was realized through the changes
in ﬂuorescence. In addition, the aptamers on the ﬁlm serve as a
cage to trap the bacteria, thus supporting the AgNCs with
antibacterial activity. The results thus indicate that the
biodegradable PLA−AgNC aptasensor nanoﬁlm shows both
excellent ﬂuorescence properties and antimicrobial activity.
The DNA template sequence 5′-CCCTTAATCCCC-
3′(N0) was previously reported by Dickson and coworkers34
whose ﬂuorescence was enhanced by guanine-rich DNA
sequences, 5′-GGGTGGGGTGGGGTGGGGA-3′.14 As
shown in Figure 1a, S1 contained the nucleation sequence
for the weak-ﬂuorescent AgNCs and hybridization sequence,
which can be partially complementary to the aptamer of S.
aureus, while S2 comprised an aptamer sequence and a G-rich
sequence. The weak-ﬂuorescent AgNCs were illuminated by
the G-rich sequence after the hybridization of S1 and S2.
Furthermore, the spectral properties of Nx-AgNCs with
diﬀerent numbers of complementary sequence bases were
also discussed. The N0-templated AgNCs (N0-AgNCs)
exhibited an emission wavelength (λem) at 573 nm, as reported
by Dickson and coworkers (Figure 1c, black line).34 The
ﬂuorescence emission was red-shifted when extended to two
DNA bases on the C-rich template (Figure 1c, red line). With
further increases in length, the emission peaks were maintained
near 540 nm. In addition, all of the Nx-AgNCs, with the
exception of N6-AgNCs, showed weak ﬂuorescence (Figure 1b,
top). Among Nx-AgNCs, the ﬂuorescence intensity of N6-
AgNCs reached the maximum, which was approximately 4
times that of other AgNCs. These results suggested that the
side bases of the nucleation sequence could also greatly
inﬂuence the ﬂuorescence spectra of AgNCs.
The addition of the Apt-G sequence into the Nx-AgNCs (x
= 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 15) was found to increase the ﬂuorescence
intensity of all samples, with changes to their emission
wavelengths observed (Figure 1d,e). The Nx-AgNCs/Apt-G
showed a similar wavelength near 528 nm, with excitation

The strongest green ﬂuorescence emission was observed in the
N4-AgNCs, which increased by ∼6 times under the addition

sequently, the N4-AgNCs were selected for use in subsequent

Apt-G and N4-AgNCs were observed to be approximately 1
and 3 nm, respectively (Figure 1d−f), which is consistent with
that of the previously reported ﬂuorescent AgNCs (∼2 nm).35
The TEM images further demonstrated the uniform and
dispersed size of AgNCs (Figure S1). Consistent with previous
reports, the AgNCs formed on the nucleation sequence 5′-
CCCTTAATCCCC-3′ ranged from 1.19 to an average size of
3 nm36−38 as a result of the diﬀerence in the side bases of the
nucleation sequence and synthesis conditions. In addition,
DNA secondary structures could provide more rigid
conﬁgurations and more stable skeletons for reduced atom
clusters. Therefore, the reduction in the size of the AgNCs
could be attributed to the structure of the AgNCs with the
Apt-G sequence being more compact. The zeta potential was
also used to evaluate the stability of the nanoparticles. As
shown in Figure S2, hybridization with the Apt-G sequence of
N4-AgNCs caused the zeta potential to become more positive,
suggesting that a large amount of negatively charged DNA on
the surface of AgNCs increases stability.
Since the population of ﬂuorophores was deeply dependent
on the DNA/Ag+ molar ratio,39 this factor was investigated
using diﬀerent concentrations of Ag+ (Figure S3). The results
showed that the ﬂuorescence intensity of the N4-AgNCs/Apt-
G was mostly dependent on the ratio of DNA to Ag+. The
AgNCs showed almost the same emission wavelengths at 520
nm, with the highest emission at the molar ratio of 8. However,
at molar ratios greater than 8, the ﬂuorescence decreased as the
concentration of Ag+ increased, possibly due to the formation
of plasmonic nanoparticles caused by the excess Ag+.17
Furthermore, the ﬂuorescence stability of AgNCs was
evaluated by keeping the N4-AgNCs/Apt-G in the dark at
room temperature for several months. As shown in Figure S4,
the ﬂuorescence of the prepared AgNCs did not decrease
signiﬁcantly in the ﬁrst two months and retained approximately
50% ﬂuorescence after as long as four months. The above
results indicated that the synthesized AgNCs had good optical
stability and were, thus, suitable for applications in
ﬂuorescence sensing.

The incorporation of N4-AgNCs/Apt-G into the PLA
nanoﬁlm was achieved by electrospinning. In order to keep
the ﬁbers thin and homogenous while maintaining good
antimicrobial activity, 100 g L−1 PLA was tested to be the most
suitable concentration at which PLA nanoﬁlms were uniform
and bead-free. The smallest nanoﬁbers were obtained at the
trichloromethane/DMF ratio of 9:1, and the smallest nano-
ﬁbers were obtained, while ratios higher or lower than that
were found to be unsuitable for ﬁber production. The
concentration of N4-AgNCs/Apt-G aﬀected not only the
morphology but also the antimicrobial activities of the
nanoﬁlm. As shown in Figure S5, the obtained PLA/N4-
AgNC/Apt-G nanoﬁlm exhibited a diameter range of about
200−600 nm. Under a ﬂuorescence microscope, the green
ﬂuorescence of the N4-AgNCs/Apt-G was observed during the
electrospinning, as shown by the red arrow in Figure S6. The
SEM−EDS image in Figure 4a shows that the distribution of
silver was uniform on the surface of the PLA/N4-AgNC/Apt-G
nanoﬁlm. While the main constituent of PLA is carbon, the
characteristic constituents of N4-AgNCs/Apt-G, namely,
nitrogen, phosphorus, and silver, are determined to constitute

an antimicrobial agent.

Finally, the eﬃciency of this nanoﬁlm was evaluated via milk
samples as follows. The samples were analyzed without
pretreatment. The PLA/N4-AgNC/Apt-G aptasensor nanoﬁlm
was applied to the analysis of the S. aureus residue to evaluate
its practicality, and recovery studies were carried out by spiking
various amounts of S. aureus into the samples. The calculated
mean S. aureus concentrations for each sample are listed in
Table 1. In the unspiked milk samples, S. aureus was not
detected in the unspiked milk samples. In the spiked milk
samples, recovery rates ranging from 88% to 115% were
obtained, thus indicating the validity of the PLA/N4-AgNC/
Apt-G aptasensor for the determination of S. aureus in practical
samples. These results verify the applicability of the PLA/N4-
AgNC/Apt-G aptasensor for the accurate detection of S. aureus
residues in practical samples. Furthermore, the comparative
advantages of this method over other metal NC-based
ﬂuorescence methods are presented in Table S2. In summary,
the advantages of AgNCs include easy synthesis, wide
tunability, bright ﬂuorescence, and excellent photostability
and biocompatibility. Although the method we developed is
not the ﬁrst choice for detecting the low concentration of
bacteria, it does achieve visualization while also being
antibacterial, which are the advantages not found in other
metal nanomaterials.
4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, in this study, the optical and antibacterial
advantages of AgNCs were optimized to successfully achieve a
novel strategy for the visual detection and eﬀective elimination
of bacteria, thus providing a feasible method for further
exploring the properties and expanding the applications of
DNA-AgNCs. In particular, the DNA-AgNCs have shown
enhanced ﬂuorescent activity and selectivity toward the target

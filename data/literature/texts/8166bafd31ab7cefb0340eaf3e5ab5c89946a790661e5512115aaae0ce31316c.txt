CONSPECTUS: Nanozymes, nanomaterials with enzyme-like
activities with high structural stability, adjustable catalytic activity,
functional diversity, recyclability, and feasibility in large-scale
preparation, have become a hot spot in the ﬁeld of artiﬁcial
enzymes in recent years and are expected to become potential
surrogates and competitors for natural enzymes in practical
applications. With the development of in-depth research and a
wide range of application requirements, creating nanozymes with
catalytic performance comparable to or even surpassing that of
natural enzymes has been the key research topic in this ﬁeld. Most
of the nanozymes reported in the past were obtained based on
random synthesis and screening, for which the catalytic eﬃciency is
far inferior to that of natural enzymes. Natural enzymes that have
evolved over hundreds of millions of years have developed a lot of high-eﬃciency catalysis know-how hidden in their structural
features. To create highly active nanozymes, we assumed that there is a general structure−activity relationship between nanozymes
and natural enzymes and proposed the nanozyme optimization strategy by grafting the catalytic principles of natural enzymes into
the rational design of nanozymes. On the basis of this bioinspired strategy, a series of nanozymes that exhibit similar catalytic
activities that are closer to or even beyond those of natural enzymes have been successfully synthesized. By now, rationally designed
high-activity bioinspired nanozymes have become a hot topic in the current research on nanozymes.
In this Account, we focus on recent representative research progress in the systemic design and construction of bioinspired
nanozymes and are devoted to introducing strategic concepts in the bioinspired optimization of nanozymes. We show that the de
novo design of nanozymes by simulating the amino acid microenvironment and using metal-free architecture and the coordination
structure of metal active sites in natural enzymes is an eﬀective strategy for signiﬁcantly improving the catalytic performance of
nanozymes. A future perspective of the challenges and countermeasures of bioinspired nanozymes is proposed on the basis of these
achievements. We hope that the biologically inspired perception will arouse widespread interest in fundamental research and
practical applications as well as provide inspiration for the rational design of nanozymes.
1. INTRODUCTION
Orderly chemical reactions in organisms rely on the eﬃcient
and speciﬁc catalysis of natural enzymes. The important
biological functions and excellent catalytic ability of natural
enzymes endow them with great application requirements in
the ﬁelds of biomedicine and industrial catalysis. However, the
inherent drawbacks of natural enzymes, such as the easy loss of
activity due to molecular denaturation or subunit depolyme-
rization and diﬃculties in puriﬁcation, storage, and recycling,
greatly limit their practical applications. To meet the current
huge application needs, artiﬁcial enzymes with more stable
structures and lower costs have been developed as substitutes
for natural enzymes in the last half century. Nanozymes, which
are nanomaterials with intrinsic enzyme-like activities, have
become the most popular and promising artiﬁcial enzymes in
the past decade owing to their unique advantages over natural
enzymes and conventional artiﬁcial enzymes, such as stable
structures, high-eﬃciencies, adjustable activities, low costs, and
large-scale preparation and recyclability.1,2 Given these
advantages, nanozymes are committed to replacing and
expanding the applications of natural enzymes in catalysis-
related ﬁelds. To achieve this goal, tremendous eﬀort has been
expended on struggling random synthesis and batch screening
of highly active nanozymes but with little success. The catalytic
activity and selectivity of most nanozymes are still diﬃcult to
reach with respect to the level of natural enzymes.
Compared with passive and aimless screening, the strategy
of actively and rationally designing nanozymes inspired by the
structural characteristics of natural enzymes proposed in recent

are able to be constructed rationally, it is a very attractive idea

the metal-free architecture, and the coordination structure of
the active center and the attached cofactors. Importantly, we
discuss the conceptual framework of bioinspired nanozymes
and propose some strategies worth trying in the future, with
the hope of communicating our ideas to interested researchers.
2. BIOINSPIRED NANOZYMES WITH ACTIVE SITES IN
TAILORED AMINO ACID MICROENVIRONMENTS
Natural enzymes eﬀectively catalyze the chemical reactions of
substrates with high activity and selectivity. The structure and
composition of the natural enzyme binding pocket near the
active center provide a favorable hydrophobic spatial micro-
environment, which is particularly important for high catalytic
eﬃciency.3,4 In particular, some amino acids in the binding
pocket are located near the catalytically active sites of natural
enzymes, which can help enrich, organize, and activate
substrates through noncovalent interactions.5−7 Inspired by
these unique features, we envisioned that by installing speciﬁc
amino acids and constructing favorable spatial structures,
customizing the catalytic microenvironment around the active
sites may be a promising strategy for making nanozymes that
more closely mimic natural enzymes.
Fe3O4 nanoparticles are one of the most classic nanozymes
and were ﬁrst reported by our group in 2007 to exhibit
peroxidase-like activity.8 Subsequent studies reported that
Fe3O4 nanoparticles also exhibit catalase-like activity under
neutral or alkaline conditions.9 The reason that Fe3O4
nanomaterials possess peroxidase-like activity is that there is
a large amount of trivalent iron and divalent iron available on
the surface,10,11 which may be catalyzed in a manner similar to
that of the heme group in the active site of horseradish
peroxidase (HRP).8,12,13 Catalytic kinetic analysis shows that
the catalytic eﬃciency (kcat) of Fe3O4 against the substrate is
comparable to HRP, but the ability to bind to the substrate
(with a much higher KM) is much lower than HRP.8 The lower
aﬃnity of Fe3O4 nanozymes for substrates is attributed to the
lack of the amino acid environment like that in the catalytic
center of HRP.13 In the active center of HRP, there are two
histidine residues, His42 and His170, located at the distal and
proximal ends of the porphyrin iron atom, respectively (Figure
1A,B).13 Among them, the terminal imidazole of the His42
residue assists H2O2 to enter the active site cavity through the
H-bond interaction and the formation of the initial compound
I (Figure 1C), which helps to ﬁx H2O2 in the proper position,
facilitate heterolytic cleavage of the O−O bond of peroxide to
form Fe4+O, and stabilize higher oxidation states of the
heme iron during the catalytic cycle.14−16 Therefore, we were
inspired by this to covalently modify histidine residues on the
surface of Fe3O4 to make them H2O2 binding sites to improve
the substrate aﬃnity of Fe3O4 nanozymes (Figure 1D).13
As expected, the Michaelis−Menten steady-state kinetics
results show that Fe3O4 modiﬁed with histidine (His-Fe3O4)
exhibited a signiﬁcantly higher aﬃnity for H2O2 than
unmodiﬁed Fe3O4 (Naked-Fe3O4), as shown by a 10 times
lower KM. Moreover, the KM value of His-Fe3O4 was also
signiﬁcantly lower than that of Fe3O4 modiﬁed with alanine
(Ala-Fe3O4) with methyl as the side chain only, which
indicates that the enhancement of its aﬃnity for H2O2
comes from the imidazole side chain in histidine instead of
the amino or carboxyl group. In fact, in addition to alanine
modiﬁcation, other standard amino acids may also aﬀect the
KM of Fe3O4 to H2O2, but they were far less impressive than
histidine modiﬁcation. With the increase in aﬃnity, His-Fe3O4
exhibited an ultrahigh catalytic eﬃciency (kcat/KM) that is 7.1
times higher than that of Ala-Fe3O4 and 20.8 times higher than
that of naked-Fe3O4. However, histidine modiﬁcation did not
help to improve the aﬃnity of Fe3O4 for 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethyl-
benzidine (TMB) substrates and even increased the KM value
for TMB substrates. This may be due to the electrostatic

The mechanism analysis shows that the hydrogen bond
formed between histidine and H2O2 (initial state) during the
catalysis process not only weakened the strength of the O−H
bond but also caused O to have more negative charges (Figure
1E). The former process was conducive to splitting the O−O
bond of H2O2, while the latter process enhanced its adsorption
onto the Fe3O4 nanozyme (ﬁnal state). Therefore, the histidine
modiﬁed on the surface played a role similar to that of His42 in
the active site of HRP, which greatly improved the enzymatic
performance of the Fe3O4 nanozyme.
Likewise, inspired by the structure of the active site and the
electron-transfer pathway of laccase, Wang et al. prepared a
nanozyme (denoted as CH-Cu) with laccase-like activity by
coordinating Cu+/Cu2+ with a cysteine (Cys)-histidine (His)
dipeptide.17 The CH-Cu nanozymes had KM and Vmax values
that were similar to and higher than those of laccase,
respectively, at the same mass concentration and exhibited
excellent substrate universality and recyclability. Also, Wu et al.
prepared a highly eﬃcient peroxidase-like nanozyme (SWNT-
NH2 @ hemin) by loading heme on amino-modiﬁed single-
walled carbon nanotubes (SWNT-NH2).18 The positively
charged −NH2 group in SWNT played a role similar to that of
arginine in HRP, which enhanced the peroxidase-like activity
of heme through π−π interactions, stabilized the intermediate
during the catalysis process, and promoted the cleavage of the
O−O bond.
More recently, inspired by the structure of the active center
of HRP, Xin et al. designed a novel histidine-functionalized
graphene quantum dot (His-GQD)/heme complex as a
peroxidase mimic.19 The heme portion, histidine functional
group, and hydrophobic base surface of GQD contained in this
bioinspired nanozyme simulated the heme active site, histidine
ligand, and hydrophobic substrate binding site of HRP,
respectively. This bioinspired nanozyme exhibited catalytic
activity very close to that of HRP and has a ping-pang catalytic
mechanism similar to that of HRP.
In addition to the above-mentioned design of oxidoreduc-
tase-like nanozymes, bioinspired nanozymes with hydrolase or
lyase activity can also be created by simulating the amino acid
microenvironment of metalloenzymes. Inspired by the
interaction of zinc ions and amino acids in the structure of
zinc ﬁnger hydrolases, a metallo-nanozyme was constructed by
Han et al. through amino acid coordinated self-assembly by
using histidine derivatives and Zn2+ ions as the building
blocks.20 In catalyzing the hydrolysis of p-nitrophenyl acetate
(p-NPA), the obtained bioinspired nanozyme exhibited high,
stable activity comparable to that of natural lipase. Similarly,
inspired by the interaction between two Zn2+ ions and four
histidines in organophosphate hydrolase (OPH), a zeolite
imidazolate framework-90 (ZIF-90) nanozyme was synthesized
by Fu et al. through the self-assembly of imidazole and Zn2+
ions.21 With zinc as the active center and imidazole as the
coordinating ligand, the obtained ZIF-90 nanozyme eﬀectively
degraded the organophosphorus nerve agent methyl parathion
(MP) in an OPH-like manner. In another study, Chen et al.
found that zeolitic imidazole framework-8 (ZIF-8) has a
geometric structure similar to the active center of human
carbonic anhydrase II (hCAII).22 With the structural similarity
to hCAII, ZIF-8 eﬀectively catalyzed CO2 hydration and the
hydrolysis of p-NPA. The hCAII-like activity of ZIN-8 comes
from the unsaturated coordination microenvironment created
by 2-methylimidazolate (2-mIM) ligands for Zn2+. Also,
inspired by the geometric structure of hCAII, Liang et al.
constructed a series of Zn-triazole coordination polymers
(ZnTazs) with similar coordination structures analogous to the
active center of natural hCAII.23 These ZnTazs nanozymes
exhibited inherently mimetic function with natural hCAII to
catalyze the hydrolysis of p-NPA. However, the catalytic
activity of ZnTazs nanozymes is inferior to that of natural
enzymes, and their intrinsic catalytic mechanism still needs to
be elucidated.
The rational use of chiral amino acids as the framework
motifs may also enable nanozymes with isomerase-like activity.
Li et al. reported a chiral cysteine-derived carbon dot (CD)
that could mimic topoisomerase I (Topo I) to enantiose-
lectively mediate the topological rearrangement of supercoiled
DNA.24 The
L-CDs and
D-CDs are synthesized via a
hydrothermal method using L-cysteine and D-cysteine as the
carbon sources, respectively.
D-CDs were inserted and
combined with the DNA double helix more ﬁrmly than was
L-CD. The enantioselectivity origin of chiral CDs toward DNA
was derived from the diﬀerence in the aﬃnity of chiral cysteine
to DNA. Intercalated CDs as Topo I did catalyzed the
generation of hydroxyl radicals to cut the phosphate backbone
in one strand of the DNA double helix, leading to the
topological rearrangement of supercoiled DNA.
These proof-of-concept studies conﬁrmed that it is a feasible
and eﬀective strategy to create a favorable catalytic micro-
environment for nanozymes by learning from the role of amino
acids in the active sites of natural enzymes.
3. BIOINSPIRED NANOZYMES WITH METAL-FREE
ACTIVE SITES
Most of the nanozymes reported at present are metal or metal-
containing oxide and sulﬁde nanomaterials, which transfer
electrons through variable valence metals to perform enzyme-
like catalysis.2,10 However, as far as natural enzymes are
concerned, although some enzymes use metal ions as cofactors,
most other enzymes do not require metal elements to express
full enzymatic activity.25,26 The nonmetallic elements in the
amino acids are combined in an orderly combination at the
active site of an enzyme to perform a variety of catalysis.27−30
This informed us that using only the basic nonmetallic
elements (C, O, N, P, S, etc.) as the elementary building blocks
in synthesis may be a feasible strategy for economically
creating green nanozymes.
To verify the feasibility of our biomimetic strategy,
considering that proteins use carbon chains as the elementary
skeleton, we choose carbon-based materials as the basic
framework for fabricating bioinspired nanozymes. Moreover,
carbon-based nanomaterials have also been widely found to
have enzyme-like activities, such as carbon nanotubes,31 carbon
nanodots,32 carbon nitride,33 graphene,34 fullerene,35 and other
forms of carbon nanomaterials.36 However, most carbon-based
nanomaterials generally have limited catalytic types with no or
weak enzyme-like activities, such as peroxidase- and catalase-
like activities.2,12,37−39 A proven eﬀective strategy for the
development of high-eﬃciency carbon-based nanozymes is
heteroatomic doping, to which much eﬀort has been devoted
by our laboratory.
Along with carbon, nitrogen is also one of the most critical
elements of enzymes and cofactors, which play important roles

reactions similar to the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR)
under acidic conditions, while catalase- and SOD-like activities
tend to act on reversible reactions similar to the oxygen
evolution reaction (OER) under basic conditions. Both
nitrogen doping and a graphitic structure were required to
achieve the above enzyme-like activities. The four types of
enzymatic activities of N-PCNSs with a high nitrogen doping

a low nitrogen doping level (N-PCNSs-5) or without nitrogen
doping (PCNSs) (Figure 2C). Further analysis showed that a
N-heterocycle was formed by the nitrogen dopant in the
graphitized carbon nanospheres, which acted in nanozymes
similar to the active sites in proteic enzymes. Intriguingly, after
entering the acidic compartment of tumor cells, N-PCNSs
eﬀectively implemented this catalysis and inhibited tumor
growth by selectively regulating the generation of large
amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS).
Studies have shown that the double doping of heteroatoms
increases electron transfer in the reaction process catalyzed by
carbon nanomaterials compared with the single doping of
heteroatoms.45,46 Therefore, we tried to further dope boron
(B), phosphorus (P), and sulfur (S) into N-doped carbon
nanomaterials to create an upgraded version of metal-free
nanozymes with higher catalytic performance.47 Prior to this,
we ﬁrst used density functional analysis (DFT) calculations to
predict the peroxidase-like activities of N, B, P, and S single-
doped and B−N, P−N, and S−N double-doped carbon
nanomaterials (graphene) (Figure 3A). Our calculation results
show that P−N graphene had the highest peroxidase-like
activity with the lowest energy barrier of 10.9 kcal mol−1,
which was about 40% and 48% lower than for N and P single-
doped coronene, respectively (Figure 3B). The calculated
reaction energy curve corresponding to the peroxidase activity
of P- and P−N-coronene is shown in Figure 3C. The activation
of H2O2 was a step that determines the reaction rate of P- and
P−N-coronene. The single O-adatom was the key active
intermediate responsible for the peroxidase-like activity, which
could capture two H atoms from two TMB molecules. It is
worth noting that although the single O-adatom was located at
heteroatom P in P-coronene, it was located at the C atom
adjacent to the P atom in P−N-graphene (Figure 3C). The P
atom formed a hump on the graphene conjugated plane due to
its larger atomic radius, which caused the distortion of adjacent
sp2 C and increased its π-orbital axis vector (POAV) value.
The sp2 C with a larger POAV value has a higher reactivity.
The doped P atoms in P−N graphene lowered the energy
barrier by enhancing the radical properties of adjacent sp2 C.

Likewise, Zhang et al. reported a bifunctional metal-free
nanozyme of modiﬁed graphite carbonitride (AKCN) via the
one-pot thermal polycondensation of melamine, modiﬁed by

In addition to carbon-based materials, metal-free nanozymes
have also been synthesized using polymers and peptides. Yin et
al. developed a metal-free polymer nanozyme with peroxidase-
like activity through the one-pot covalent reaction of
hyperbranched polyethylenimine (PEI) and redox dihydroxy-
benzaldehyde (DHB), which could be speciﬁcally inhibited by
negatively charged heparin.50 More recently, Liu et al.

which had a catalytic function similar to that of heme-
dependent peroxidase that eﬀectively catalyzed the H2O2
reduction reaction through the formation of reactive ternary
complex intermediates.51 These studies, together with our
work, have conﬁrmed the feasibility of the biomimetic
synthesis of metal-free nanozymes, opening up novel avenues
for the development of green economic nanozymes.
4. BIOINSPIRED NANOZYMES WITH ACTIVE SITES
FOLLOWING NATURAL COORDINATION
CHEMISTRY PRINCIPLES
Metals exist as active sites in many nanozymes. However,
although the number of metal sites exposed on the surface is
far more than that of natural enzymes, the overall catalytic
eﬃciency of nanozymes is lower than that of natural enzymes.
This phenomenon is attributed to the fact that the metal ions
in natural enzymes are precisely regulated by the ﬁne
coordination structure, while the random, rough structure of
nanozymes limits the utilization of its metal sites. In the active
center of metalloenzymes, metal ions cofactors are usually
coordinated with donor groups such as nitrogen, oxygen, and
sulfur belonging to the side chains of amino acid residues in
the protein.52 The amino acids in the active site of
metalloenzymes coordinate with the metal atom and deﬁne
the substrate orientation through noncovalent bonds, such as
hydrophobic interactions, van der Waals forces, hydrogen
bonds, and electrostatic interactions.53 In addition to the
donor groups provided by amino acid residues, many organic
cofactors function as ligands, the most famous of which is the
tetradentate N4 macrocyclic ligand embedded in the heme
protein.54 The formation of a suitable coordination structure is
crucial for metal ion cofactors as the catalytic group of the
enzyme to participate in the catalytic reaction and transfer
electrons. Inspired by this, we envisaged that it should be an
eﬀective strategy for improving the catalytic performance of
nanozymes by designing active structures following the
coordination chemistry theory of natural enzymes.
With the development of nanotechnology, it is no longer a
fantasy to design and synthesize various nanomaterials with
speciﬁc spatial coordination structures at will. In particular,
single-atom catalysts (SAC) containing atomically dispersed
metal active sites with the largest metal atom utilization, high
activity, and selectivity have recently become a new frontier in
catalysis ﬁeld.55−57 Moreover, the M−N−C (M = metal, Fe,
Co, Mn, etc.) SAC derived from the transition-metal-
containing macrocycle has an M−Nx site similar to that of
natural metalloenzymes.58,59 Therefore, we hypothesize that

Studies have shown that Zn-substituted HRP eﬀectively
mediated the activation of H2O2 through the formation of a
porphyrin π-cation radical.60−62 On the basis of this intuition,
we synthesized a highly active peroxidase-like nanozyme
containing zinc porphyrin structure (denoted as PMCS) by
using a metal organic framework (MOF) ZIF-8 as a precursor
via a mesoporous silica (mSiO2)-protected pyrolysis strategy
(Figure 4A).63 As expected, PMCS exhibited signiﬁcant
peroxidase-like activity, with an aﬃnity for the H2O2 and
TMB substrates comparable to that of the natural HRP. The
catalytic activity of PMCS was revealed to be derived from the
atomically dispersed active single Zn sites. The pyrolysis
temperature during material synthesis and the coordination
number of Zn sites had an important inﬂuence on the
enzymatic activity of PMCS. As the carbonization temperature
increased, the structure of N coordinated with Zn gradually
transformed from a regular tetrahedron to a plane. PMCS
showed the best peroxidase-like activity when the carbon-
ization temperature was around 800 °C, and a carbonization
temperature higher or lower than 800 °C resulted in
insuﬃcient defect sites and low peroxidase-like activity. DFT
analysis showed that the Zn site in PMCS was active only in
the unsaturated Zn−N4 coordination structure, while pyrrole
N and pyridine N as the ﬁfth ligand N species negatively
aﬀected the activation of H2O2. Then, a possible catalytic
mechanism was proposed by the DFT calculation (Figure 4B).
First, H2O2 molecules were adsorbed on the Zn−N4 active
sites in PMCS (i), with an adsorption energy of −0.45 eV (ii).
The activated H2O2 molecule easily dissociated to 2OH*
through a homolytic pathway (iii). The hydroxyl group then
desorbed from the single zinc site, resulting in the formation of
active hydroxyl groups and OH* (iv). The reaction energy
from step (i) to step (iv) was 0.24 eV, which was easily
overcome at room temperature. Finally, Zn−OH reacted with
the substrate under acidic conditions and returned to its initial
state (i). The Zn−N4 site of PMCS eﬀectively induced the
generation of ·OH by catalyzing the decomposition of H2O2, of
which the structure and catalytic mechanism were very similar
to those of Zn-substituted HRP.61,63 Due to its excellent
peroxidase-like activity, PMCS exhibited an enhanced in vitro
antibacterial eﬀect with an inhibition rate of 99.87% against
Pseudomonas aeruginosa and signiﬁcantly promoted wound
healing.

As an important component of many natural enzymes, the P
species is crucial to assisting electron tunneling from the
substrate to the active center of the enzyme. In the previous
work, we proved that the introduction of P and N double
doping47 and Fe−N4 coordination sites66 in the carbon-based
nanostructure can eﬀectively enhance the peroxidase-like
performance. In a recent study, by precisely coordinating
phosphorus (P) and nitrogen (N) to modulate the monatomic
iron (Fe) active center, we developed an atomically engineered
FeN3P single-atom peroxidase-like nanozyme (FeN3P-SA-
zyme) (Figure 6A).68 To synthesize FeN3P-SAzyme, we ﬁrst
prepared ZIF-8 by a solvothermal method as a precursor of
carbon and nitrogen. Then, Fe ions and the P precursor
poly(cyclotriphospazeneco-4,4′-diaminodiphenyl ether)
(PZM) monomer are used to coat the surface of ZIF-8 by
polymerization to obtain the Fe/ZIF-8@PZM core−shell
composite. FeN3P-SAzyme was ﬁnally obtained by pyrolyzing
Fe/ZIF-8@PZM under ﬂowing nitrogen at 950 °C for 3 h.
FeN3P-SAzyme presents a polyhedral shape with surface
shrinkage, with Fe, C, N, and P elements evenly distributed
throughout the whole structure. The atomic structure analysis
results show that the three coordination shells of Fe−N/O,
Fe−P, and Fe−C in the FeN3P-SAzyme structure have
coordination numbers of 3.6, 1.1, and 4.3, respectively,
indicating a porphyrin-based FeN3PC4 moiety with an axial
oxygen ligand. This FeN3PC4 moiety structure was supported
by the combined result of Fe and P K-edge EXAFS, which
indicated that N was substituted by P atoms around the Fe
center. The synthesized FeN3P-SAzyme possesses a chemical
coordination environment similar to that of HRP and can
eﬃciently catalyze the oxidation and color reaction of the
substrates (for example, TMB, diazoaminobenzene (DAB),
and o-phenylenediamine (OPD)) of HRP in the presence of
H2O2. Compared with the most widely used Fe3O4 nanozyme
(9.12 U mg−1) or FeN4-SAzyme (33.8 U mg−1) without P
coordination, FeN3P-SAzyme (316 U mg−1) has higher
catalytic activity (Figure 6B). Importantly, the catalytic
eﬃciency (kcat/KM = 1.40 × 108 M−1 min−1) and selectivity
(KM = 2.06 × 10−3 mM) of FeN3P-SAzyme catalyzing the
oxidation of TMB are comparable to those of natural HRP
(kcat/KM = 1.15 × 107 M−1 min−1, KM = 5.55 mM). Consistent
with the experimental results, the DFT analysis results showed
that the higher peroxidase-like activity of FeN3P-SAzyme was
attributed to the lower barriers to the formation of O and OH
(<0.5 eV), while FeN4-SAzyme and Fe3O4(111) hardly
perform the catalytic process under acidic conditions (Figure
6C). The active center structure of FeN3P-SAzyme has single-
atom Fe sites with less positive charge and the P atoms as an
electron donor, which synergistically brings about a lower
barrier to surface O formation and leads to rapid kinetics. For
FeN4-SAzyme, the OH species on the surface are not easily
converted to H2O molecules (1.05 eV), which adhere to the
active center of Fe atoms, thereby inhibiting the entire catalytic
process. For Fe3O4(111), the OH species on the surface can be
instantly converted to H2O molecules (0.11 eV), which makes
it diﬃcult to accomplish the entire catalytic process. On the
basis of high peroxidase-like activity, FeN4-SAzyme exhibited
an eﬀective therapeutic eﬀect of inhibiting tumors by catalyzing
the formation of abundant oxidative species in the acidic
environment of tumors.
Until now, many nanozymes with bioinspired metal−
nitrogen coordination structures such as Fe−N,69−72 Cu−
N,73,74 Zn−N,73 Mn−N,75 and Ni−N76 have been developed
following our work to mimic the structure and activity of
oxidase, peroxidase, catalase, and superoxide dismutase.
Therefore, we hypothesize that by following the chemical
coordination principles of natural enzymes it is possible to
construct a series of bioinspired nanozymes with adjustable
enzyme-like activities.

5. CONCLUSIONS AND PERSPECTIVE
Huge progress in the synthesis and optimization of nanozymes
has been achieved in the past 15 years, with more than 1000
nanozymes being developed to mimic the activities of
oxidoreductase, hydrolase, lyase, and isomerase. Along with
the continuous discovery of new-type enzyme-like materials,
the theoretical mechanism, basic concepts, catalytic kinetics,
and measurement standards of nanozymes have also been
gradually established. On this basis, how to create nanozymes
with catalytic performance comparable to or even higher than
that of natural enzymes is the most concerning issue at the
moment. The approach of grafting the structural properties of
natural enzymes into the rational design of nanozymes
developed in recent years is one of the most promising
strategies for optimizing catalytic performance. In this
Account, we introduce the currently proven bioinspired
strategies for nanozymes, covering the omnidirectional
simulation of natural enzymes from an amino acid micro-
environment to metal-free architecture to a metal coordination
structure. The advances introduced are strong evidence
supporting the sharing of universal structure−activity relation-
ships between nanozymes and natural enzymes and conﬁrming
the feasibility of constructing bioinspired nanozymes.
Although many bioinspired nanozymes have been developed
in recent years, they still face various challenges to be solved as
an emerging research hot spot. Next, based on our knowledge,
we discuss the current problems that bionic nanozymes need
to solve and give some possible solutions.
1. Improving the speciﬁcity of catalysis. At present, most if
not all bioinspired nanozymes have multienzyme activity
that catalyzes many types of chemical reactions, which is
exactly what natural enzymes try to avoid. Although
some enzyme-like activities are able to be cascaded to
catalyze, the uncontrolled multienzyme activity may
cause unwanted byproducts during the catalysis process
and may compromise the desired activity. The reason for
this issue is that the unharnessed active sites of
nanozymes directly exposed on the surface lack a ﬁne
structure regulation like that of natural enzymes. For
example, the active site of many nanozymes mimics the
porphyrin structure. However, although porphyrins exist
as active centers in many natural enzymes such as
peroxidase, oxidase, and catalase, they play completely
diﬀerent roles in diﬀerent enzymes due to their diverse
catalytic microenvironments. The regulation of catalytic
speciﬁcity in natural enzymes may be based on two
aspects. On the one hand, the highly speciﬁc binding to
the substrate makes the active site contact only a single
substrate and thus catalyze only a speciﬁc reaction. On
the other hand, the coordination environment of the
active site precisely regulates the direction of electron
transfer so that only speciﬁc types of reactions are
allowed. For example, the diﬀerence in the micro-
environment of iron porphyrin in peroxidase and
catalase makes it catalyze a completely diﬀerent reaction
of H2O2. For all enzymes, the shape of the active site is
crucial. Therefore, to create a bioinspired nanozyme
with high speciﬁc activity, we may start with promoting
the speciﬁc binding to the substrate and controlling the
electron transfer pathway of the active site. To achieve
this, the design of nanozymes in the future requires in-
depth imitation on the basis of understanding the
structure−activity relationship of natural enzymes in
terms of substrate recognition and electron transfer.
2. Unearthing the catalytic potential of nonmetal elements.
Although metal ions exist as core catalytic sites in some
metalloenzymes, the key to eﬃcient and speciﬁc catalysis
of all enzymes lies in their support framework and
catalytic microenvironment composed of nonmetallic
elements. Moreover, due to the limitation of the variable
valence characteristics, the metal ions in natural enzymes
mediate only redox reactions in most cases. Most of the
current research focuses on exploring and maximizing
the catalytic potential of metal atoms in nanozymes. This
situation makes nanozymes only able to imitate the
activities of some metalloenzymes, most of which are
redox enzymes. So far, in addition to redox nanozymes,
only a few nanozymes have been reported to have
hydrolase-, hydrolyase-, or isomerase-like activity, while
no nanozymes with synthetase- or transferase-like
activity have been reported. To break through the
bottleneck of nanozymes in catalytic type, it is essential
to reveal the role of nonmetallic elements in enzymatic
catalysis. Although some metal-free nanozymes have
been developed, it is far from enough for us to
understand the structure−activity relationship and
carry out rational design. In the current rapid develop-
ment of artiﬁcial intelligence, based on the big data
analysis of the nonmetallic elements in natural enzymes,
it may be an optional strategy to use computer
simulation to assist with the design of metal-free
bioinspired nanozymes.
3. Improving the ﬂexibility and variability of the active
sites. Natural enzymes folded by a ﬂexible polypeptide
have a variable backbone and active sites with moderate
tension, which is suﬃcient to spatially recognize and
bind substrates and release products in an orderly
manner. To obtain adequate stability, natural enzymes
consume most of the amino acid motifs to construct the
protein backbone to support the active site. In contrast,
nanozymes mostly have a solidiﬁed backbone and ﬁxed
active sites, which recognize and catalyze substrates on
rigid surfaces in a random manner. The solid structure
makes nanozymes more advantageous than natural
enzymes in terms of stability, but at the expense of
greatly compromising catalytic eﬃciency and substrate
selectivity. Therefore, to create bioinspired nanozymes
with high stability, catalytic eﬃciency, and substrate
selectivity, it is necessary to give active sites moderate
ﬂexibility on the basis of maintaining the rigidity of the
frame, with which it can undergo orderly conformational
changes to serve the catalysis of substrates more
eﬀectively.
4. Unveiling the key structure−activity relationship of
nanozymes. The designs of bioinspired nanozymes
introduced here are based on the general structure−
activity relationship between nanozymes and natural
enzymes. However, most nanozymes are composed of
inorganic materials, and their catalytic mechanism is
bound to have many diﬀerences from natural enzymes
composed of organic proteins. In addition to grafting the
structure of natural enzymes, it is necessary to clarify the
catalytic mechanism of nanozymes and discover the key
structure−activity relationships that determine enzyme-
like activity. Many studies have shown that the enzyme-

like activity of nanozymes is closely related to factors
such as size, morphology, composition, crystal plane, and
surface modiﬁcation. However, a systematic theoretical
system that can accurately guide the design of
nanozymes has not been developed. We hypothesize
that there may be some unrevealed key sites that directly
determine the activity of nanozymes. Any factor that
aﬀects these key sites may aﬀect the catalytic perform-
ance of nanozymes. Therefore, future research should
focus on ﬁnding out the key structure−activity relation-
ships so that the catalytic mechanism of nanozymes can
be understood on that basis and that advanced
nanozymes can be rationally designed.
5. Evaluating biosafety in depth. Compared with natural
enzymes, the biological safety of nanozymes composed
of inorganic materials is still an important issue that
needs careful consideration. Nanozymes may cause
safety hazards due to the following factors. First, many
nanozymes are stable structures built from inorganic
elements, of which the in vivo biodegradability and
metabolizable pathways are mostly unknown. Second,
although most nanozymes are composed of elements
inherent in organisms, mainly including C, N, O, P, Fe,
Zn, etc., they may disrupt the element metabolism in
organisms. Third, some nanozymes have multiple
enzyme-like activities, which may cause catalytic damage
to cells if they are not eﬀectively controlled. In general,
for bioinspired nanozymes to truly beneﬁt the clinic, in-
depth biosafety and biodegradability research is
necessary.
Taken together, we present our progress and philosophy in
the research of bioinspired nanozymes in this Account. We
believe that the rational design of nanozymes based on the
simulation of the structural characteristics of natural enzymes is
a promising strategy. If we fully discover and utilize the secrets
of high-eﬃciency catalysis hidden in natural enzymes, then we
will be able to construct ideal nanozymes as we wish. Future
research needs to integrate the wisdom of researchers from
multiple disciplines to deeply address the real challenge in the
topic of bioinspired nanozymes. We anticipate that bioinspired
nanozymes will become a highlight of future research, and we
hope that this Account will provide inspiration and attract
attention for the rational design of bioinspired nanozymes.

Biographies
Ruofei Zhang is currently a Ph.D. candidate at the Institute of
Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) under the
supervision of Prof. Xiyun Yan and Prof. Kelong Fan. He received
his B.S. degree from the Wuhan University in China in 2016. His
research focuses on the catalytic mechanism and application of
nanozymes.
Xiyun Yan obtained her B.S. degree in 1983 from the Henan Medical
College and then studied cell biology in Professor Shizhang Bei’s
laboratory at the Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of
Sciences (CAS) until leaving for Germany in 1989. After receiving her
medical doctor’s degree from Heidelberg University in Germany, she
joined the Memorial Sloan-Kettering Institute in New York as a
postdoctoral research fellow. In 1997, Dr. Yan returned to China
through her selection in the Hundred Talents Program by CAS. She
has since studied tumor biology, ﬁnding novel targets and developing
new methods for tumor diagnosis and therapy. Her laboratory
reported the ﬁrst nanozyme in 2007 and pioneered the development
of nanozyme research. Her work has been well recognized and
respected internationally through honors such as the National Prize
for Natural Science and the Atlas Award by Elsevier.
Kelong Fan received his Ph.D. degree in cell biology from the
Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences in 2014. After
this, he stayed to pursue 3 years of postdoctoral training and 2 years
of associate professor work experience before attaining a full professor
position in 2019. He is interested in exploring the novel functions and
applications of nanozymes in biomedicine, with the top priority of
designing a functional nanozyme by learning from nature and
developing novel strategies for disease theranostics. He now serves as
an associate editor of Exploration and as a guest editor of Frontiers in
Chemistry.

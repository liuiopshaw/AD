Precise Surface Engineering of Metal Nanoclusters: Ligand
Programming for Functionality Design
Zhucheng Yang, Yifan Wang, Ruixuan Zhang, Tiankai Chen,* and Jianping Xie*
Precise surface engineering of metal nanoclusters (MNCs) has emerged as a
powerful strategy for tailoring their structural, electronic, and catalytic proper-
ties. On the basis of traditional surface chemistry, ligand programming enables
atomic-level control over MNC functionality by modulating metal-ligand coor-
dination, intra/inter-ligand interactions, and interfacial chemistry. These three
layers within the “Engineering Zone” deﬁne structural ﬂexibility, charge transfer
pathways, molecular rigidity, and surface accessibility, key factors in optimizing
catalytic activity, luminescence eﬃciency, and molecular recognition. This
Perspective explores the chemistry governing ligand-directed MNC behavior in
the three layers, highlighting its role in catalysis, photonics, and biomedicine.
By integrating coordination chemistry, intra/inter-ligand design, and interfacial
interactions, how ligand programming transforms MNCs is demonstrated
into tunable platforms for next-generation functional materials. Insights from
MNCs also provide a model for advancing surface engineering in broader
nanomaterials, paving the way for innovative applications in materials science.
1. Introduction
Surfaces are fundamental in both natural and engineered sys-
tems, acting as critical interfaces where matter interacts with
its environment. Across diverse applications, including cataly-
sis, energy conversion, sensing, and biological recognition, sur-
face chemistry governs material stability and functionality.[1,2]
As materials shrink to the nanoscale, their surface-to-volume ra-
tio increases dramatically, making surface eﬀects dominant in
determining their chemical and physical properties.[3,4] This is

Unlike larger nanoparticles, where proper-

at the surface, making their reactivity, pho-

Early studies on MNCs often involved
gas-phase species, providing fundamental
structures but lacking long-term stability.[10]
The advent of colloidal synthesis introduced
ligands as stabilizing agents, allowing con-

their integration into functional materials.
Traditionally, ligands were viewed as “passive stabilizers” that
prevented aggregation and decomposition.[11,12] However, it is
now increasingly recognized that ligands play a profound role in
deﬁning MNCs behaviors and properties as “active modulators”,
inﬂuencing charge transfer, catalytic activity, photoluminescence
(PL), and molecular recognition.[13,14] This shift has led to ligand
programming, where chemical modiﬁcations to the ligand shell
provide atomically precise engineering of material properties. By
tuning metal-ligand coordination, intra/inter-ligand eﬀects, and
interfacial interactions with external species, researchers can pre-
cisely control shell rigidity, charge distribution, and surface acces-
sibility of MNCs through these chemical landscapes in diﬀerent
application scenarios.
One of the most striking features of ligand program-
ming is its adaptability to diﬀerent functional demands.
MNCs have been developed driven by diverse applications,
ranging from model catalysts,[15,16] hybrid glasses,[17] photon
sensitizers,[18,19] nano-emitters,[20,21] waveguides,[22,23] to lithog-
raphy photoinitiators.[24,25] Speciﬁcally, optimal catalytic per-
formance requires controlled exposure of active metal sites,
which can be achieved through steric regulation,[26,27] lo-
cal microenvironment modulation,[28,29] or templated surface
restructuring.[30,31] In contrast, high photoluminescence quan-
tum yields (PLQYs) demand surface rigidiﬁcation to suppress
non-radiative decay, facilitated by inter-ligand interactions such
as hydrogen bonding or 𝜋···𝜋stacking.[32–34] Similarly, molec-
ular recognition-based applications beneﬁt from ligand shells
that enable selective responses to external physical and chemical

To conceptualize the precision of ligand programming, we
deﬁne the “Engineering Zone”, a spatial region that extends
from the metal-ligand interface to the outermost ligand shell.
Within this Angstrom-scale zone, three interconnected layers of
chemical control dictate MNCs’ properties: Coordination chem-
istry, which deﬁnes the fundamental bonding between the metal
core and ligands, governing structural ﬂexibility, charge trans-
fer, and dynamic ligand exchange; Intra/inter-ligand chemistry,
which encompasses physical and chemical interactions within
and between ligands, introducing additional layers of control
over molecular rigidity, charge localization, and energy relaxation
pathways; Interfacial chemistry, which mediates interactions be-
tween MNCs and external species as exempliﬁed in guest organi-
zation, directed long-range order, bio-nano interfaces, and hybrid
material integration with templated frameworks. Each layer pro-
vides unique opportunities for ligand programming, forming a
comprehensive toolbox for precise surface engineering.
In this Perspective, we explore how ligand programming em-
powers precise surface engineering of MNCs by analyzing how
coordination chemistry, intra/inter-ligand chemistry, and inter-
facial chemistry dictate MNCs behaviors in functionality design
(Figure 1). Through selected case studies, we illustrate how these
principles translate into tailored applications, including catalysis,
photonics, and biomedicine. Insights from ligand programming
provide valuable chemical landscapes for precise surface engi-
neering, oﬀering new directions for designing next-generation
functional materials with atomically precise control.
2. Coordination Chemistry: Deﬁning Structural
Flexibility
The coordination chemistry of MNCs is central to stabilizing
structures, directing charge transfer, and enabling dynamic sur-
face transformations. The metal-ligand interface, where elec-
tronic interactions between the metal core and ligand shell occur,
serves as a programmable site for precise control over MNC prop-
erties in the “Engineering Zone”. Through careful ligand selec-
tion and coordination mode engineering, researchers can mod-
ulate metal-ligand bonding strength, tailor electron distribution,
integrate atomic ligands, and control ligand exchange dynamics,
all of which dictate stability, catalytic reactivity, and optical behav-
ior.
In this section, we discuss the coordination chemistry in
MNCs, where the metal-ligand interactions take place at the in-
terface, focusing on how diﬀerent ligand types, binding modes,
and exchange processes inﬂuence structural and electronic ﬂex-
ibility. Speciﬁcally, we examine four key aspects: 1) Coordina-
tion mode engineering, which explores the diversity of metal-
ligand bonding motifs and their impact on chemical stability and
electronic properties; 2) Charge transfer modulation, which in-
vestigates how ligand identity and bonding details control elec-
tron ﬂow across the interface; 3) Dynamic ligand competition,
which enables post-synthetic surface reconﬁguration for tuning
reactivity and function; and 4) Atomic ligand integration, which
expands the available coordination environments by incorporat-
ing halides, sulﬁdo, and hydrides. Together, these facets illustrate
how coordination chemistry governs the ﬂexibility of MNC sur-
faces, providing a structural and functional framework for pre-
cise surface engineering and rational ligand programming.
2.1. Coordination Mode Engineering
The choice of coordination atoms in MNCs fundamentally de-
termines the metal-ligand bonding nature, thereby dictating sta-
bility, electronic structure, and functionality. In MNCs, ligands
can bind through diﬀerent donor atoms, including S from thi-
olates and thiophenes, Se from selenolates, N from amines, P
from phosphines, and C from NHCs (NHC = N-heterocyclic car-
benes) and alkynyl groups. These donor atoms diﬀer in their

atoms without forming an Au(I) monolayer.[38–42] However, a
milestone in the structural understanding of thiolate-protected
MNCs was achieved in 2007, which revealed that thiolate lig-

a mixed-valence Au(0)/Au(I) core in the case of Au102(pMBA)44

also be terminal ligands, they often engage in bridging in-

and the rigidity, which provides additional control over structure
tuning.[47–49] Moreover, multidentate ligands, such as dithiolates
(e.g., BINAS (1,1′-binaphthyl-2,2′-dithiol)), bidentate phosphines
(e.g., BINAP (2,2′-bis(diphenylphosphino)-1,1′-binaphthyl)), and
bisNHCs, further expand the complexity by introducing rigid
coordination frameworks.[50–52] Recent studies also underscored
that multi-nitrogen donor ligands like pyridyl, amido, and amid-
inate groups could provide additional coordination diversity
but in a relatively loose way.[53,54] These diverse coordination
chemistries highlight the programmability of MNCs and their
structural ﬂexibility deﬁned by coordination atoms.
8-valence-electron icosahedral [Au13]5+ is one of the most stud-
ied structural units in MNCs with a great variety of coordination
modes. This enables a systematic examination of how diﬀerent
ligands alter the most fundamental properties of MNCs, includ-
ing electronic structures, stability, and optical properties. A se-
ries of [Au13]5+ NCs have been synthesized with phosphine and
NHC ligands, revealing that despite diﬀerences in ligand identity,
their optical absorption spectra remain largely similar, with char-
acteristic peaks observed at ≈340 and ≈430 nm.[44,45,55] Compu-
tational studies further conﬁrm that replacing phosphines with
NHCs induces only marginal perturbations in the electronic
structures, leading to nearly identical luminescence and optical
properties regardless of whether the ligand is monodentate or
bidentate.[47,48] However, the higher thermal stability of NHC-
protected [Au13]5+ NCs compared to phosphine-protected ones
demonstrates that coordination strength (321–371 kJ·mol−1 for
Au−NHC bond while 121 kJ·mol−1 for Au−PR3) is a key deter-
minant of stability, even when optical and electronic features
remain unaﬀected (Figure 2b(ii–iv)).[56,57] Additionally, the rich
inter-ligand 𝜋···𝜋interactions are taking eﬀect, as evidenced by
the higher thermal stability observed in bisNHC-protected Au
NCs (Figure 2b(i, ii)).[58]
The introduction of bridging thiolate ligands further compli-
cates the structure of 8-valence-electron [Au13]5+, as metal atoms
become incorporated into the ligand shell, forming staple-like
motifs. A key example is [Au25(SR)18]−(SR = thiolates),[55,59]
which consists of six symmetric [Au2(SR)3] staples protecting
a [Au13]5+ core but making no contributions to valence elec-
tron (Figure 2b(vi)). It should be emphasized that the synthetic
pathways toward [Au25(SR)18]−have been extensively unveiled
by real-time mass spectrometry in aqueous system.[5,60,61] From
the cyclic Aun(SR)n precursors to product, the bridging coordi-
nation feature of thiols has been preserved and leads to semi-
cyclic or cyclic motifs, which also stands as the core foundation
of versatile intermediate Au NCs. Therefore, motifs consisting of
bridging thiols and metal atoms signiﬁcantly inﬂuence the elec-
tronic states of MNCs. Taking the well-deﬁned [Au25(SR)18]−, the
staple-like motifs induce a distinctive UV–vis absorption peak at
≈690 nm, while maintaining the HOMO-LUMO gaps at 2.75 eV
(≈450 nm) and 3.1 eV (≈400 nm).[55] By ﬁne-tuning these sta-
ple motifs, it is possible to induce contraction or expansion of
the Au13 core, as reﬂected in the spectral shifts of the 690 nm
peak. Increasing ligand aromaticity, for example, expands the
core, leading to enhanced thermal stability and improved cat-
alytic performance.[62] Similar red-shifts and stability enhance-
ments have been observed in selenolate-protected counterparts,
reinforcing the impact of ligand selection on MNCs properties
(Figure 2b(v,vi)).[44]
While thiolate-based staples exhibit ﬂexibility in surface adap-
tation, the alkynyl-protected counterpart introduced drastically
diﬀerent structural and electronic eﬀects. In [Au25(C≡CAr)18]−
(Ar = 3,5-bis(triﬂuoromethyl)phenyl), 𝜎- and 𝜋-interactions dic-
tate metal-ligand interfaces, leading to a fundamentally distinct
surface arrangement compared to thiolates, while the Au13 core
remains intact (Figure 2b(vi,vii)).[48] This divergence arises from
the linear Au−C−Au angle (≈180°) in alkynyl ligands, contrast-
ing sharply with the bent Au−S−Au angle (≈97.75–105.78°) in
thiolates. The greater ﬂexibility of Au−S bonding allows for re-
arrangement to minimize steric repulsion, whereas the rigid
alkynyl geometry enforces a more deﬁned electronic structure,
as reﬂected in notable diﬀerences in UV–vis absorption proﬁles.
Compared to thiolate-Au25, alkynyl-Au25 exhibited higher ther-
mal stability and a featured peak red-shifting from 690 to above
700 nm, accompanied with two small shoulders at ≈590 and
820 nm. These ﬁndings underscore that ligand coordination is
not merely a stabilizing factor but a key design tool for tuning
optical and catalytic properties, demonstrating the programma-
bility of the metal-ligand interface in MNCs.
While stability is a crucial desire in coordination mode de-
sign, excessive stabilization can hinder catalytic performance by
limiting the availability of active sites. This reﬂects a funda-
mental trade-oﬀin ligand programming, where enhanced sta-
bility through strong coordination may reduce surface accessi-
bility, while increased lability or structural openness can boost
reactivity at the cost of robustness. Recent studies have revealed
that metal-ligand bond dissociation is often intrinsic to catalytic
processes, where the dynamic restructuring of the metal-ligand
interface plays a key role in activating surface sites.[63] For in-
stance, Seong et al. demonstrated through experimental and den-
sity functional theory (DFT) studies that electrochemical activa-
tion of [Au25(SR)18]−leads to partial dethiolation, exposing Au
sites within staple motifs that serve as active centers for CO2 elec-
troreduction to CO.[64] Similarly, in hydrogen evolution reactions
(HERs), optimizing surface ligand coverage or tailoring ligand
environments for proton and electron transfer has been shown
to signiﬁcantly enhance catalytic eﬃciency.[65,66] These ﬁndings
suggest that ligand loss or rearrangement under reaction con-
ditions is not necessarily detrimental but can be leveraged as
a means of dynamic site activation. Building on this concept,
a loose coordination strategy was recently introduced to expose
open sites while maintaining overall structural integrity. A com-
pelling example is the Au26 cluster stabilized by amidinate and

2.2. Charge Transfer Modulation
The metal-ligand interface is one of the critical locations where
charge transfer processes take place in coordination chemistry.
The electronic coupling between the metal core and ligand shell
of MNCs via covalent or coordination bonds dictates energy re-
laxation pathways and optical properties. While the core is a fun-
damental factor in deﬁning the HOMO-LUMO transitions and
overall electronic states, precise coordination chemistry at the
interface enables tunable charge redistribution, enhanced radia-
tive eﬃciency, and controlled non-radiative decay.[68] This control
can be achieved through modulation of charge transfer eﬃciency,
tuning of vibration modes, and unconventional methods such as
high-pressure engineering, all of which mainly inﬂuence the PL
behavior of MNCs.
Diﬀerent metal-ligand interfaces create distinct charge trans-
fer channels, signiﬁcantly impacting charge transfer eﬃciency.
The identity of the coordinating atom, along with bonding
strength and electronic coupling, plays a central role in me-
diating charge transport. For example, replacing S with Se in
[Au25(SR)18]−leads to a noticeable decrease in PL intensity, in-
dicating that charge transfer at the Au−Se interface is less ef-
ﬁcient than at the Au−S interface.[44] A similar trend is ob-
served in Au24(SCH2PhtBu)20 versus Au24(SePh)20, where the
selenolate-protected cluster exhibits negligible emission, rein-
forcing the idea that the stronger covalency of Au−Se bonds re-
stricts charge delocalization and inhibits radiative recombina-
tion (Figure 3a).[69] While the two clusters share similar com-
positions, subtle diﬀerences in their core structures and lig-
and conﬁgurations may also contribute to the observed opti-
cal behavior. In contrast, alkynyl ligands provide a more eﬃ-
cient charge transfer channel due to their rigid 𝜋-conjugated
framework than thiolates, which enhances orbital overlap and
accelerates electronic relaxation.[70] Transient absorption spec-
troscopy conﬁrms this eﬀect, showing that face-centered cubic
[Au110(pCF3PhC≡C)48]3−exhibits a 10 ps carrier recombination
time, nearly an order of magnitude faster than the 1 ns re-
combination time observed in face-centered cubic Au92(TBBT)44
(TBBT = 4-tert-butylbenzenethiolate), highlighting the superior
charge delocalization enabled by alkynyl ligands.[71] Although
structural diﬀerences exist between these two clusters, the au-
thors attributed the signiﬁcant lifetime diﬀerence primarily to
ligand eﬀects, supported by DFT calculations showing the ac-
tive involvement of alkynyl ligands in the frontier orbitals and
their impact on nonradiative relaxation pathways.[72] A similar
trend is evident in Au28(tBuPhC≡C)20 (Au28-C) and Au28(TBBT)20
(Au28-S), where both samples share an identical Au20 core, yet
Au28-C exhibits a tenfold PL intensity enhancement compared
to Au28-S (Figure 3b).[73] Ultrafast excited-state dynamics studies
revealed that Au28-C has a larger triplet-state population, reﬂect-
ing stronger charge delocalization and more eﬃcient radiative
recombination.
Beyond direct charge transfer, electron-phonon interactions
at the metal-ligand interface signiﬁcantly modulate excited-state
relaxation dynamics and energy dissipation. When an electron
undergoes excitation, its coupling with metal-ligand vibrational
modes inﬂuences non-radiative decay rates and charge redis-
tribution pathways. Strong electron-phonon coupling acceler-
ates non-radiative relaxation, usually reducing PLQYs, while
weaker coupling promotes charge separation and enhances ra-
diative transitions.[68,74] The Au−S vibrational modes in staple
motifs strongly impact charge relaxation in thiolate-protected
MNCs. Raman spectroscopy identiﬁes Au−S stretching frequen-
cies at 300–330 cm−1 in Au2(SR)3 motifs, which correlate with
optical phonon modes at 330 cm−1 in [Au25(BM)18]−(BM =
benzyl mercaptan) and 390 cm−1 in [Au25(PET)18]−(PET = 2-
phenylethylthiolate).[74,75] These phonons interact with free elec-
trons through Fröhlich interactions, directly aﬀecting charge
transfer eﬃciency. In contrast, aromatic thiol ligands, such
as [Au25(SPh)18]−and [Au25(3,5-DMBT)18]−(DMBT = dimethyl-
benzenethiol), signiﬁcantly lower phonon energies to 200 and
170 cm−1, respectively. Since these ligands have similar molec-
ular weights to non-aromatic thiols, the suppression of high-
frequency phonon modes suggests that aromatic ligands re-
duce electron-phonon coupling, altering HOMO-LUMO relax-
ation and improving PL eﬃciency (Figure 3c).[75] Further compar-
ative studies on Au and Ag NCs conﬁrm that Au2(SR)3 exhibits
stronger electron-phonon coupling than its Ag counterparts,
which accelerates charge redistribution and decreases PLQY.[76]
Thus [Ag25(SR)18]−and [AuAg24(SR)18]−display weaker coupling,
leading to longer carrier lifetimes and stronger luminescence.
These [Au25(SR)18]−derivatives-based ﬁndings indicate that tun-
ing electron-phonon interactions through ligand programming
provides an eﬀective route for optimizing MNC-based optoelec-
tronic applications.
Extreme external stimuli such as high pressure oﬀer an alter-
native method for directly modulating charge transfer and elec-
tron redistribution through metal-ligand interface.[77–80] High-
pressure optical studies on [Au25(PET)18]−reveal that a 0.1
Å decrease in Au−Au bond length induces a 30 nm blue-
shift in the PL spectrum, providing direct evidence that struc-
tural compression alters electronic redistribution pathways.[80]
More broadly, under high pressure of 12 GPa, MNCs often
exhibit a red-shift in absorption onset, attributed to electron
migration from the core to the ligand shell via the Au−S in-
terface (Figure 3d). Furthermore, PL intensity enhancements
up to 200-fold have been observed at pressures approaching
∼7 GPa, demonstrating that external forces can dynamically
modulate charge transfer pathways, further optimizing optical
performance.[77]

theoretically due to the robust yet dynamic nature of metal-
sulfur bonds.[82,85–87] Typically, homologous thiolate exchanges

linking MNCs into supramolecular assemblies while preserv-
ing the fundamental coordination environment. For instance,
Sels et al. reported that intense circular dichroism signals appear
on chiroptically inactive [Au25(PET)18]−upon exchange with the
bidentate BINAS ligand, conﬁrming the induction of chiroptical
properties.[90] More interestingly, the distance between the two
sulfur atoms in BINAS aligns perfectly with the inter-staple bind-
ing mode, allowing for controlled dimerization and trimeriza-
tion of Au25 through diBINAS ((1R,1′R)-6,6′-(1,4-phenylene)di-
1,1′-binaphthyl-2,2′-dithiol) linkers (Figure 4b).[91] In these poly-
meric assemblies, rigidiﬁcation of the ligand shell restricts intra-
molecular motions, leading to a remarkable 100-fold enhance-
ment in PL intensity in the tetramer compared to the monomeric
form. Additionally, the bridging electron transfer pathways

2.4. Atomic Ligand Integration
Atomic ligand represents a special class of ligands in coordina-
tion chemistry, and the incorporation of atomic ligands, such
as halides, chalcogenides, and hydrides, into MNCs presents a
promising avenue for modulating their structural stability, elec-
tronic states, and optical properties. While halides (Cl−, Br−, I−)
typically function as terminal ligands, chalcogenides like sul-
ﬁdo ligands (S2−) often adopt multi-coordinating modes, leading
to more intricate surface architectures and inﬂuencing electron
relaxation pathways. Despite their potential, atomic ligands re-
main underexplored in MNCs due to their relatively weak protect-
ing ability or limited synthetic methods, and fully atomic-ligand-
protected MNCs have yet to be experimentally realized.[96–98]
Speciﬁcally, halides frequently participate in MNCs growth due
to their moderate coordination as auxiliary agents or emerge
as dopants through reduction processes (e.g., precursor like
HAuCl4, Me2S–Au–Cl or solvents like CH2Cl2).[60,99–101] Con-
versely, chalcogenides adopt more versatile coordination in
MNCs, where sulﬁdo ligands are usually derived from bulky thi-
ols by breaking C─S bonds or direct integration by competing
bonding.[102–106] Importantly, the multi-coordinating sulﬁdo lig-
ands actively participate in ligand-to-metal-charge transfer to illu-
minate MNCs.[107,108] Herein, the challenge lies in precisely con-
trolling their incorporation, yet their integration oﬀers unique
opportunities for programming MNC surfaces at the atomic level
to ﬁne-tune optical and catalytic properties.
A notable example demonstrating the moderate coordination
strength of halides is their ability to deﬁne open metal sites when
combined with bulky thiol ligands. In [Ag141X12(SAdm)40]3+
(X═Cl, Br, I; SAdm = 1-adamantanethiolate), the sterically-
demanding SAdm creates low ligand coverage, exposing sur-
face sites for halide coordination (Figure 5a). The relatively
weak Ag─X bonds enhance surface reactivity and facilitate lig-
and exchange to impart water solubility to the clusters.[109]
More recently, Jing et al. achieved precise control over the
degree of halide incorporation in Ag NCs.[110] In this study,
three [Ag25(SPhCl)16−xClx(DPPB)6]2+ (x = 0, 1, 4, DPPB = 1,4-
bis(diphenylphosphino)butane) featuring thiol and chlorido com-
binations are synthesized and investigated. Interestingly, Ag25-
Cl4, which contains fewer sterically hindered thiols, exhibited
the highest PLQY up to 73% than the fewer-halide contain-
ing analogs. This enhancement was attributed to chlorido-
induced compression of the ligand shell, which reduces non-
radiative decay and enhances radiative recombination. Further-
more, diﬀerences between halides are also highlighted in the
comparison of isostructures of [Au13(DPPE)5X2]3+ (DPPE = 1,2-
bis(diphenylphosphino)ethane). From Cl to Br to I, both the
HOMO-LUMO gaps and PL intensities are slightly decreased,
which is attributed to the diverse halide-to-metal charge transfer
processes (Figure 5b).[111]
Chalcogenides, especially sulﬁdo ligands, have emerged as
highly eﬀective dopants, owing to their stronger bonding aﬃn-
ity for metal compared to thiols.[106] Unlike thiolate ligands, sul-
ﬁdo ligands lack organic residues, enabling more direct partici-
pation in complex coordination environments.[112] Single crystal
structure analysis has revealed that sulﬁdo ligands can serve as
bridging ligands, as evidenced by the triply Au-coordinated μ3 sul-
ﬁdo bridge in the 19th sulfur of Au30S(StBu)18,[113,114] an unprece-
dented observation for thiolate-protected Au NCs. Other NCs,
including Au18S2(STipb)12 (STipb = 2,4,6-triisopropylbenzyl
mercaptan),[115] Au21S(SAdm)15,[116] Au38S2(SAdm)20,[102,105] and
Au103S2(SNap)41 (SNap = 2-naphthalenethiolates)[104] also exhib-
ited the μ3–S bridges. Of interest, μ4–S bridging atoms were
reported by Gan et al. in the surface motifs of Au60S6(BM)36
(Au60S6) and its derivative Au60S7(BM)36 (Au60S7), further
demonstrating the diversity in coordination facilitated by sulﬁdo
ligands.[117] Of note, the multi-coordinating nature and strong
Au−S bonds induced by sulﬁdo atoms can trickly change both
internal structure and crystallographic arrangement. (Figure 5c).
This is evidenced by the aforementioned case where a single

3.1. Intra-Ligand Electronic Eﬀects
In organic chemistry, electronic eﬀects such as conjuga-
tion, inductive eﬀects, including electron-donating or electron-
withdrawing eﬀects, and dynamic electronic rearrangements
play a crucial role in dictating molecular reactivity, stability,
and optical properties. These eﬀects arise from the intrin-
sic electronic nature of substituents and their ability to redis-
tribute electron density across molecular frameworks. For in-
stance, 𝜋-conjugation extends electronic delocalization, facili-
tating charge transport and modulating excited-state dynam-
ics, while electron-withdrawing groups lower electron density
and tune redox potential and reactivity. However, in MNCs,
these electronic eﬀects extend beyond the ligands themselves
and are further transmitted to the metal core via coordination
interactions. This unique interplay between ligand electronics
and MNCs’ properties enables precise tuning of charge distri-
bution, PL, and catalytic activity. Through rational ligand pro-
gramming, one can leverage intra-ligand electronic eﬀects to
engineer charge redistribution across the metal-ligand inter-
face, optimize energy relaxation pathways, and enhance surface
reactivity.
Electron-donating and electron-withdrawing eﬀects modu-
late charge transfer for PL and catalysis of MNCs. One of
the earliest demonstrations of ligand inﬂuence on PLQY was
conducted by Wu et al. using [Au25(SR)18]−, where electron-
rich thiolate ligands (e.g., GSH) signiﬁcantly enhanced emis-
sion through stronger charge transfer interactions.[129] Simi-
larly, in [Ag44(SR)30]4−, ligands with varying electron-withdrawing
strengths, such as TP (TP = thiophenol), FTP (FTP = 4-
ﬂuorothiophenol), and CTP (CTP = 4-chlorothiophenol), were
shown to inﬂuence electron-phonon coupling and energy relax-
ation pathways (Figure 6a (i)).[130] Stronger electron-withdrawing
ligands slowed core-to-surface relaxation dynamics, prolonging
carrier lifetimes and modifying emission properties, highlight-
ing how ligand electronics dictate excited-state processes. Be-
yond Au NCs, by systematically modifying the vertex phosphine
ligands in the templated Pt1Ag28(SAdm)18(PR3)4, nine variants
with identical frameworks but distinct PR3 substituents were syn-
thesized and structurally characterized.[131] The introduction of
electron-donating groups (e.g., −OMe, −Me) on phosphines en-
hanced PL intensity, while electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., −F,
−Cl) led to quenching.
Beyond optical properties, inductive eﬀects play a pivotal
role in catalysis by altering electronic structures and local mi-
croenvironments. A study on Au15(SR)13 revealed that ligand
identity signiﬁcantly inﬂuences enzyme-mimetic catalysis.[132]
Thiolate-protected Au15 clusters exhibited distinct catalytic be-
haviors depending on the electronic nature of the ligand:
Au15(NAC)13 (NAC = N-acetyl-L-cysteine) demonstrated su-
perior peroxidase-like catalytic activity and structural stabil-
ity compared to its analogues, Au15(MMPA)13 (MMPA = 3-
mercapto-2-methylpropanoic acid) and Au15(MPA)13 (MPA = 3-
mercaptopropionic acid) (Figure 6a(ii)). This variation was at-
tributed to diﬀerences in the structures of the substituents within
the ligand shell, which aﬀected both substrate accessibility and
catalytic site stabilization. More broadly, inductive eﬀects can be
strategically utilized to create electron-rich or electron-deﬁcient
local environments, improving substrate adsorption and reac-
tivity. For instance, in electrocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction
(CO2RR), nitrogen-doped polymers are known for their abil-
ity to capture CO2 due to strong electrostatic interactions be-
tween electronegative nitrogen (N𝛿−) and electropositive car-
bon (C𝛿+) in CO2.[133] Inspired by this, nitrogen-heterocyclic
thiolate ligand (2-thiouracil-5-carboxylic acid, TCA) was inte-
grated into the ligand shell of [Au25(SR)18]−to enhance lo-
cal CO2 concentration near the catalytic sites (Figure 6a(iii)).
When pyrimidine-based thiolate ligands were incorporated into
[Au25(pMBA)18]−, they increased CO2 binding aﬃnity and fa-
cilitated mass transport, leading to a remarkable Faradaic ef-
ﬁciency of 98.6% for CO2-to-CO conversion.[28] This strategy
demonstrated how inductive eﬀect-driven microenvironment en-
gineering can mimic enzymatic pocket-like interactions to boost
catalysis.
Conjugation within ligands is particularly crucial in govern-
ing intra-ligand electronic eﬀects, inﬂuencing both optical and
catalytic behavior. This eﬀect can be achieved either by extend-
ing 𝜋-conjugation through benzenyl groups or by directly cou-
pling the ligand’s 𝜋-system with the coordination interface, as
shown in alkynyl ligands (e.g., PhC≡C−). In emissive MNCs,
𝜋-conjugation within ligands modiﬁes electron-phonon interac-
tions and provides additional 𝜋···𝜋stacking, leading to enhanced
emission, as demonstrated in both thiolate- and NHC-protected
clusters.[75,134] The larger conjugation in alkynyl groups com-
pared with thiolate ligands, making alkynyl ligands less partic-
ipate in the frontier orbitals of the MNCs. This can induce dis-
tinct optical adsorption even for MNCs with the same cores, as

3.2. Reactive Sites Pre-Organization
An emerging strategy in ligand programming is the pre-
organization of reactive moieties within the ligand framework be-
fore synthesis, introducing chemically accessible compartments
in the “Engineering Zone”. These pre-organized sites inﬂuence
electronic properties and surface reactivity by dictating speciﬁc
interactions with metal atoms, thereby enabling precise control
over catalytic activity and post-synthetic modiﬁcations. Generally,
this approach follows two primary design principles: 1) Structural
pre-organization of open sites, where ligands incorporate reac-
tive hotspots (e.g., Lewis acid-base pairs,[139,140] organometallic
motif,[141] or active metal complexes[142]) to create a well-deﬁned
microenvironment that enhances catalysis; 2) Integration of
chemically reactive moieties, where functional groups such as
azide (−N3),[36,143] alkyne (−C≡C−),[107] or aldehyde (−C═O)[144]
are embedded within the ligand backbone, facilitating molecular
recognition and nano-orthogonal reactions for targeted function-
alization of MNCs.

ligands enable the selective immobilization of a single metal

3.3. Geometric Ligand Control
The geometric eﬀects of ligands play a pivotal role in modulating
the surface accessibility, stability, and reactivity of MNCs. Un-
like electronic factors that govern charge transfer at the metal-
ligand interface, geometric factors physically inﬂuence the steric
arrangement of ligands, thereby dictating the exposure of metal
sites and the structural preferences of MNCs. Two primary mech-
anisms emerge as key tools in precise surface engineering: 1)
Steric repulsion to create open metal sites: by introducing bulky
ligands or sterically mismatched ligand pairs, it is possible to dis-
rupt full surface coverage and expose active metal sites. This ap-
proach enhances catalytic reactivity by increasing accessibility to
reactants while maintaining overall stability; 2) Positional eﬀects
of ligands in determining stability and reactivity: variations in lig-
and substitution patterns at a more reﬁned molecular level, such
as regioisomerism of benzene-based thiols, can strongly inﬂu-
ence growth, metal-ligand binding strength, and overall chemical
functionality. These eﬀects highlight how geometric ligand con-
trol in the “Engineering Zone” can be a precise tool for ligand
programming in MNC functionality design.
One of the most eﬀective ways to create exposed metal sites
is through bulkiness-driven steric repulsion. When ligands with
large steric footprints are introduced, they can prevent complete
surface coverage, forcing ligands apart and leaving accessible
metal atoms for catalysis. A pioneering work used calix[4]arene
phosphine ligands as macrocyclic steric shields to protect Au
NCs. Due to their extreme bulkiness, the calix[4]arene-bound
clusters exhibited an unusually high level of metal exposure, with
up to 25% of Au atoms remaining accessible.[26] Simulations
conﬁrmed that the voluminous calix[4]arene groups physically
prevent full ligand coverage, leaving the metal surface available
for small molecule adsorption.[150] Further exploration replaced
the ─CH2 linkage with thioether moieties, which gives rise to
8 coordination sites in thiacalix[4]arene, to enhance the struc-
tural integrity while maintaining the exposure of the active metal
surface.[151,152] In the case of Ag35(H2TC4A)2(TC4A)(C≡CtBu)16
(TC4A = para-tert-butylthiacalix[4]-arene), the macrocyclic eﬀects
enabled a cavity-induced “exposed” Ag atom discovered in one of
the bowl of thiacalix[4]arene, which can be squeezed out or re-
covered by control of hydrogen bonding (Labeled dark blue in
Figure 8a).[151] Moreover, multidentate phosphine ligands have
also been employed to induce steric repulsion eﬀects by inter-
ligand spacing. For instance, in Au22(DPPO)6 (DPPO = 1,8-
bis(diphenylphosphino)octane), steric clashes between the large
phosphine ligands similarly led to a high exposure ratio of Au
atoms, with eight surface Au atoms remaining unprotected (La-
beled orange in Figure 8b).[153] The catalytic oxidation of CO
demonstrated that these exposed sites actively participate in reac-
tant adsorption, directly validating the role of steric-driven metal
accessibility in enhancing catalytic performance.[154,155]
Another strategy to increase metal exposure is through surface
geometric mismatch, which arises when two sterically distinct
ligands are co-assembled on the MNC surface. The spatial incom-
patibility between ligands with diﬀering steric proﬁles disrupts
uniform ligand coverage, creating additional open sites.[27,156]
Yuan et al. demonstrated this geometric mismatch strategy us-
ing a combination of negatively charged, multidentate dipyridy-
lamine ligands and TPP in [Au23(TPP)10(dipyridylamine)2Cl]2+
(Figure 8c).[27] Due to the strong repulsion between the two lig-
and types, the cluster adopted a disrupted surface structure with
increased accessibility to uncoordinated Au atoms. The accessi-
bility of these sites was experimentally validated using SNap PL
titration, where the quenching and recovery of PL provided direct
evidence of uncoordinated Au atoms available for ligand bind-
ing (Labeled orange in Figure 8c). The ﬁnal analysis estimated
that ≈35% of Au atoms remained exposed, signiﬁcantly higher
than the 25% observed in calix[4]arene-capped clusters. This ge-
ometric mismatch resulted in superior catalytic activity, with a
69% conversion eﬃciency in benzyl alcohol oxidation, vastly out-
performing thiolate- and alkynyl-protected counterparts (which
achieved only 1% and 6% conversion, respectively). It should be
noted that SNap PL titration has been one of the very limited
chemical methods capable of directly visualize open metal sites,
which fundamentally relies on the PL quenching after the forma-
tion of Au−S bonds.[26,27,67] This calls for more development of a
visualization strategy to capture the dynamic evolution around
the open sites.

the molecular level can also strongly impact MNCs formation,

4. Interfacial Chemistry: Directing Molecular
Interactions
The interfacial chemistry of MNCs extends beyond direct metal-
ligand coordination and intra/inter-ligand design, encompass-
ing the third layer of dynamic interactions with surrounding
molecules that inﬂuence their behaviors. Unlike traditional sur-
face chemistry, which primarily focuses on ligand stabilization,
interfacial chemistry governs how external entities, such as sol-
vents, counterions, additives, other MNCs, and frameworks, in-
teract with MNCs in a controlled manner. These interactions en-
able spatially directed molecular organization, promote charge
transfer, and facilitate structural assembly, allowing MNCs to
function as programmable platforms for catalysis, sensing, and
optoelectronics.
In this section, we discuss the interfacial chemistry of MNCs,
where their surfaces interact with and organize surrounding
species to modulate their functionalities. This discussion encom-
passes three major aspects: 1) Surface-mediated guest assembly,
which explores how MNC surfaces direct the spatial arrangement
of surrounding guests into well-deﬁned microenvironments,
leveraging electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and van
der Waals interactions to regulate emission, charge transfer, and
catalytic eﬃciency; 2) Ordered cluster superstructures, which
focus on crystallization and self-assembly processes governed
by interfacial forces, balancing attractive and repulsive interac-
tions to achieve highly ordered architectures; and 3) Encapsu-
lation in templated frameworks, which investigates the integra-
tion of MNCs into templated frameworks such as metal–organic
frameworks (MOFs), covalent organic frameworks (COFs), and
biomolecular matrices, where ligand-directed interactions enable
enhanced stability and modiﬁed carrier ﬂow for catalysis. These
facets of interfacial chemistry highlight how MNC surfaces func-
tion as dynamic molecular interfaces, enabling precise structural
and functional tuning for diverse applications.
4.1. Surface-Mediated Guest Assembly
MNCs act as nanoscale platforms that guide the spatial orga-
nization of surrounding molecules, creating well-deﬁned mi-
croenvironments that inﬂuence their optical, electronic, and cat-
alytic properties. Unlike homogeneous solutions where guest
molecules remain randomly dispersed, MNC surfaces provide
conﬁned and directional binding sites, allowing for cooperative
interactions that can regulate energy transfer pathways, stabi-
lize reactive intermediates, and control substrate accessibility in
catalytic processes. These interactions are dictated by the ligand
shell composition, ranging from electrostatic interactions and hy-
drogen bonding in water-soluble MNCs to van der Waals and
hydrophobic forces in organic-phase ones. Beyond simple ad-
sorption, these guest assemblies can alter the local electronic
structure of MNCs, inﬂuencing excited-state relaxation dynam-
ics, triplet energy transfer (TET) eﬃciency, and charge separation
in photocatalysis. By leveraging surface-mediated guest organi-
zation, one can ﬁne-tune emission behavior, enhance molecular
recognition, and optimize catalytic selectivity at the atomic level.
Solvents, as the most ubiquitous molecules interacting with
MNCs, have received considerable attention for their roles in
modulating MNCs’ properties. It was ﬁrst reported in 2012 that
the reduced solubility of MNCs in poor solvents can trigger
AIE via metallophilic interactions, a strategy widely applied to
enhance luminescence in weakly emissive clusters (Figure 10a
(i)).[169,170,174] Beyond solubility eﬀects, recent studies have re-
vealed that even preferred solvent molecules can actively partic-
ipate in charge transfer and relaxation dynamics.[175,176] For in-
stance, excited-state concerted electron-proton transfer was ob-
served in Au10(ATT)6 (ATT = 6-aza-2-thiothymidine), where a si-
multaneous electron transfer from the Au(0) core to the Au(I)–
ATT surface occurs alongside proton transfer from H3O+ to
the ATT ligand. This process leads to PL quenching but en-
hances charge transfer eﬃciency in photo-induced catalysis.[177]

and covalent modiﬁcations. For example, though interactions
between small molecules with lipophilic ligands are limited
compared to their water-soluble counterparts, an eﬀective sup-

tuning surface rigidity, leading to enhanced PLQY and acceler-

Counterion eﬀects also play a crucial role in catalysis, where
they mediate charge transfer and stabilize reactive intermedi-
ates in electrochemical reactions. In CO2RR, cations can act
as charge relays, facilitating electron transfer across interfa-
cial sites rather than merely blocking active centers. Deproto-
nation of [Au25(MHA)18]−(MHA = 6-mercaptohexanoic acid)
enhances CO2RR activity by enabling a cation-relayed elec-
tron transfer mechanism through the anionic terminal groups,
where the reaction rate follows the trend Li+ < Na+ < K+ <
Cs+ (Figure 10c(i)).[181] A similar eﬀect was observed in the
CO2RR performance of alkynyl-ligand-protected Ag15 clusters,
where inner-sphere Na+−CO2 complexes stabilized key reac-
tion intermediates and induced a local alkaline environment,
accelerating proton transfer steps (*CO2 →*COOH →*CO)
(Figure 10c(ii)).[182,183] These studies underscore the importance
of interfacial chemistry in modulating the local catalytic mi-
croenvironment, demonstrating that counterions can serve as
active participants in reaction pathways rather than passive
stabilizers.
Interfacial interactions also govern the role of MNCs as triplet
sensitizers in photon UC systems. MNCs typically possess in-
trinsic triplet-state properties, making them ideal candidates for
triplet-triplet annihilation upconversion (TTA-UC).[184] This pro-
cess, which converts low-energy photons into high-energy emis-
sions, requires eﬃcient TET between the MNC sensitizer and
annihilator molecules (Figure 10d(i)). Niihori et al. ﬁrst dis-
covered that ligand-protected MNCs can sensitize the triplet
states of ﬂuorescent dyes, leading to the exploration of MNCs
in TTA-UC studies. The ﬁrst observation of MNC-mediated
TET occurred in a PtAg24(2,4-DMBT)18 mixed with perylene and
9,10-bis[(triisopropylsilyl)ethynyl]anthracene in 2021.[185] Sub-
sequent studies on [Au4Cu4(SAdm)5(DPPM)2]+187 and triplet-
mediator ligand-protected Au2Cu6(SAdm)6[P(DPA)3]2 (DPA =
9,10-diphenylanthracene)[187] (Figure 10d(ii)) further conﬁrmed
the viability of MNCs as triplet sensitizers. Additionally,
Au16Cu6(tBuPhC≡C)18, with a near-unit PLQY in NIR-I region,
exhibits fast and eﬃcient TET to perylene, yielding blue UC emis-
sion with an anti-Stokes shift of 0.68 eV (Figure 10d(iii)).[19] These
studies highlight the crucial role of interfacial chemistry in mod-
ulating TET eﬃciency in MNC-based UC systems. By tailoring
ligand composition, electronic structure, and molecular docking
sites, MNCs can be further optimized as excellent triplet sensi-
tizers.
4.2. Ordered Cluster Superstructures
The formation of highly ordered cluster superstructures, whether
through crystallization or self-assembly, is also fundamentally
governed by interfacial chemistry. The process requires a delicate
balance of attractive and repulsive forces to guide clusters into
precise arrangements while maintaining their stability in solu-
tion. Crystallization, in particular, relies on minimizing interfa-
cial energy, often necessitating weak and directional interactions
to facilitate systematic packing. In contrast, self-assembly lever-
ages a more dynamic interplay of non-covalent interactions, in-
cluding van der Waals forces, 𝜋···𝜋stacking, CH···𝜋, and electro-
static interactions, to drive the organization of MNCs into well-
deﬁned architectures.[188,189] Importantly, the surface charge dis-
tribution of MNCs plays a crucial role in determining the path-
ways for both processes. Maintaining charge neutrality (e.g., pro-
tonation) or achieving partial charge compensation (e.g., counte-
rions or even oppositely-charged MNCs)[190,191] is often essential
to prevent excessive Coulombic repulsion, while weak interfacial
interactions help regulate cluster alignment and ensure the for-
mation of long-range order. These principles guide the design
of superstructures with tailored properties and unlock new func-
tionalities.
In organic phases, crystallization remains the predominant
route to highly ordered structures, oﬀering systematic pack-
ing and emergent optical properties. One particularly com-
pelling application is crystallization-induced emission enhance-
ment, where MNCs exhibit signiﬁcantly stronger PL in the
crystalline state than in solution or in the amorphous state.
This eﬀect arises from extensive inter-molecular interactions,
such as CH···𝜋interactions, which restrict intra-molecular mo-
tions and suppress non-radiative decay pathways. For example,

While crystallization provides highly ordered structures, self-
assembly oﬀers greater structural ﬂexibility and introduces new
functionalities. A notable distinction is that crystallization can
induce spontaneous enantiomer separation of chiral MNCs,
whereas self-assembly can preserve or even induce chirality
from scratch. Chiral assembly occurs when anisotropic MNCs
interact through directional interfacial forces, such as van der
Waals attraction and steric repulsion, to form structures with
inherent chiroptical activity. For instance, Au29(SAdm)19, with
their heterodimeric nature and four distinct surface motifs, as-
sembles into a double-helical conﬁguration analogous to DNA
(Figure 11c).[195] In this system, interfacial interactions selectively
align surface motifs between enantiomers, mimicking base pair-
ing interactions and yielding an ordered yet anisotropic struc-
ture. Similar interfacial control has been employed to construct
triple-helical assemblies, where weak directional forces orches-
trate the formation of helical motifs from symmetric clusters.
Remarkably, even achiral clusters can acquire chirality through
templated self-assembly with achiral counterions. In the case
of [Ag25(2,4-DMBT)18]−with the achiral macrocyclic counterion
N,N′,N″-trialkyltriazatriangulenes, the charge compensation and
alkyl···𝜋interactions drive the spontaneous formation of enan-
tiomers, reinforcing the critical role of interfacial chemistry in
directing chiral organization (Figure 11d).[196]
However, crystallization or self-assembly in aqueous media
presents additional challenges due to the dominance of strong
electrostatic and solvation eﬀects, which often hinder the for-
mation of well-ordered superstructures. Inspired by their charge
compensation roles, counterions play a pivotal role in miti-
gating these issues by tuning interfacial interactions. For in-
stance, tetraethylammonium cations (TEA+) modulate the self-
assembly of [Au25(pMBA)18]−by stabilizing its partially detached
SR−[Au(I)−SR]2 motifs, allowing controlled aggregation without
disrupting the overall integrity (Figure 11e).[197] Through a com-
bination of CH⋯𝜋and electrostatic interactions, various coun-
terions were found able to assist the charge compensation and
long-range assembly of water-soluble ligand-protected MNCs.
Signiﬁcantly, the low-dose TEM visualized these sensitive sur-
face dynamics, capturing [Au25(pMBA)18]−connected through
detached motifs. Charge neutralization through protonation is
another critical method for MNCs assembly behavior in aque-
ous media. In the case of [Au25(pMBA)18]−, precise pH adjust-
ment through dialysis could partially protonate the ligands and
lead to emissive assembly of nanoribbons.[198] Importantly, the
shortened distance between clusters by assembly induces PL
emission from aurophilic interactions. Hydrogen bonding is an-
other key factor of aqueous self-assembly, as demonstrated in
Ag44(MNBA)30 (MNBA = 5-mercapto-2-nitrobenzoic acid), where
pH-dependent shifts in hydrogen bonding networks induce a
transition from dispersed clusters to a hexagonal close-packed
structure (Figure 11f).[199] These interactions highlight the in-
tricate interplay of interfacial forces in determining the assem-
bly pathways of MNCs in solution. It is particularly intrigu-
ing to note that the structural insights into MNCs began since
2007 with Ag102(pMBA)44,[43] underscoring the signiﬁcance of
𝜋···𝜋stacking in superstructures of water-soluble MNCs. Such
is the case in the recent advancements successfully achieved
long-pursued ─COOH-functionalized MNCs with a biomimetic
crystallization.[200] The potential of alternative ligands, particu-
larly chain-like ones, remains largely unexplored and warrants
further investigation.
4.3. Encapsulation in Templated Frameworks
Encapsulation in templated frameworks provides a powerful
strategy to integrate MNCs into extended porous materials, en-
hancing their stability, catalytic eﬃciency, and electronic prop-
erties. At the heart of this approach is the interfacial chemistry
of MNCs, where the ligand shell not only anchors clusters to
the host framework but also governs charge transfer, molec-
ular diﬀusion, and structural ordering. Unlike simple deposi-
tion onto supports like graphene[201] and metal oxides,[202] en-
capsulation within well-deﬁned scaﬀolds, such as MOFs, COFs,
HOFs (hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks), zeolites, and even
biomolecular templates, ensures precise spatial positioning of
clusters while modulating their photophysical and catalytic be-
havior. The templated conﬁnement stabilizes otherwise fragile
clusters against aggregation and decomposition while introduc-
ing new functionalities through controlled electronic coupling
and selective permeability. This section explores three primary
approaches to MNC encapsulation: in situ growth within frame-
works, post-synthetic impregnation of preformed clusters, and
ligand-directed framework assembly around MNCs.
In situ growth of MNCs within preformed frameworks re-
lies on ligand-mediated nucleation, where functionalized frame-
works provide anchoring sites that guide metal atom deposi-
tion into spatially conﬁned clusters. A striking example involves
thiol-functionalized COFs, where strong Au−S interactions stabi-
lize Au NCs while mitigating degradation under prolonged light
exposure.[203] This architecture plays a crucial role in an arti-
ﬁcial Z-scheme photocatalytic system, where Au−S−COF link-
ages improve charge separation eﬃciency, highlighting the im-
pact of such encapsulation in interfacial charge transfer. Simi-
larly, a NHC-functionalized MOF (UiO-68-NHC) was used to sta-
bilize Au NCs for CO2 photoreduction, where the Au–NHC co-
valent bonds facilitated eﬃcient electron transfer from Au NCs
to Zr6O4(OH)4 in the framework, enhancing catalytic activity
through a promoted injection of hot electrons into the conduc-
tion band of UiO-68 (Figure 12a).[31]
An alternative approach involves the post-synthetic en-

alternative templating environments for MNC encapsulation.

synthesis of MNCs without extra ligands, which depends on
the functional groups to coordinate with metal.[213] Early stud-

promising for bioimaging and targeted drug delivery. Addition-

varied emissions, as the nucleobase sequence is decisive to emis-
sion colors.[217,218] For example, an amphiphilic DNA framework
has been reported to generate homogeneous and highly lumi-
nescent MNC nanoaggregates with diverse hydrophobic MNCs,
which allows precise control of the aggregation behavior and
results in monodispersed nanoaggregates with sizes of 10 nm
(Figure 13b).[219] Notably, this amphiphilic DNA framework not
only signiﬁcantly enhanced the PLQYs of MNCs up to 3011-
fold, but also provided a programmable platform to regulate the
biorecognition interface of MNCs nanoaggregates.

tailor nanomaterials at atomic level, oﬀering tunable platforms

would inspire the rational design of next-generation MNCs with
even more versatile and sophisticated properties, opening new
avenues for their use in diverse applications. Looking ahead, sev-
eral key directions in this vibrant ﬁeld hold huge promises for
further advancing ligand-programmed MNCs:
1) Ligand encoding for programmable and systematic synthesis
of MNCs. A key bottleneck in precisely understanding ligand
eﬀects at the atomic scale lies in the synthetic challenge of
producing isostructural MNCs with diverse ligands. Bench-
marks like Au13, Au25(SR)18, Ag29, Ag44(SR)30 oﬀer model sys-
tems, yet systematic ligand screening remains limited by syn-
thetic complexity and trial-and-error workﬂows. Future ad-
vances may rely on ligand encoding frameworks, which quan-
tify ligand descriptors (e.g., steric volume, electron-donating
strength, 𝜋-conjugation) to support high-throughput screen-
ing and synthesis planning. In parallel, AI-driven approaches,
including inverse design, generative models, and structure-
to-property prediction, can propose candidate ligands tailored
for speciﬁc functions, such as enhanced stability, catalytic ac-
tivity, or responsiveness.[220,221] Together, these strategies will
enable a more programmable and rational route to explore the
vast chemical space of surface ligands.
2) Stimuli-responsive ligand frameworks for adaptive MNC
functions.
Drawing
inspiration
from
smart/responsive
molecular materials, stimuli-responsive ligands (e.g., azo,
spiropyran, viologen units) oﬀer a path toward switch-
able nanocluster properties under light, pH, or redox
control.[222,223] However, integrating such motifs into MNC
ligands poses challenges: their bulkiness, chemical insta-
bility under reduction, and poor compatibility with cluster
formation protocols often lead to poor yield or structure
disruption. Overcoming this requires modular ligand scaf-
folds that decouple the coordination site from the responsive
moiety, and orthogonal synthetic strategies to introduce
stimuli-responsiveness
post-assembly.
These
advances
could enable real-time control of emission, recognition, or
catalysis, especially in biosensing and dynamic molecular
electronics.[224–226]
3) AI-assisted modeling for dynamic surface behavior predic-
tion. While great progress has been made in core-level struc-
ture prediction for MNCs, simulating the dynamic behavior
of ligand shells on MNC surfaces remains a major challenge
due to the large conﬁgurational space and complex metal-
ligand interactions. AI-assisted approaches, such as machine
learning-based interatomic potential and GraphBNC, have of-
fered eﬃcient and accurate alternatives to traditional molec-
ular dynamics in terms of the prediction of inter-cluster reac-
tions and binding sites of proteins for MNCs.[227,228] These in-
sights are crucial not only for understanding surface-property
relationships but also for guiding cluster assembly, predict-
ing interactions with external environments, and designing
MNCs for targeted applications in catalysis and sensing.
4) Ligand microenvironment programming for enzyme-like se-
lectivity. The quaternary structural resemblance between
MNCs and proteins opens exciting prospects for biomimetic
catalysis, where selectivity arises from a well-deﬁned ligand
microenvironment rather than core reactivity alone. Current
designs often lack such complexity—most rely on general
steric bulk rather than site-speciﬁc interactions. To emu-
late enzymes, ligands must be tailored to create electrostatic
ﬁelds, hydrogen bonding networks, or hydrophobic pockets
that stabilize speciﬁc transition states.[6,229] Realizing this vi-
sion demands ligand design principles informed by protein
catalysis and multi-scale simulations to capture the entropic
and solvation eﬀects in conﬁned ligand spaces. This approach
could unlock MNCs for challenging transformations with pre-
cise chemo-, regio-, or enantioselectivity.
5) Integrating MNCs into hierarchical materials for synergistic
functionality. Coupling atomically precise MNCs with tem-
plated or porous materials oﬀers access to hybrid systems
where individual nanocluster functions are augmented or
coupled. While enhanced PL or catalysis has been demon-
strated, emerging directions include energy conversion (e.g.,
photothermal therapy) and tandem catalysis.[230,231] The chal-
lenge lies in preserving atomic precision during integration
and achieving controlled spatial distribution on supports. Ad-
vances in host-guest templating, supramolecular assembly,
and even lithography can enable new degrees of functional-
ity, especially when paired with in situ characterization tools
to understand interfacial eﬀects.
By integrating these emerging strategies, ligand programming
will remain a cornerstone of MNC surface engineering, driving
innovations in nanoscale reactivity, optoelectronics, and bioin-
spired catalysis. Insights gained from these studies will not only
deepen our understanding of cluster chemistry but also provide
design principles applicable to broader nanomaterial systems,
paving the way for the next generation of adaptive and multi-
functional nanomaterials.

A B S T R A C T
Optical imaging and biosensing in the second near-infrared window (NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm) have gained sig-
nificant interest due to their high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and excellent spatial and temporal resolution. These
features enable accurate monitoring and treatment of deeper bio-pathological processes. Gold nanoclusters (Au
NCs) have emerged as promising candidates for NIR-II emission, offering advantages such as their ultra-small
size, outstanding biocompatibility, large Stokes shift, and favorable photoluminescence (PL) properties. This
review begins by highlighting the benefits and applications of NIR-II luminescent Au NCs in bioimaging and
biosensing. We then explore current strategies to enhance the photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) of Au
NCs, including techniques such as metal doping, ligand engineering, and the aggregation-induced emission (AIE).
Furthermore, we discuss advancements in red-shifting the emission wavelength of Au NCs. Our goal is to provide
researchers with fresh insights into improving and fine-tuning the photoluminescent properties of Au NCs,
thereby broadening their potential applications in NIR-II bioimaging and biosensing.
1. Introduction
Biomedical imaging techniques are essential in modern medicine,
aiding clinicians in diagnosing conditions and guiding surgical proced-
ures [1,2]. However, traditional methods such as ultrasound imaging
(UI), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and computed tomography
(CT) present challenges, including radiation exposure, high costs, and
limitations in resolution, sensitivity, and real-time detection [3,4].
These drawbacks have prompted the development of optical imaging
and sensing technologies, which offer high resolution, sensitivity, and
molecular targeting capabilities [5,6]. And these technologies enable
detailed observation of biological tissue structures and the detection of
low biomarker concentrations, crucial for early disease diagnosis and
understanding biological systems [7–11]. Furthermore, optical imaging
devices are typically more portable, non-invasive, and cost-effective,
making them suitable for real-time applications in various settings,
such as point-of-care diagnostics, intraoperative guidance, and health-
care monitoring [12–15].
Despite these advantages, optical imaging faces its own challenges
[16]. Most techniques are limited to the visible (400–700 nm) and near-
infrared I (NIR-I, 700–900 nm) windows, where biological tissue auto-
fluorescence, light absorption, and scattering can hinder imaging reso-
lution and signal-to-background ratios (SBR, the ratio of the signal
intensity between target and the background, measuring the “contrast”
of the imaging) [17,18]. In contrast, the NIR-II region (1000–1700 nm),
particularly the NIR-IIa window (1300–1400 nm) and the NIR-IIb win-
dow (1500–1700 nm), offers minimal autofluorescence, high signal-to-
noise ratios (SNR, the ratio of the signal strength between target and
the background noise, reflecting the “clarity” of the signal), high spatial
resolution, and deep tissue penetration, making it a promising area for
research (Fig. 1A and B) [19–21]. In this window, autofluorescence is
significantly reduced, enhancing imaging performance in tissues with
high endogenous autofluorescence (Fig. 1C–E) [22–25]. Consequently,
NIR-II imaging shows great potential for medical diagnostics, pathology
analysis, and therapeutic interventions.

While longer wavelengths scatter less, many luminescent materials
struggle to achieve high emission wavelengths within the NIR-II win-
dow, which is necessary to minimize tissue autofluorescence and
background interference [28]. Various NIR-II light-emitting materials
have been explored, including rare earth nanoparticles [29–31], quan-
tum dots [32–34], organic nanoparticles [35–37], and single-walled
carbon nanotubes [38–40]. Unfortunately, these materials frequently
suffer from low QY, poor stability, and limited biocompatibility [41,42].
Therefore, there is a pressing need to enhance their performance or
develop new, efficient, and stable NIR-II luminescent materials.
Gold nanoclusters (Au NCs) are ultra-small, atomically precise par-
ticles with a core-shell structure, typically consisting of a metallic core
made of several to hundreds of gold atoms, surrounded by a shell of
metal-ligand motifs [43–45]. The motifs are arranged in a “short-bridge”
structure, providing a protective layer around the core [46]. The Au
NCs, with sizes ranging from 1 to 3 nm, exhibit a unique combination of
properties found in both larger nanoparticles and smaller organome-
tallic molecules [47,48]. This size range enhances their versatility and
broadens their potential applications [49,50]. They also possess well-
defined structures and demonstrate molecular-like transitions between
their highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMO) and lowest unoccu-
pied molecular orbitals (LUMO) [51–54]. The core-shell structure can be
finely tuned at the atomic level, allowing for significant alterations in
their physicochemical properties [55–57]. Additionally, Au NCs are
known for their simple synthesis, low toxicity, large Stokes shift,
excellent biocompatibility, and high renal clearance, making them a
promising class of luminescent materials for NIR-II imaging and sensing
[58–60].
Currently, research on NIR-II luminescent Au NCs is rapidly
advancing [61,62]. The development of Au NCs with atomically precise
structures and predictable luminescent properties holds great potential
for enhancing PLQY and controlling emission wavelengths, benefiting
bioimaging
and
biosensing
applications
[52,63–65].
However,
achieving precise control over the PL properties of Au NCs remains a
complex challenge [19,66]. Researchers face significant technical hur-
dles in managing cluster structures and optical properties to improve PL
efficiency and stability [67].
While several reviews have summarized progress in NIR-II lumi-
nescent materials, focusing on synthesis, characterization, and applica-
tions, there is a lack of comprehensive summaries on strategies for
controlling PLQY and red-shifting emission wavelengths [44,68–70].

2. NIR-II gold clusters for bioimaging and biosensing
2.1. Bioimaging
Medical imaging technologies have significantly advanced our un-
derstanding of diseases and improved early diagnosis [71–73]. Recently,
NIR-II photoluminescent imaging probes have emerged as non-invasive,
efficient, and safe tools for detection and treatment [74–76]. These
probes have found applications in tumor, kidney, bone, and vascular
imaging [77–79]. By utilizing the optical properties of the NIR-II win-
dow, they reduce background interference, enabling penetration
through soft tissue up to centimeters and providing high spatial reso-
lution of approximately 10 μm [42,80]. As a result, NIR-II probes are
effective for visualizing and quantifying molecular information in
biomedical research [1]. Moreover, optimizing the optical properties
and biocompatibility of Au NCs has further enhanced the sensitivity and
specificity of NIR-II imaging, paving the way for clinical applications
and broader use in medical diagnostics and therapies [81,82].
For example, glutathione (GSH) in tumor cells can bind to platinum
(Pt), forming GSH-Pt conjugates, which lead to resistance against
platinum-based chemotherapy [83]. Reducing GSH levels in tumor cells
is essential for improving sensitivity to these treatments [84]. Yang et al.
developed AuNCs-Pt, a dual-function cluster drug that not only excels in
NIR-II imaging but also enhances the effects of platinum chemotherapy
(Fig. 2A) [85]. It displays two emission peaks at 945 and 1035 nm, with

NIR-II imaging also allows for precise monitoring of renal function,
enabling early detection of issues and timely interventions to prevent
renal disease progression [86]. Recent studies synthesized dual-ligand-
stabilized Au NCs by combining mercaptoundecanoic acid (MHA) with
various ligands, including cysteamine (Cystm), 2-mercaptoethanol
(ME), and DL-dithiothreitol (DTT) (Fig. 2F) [87]. These Au NCs
exhibited a NIR-II emission peak at 1064 nm and QYs ranging from 1.4
% to 2.0 %. In vivo experiments showed that MHA/Cystm-AuNCs
provided a strong SBR of 4.53, highlighting their potential for high-
contrast renal imaging (Fig. 2G). Following tail vein injection, the kid-
neys were clearly distinguishable within 5 min, and after 10 min, the
signal shifted to the bladder as the Au NCs were cleared in urine
(Fig. 2H). These findings indicate that Au NCs not only enable high-
resolution imaging of deep tissues but also exhibit excellent renal
clearance, making them particularly useful for monitoring conditions
such as renal ischemia-reperfusion (RIR) and unilateral ureteral
obstruction (UUO).

can have severe side effects with prolonged use [89]. Therefore, devel-

Despite advancements in NIR-II imaging for cancer and renal di-
agnostics, developing high quantum yield vascular contrast agents with
good biocompatibility and minimal organ accumulation remains chal-
lenging [91]. Yu et al. developed water-soluble gold nanocluster
(AuMHA/TDT NCs) by utilizing mercaptohexanoic acid (MHA) and tetra
(ethylene glycol) dithiol (TDT) as ligands (Fig. 3F) [92]. The AuMHA/
TDT NCs exhibited emission peaks above 1250 nm and a high QY of
approximately 6 %. In vivo experiments showed that intravenous
injection of AuMHA/TDT NCs allowed clear visualization of the vascular
network, enabling quantitative detection of vascular complexity at
depths exceeding 4 mm. This approach facilitated non-invasive identi-
fication of subtle vascular abnormalities in mice lacking bone morpho-
genetic protein 9 (Bmp9). By integrating a non-invasive whole-body
vascular imaging system with Monte Carlo constrained reconstruction
(MCR) image processing, spatial resolution in the short-wave infrared
(SWIR) window (1250–1700 nm) improved by 59 %, with contrast
enhanced by an order of magnitude (Fig. 3G–J). This combination of
high-brightness nanoprobes and advanced imaging techniques repre-
sents a significant advancement in non-invasive vascular disease diag-
nosis and biomedical applications.
NIR-II Au NCs have shown considerable promise in bioimaging [93].
Through strategies such as ligand modification and surface functional-
ization, researchers have developed various NIR-II Au NCs with benefits
including longer emission wavelengths, excellent biocompatibility, and
high spatial resolution [94,95]. As synthesis technologies improve and
our understanding of NIR-II Au NCs' biological behavior deepens, these
nanomaterials are set to play a vital role in disease diagnosis, opening
new avenues for biomedical research and clinical applications [96].

For instance, hydrogen sulfide (H2S), a physiological gas signaling
molecule, plays a crucial role in various biochemical processes,
including inflammation, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer [99]. H₂S
can disrupt the Au(0)@Au(I) structure within Au NCs, altering their
electronic states and leading to photoluminescence quenching [100]. To
address this, Li et al. developed a core-satellite cluster by assembling
lanthanide-doped Er@YNPs (Ln@AuNCs) through ligand anchoring
(Fig. 4A) [101]. Under 808 nm laser excitation, Ln@AuNCs exhibited
dual emission bands centered at 1100 and 1550 nm, with strong NIR-II
photoluminescence at 1550 nm attributed to Er3+. The NIR-II photo-
luminescence at 1100 nm was enhanced by approximately 2.5-fold due
to restricted intramolecular movement of thiolate ligands. Treatment
with varying concentrations of NaHS resulted in rapid and significant
quenching of the NIR-II photoluminescence at 1100 nm within 30 min,
while the 1550 nm emission remained largely unaffected. Additionally,
the photoluminescence intensity of Ln@AuNCs remained stable after
exposure to various potential interfering substances, such as peroxyni-
trite (ONOO−), bovine serum albumin (BSA), and ascorbic acid (AA).
These results indicate that the high sensitivity and selectivity of this
quenching response to H2S make Ln@AuNCs promising candidates for
in vivo visualization of hepatic H2S detection, delivery, and prodrug
screening, which could broaden the application of AuNCs-based bio-
sensing technology.
Traditional methods for detecting gastric acid, such as gastric juice
extraction, digestible electronic capsules, and endoscopy, are often
complex, time-consuming, and uncomfortable for patients [102,103].
To overcome these challenges, Liang et al. encapsulated GSH-AuNCs
with polydopamine (PDA) to enhance NIR-II luminescence and stabil-
ity [104]. They conjugated methylene blue (MB) onto the surface of
PDA-coated Au NCs to impart pH-responsive properties for gastric acid
imaging (Fig. 4B). In this mechanism, the MB adsorption layer quenches
the NIR-II photoluminescence emission of Au NCs at 1050 nm through
photoinduced electron transfer (PET). Under acidic conditions in the
stomach, protonation of the PDA surface leads to the detachment of MB,
restoring the NIR-II photoluminescence of Au NCs (Fig. 4C and D). This
pH-sensitive “off-on” nanoprobe provides a simple, safe, and non-
invasive method for in vivo gastric acid detection, suitable for moni-
toring changes in gastric acid due to dietary intake, acid-reducing
medications, and gastric ulcer diseases. This offers significant implica-
tions for assessing gastric function and diagnosing related conditions.

3. Strategies for QY enhancement
Non-radiative relaxation is the main process that deactivates the
excited state in NIR-II Au NCs, leading to low QYs, often below 1 %
[110]. To enhance the QYs of Au NCs and broaden their applications in
bioimaging and biosensing, it is important to explore effective strategies
for improvement [19]. The PLQY can be expressed as PLQY = kr/(kr +
knr), where suppressing non-radiative decay (knr) and increasing radi-
ative decay (kr) can effectively improve QY [111]. Various strategies
have been developed to enhance the QY of Au NCs, including metal
doping [112], ligand engineering [113], host-guest interactions [114],
and the aggregation-induced emission (AIE) [115]. These strategies aim
to enhance the rigidity of the Au NCs surface [116], expand the HOMO-
LUMO energy gap [117], optimize ligand-to-metal charge transfer
(LMCT)
[118],
and
strengthen
metal-metal
interactions
through
aggregation-induced effects [119]. These approaches effectively sup-
press non-radiative relaxation or energy transfer and significantly in-
crease the QYs of Au NCs.
3.1. Metal atom doping
Metal doping can significantly influence the electronic and geomet-
rical structure of metal NCs due to the synergistic effect between
different metals, thus exhibiting enhanced physical and chemical
properties [117]. Moreover, the introduction of heterogenous metal
atoms typically does not significantly alter the core structure of the Au
NCs [120]. Therefore, studying doped metal NCs not only aids in the
design of novel alloy NCs with enhanced QY but also provides valuable
insights into the PL mechanisms of the alloy NCs [121].
Since the initial report of the Au24Cd1(SR)18 cluster in 2015, interest
in research related to this cluster has steadily increased [122,123]. For
instance, Huang et al. reported on the synthesis of the Au24Cd1 cluster
through the doping of a single cadmium (Cd) atom onto the staple motif
of Au25 [124]. The PL intensity for Au24Cd1 increased by 51.1-fold
compared to that of the pure Au25 cluster (Fig. 5 A and B). The QY of
Au24Cd1 was approximately 1.1 %. The enhanced PL intensity associated
with Cd doping is attributed to the shorter Cd–Au bonds and the lower
electronegativity of Cd (1.69) compared to Au (2.54), which strengthens
the interaction between the two metals and facilitates electron transfer
from Cd to Au (Fig. 5C). This shift in electron density from the staple to
the core introduces a new energy level that promotes radiative recom-
bination. Furthermore, the Au24Cd1 clusters demonstrated significant
potential in improving renal function and reducing oxidative stress and
inflammation in an acute kidney injury (AKI) murine model, indicating
their promise for noninvasive detection and treatment of AKI.
Hirai et al. synthesized a series of MAu12(dppe)5Cl2 clusters (MAu12;
M = Au, Pd, Pt, Rh; dppe = 1,2 - bis (diphenylphosphino) ethane) [112].
Compared to the QY of the undoped Au13 cluster (0.117), the QYs for
PtAu12, RhAu12 and IrAu12 were significantly enhanced, measuring
0.606, 0.464, and 0.665, respectively. Notably, the IrAu12 in dichloro-
methane exhibited the highest reported PLQY among M@Au12 clusters,

3.2. Ligand engineering
It is well established that the protective ligands of Au NCs signifi-
cantly influence the electronic structure, morphology, and optical
properties [128]. In particular, the LMCT process often dominates the PL
characteristics of NCs [118,129]. Ligand engineering is a straightfor-
ward and effective approach to design functional materials such as Au
NCs with high PLQY [130]. Consequently, several strategies have been
proposed to optimize the photoluminescent performance of Au NCs
through ligand engineering, including increasing the structural rigidity
of ligands [131], and fine-tuning ligand surfaces [113], etc [132].
Additionally, the spatial environment around the ligand surface also
exerts an influence on the luminescent performance [133].

alters the electronic density and vibrational modes of Au NCs, conse-

photoexcited electrons and further improves the QY of Au25. In the
structural framework of Au25 nanoclusters, electron transitions from the

while suppressing non-radiative transitions, leading to a significant

PolySC4AP/FGGC@AuNC assembly with a mitochondrial-targeting
group, triphenyl (2-propenyl) phosphonium bromide (TPP).
Similarly, Deng et al. utilized the aldehyde groups in 2,6-pyridinedi-
carboxaldehyde (PDA) to react with the amino groups on the surface of
the water-soluble glutathione (HSG) protected Au22(SG)18 (Fig. 8C)
[144]. This formed imine (−CH = N−) bonds, which endowed
Au22(SG)18 with the advantageous properties of Schiff bases, including
enhanced stability, minimal by-products, and increased luminescence.
The research found that cross-linking Au22(SG)18 with PDA reduced the
core-shell distance and formed a larger π⋅⋅⋅π conjugated system, result-
ing in a reduction in Stokes shift (approximately 0.06 eV). The results
indicate that the surface-bound complex restricted intramolecular mo-
tion (RIM), significantly reduced non-radiative decay rates, and
increased radiative decay rates. Consequently, the PLQY of PDA-
Au22(SG)18 reached 48 ± 1.4 % at room temperature, which is nearly 11

providing additional evidence that the non-radiative pathways in PDA-
Au22(SG)18 were suppressed (Fig. 8E and F). The cross-linking did not
lead to significant aggregation of Au22(SG)18, ruling out the possibility
that the increased QY was due to AIE effects.

by

arrangement of metal cores and conjugated ligands may offer new
pathways to modulate charge transfer dynamics and extend emission
lifetimes.

3.4. AIE enhancement
Recent research into the PL mechanisms of Au NCs has revealed the
potential of AIE, a phenomenon first identified by the Tang team [145].
AIE is characterized by a transition from weak to strong emission as
materials aggregate [146]. This process alters the intermolecular in-
teractions within the clusters, effectively reducing π–π stacking and
preventing the quenching of excited states [147]. As a result, more en-
ergy from excited states is released as luminescence, significantly
enhancing the possibility of radiative transitions [148,149]. The AIE
properties of Au NCs are associated with LMCT or LMMCT, and are
highly influenced by the surface characteristics of the clusters [150].
Various strategies can be employed to enhance AIE, such as improving
the electron-donating capacity of protective ligands, increasing the
metal(I) content in surface motifs, and anchoring metal cores with
ligand-rich crosslinking sites [151,152]. These approaches can minimize
non-radiative energy loss and improve the rate of radiative energy
transfer, thereby increasing the PLQY of Au NCs. Additionally, AIE can
be triggered by stimuli such as Cd2+ [115], Ag+ [153], Pb2+ [154], and
Gd3+ [155], which further boosts the luminescence intensity of Au NCs.
The exploration and application of AIE in Au NCs not only provide
valuable insights into PL behavior but also facilitate the development of
highly efficient and stable luminescent probes for various bioimaging
and biosensing applications [156–158].

Au NCs. During solvent-induced aggregation, the Au(I)-thiolate com-

GSH-AuNCs exhibited a 3.5-fold increase in PL intensity, and a 2.1-fold
extension in luminescence lifetime (Fig. 9J). The research demonstrated
that the aggregation of GSH-AuNCs increases the average distance be-
tween Au(I) atoms, and hinders intramolecular rotation and vibration of
GSH-AuNCs. As a result, the polyArg-GSH-AuNCs displayed significantly
enhanced luminescence intensity. Furthermore, by stimulating the AIE
of GSH-AuNCs with four different cationic peptides (including AG73,
GR, pYR, and RGDR5), four types of biosensors were developed, which
have the ability to detect heparin, trypsin in bodily fluids, and ALP in
cancer cells.
3.5. Impact of ambient conditions
To optimize the QY and luminescent performance of Au NCs for
further applications, it is essential to consider not only the aforemen-
tioned strategies but also to take into account the effects of ambient
conditions, including temperature and pH, etc [162–165]. Specifically,
variations in pH can modify the chemical ambient conditions on the
surface of Au NCs, thereby influencing the luminescence intensity and
stability [166,167]. Similarly, changes in temperature can affect the
luminescence behavior of Au NCs by influencing the structural or elec-
tronic states [168,169]. Therefore, when designing and applying Au
NCs, it is essential to consider and optimize these ambient conditions
parameters to ensure optimal luminescent performance and application
outcomes under specific conditions [163,164,170].
For example, Zhou et al. synthesized pH-responsive near-infrared
emitting Au NCs (PDMAEMA-AuNCs) by incorporating poly [2-(dime-
thylamino) ethyl methacrylate] (PDMAEMA) with thiol (TA) groups into
the ligand (Fig. 10A) [166]. At pH = 11.0, the QY is 0.84 %, but it in-
creases to 3.5 % at pH = 4.1 with an average PL lifetime of 3.6 μs
(Fig. 10B and C). The observed pH-dependent PLQY is due to the pro-
tonation and deprotonation of TA groups under varying pH conditions,
which alters the electronic structure of PDMAEMA-AuNCs and signifi-
cantly impacts their optical performance. Specifically, under alkaline
conditions (pH = 7–11), deprotonated TA groups have low-energy
molecular orbitals that facilitate photo-induced electron transfer be-
tween TA groups and the Au–S bonds on the cluster surface, which
disrupts radiative transitions and leads to a substantial decrease in PL
intensity. Conversely, under acidic conditions (pH = 4–7), protonated
TA groups possess higher-energy molecular orbitals which impede
electron transfer to the Au–S motif, thus restoring the PLQY intensity.
Similarly, Liu et al. reported significant changes in the PL properties of
Au25Capt18 (Capt = captopril) under varying pH conditions [167]. For
example, under neutral conditions (pH = 7), the emission intensity of
Au25Capt18 is relatively low, whereas at pH = 2, the emission intensity
increases by an order of magnitude, with the QY rising from 0.37 % to
3.9 %. The enhancement in luminescence is attributed to the low pH
treatment converting Au25Capt18 into a more luminescent Au23Capt17,
resulting in a 13-fold increase in PL intensity. These studies demon-
strated that the pH-sensitive PL enhancement is closely related to the
changes in the structure of cluster and composition, providing valuable
insights for further applications.

4. Strategies for red-shifting emission wavelengths
Red-shifting the emission wavelengths of Au NCs into the near-
infrared region enables deeper tissue penetration and reduces back-
ground interference in biological systems, which is essential for
enhancing their applications in bioimaging and biosensing [20,93].
According to Planck formula (λ = hc/E, where h is Planck constant and c
is the speed of light) [171], the photon energy is inversely proportional
to the emitted wavelength, the lower the energy, the longer the wave-
length. Therefore, reducing the HOMO-LUMO gap (E) is a key
mechanism for achieving red-shifted emission [172]. When a photon
excites an electron from the HOMO to the LUMO, the electron enters an
excited state. As it returns to the ground state, it emits a photon with
energy directly related to the HOMO-LUMO gap. A smaller HOMO-
LUMO gap means that less energy is required for this transition,
resulting in emitted photons with lower energy and a corresponding
shift in emission wavelength toward the NIR region [109,173–175]. In
this chapter, we will explore the fundamental principles and methods for
reducing the HOMO-LUMO gap in Au NCs. We will discuss strategies
such as heteroatom doping [176], structural modulation of the metal
core [177], ligand effects [178], and optimization of ambient conditions
[170]. These approaches not only effectively tune the electronic struc-
ture and optical properties of Au NCs but also open new avenues for
designing
nanomaterials
with
specific
functionalities
[179–182].
Therefore, it is essential to examine these strategies in detail, focusing on
their roles and mechanisms in red-shifting the emission wavelengths of
Au NCs. This exploration aims to provide both a theoretical foundation
and practical guidance for developing Au NCs with enhanced optical
properties.

and significant red shifts in both optical absorption and emission.

Moreover, due to steric effects, larger ligands can alter the geometric
structure of the Au NCs, affecting their electronic energy levels
[188,189]. Therefore, ligand engineering is one of the most employed
strategies for achieving a red shift in the emission wavelength of Au NCs
[190,191].
For example, Bai et al. synthesized Au25DTX-Cl and Au25DTX-Br NCs
using o-ethyl dithiocarbonate (DTX) bidentate ligands (Fig. 13A) [178].
These Au NCs belong to the P1 space group and consist of a 25-atom gold
core (Au25), ten PPh3 protecting ligands, five DTX ligands at the waist,
and two halide atoms (Cl or Br). The unidirectional arrangement of DTX
ligands at the waist is considered the source of chirality (Fig. 13B).
Compared to thiol-stabilized Au25, which has an emission peak wave-
length around 990 nm, the Au25DTX-Cl and Au25DTX-Br exhibit red-
shifted emission wavelengths in the NIR-II range at 1075 nm and
1038 nm, respectively (Fig. 13C). The introduction of DTX ligands
transforms the D5d symmetric structure of Au25 into a D5 chiral config-
uration, forming a staggered arrangement of Au25 nanorods. This
change lowers the energy of the triplet state (T1) responsible for NIR-II
emission from the metal cluster center (1.439 eV for overlapped Au25
nanorods vs. 1.283 eV for staggered Au25 nanorods) (Fig. 13D and E).
This ligand-induced structural change is considered the main reason for
the red shift in the emission wavelength of the Au NCs.

[181]. Under 450 nm excitation, the Au NCs exhibited progressively red-
shifted emission peaks at 658 nm, 660 nm, 670 nm, and 987 nm,
respectively (Fig. 13G). It was found that the red shift is due to shorter
Au(I)-SR motifs bringing the ligands closer to the Au core, which en-
hances the d-orbital overlap and electronic coupling between Au atoms.
This promotes the formation of a more compact structure in the Au NCs,
which strengthens the interactions between Au atoms, increasing elec-
tron delocalization within the core, and consequently reducing the
HOMO-LUMO gap. Therefore, maintaining shorter Au(I)-SR motifs can
red-shift the emission wavelength into the NIR-II region, enabling
broader applications.

5. Conclusion and future perspectives
Biomedical imaging plays a crucial role in disease diagnosis [1,10].
Among the various imaging methods, optical imaging has garnered
significant attention due to its high resolution, real-time capability, and
non-invasiveness [5,6]. Notably, imaging in the NIR-II region allows for
enhanced resolution and deeper tissue penetration [10,21]. Recently,
gold nanoclusters (Au NCs) have been widely utilized in NIR-II imaging
and sensing applications due to their exceptional optical properties
[63,93]. The PLQY and emission wavelength of Au NCs significantly
impact imaging performance: a high PLQY enhances image brightness
and SNR, resulting in clearer images; while emission wavelengths in the
1000–1700 nm NIR-II region notably improve tissue penetration and
reduce autofluorescence interference, facilitating deep-tissue imaging.
These factors contribute to imaging that offers high brightness, contrast,
and deep tissue penetration. In this review, we summarize recent ad-
vances in the use of NIR-II Au NCs for bioimaging and biosensing. We
specifically discuss strategies to enhance the PLQY and red-shift the
emission wavelengths of Au NCs, including metal doping, ligand effects,
and variations in ambient conditions. It is worth noting that NIR-II op-
tical imaging technology based on Au NCs is still in its infancy. While
significant progress has been made, most Au NCs continue to suffer from
low PLQYs and short emission wavelengths, limiting their luminescent
efficiency. Moreover, the PL mechanisms underlying these Au NCs
remain poorly understood. To address these challenges, further in-depth
research is essential to improve their luminescent properties and unlock
their full potential for broader applications in bioimaging and bio-
sensing. Key directions for future research are outlined below.
(1) Enhancing PLQY: First, non-radiative relaxation can be effec-
tively reduced by limiting core and surface vibrations within the
Au NCs through surface hardening or the introduction of rigid
ligands [137,193]. In future studies, molecular rotation and vi-
bration can be effectively restricted by ligand introduction of
non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonding, electro-
static forces and C −H⋅⋅⋅π interactions. In addition, the induction
of ionic dipole interactions or hydrophobic effects through guest
molecules with multifunctional groups can also be effective in
restricting molecular motions and thus reducing non-radiative
relaxation. Second, anchoring chromophores to the surface of
Au NCs can sensitize the clusters through resonance energy
transfer, thereby enhancing the PLQY [194]. The effective energy
transfer relies on the strong overlap between the absorption
spectrum of the chromophore and the emission spectrum of the
Au NCs, which is a critical factor in facilitating energy transfer
[195]. This process holds significant importance in the design and
application of Au NCs, paving a way for enhancing the lumines-
cent properties of Au NCs in the future. Third, it is generally
observed that as the Au core size decreases, the energy levels of
electrons and holes become discrete, resulting in an increase in
the HOMO-LUMO gap [183,184]. Upon excitation, electrons at
higher energy levels can avoid non-radiative recombination
processes with lower energy states [196]. Additionally, estab-
lishing the relationship between core size and QY is quite chal-
lenging when focusing solely on the size of the Au core without
considering the specific structures resulting from metal doping or
different ligands. The electronic structures of ligands and/or
doped metals influence the electron density of the clusters and
can also modify the mechanisms of LMCT and MLCT [130],
thereby profoundly affecting the optical properties of Au NCs. In
the future, it is necessary to maintain relative consistency in
experimental conditions. Only in this way can achieve a more
comprehensive understanding of how Au core size or other fac-
tors influence the mechanisms of PLQY, thereby leading to the
optimization of Au NCs for bioimaging and sensing. Fourth, ac-
cording to the Beer-Lambert law [197], absorbance (A) is a
measure of the light absorption characteristics of a material, and
is determined by the product of the molar absorption coefficient
(ε), path length (l), and concentration (c) of the solution. The QY
can also be defined as the ratio of emitted photons to absorbed
photons. Therefore, if the material exhibits fewer non-radiative
processes, a higher absorbance can enhance its emission effi-
ciency, thereby improving the QY. The size and shape of Au NCs
significantly affect their molar absorption coefficient [198]. In
general, smaller Au NCs typically exhibit broader absorption
bands and stronger absorption capacities [199], while non-
spherical Au NCs (e.g., nanorods) tend to have stronger optical
absorption properties [165,200]. Thus, by modulating the size
and shape of the nanoclusters, their absorption at specific
wavelengths can be enhanced, which in turn boosts the QY.
Additionally, the use of electron-donating ligands can increase
the electron density on the Au NCs, raising the energy levels of
the valence band and thus improving light absorption. However,
it is important to note that if non-radiative transitions are likely to
occur, the QY may remain low, even if the Au NCs demonstrate
strong absorption capabilities.
(2) Red-shifting Emission Wavelengths: In the study of the PL
emission wavelength of Au NCs, the HOMO-LUMO gap directly
influences the efficiency of electron transitions [201]. A smaller
HOMO-LUMO gap results in a decreased energy of electron
transitions, making clusters more prone to redshift of the emis-
sion wavelength. First, metal doping can introduce new elec-
tronic states and charge transfer channels. Especially when the
dopant elements have different electronegativities, they will ex-
change charges with the electrons in the Au NCs, changing the
overall energy level structure and electron density, and thus
modulating the HOMO-LUMO gap [176,179]. Therefore, in
future studies, exploring the incorporation of transition metals
such as zinc, nickel or cobalt, could be beneficial to lower HOMO-
LUMO gap. Additionally, investigating different doping ratios to
identify the optimal conditions for red-shifting the emission
wavelength is also an important research direction. Second, li-
gands can affect the energy levels and the overall electronic dis-
tribution of the cluster [190]. Ligands with strong electron-
donating or electron-withdrawing properties can modulate the
HOMO-LUMO gap, leading to shifts in emission wavelengths
[191]. In the future, the use of bifunctional ligands containing
electron-donating and electron-accepting groups, may help
modulate the electronic structure of the Au NCs, thereby lowering
the HOMO-LUMO gap.
(3) Balancing of QY and Emission Wavelengths: In the process of
enhancing QY, it is necessary to reduce non-radiative pathways to
achieve more efficient optical transitions. This process tends to
increase the energy of the excited state, leading to an expansion
of the HOMO-LUMO gap and thus a blue shift in the emission
wavelength. Conversely, to achieve a red shift in the emission
wavelength, it is generally required to lower the HOMO-LUMO
gap to facilitate low-energy transitions, which results in a
reduction in QY. Therefore, future research faces a significant
challenge in optimizing the design and synthesis of Au NCs to
enhance PLQY while simultaneously achieving a red shift in the
emission wavelength. This endeavor may involve the fusion of
various regulatory strategies and a deeper understanding of the
PL mechanisms in Au NCs.
(4) Deepening Understanding of PL Mechanisms: Although the PL
properties of Au NCs have garnered significant attention, the
underlying PL mechanisms are not yet fully understood. Unlike
traditional dyes, the luminescence mechanism of Au NCs is more
complicated, involving various emission processes such as fluo-
rescence [137], phosphorescence [202], and TADF [203].
Currently, the effects of ligand chemistry, gold core size, metal
doping, temperature, and solvent on the emission mechanisms of

sufficient experimental validation. To advance the understanding

understanding of the AIE mechanism and detailed assessment
of external ambient conditions on luminescence properties are
also essential. Further study should focus on integrating theo-
retical calculations, such as density functional theory (DFT)
[204,205], with experimental results. This approach will enhance
our understanding of the relationship between the structures of
Au NCs and their optical properties, providing a solid theoretical
foundation for the design of innovative, high-performance NIR-II
probes.
(5) Enhancing Targeting Ability of NIR-II Probes: To improve the
targeting ability of NIR-II probes, the primary challenge that
needs to be addressed is how to achieve specific activation in
target tissues after the probes have distributed throughout the
body [206,207]. Currently, many probes generate false positives
due to the non-specific distribution of pathological parameters,
making the reduction of background interference a key challenge.
Additionally, most probes can only perform qualitative analysis.
However, quantitative analysis is more important, as it can
effectively monitor disease progression, assess treatment efficacy,
and determine prognosis. Consequently, future research should
focus on the development of PL probes with in vivo quantitative
analysis capabilities. Furthermore, NIR-II Au NCs biosensing
probes are scarce. Most of these probes are single-emission “turn-
on” type probes susceptible to non-specific factors. In contrast,
the probes with dual-emission PL signals can enhance detection
accuracy, aiding in the better resolution of complex physiological
and pathological processes.
(6) Improving Biocompatibility of NIR-II Probes: In the biomed-
ical field, biocompatibility of NIR-II probes is crucial. While most
Au NCs demonstrate excellent performance in PL imaging and
have sizes below the renal filtration threshold (approximately
5.5 nm), their high hydrophobicity can result in accumulation in
the liver. This accumulation can slow down in vivo metabolism
and restrict their broader use. In contrast, hydrophilic Au NCs,
featured by non-toxicity, good biocompatibility, and photo-
stability, have become ideal materials for biological applications.
For instance, employing water-soluble molecules like γ-gluta-
thione (GSH) and cysteine (Cys) as ligands not only increases the
water solubility of NCs but also enhances their biocompatibility
and potential for functionalization. Therefore, future research
should focus on enhancing the biocompatibility of Au NCs while
retaining their NIR-II PL properties.
(7) Promoting Clinical Application and Translation: As research
on the PL of Au NCs advances, their clinical application prospects
become increasingly promising. In the future, researchers should
focus on transitioning high-performance Au NCs from the labo-
ratory to clinical settings, particularly in critical areas such as
early disease diagnosis, precision therapy, and dynamic imaging.
NIR-II emitting Au NCs could provide higher resolution, deeper
imaging capabilities, and more precise sensing functions. Addi-
tionally, integrating Au NCs with biological markers and drug
delivery systems enhances their multifunctionality, enabling
comprehensive applications in “theranostics”, including photo-
thermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT). This
integration is expected to propel the development of biomedical
technology, tackle challenges in the field, and ultimately facili-
tate breakthroughs from basic research to clinical applications.
In summary, future research should continue to optimize the struc-
ture and functionality of Au NCs to enhance their bioimaging
capabilities in the NIR-II window while improving their stability and
specificity in complex biological environments. We anticipate that in the
near
future,

will

to

tings, leading to groundbreaking advancements in the biomedical field.

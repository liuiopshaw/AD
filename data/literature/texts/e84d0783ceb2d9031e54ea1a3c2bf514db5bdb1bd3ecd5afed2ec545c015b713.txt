A B S T R A C T   
The aim of this paper is to achieve in situ photochemical synthesis of silver nanoclusters (AgNCs) stabilized by the 
multiple-amine groups of chitosan (Ch@AgNCs) with luminescent and photothermal properties. Ch@AgNCs 
were obtained by applying a fast and simple methodology previously described by our group. Direct function­
alization of AgNCs with chitosan template provided new nanohybrids directly in water solution, both in the 
presence or absence of oxygen. The formation of hybrid AgNCs could be monitored by the rapid increase of the 
absorption and emission maximum band with light irradiation time. New Ch@AgNCs not only present photo­
luminescent properties but also photothermal properties when irradiated with near infrared light (NIR), trans­
ducing efficiently NIR into heat and increasing the temperature of the medium up to 23 ◦C. The chitosan 
polymeric shell associated to AgNCs works as a protective support stabilizing the metal cores, facilitating the 
storage of nanohybrids and preserving luminescent, photothermal and bactericide properties.   
1. Introduction 
Metal nanoclusters (NCs) range from a few dozen to hundreds of 
reduced metal atoms conveniently protected with a defined ligand layer. 
The reduced dimensions and confinement of these NCs favor the 
development of new properties that are not present in bulk (e.g. elec­
tronic, optical, magnetic, catalytic, antimicrobial, etc.) (Khandelwal & 
Poddar, 2017; Losch et al., 2019; Tao, Li, Ren, & Qu, 2015; Yang, Wang 
et al., 2018) without neglecting other important properties as the po­
tential antimicrobial activity related to its metallic nature (Zheng, 
Setyawati, Leong, & Xie, 2017). For example, iron oxide or cobalt does 
not exhibit any magnetic behavior in bulk, but nanoparticles (NPs) 
synthesized from these metals can exhibit superparamagnetism 
(Heuer-Jungemann et al., 2019; Laurent et al., 2008). 
Additionally, when some metals are confined to sub-nanometer scale 
comparable to those of the typical quantum-mechanical lengths such as 
the Fermi wavelength of an electron (≤2 nm), the electronic structure 
and optical responses are modulated by quantum effects (Park, Kim, 
Park, & Kim, 2016). Within this confinement scale, metal clusters could 
exhibit discrete electronic structures and typical properties of small 
molecules, such as having size-dependent HOMO–LUMO transitions and 
high luminescence (Khandelwal & Poddar, 2017). Because of their 
ultra-small hydrodynamic diameters, chemical stability and adaptable 
surface properties, metallic nanoclusters are revealed as promising 
fluorescent probes for innovative diagnostic imaging in biomedicine, 
showing extraordinary luminescence and low toxicity (Benavides, 
Quijada-Garrido, & García, 2020; Hu et al., 2016; Hu, Liu, Zhang, Chai, 
& Huang, 2018; Kang & Zhu, 2019; Khandelwal & Poddar, 2017). 
In the last decade, important advances in the development of fluo­
rescent NCs, mainly involving Au and Ag, have been made to take

advantage of their attractive physicochemical properties (Adnan, 
Ahmad, Kuchel, & Boyer, 2017; Kang & Zhu, 2019; Wang, Xu, & Xu, 
2011; Yang, Wang et al., 2018). These NCs have attracted the attention 
of biomedical researchers due to their potential applications as sensors, 
imaging probes or drug delivery systems (Jin, Zeng, Zhou, & Chen, 
2016; Yarramala, Doshi, & Rao, 2015; Zhang & Wang, 2014). However, 
the preparation of stable, monodisperse and well-defined NCs is still a 
challenge due to their high tendency towards irreversible coalescence 
and rapid loss of their associated photophysical properties. Small bio­
molecules such as different amino acids or macro-biomolecules such as 
enzymes, DNA or polysaccharides have been used as stabilizing ligands 
of these metallic NCs (Emam & Ahmed, 2016; Heuer-Jungemann et al., 
2019; Kumar, Roy, Alex, Chandrasekaran, & Mukherjee, 2016; Maretti, 
Billone, Liu, & Scaiano, 2009; Pandya, Lad, Singh, & Shanker, 2016; 
Zhou, Zhang, Yang, Wang, & Wang, 2017). The stabilizing ligand de­
termines the photophysical properties of NCs, including their emission 
wavelength. For example, when carboxylic acid groups are involved in 
the chemical stabilization of AgNCs, the emission wavelength of NCs is 
usually in the red region. On the contrary, if amine groups are involved 
in the stabilization process, the emission wavelength is shifted to yellow 
(Chen et al., 2016; Maretti et al., 2009). Moreover, not only the pho­
tophysical properties but also the antimicrobial properties of AgNCs 
depend on the size, shape, surface charge, surface coating, solution 
chemistry, solubility and nature of the stabilizing ligand (Díez et al., 
2011; Neissa et al., 2015; Xiu, Zhang, Puppala, Colvin, & Alvarez, 2012), 
which exhibit increased chemical activity due to their large surface to 
volume ratios and crystallographic surface structure. The study of new 
bactericidal nanomaterials is particularly timely for the development of 
alternative, cost-effective and efficient antimicrobial agents that over­
come antimicrobial resistance (Dur´an et al., 2016; Rai, Deshmukh, Ingle, 
& Gade, 2012). Today AgNPs is present directly on the surface of 
products as a coating agent, or released through aerosol or spray, or in 
solid products as creamy or liquid products, especially because of its 
antimicrobial effects to improve the application of existing products 
(Silva, Silveira, Bonatto, Reis, & Milreu, 2017). 
In addition to high luminescence quantum yields, high molar 
extinction coefficients and excellent photostability (Choi, Dickson, & 
Yu, 2012; Díez & Ras, 2011; García-Bosch, Liras, Quijada-Garrido, & 
García, 2016), one of the most interesting characteristic of AgNCs is the 
ability to convert light energy into heat due to their localized surface 
plasmon resonance (Sasidharan, Poojari, Bahadur, & Srivastava, 2018). 
After irradiation with a specific range of wavelengths, AgNCs generate 
heat, increasing the temperature of the environment where they are 
included. This feature, exhibited by some metal NCs, has been proposed 
for cancer therapy or the treatment of antibiotic-resistant bacteria 
(Jaque et al., 2014; Xu, Yao, & Xu, 2019). 
AgNCs are usually prepared by the chemical reduction of silver salts 
precursors with aggressive reducing agents like ammonia, formaldehyde 
or sodium borohydride, whose toxicity limit the use of resulting NPs in 
biological applications (Emam & Ahmed, 2016). To alleviate this, 
different methods based on the treatment of aqueous solutions of silver 
salts with laser light, ultrasonic waves, gamma rays, ionizing or ultra­
violet (UV) light radiations are used as alternative preparation methods 
of NCs with lower toxicity (Basu & Mandal, 2019; Chen et al., 2017; 
Ermakov et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2009; Lei et al., 2017; Shameli et al., 
2010; Zhang et al., 2012). Our group previously developed a fast and 
highly efficient photo-reduction process for the direct synthesis and 
functionalization of hybrid AgNCs in organic media in the presence of 
new synthetic multi-amino polymers (García-Bosch et al., 2016). 
Chitosan is a semi-synthetic molecule obtained by the N-deacetyla­
tion of chitin with good biocompatibility and intrinsic antimicrobial 
properties. The linear structure and the presence of primary amine 
groups in the chitosan chain (poly [β-(1→4)-2-amino-2-deoxy-D- 
glucose]) make this polymer soluble in mild acidic conditions and very 
attractive for the production of new core-shell hybrid materials based on 
AgNCs. Due to its reducing behavior and protective role during the auto- 
assembly process of the silver metal cores, chitosan is an attractive 
priming bio-macromolecule for the production of new nanohybrids, as 
recently reported for the preparation of chitosan/AgNPs nano­
composites or hybrid microspheres and nanoparticles (Kalaivani et al., 
2018; Liang et al., 2019; Qin, Liu, Yuan, Yong, & Liu, 2019; Shen, Jin, 
Qi, Jiang, & Gao, 2017; Wongpreecha, Polpanich, Suteewong, Kaewsa­
neha, & Tangboriboonrat, 2018; Ye, Cheng, & Yu, 2019). 
We hypothesize that the intrinsic multi-amino functionality present 
in chitosan could act as effective macromolecular cover that can control 
the immediate vicinity of AgNCs, providing stabilization of the metal 
cores in biological environments and giving rise to new properties owing 
to the interaction between the nanoparticle components. 
In this work, we report the versatile in situ photochemical synthesis, 
characterization and stabilization of hybrid nanoparticles (Ch@AgNCs) 
by UV irradiation in aqueous solution. According to their properties, we 
studied the potential application of the synthesized nanohybrids as 
luminescent agents capable of efficiently transduce NIR light into heat. 
The wavelength range from 650 to 900 nm is minimally absorbed by 
biological tissues, leading to minimal tissue damage and deep tissue 
penetration and negligible tissue damage. The combination in a single 
agent of luminescent and photothermal activities is of increasing interest 
for the development of new therapeutic protocols based on hyperther­
mia that enable the tracing of the nanophotoabsorber into the targeted 
tissue. Interestingly, we observed that these dual-response nanohybrids 
to light energy have a certain antibacterial activity, which we have 
characterized in the present work. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials 
For synthesis of silver nanoclusters the chitosan (deacetylation de­
gree 85 %; molecular weight 50−150 kDa) was purchased from IDEBIO 
S.L. and purified before use by dissolution on acidic medium (acetic acid 
solution 0.5 M), filtration and precipitation on a sodium hydroxide so­
lution (10 % p/v) until pH = 8. Silver nitrate salt (AgNO3, Sigma- 
Aldrich) and IRGACURE® 2959 (I-2959, Ciba®) were used as received. 
For the stability studies, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, Panreac, (33 % 
w/v) and phosphate buffered saline (PBS) solutions at different pH 
values were prepared using anhydrous sodium dihydrogen phosphate 
(Fluka, ≥99 %), disodium hydrogen phosphate (Panreac, ≥98 %), 
orthophosphoric acid (Panreac, 85 %), NaOH (Panreac, 85 %) and so­
dium chloride (Panreac, ≥99.5 %) to keep the ionic strength constant. 
All reagents were commercially available and used without further pu­
rification. The water used was Milli-Q water, obtained from a water 
purification facility (Millipore Milli-U10).

Ch@AgNCs were synthesized and simultaneously functionalized 
from silver salt precursor in the presence of multi-amino functionalized 
natural polysaccharide (chitosan) as unique ligand stabilizer template, 
following the procedure previously described (García-Bosch et al., 
2016). Ch@AgNCs where photochemically synthetized in a chitosan

2.3. Physical characterization 
2.3.1. Particle size 
The particle size distribution by number of the hybrid materials were 
determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Malvern Nano­
sizer NanoZS Instrument equipped with a 4 mW He-Ne laser (λ = 633 
nm) at a scattering angle of 173◦. All measurements were performed in 
water in square polystyrene microcuvettes (SARSTEDT) at 25 ◦C. The 
nanometric particle size distributions and the apparent hydrodynamic 
diameter by number (Dh) were obtained. 
2.3.2. UV/Vis spectroscopy: absorbance and emission 
UV/Vis absorption and fluorescence emission spectra evolution were 
recorded on a Perkin-Elmer Lambda-35 and Perkin-Elmer LS5OB spec­
trophotometer, respectively. Samples were excited at 450 nm and 
fluorescence signals were recorded in the “front-face mode” by orienting 
the sample at angles of 35◦and 55◦with respect to the excitation and 
emission beams. 
2.3.3. Morphology analysis 
Morphology of Ch@AgNCs was analyzed by Scanning Transmission 
Electron Microscopy (sTEM) and cryoTEM. sTEM images were taken 
using a FE-SEM HITACHI-SU8000 microscope operating at 100 kV 
equipped with a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera. Briefly, 10 μL of a 
dilute aqueous solution of hybrid Ch@AgNCs (~0.5 mg/mL) were left to 
dry under vacuum on a formvar/carbon film supported on a 400 mesh 
copper grid (Electron Microscopy Sciences). 
CryoTEM images were taken in a Jeol 1230 electron microscope 
operated at 100 kV and equipped with a Gatan liquid nitrogen specimen 
holder and a CMOS Tvips TemCam-F416 camera, at 40,000 nominal 
magnification under low dose conditions. Samples were applied to holey 
carbon grids (Quantifoil) after glow-discharge and immediately blotted 
and vitrified using a FEI Vitrobot cryo-plunger. 
2.3.4. Photothermal evaluation 
Suspensions containing hybrid Ch@AgNCs synthetized from 
different chitosan concentrations (0.4, 1.0 and 2.0 mg/mL) were placed 
into polystyrene cuvettes and exposed to laser light of 808 nm emitting 
at 44 mW/mm2, using the experimental setup described elsewhere 
(Cebri´an et al., 2013). Temperature changes in Ch@AgNCs suspensions 
were monitored by infrared thermography using a Testo 875-2i thermal 
imaging camera (Instrumentos Testo S.A.). The temperature of the 
hottest point on the cuvette surface was measured every 60 s. 
2.3.5. Stability evaluation 
To study the fluorescence emission as a function of pH the concen­
trated hybrid AgNCs were diluted in buffer phosphate solutions at 
different pHs (2, 4, 5, 7 and 9). Samples at the selected pH values were 
left to equilibrate for a minimum of 2 h before recording absorption and 
fluorescence emission spectra. 
The resistance to oxidation was studied via incubating the samples 
(minimum of 2 h) in solutions of increasing hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) 
concentration, from 1 μM to 10 mM) and determining the effects on the 
absorbance and fluorescence emission signal. 
In all stability experiments, absorption and emission spectra (λexc =
450 nm) were recorded employing a small volume microcuvette (50 μL). 
The fluorescence signals were recorded in front-facing mode via ori­
enting the samples at angles of 35◦and 55◦with respect to the excitation 
and emission beams. The recorded fluorescence spectra were averages of 
at least three independent measurements made until reproducible 
fluorescence intensities were obtained. 
2.4. Biological activity assays 
2.4.1. Antibacterial activity 
Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli) and gram-positive bacteria 
(Staphylococcus aureus) were cultured in nutrient broth medium over­
night and the final suspension was prepared by adjusting the optical 
density at 600 nm of the solution to 0.2 (OD600 = 0.2). 
The antibacterial activity tests were performed on Gram-negative 
bacteria by a dynamic contact assay. 250 μL of bacteria inoculum 
(OD600 = 0.2) was mixed with 250 μL of Ch@AgNCs at different con­
centrations and incubated at 37 ◦C. After 24 h the bacterial suspension 
was serially diluted in PBS (from 1:10 to 1:100000), cultured on agar 
plates and incubated for 24 h. The reported results are relative to control 
values (250 μL of bacteria inoculum plus 250 μL of PBS). The results 
were expressed as relative cell viability (RCV):  
RCV(%)=N/Ncontrol ⋅ 100                                                                        
where N is the number of colony forming units (CFU) on 1 cm2-sample. 
A similar assay was carried out on Gram-positive bacteria. To eval­
uate the antimicrobial activity, 40 μL of each Ch@AgNCs (1 mg/mL) 
were added to 160 μL of a suspension of exponentially growing cells. The 
samples were incubated at 37 ◦C. At different times, 10 μL of the bac­
terial suspension was serially diluted in PBS (from 1:10 to 1:100000), 
cultured on agar plates and incubated for 24 h. The reported results are 
relative to control values (t = 0). The results were expressed as RCV.

(Fig. 1A and B). As expected, the absence of any of the components of the 
synthesis system, i.e. photoinitiator, light, silver or polymer; prevented 
the formation of hybrid nanomaterials. In previous studies (García-­
Bosch et al., 2016), polymer-stabilized AgNCs required the absence of 
oxygen in organic medium to be produced. The synthesis system pre­
sented here is capable of producing AgNCs regardless of whether oxygen 
is available or not in the aqueous solution (Fig. 1A and B), which greatly 
increases the versatility of this method of synthesis. The formation of 
AgNCs in the chitosan solution at different concentrations was followed 
by UV/Vis spectra measurement as a function of irradiation time. 
Fig. 1A–C, show the appearance and the increase of the silver 
plasmon-like absorption band with the irradiation time in all the tested 
samples. The maxima absorption peak were centered between 410−440 
nm depending on the experimental conditions and demonstrate the 
formation of AgNCs with the irradiation time (García-Bosch et al., 2016; 
Maretti et al., 2009; Monti, Fourkas, & Nesbitt, 2004; Pacioni, Borsarelli, 
Rey, & Veglia, 2015; Zhang, Xu, & Kumacheva, 2005; Zheng, Nicovich, 
& Dickson, 2007). These results are in accordance with those obtained 
by other authors and have been attributed to the presence of few silver 
atoms present in the AgNCs (Ag12) (Kang & Zhu, 2019; Kang, Xiong, 
Wang, Pei, & Zhu, 2017; Sun et al., 2020; Yang, Chai et al., 2018) and to 
the small size of the metal core, which was estimated to be around 2.1 ±
0.2 nm (Bakr et al., 2009). 
Althougth the optical properties of AgNCs are also markedly 
dependent on the synthesis conditions, e.g. reagent concentration, type 
of solvent, reduction process, or ligand nature. UV/Vis absorption band 
obtained in the presence of oxygen was slightly wider and displaced to 
the red region of the spectrum (λmax = 440 nm) (Fig. 1B) as compared to 
the absorption band obtained for the reaction performed in the absence 
of oxygen (λmax = 410 nm) (Fig. 1A), and stronger background scat­
tering at wavelengths > 600 nm was detected probably due the large 
size of these hybrid NPs. In all tested samples, the absorption band peak 
was more pronounced as the irradiation time at 365 nm increases, 
suggesting that the longer the UV light treatment the higher the con­
centration of Ch@AgNCs (Fig. 1C). The synthesis of Ch@AgNCs in an 
oxygen-free atmosphere was not affected by the concentration of chi­
tosan in the medium. However, appreciable differences can be observed 
in those systems irradiated in the presence of oxygen. An inhibition 
period of 60 s in the formation of Ch@AgNCs was observed in the ox­
ygen atmosphere, which might be explained by the quenching of the 
carbon-centered radicals with the oxygen dissolved in the solution. This 
effect resulted in decreased radical generation efficiency which was 
extended until the oxygen was consumed by the initiator radicals (Sas­
tre, Conde, & Mateo, 1988). Therefore, synthesis reactions carried out 
under conditions of nitrogen purging were faster and more efficient 
(Fig. 1A and C). 
In all cases, evolution of the plasmon band is slower in the presence 
of oxygen, an effect attributed to the fact that a fraction of the radicals 
react with oxygen, which is an excellent radical scavenger (Maillard, 
Ingold, & Scaiano, 1983). The effect of oxygen on the photosynthesis of 
AuNPs (Marin et al., 2008) or AgNCs (Maretti et al., 2009) with different 
primary amines was previously examined by others authors in organic 
media. As in the present work, the only significant effect observed in 
aerated solutions was an approximately 1−2 min induction period, 
which have been attribute to the sacrificial use of some of the initially 
photogenerated radicals to consume the dissolved oxygen. Interestingly, 
the reaction of ketyl radicals with oxygen yields superoxide radicals (or 
HO2
• in nonpolar solvents) (Ingold, Paul, Young, & Doiron, 1997) and, 
under this experimental conditions, it would seem that HO2
• does not 
reduce Ag+ to Ag◦. So, this induction period represents the time required 
to chemically remove molecular oxygen from the system. Previous 
works has demonstrated that the benzoyl and ketyl radicals generated 
from ketone α-cleavage can be quenched by oxygen at near 
diffusion-controlled rates (>109 M−1 s−1) to give peroxyl radicals or 
HO2
• and hence act as sacrificial oxygen scavengers (Maillard et al., 
1983). 
Despite the differences in the induction times and the final absorp­
tion intensities of the different systems, a similar growth pattern is 
observed for nitrogen and air, as indicated by the similar slopes of the 
growth stage of the curves. This implies that once the oxygen has been 
consumed, these two samples undergo similar growth rates. 
In water, synthesized Ch@AgNCs exhibited a fluorescence emission 
peak centered on 620 nm after excitation at 450 nm when recorded in 
“front face mode” (Fig. 2A and B). Overall, the fluorescence emission of 
hybrid AgNCs obtained in the absence of oxygen increased with the time 
of UVA irradiation for each chitosan concentration employed (Fig. 2B 
and D), while the emission detected in the samples obtained in the 
presence of oxygen was practically independent of the irradiation times 
or the concentration of chitosan used (Fig. 2A and C). This emission 
profile was similar to that found previously for AgNCs synthesized with 
a different type of polymeric ligand (García-Bosch et al., 2016; Shen 
et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2005). 
This emission band has been attributed to the reduction of Ag+ to Ag◦
when AgNCs are formed, as previously described (Basu & Mandal, 2019; 
García-Bosch et al., 2016; Maretti et al., 2009). As a possible reaction 
mechanism for AgNCs, the formation of radicals and macro-radicals in 
the chitosan chains was triggered followed by silver reduction. Reduced 
Ag◦species interact with Ag+ ions for the nanoclusters formation, at the 
same time the Ag+ ions interact with chitosan by chelation between the 
metallic ions and the free amine groups and oxygen atoms (as resumed 
in Scheme S1) (Latif, Al-Rubeaan, & Saeb, 2015). Therefore, the high 
amount of amine groups in the chitosan favors its ability for 
complexation. 
It is generally accepted that the photophysical properties of AgNCs 
depend on their morphology, oxidation state and surface ligand. It was 
reported that the maximum emission wavelength of AgNCs shifts from 
450 to 680 nm upon increasing the silver atom number from 5 to 28 in 
each NC (Cui et al., 2011). 
There are at least two main mechanism suggested to explain the 
fluorescence mechanism of the metal nanoclusters: (i) The intrinsic 
quantization of metal core due to their small size, so that electronic 
energy states are discretized and (ii) the formation of surface states 
governed by the chemical interaction between the metal core and the 
functional groups present in the encapsulating matrix. Some kind of 
these new electronic states supports electronic transition such as ab­
sorption and fluorescence (Chakraborty & Pradeep, 2017; Zheng et al., 
2007). 
3.2. Morphological characterization of Ch@AgNCs 
Structural and morphological characterization of the Ch@AgNCs has 
been performed using cryoTEM (Fig. 3) and sTEM (Fig. S1) while their 
hydrodynamic size and polydispersity have been determined using DLS 
(Fig. 4). 
Regardless of the synthesis atmosphere, the obtained samples 
showed round-shape NPs with diameters ranging from 10 and 60 nm, 
while the presence of large aggregates was excluded (Figs. 3, S1 and 4). 
It should be noted that nanohybrids synthesized in an oxygen-free at­
mosphere are smaller than those obtained in an oxygen atmosphere 
(Fig. 4). The nanohybrids present core-shell morphology (Fig. 3), where 
the AgNCs are clearly surrounded by a protecting chitosan polymeric 
shell associated to silver nanoclusters in a multidentate mode, stabiliz­
ing the hybrid AgNCs and providing their totally water solubility. 
Ch@AgNCs obtained in oxygen atmosphere showed hydrodynamic 
sizes between 30−60 nm, while increased chitosan concentration in the 
medium resulted in larger size of Ch@AgNCs at the lowest UVA irradi­
ation time (Fig. 4A). This effect can be attributed to the quenching effect 
that might occur in the first stage of Ch@AgNCs formation in the pres­
ence of oxygen.

3.3. Stability studies 
For practical applications it is important to study the stability of the 
new hybrid AgNCs to assess the degree of protection provided to the 
AgNCs by the polysaccharide coverage against possible variations in 
environmental conditions. Therefore, both the influence of the variation 
of the pH of the medium and oxidation resistance on the photophysical 
properties of the new hybrids were tested against pH and different 
concentrations of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, from 1 μM to 10 mM), 
respectively. 
Taking into account that the high amount of primary amino groups 
present in the chitosan favors its ability to complex with the silver 
nanoclusters to form the new hybrids, and these amino groups are easily 
ionizable with the variation of the pH of the medium, we have analyzed 
the stability of the Ch@AgNCs by studying the influence of this 
parameter on the photophysical properties of the obtained nanohybrids. 
As can be seen in Fig. 5A the silver nanohybrids does not show any pH 
response, the absorbance and emission do not experience sharp or in­
tensity changes with pH, indicating its high stability and solubility of the 
nanohybrids along the pH range studied (from 2 to 9), indicating the 
high stability of the complexation between the amino groups of chitosan 
with the AgNCs. 
Additionally, the polymeric AgNCs were incubated with H2O2 solu­
tions, and no significant variations in the absorption or fluorescence 
emission were observed, even when using high concentrations of H2O2 
(see Fig. 5B). This fact demonstrates the high resistance to oxidation of 
the as-synthesized Ch@AgNCs in accordance with those reported by 
other authors (Benavides et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2020). 
The higher stability of AgNCs probably mainly originates from the 
encapsulation and protection by the multi-amino polysaccharide 
coating, as well as the strong interaction between the electron-donating 
N-atoms of chitosan and the silver core atoms.

3.4. Photothermal properties 
In order to study the photothermal behavior of Ch@AgNCs, water 
suspensions of nanomaterial synthesized with different chitosan con­
centrations after 120→300 s of UVA treatment were irradiated with an 
808 nm NIR laser during 5 min. The maximum temperature rises in the 
samples were measured using infrared thermography (Fig. 6). 
After 5 min of NIR irradiation, the average heating of Ch@AgNCs 
samples synthesized in an oxygen-free atmosphere ranged from +5 to 
+15 ◦C, while Ch@AgNCs produced in the presence of an oxygen at­
mosphere reduced their photothermal efficiency by 50 % (Fig. 6). 
Thermographic measurements showed that chitosan concentration 
used to prepare Ch@AgNCs did not change significantly the photo­
thermal behavior of the nanohybrids. Conversely, the extension of UVA 
irradiation time during the synthesis process increased the photothermal 
efficiency of the obtained nanomaterial, independently of the assayed 
atmosphere. Consequently, NIR irradiation for 5 min of Ch@AgNCs 
samples synthesized with UVA irradiation for up to 10 min increased 
their temperature by 23 ◦C (Fig. 7). 
The comparison of photothermal behavior and UV absorbance of 
obtained Ch@AgNCs revealed increasing activity of both phenomena as 
the time of UVA treatment increased during the synthesis process. The 
collective excitation of conduction electrons and their interaction with 
an electromagnetic field give rise to the so-called localized surface 
plasmon resonance which causes a giant absorption band in the UV–vis 
domain. The localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) frequency can 
be tuned through the composition, shape and size of the nanoparticles 
(Abe, Charl´e, Tesche, & Schulze, 1982; Haberland, 2013; Sinha-Roy, 
García-Gonz´alez, Weissker, Rabilloud, & Fern´andez-Domínguez, 2017) 
but is also dependent on the environment (Campos et al., 2019). Mul­
tiple peaks gathered in a small range of frequencies originate the LSPR 
while collective mode takes place as envelopes of coherent superposi­
tions of electron-hole excitations (Brack, 1993; Lerm´e, 2011; Xia, Yin, & 
Kresin, 2009). At small sizes, the plasmon band of noble metals generally 
disappears because the collective excitation of electron is reduced, 
resulting in the fragmentation of the band into discrete peaks which are 
scattered in large frequencies. Then, electrons are likely to be lost in the 
interbands associated to d-electrons or in the bands resulting from hy­
bridization with protecting ligands associated with the NC surface 
(Bousquet, Cherif, Huang, & Rabilloud, 2015; Chakraborty et al., 2014). 
Interestingly, silver is an exception to this phenomenon because its 
relatively strong alkali-like character, with a large s-d separation, favors 
a plasmon band clearly visible down to small-size bare clusters about 
18–20 atoms (Anak, Bencharif, & Rabilloud, 2014; Harb et al., 2008; Yu 
et al., 2018).

3.5. Antibacterial activity 
The antibacterial activity of synthesized Ch@AgNCs was tested on 
E. coli and S. aureus. The results are showed in Fig. 8A and B, 
respectively. 
All tested nanohybrids showed a similar bactericide behavior with a 
minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for E. coli of 0.15 mg/mL 
(Fig. 8A). This value is similar to that obtained for other silver NPs 
(Kaweeteerawat, Na Ubol, Sangmuang, Aueviriyavit, & Manir­
atanachote, 2017) and was independent of the chitosan concentration or 
the atmosphere employed for the nanohybrid synthesis. 
As shown in Table 2, our MIC over E. coli are in the same range than 
other kind of NCs and NPs developed by other authors, but using a fast, 
green and efficient methodology. AgNCs and AgNPs are usually pre­
pared by chemical reduction of silver salts precursors with aggressive 
reducing agents like ammonia, formaldehyde or sodium borohydride, 
whose toxicity limit the use of resulting NPs in biological applications. 
For example Xiu et al. and Yuan et al. used sodium borohydride to 
prepare PEG-AgNPs and GSH-AgNCs, while Kim et al. use a St¨ober 
method by hydrolysis and condensation of tetraethoxysilane to prepare 
the AgNPs-SiO2 instead our one-step process offer the possibility to 
produce the Ch@AgNCs in situ without requiring further purification. 
Another important advantage of our system compared with other silver 
NP or NC is the photothermal properties that allow us heat the 
Ch@AgNCs solution by irradiation with NIR. This is an important 
characteristic for future applications in the field of wound healing or 
treatment of different diseases. 
The study of bactericidal activity as a function of time (Fig. 8B) 
revealed that AgNCs synthesized with the higher amount of chitosan 
takes a longer time to kill 100 % the bacterial culture of S. aureus. In the 
same period of time, 13.8 % of the bacterial population survived the first 
hour of treatment with Ch@AgNC-O2-2.0 suspensions while less than 
0.1 % or 2.9 % of the bacteria survived, respectively, to Ch@AgNC-O2- 
0.4 or Ch@AgNC-N2-2.0. These results suggest that the increase of the 
chitosan concentration in the synthesis medium could be associated with 
the formation of a more occlusive polymeric coverage around AgNCs, 
which leads to a greater isolation of the silver nanoclusters. Overall, we 
observed that Ch@AgNCs synthesized under oxygen atmosphere con­
ditions showed a lower capacity in the inhibition of the bacterial growth 
than its counterparts obtained in the absence of oxygen. This difference 
in bactericidal behavior could be due to the higher amounts of silver 
nanoparticles included in nanohybrids synthesized in the absence of 
oxygen.

procedure implies the direct “in situ” photochemical functionalization 
and stabilization of hybrid silver nanoclusters employing as template 
chitosan, a multiamino functional polysaccharide.

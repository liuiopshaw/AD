SUMMARY
Gut microbiota dysbiosis is a driving factor in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), yet the mechanisms behind remain 
elusive. Emerging evidence highlights that outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) are critical mediators of micro­
biota-host communication. Here, we observed a reduction in a gut probiotic Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 
(EcN)-like strain in AD patients, and its levels are positively associated with cognitive ability. The EcN 
OMVs containing outer membrane protein A (OmpA) translocate to the brain, reshaping the dysregulated im­
mune network. Specifically, EcN OMVs are internalized by glia and neurons, suppressing glial hyperactivation 
and restoring synaptic function, thereby reducing Aβ deposition and cognitive deficits. The results further 
show that OmpA plays an important role in vesicle trafficking and inflammatory pathways and may be the 
key regulator of inflammatory mediators in EcN OMVs, modulating astrocyte-microglia-neuron interactions 
and functionality. This work discloses the substantial therapeutic potential of the probiotic and its secreted 
OMVs in intervention and treatment of neurological disorders.
INTRODUCTION
The gut microbiota has emerged as a pivotal factor in maintain­
ing the gut and systemic homeostasis,1 with a range of beneficial 
functions predominantly related to the maturation and modula­
tion of the immune system.2–4 It is now clear that a complex bidi­
rectional communication is taking place between the microbiota 
and host. By contrast, the exchange of information rarely hap­
pens through direct cell-cell contacts due to the existence of 
various biological barriers, especially for extraintestinal or­
gans.5,6 Our previous study, as well as those of others, have 
shown that bacterial metabolites can mediate microbial interac­
tions with the host.7–11 For example, gut microbiota-derived 
short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) have been proven to regulate 
the microglial maturation and its metabolic state, thereby 
influencing microglial phagocytosis and the progression of neu­
rodegeneration.10,11 In particular, butyrate—a specific SCFA 
produced by Clostridia—has been reported to stimulate colonic 
regulatory T cells, thereby suppressing host inflammatory 
response.7 Undoubtedly, intricate crosstalk between the gut mi­
crobiota and its host is multidirectional and reciprocal, implying 
that the best-characterized derivative secreted from bacteria 
may be involved in their interplay.
Gram-negative bacteria normally secrete outer membrane 
vesicles (OMVs) during growth, which are bilayer membrane 
nanostructures that range from 20 to 400 nm in size, containing 
multiple parental components, such as nucleic acids, proteins, 
enzymes, and metabolites.12 OMVs have emerged as biological 
shuttle systems to deliver immune factors into host cells that can 
modulate host signaling pathways and cell processes.13 Recent 
studies in mice have highlighted the roles of OMVs produced by 
commensal bacteria in eliciting immunostimulatory responses 
and exerting various health-promoting effects.14 For instance, 
Escherichia coli-derived OMVs were shown to accumulate 
in the tumor tissue and produce IFN-γ within the tumor 
microenvironment to activate antitumor responses.15 OMVs 
from Akkermansia muciniphila are able to regulate the intestinal 
homeostasis by restoring disturbed gut microbiota, eliciting in­
testinal immune responses, improving intestinal barrier integrity, 
and alleviating cognitive impairments.16–18 Moreover, cyto­
plasmic membrane vesicles from Gram-positive bacteria such 
as Bifidobacterium longum, Lactobacillus plantarum, and

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a devastating, age-related neuro­
degenerative disease, closely linked to dysbiosis of the gut 
microbiota.22 An increasing number of studies showed that pro­
biotic administration conferred neuroprotective benefits and 
could attenuate cognitive deficits by restoring the disrupted 
gut microbiota.23 In this regard, probiotic E. coli Nissle 1917 
(EcN) has been reported to enhance serotonin activity and 
improve neuroinflammatory processes, which are pivotal in the 
context of neurodegenerative diseases.24–26 Exploiting their 
functional versatility, EcN OMVs have been found to modulate 
serotonin metabolism and induce inflammatory responses in 
macrophage cells.27–29 Notably, E. coli OMVs transport func­
tional biomolecules from the parental bacteria to peripheral or­
gans and even the distal brain.30 However, no evidence has 
yet revealed the beneficial effects of EcN OMVs on regulating 
brain homeostasis.
Here, we uncovered a correlation between gut microbiota al­
terations and neurological function in AD patients, potentially 
through an exosome-based transport system in the gut-brain 
axis. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that EcN effectively alle­
viated AD pathology and regulated astrocyte-microglia-neuron 
interactions by delivering outer membrane protein (OMP) A 
(OmpA)-containing OMVs into the brain and/or stimulating the 
vagus nerve signaling. Our findings establish a foundation for 
future research into OMVs as a therapeutic platform for neurode­
generative diseases.
RESULTS
Gut dysbiosis is associated with cognitive impairment
To investigate the potential relationship between the gut micro­
biota and cognitive ability, we first performed metagenomic 
sequencing on fecal samples from AD (n = 18) and healthy con­
trols ([HCs], n = 22). Our analysis revealed distinct microbiota 
composition in AD patients, characterized by the separated plots 
in the principal coordinate analysis (Figure 1A) and reduced spe­
cies richness (lower Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices, 
Figure 1B). Additionally, the abundance of specific microbial 
communities differed significantly between cohorts (Figure 1C; 
Figure S1A). In particular, the species Hungatella hominis, Dorea 
hominis, and Clostridium gallinarum are enriched in AD patients 
and negatively correlated with the Mini-Mental State Examina­
tion (MMSE) scores (Figure 1D). Conversely, the species Lach­
nospira eligens, Roseburia sp., and Butyricicoccus avicola are 
enriched in HCs and positively correlated with the MMSE score 
(Figure 1D). Meanwhile, qPCR analysis revealed that the abun­
dance of the well-studied probiotic strain EcN was negatively 
correlated with cognitive decline in AD patients (Figures 1E 
and 1F). Notably, these shifts of microbial community of AD 
patients were significantly correlated with the alterations in 
functional pathways related to exosome, protein export, trans­
port, 
GABAergic 
synapse, 
and 
glutamatergic 
synapse 
(Figure 1G; Figure S1B). This suggests a close relationship 
between intestinal microbiota and neurological function and im­
plicates a potential role of exosome-based transport system in 
gut-brain axis communication.
EcN alleviates AD pathology by secreting OMVs
EcN is a non-pathogenic commensal with well-documented pro­
biotic properties. To investigate its beneficial effects on AD pa­
thology, we orally administrated EcN or vehicles to APP/PS1 
mice (Figure S2A). EcN treatment markedly alleviated Aβ pathol­
ogy in APP/PS1 mice, as evidenced by Aβ immunostaining and 
ELISA (Figures S2B–S2E). Specifically, there was a significant 
reduction in the area and amount of Aβ plaques in the hippocam­
pus of APP/PS1 mice upon EcN treatment compared to the con­
trol mice (Figures S2B and S2C). Morphometric analysis further 
revealed a decrease in large plaques (>600 μm) in the hippocam­
pus of EcN-treated mice compared to the control mice 
(Figure S2D), whereas no significant difference in the number 
of small (<250 μm) and medium plaques (<250–600 μm) was 
observed (Figure S2D). Consistent with these findings, ELISA 
quantification also showed significant reductions in both soluble 
and insoluble Aβ1-40 and insoluble Aβ1-42 in the hippocampus of 
EcN-treated APP/PS1 mice (Figure S2E). Neuroinflammation ap­
pears to serve a primary role in exacerbating Aβ burden in AD.31
We thus examined the effects of EcN on neuroinflammation in 
APP/PS1 mice. In the hippocampus of EcN-treated mice, the 
protein expression of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, 
and TNF) was significantly reduced compared to controls 
(Figure S2F). In contrast, no changes were observed in the 
mRNA expressions of these genes of proinflammatory cyto­
kines, with the exception of a modest reduction in the Il6 gene 
(Figure S2G). We further investigated whether EcN treatment 
could improve cognitive function using Y-maze and novel object 
recognition (NOR) tests. EcN-treated APP/PS1 mice showed 
cognitive improvement, as reflected by significant increased 
exploration of the new object in the NOR test and enhanced 
spontaneous alternation in the Y-maze task (Figures S2H–S2J).
OMV secretion is one of the key pathways of Gram-negative 
bacteria to interact with the host.32,33 To determine whether 
the effects of EcN on AD depend on OMVs, we treated mice 
with EcN or GW4869-pretreated EcN (to inhibit the release of 
OMVs34). GW4869 treatment did not affect the abundance of 
EcN (Figure S3A and S3B) but largely reduced OMV secretion 
(Figure S3C). Remarkably, the inhibition of OMV production 
mostly compromised the beneficial effects of EcN in alleviating 
AD pathology (Figure S3D–S3M), implying OMVs are essential 
for EcN-mediated neuroprotection. To further validate the role 
of OMVs, we isolated OMVs from EcN cultures and confirmed

Notably, we observed that EcN OMVs translocated to multiple

the majority of tdTomato+ cells in the brain were neurons, with 
a smaller amount of tdTomato+ IBA1+ and tdTomato+ GFAP+

(Figure 3H). Interestingly, AF647 signals in the blood and brain 
peaked at 3 h post-administration and were mostly cleared by 
12 h (Figure 3I). High-resolution fluorescence imaging showed 
that most OMVs colocalized with brain cell, while a small fraction 
remained in the extracellular space (Figure 3J–3L). In addition, 
nFCM analyses confirmed a significant increase in EcN OMV 
signal in the blood, brain, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) 
compared to controls (Figures S7A–S7C). However, the accu­
mulation of ΔOmpA−/−OMVs in the brain was significantly lower 
than that of naive EcN OMVs, while no such difference was 
observed in the plasma or CSF (Figures S7A–S7C). Importantly, 
OmpA immunogold labeling coupled with TEM demonstrated 
the presence of intact EcN OMVs in the brain (Figure S7D). 
Together, these results suggest that EcN OMVs can enter the 
bloodstream and ultimately reach and/or accumulate in the 
brain, a process that is potentially mediated by the OmpA.
Considering that EcN OMVs may enter the brain through the 
circulatory system, we compared the neuroprotective effects 
of EcN OMVs administered via the gastrointestinal tract and 
intravenous (i.v.) injection (Figure S8A). Surprisingly, direct i.v. 
injection of EcN OMVs did not show a stronger effect than gastro­
intestinal administration in alleviating Aβ pathology, neuroinflam­
mation, and cognitive impairment (Figures S8B–S8J). Notably, 
i.v. injection of EcN OMVs caused a more intense peripheral in­
flammatory response compared with gastrointestinal administra­
tion, as evidenced by the significantly elevated IL-6 and TNF levels 
in blood (Figure S8K). These results indicated that i.v.-delivered 
EcN OMVs may be subject to rapid clearance or exhibit dose-in­
dependent effects, while also potentially acting on the brain by 
stimulating the vagus nerve, the major route of gut-brain axis 
communication.11 To verify the dose-independent hypothesis, 
we treated the immune cells with different concentrations of 
EcN OMVs and confirmed that the effect of EcN OMVs is indeed 
dose independent (Figure S9). Similar dose-independent effects 
have been reported in promoting proliferation and inducing 
phagocytic activities in RAW264.7 cells.28,35 Next, we evaluated 
the effect of vagotomy on gastrointestinal administration of EcN 
OMVs in regulating brain dysfunctions (Figure S10A). In vagotom­
ized APP/PS1 mice, EcN OMV treatment still showed the ability to 
alleviate Aβ pathology and cognitive impairment, albeit to a lesser 
extent than in non-vagotomized mice (Figures S10B–S10H; 
Figures 2A–2J). However, OMV treatment in vagotomized mice re­
sulted in increased neuroinflammation (Figure S10I and S10J), 
implying that the vagus nerve also contributes to the regulation 
of the central immunomodulatory effects of EcN OMVs. Moreover, 
to investigate the direct effects of EcN OMVs on AD pathology in 
the brain, we further intracerebroventricularly (i.c.v.) injected EcN 
OMVs into the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice (Figure S11A). 
Direct brain delivery of OMVs significantly reduced Aβ pathology 
and improved memory function (Figures S11B–S11H), but did 
not alter neuroinflammation (Figures S11I–S11J). Collectively, 
the above findings indicate that EcN OMVs mitigate AD pathology 
through two complementary mechanisms: an indirect pathway 
mediated by vagus nerve and a direct route via the circulatory 
system.

network in AD mice

the EcN OMV-improved AD pathology, we performed transcrip­

or EcN OMVs. Profiling revealed obvious differences in the 
mRNA expression, with 178 genes significantly upregulated 
and 56 genes downregulated (p < 0.05 and 1.5-fold change) in 
the hippocampus from EcN OMV-treated APP/PS1 mice

EcN OMVs improve neuronal function in AD mice
The above studies demonstrate that EcN OMVs are crucial in 
regulating neuronal function in APP/PS1 mice. We next sought 
to assess the impact of EcN OMVs on synaptic and neuronal 
network dysfunction. Immunostaining with anti-NeuN antibody 
showed a significant increase in the neuronal number of the 
CA1 and CA3 regions of the hippocampus of EcN OMV-treated 
APP/PS1 mice (Figures 5A and 6B). Consistently, TUNEL assay 
showed reduced neuronal death in the hippocampus of OMV- 
treated mice compared with controls (Figures 5C and 5D). Mean­
while, the beneficial effects of EcN OMV treatment on synaptic 
properties were explored (Figures 5E–5H). Increased immuno­
staining intensity of presynaptic synapse synaptophysin (SYP) 
and postsynaptic density protein-95 (PSD-95) was observed in 
the CA3 area of the hippocampus of OMV-treated mice 
compared to controls (Figures 5E and 5F). Similarly, the high-res­
olution images of co-immunostaining of SYP and PSD-95 also 
confirmed increased pre-, post-, and double-positive synaptic 
puncta in OMV-treated mice (Figures 5G and 5H).
Given that microglia are the primary mediators of synaptic 
pruning,37 we further evaluated the microglial pruning activity 
using 3D morphometric reconstruction analysis of microglia 
and postsynaptic synapse PSD-95. The volume of PSD-95- 
positive puncta within microglia is increased in EcN OMV- 
treated APP/PS1 mice (Figures 5I and 5J). In the brain, most 
synapses are formed on minute protrusions known as den­
dritic spines,38 and we found that dendritic spine density in 
the hippocampus was significantly higher in OMV-treated 
mice (Figures 5K and 5L). Given the importance of synapse 
and dendritic spine for synaptic transmission, we evaluated 
the contribution of EcN OMVs to synaptic function at mossy fi­
ber-CA3 synapses by assessing the electrophysiological 
properties in acute hippocampal slices (Figure 5M). The 
long-term potentiation (LTP) induction was significantly 
enhanced in OMV-treated mice compared to controls. These 
results further support the cognitive improvements observed 
after EcN OMV treatment (Figures 2H–2J), as memory forma­
tion is closely linked to synapses formed between axon termi­
nals and dendritic spines.
EcN OMVs regulate immune cell interactions and 
attenuate AD pathology
Astrocytes and microglia are the major glial cells in the central 
nervous system that maintain and support neuronal homeostasis 
of the neuronal functions.36 Dysregulation of these cells can lead 
to excessive synaptic pruning and subsequent brain dys­
function. We first assessed whether EcN OMV treatment 
influences glial cell-neuron interaction. In APP/PS1 mice, OMV 
stimulation significantly reduced the number of microglia and as­
trocytes located in the dentate gyrus neurons (Figures 6A and 
6B). Interestingly, a significantly increased interaction between 
astrocytes and microglia was found after EcN OMV treatment 
(Figures 6C–6E). Also, the microglia in brain parenchyma were 
positioned farther from the blood vessel upon OMV treatment, 
suggesting their aggregation around vessels to receive OMV 
influx (Figures 6F and 6G). Next, we assessed the effect of 
EcN OMV treatment on glial reactivity and AD pathology. 
Regarding microglial proliferation and activation, an increase 
(8.5%) in IBA1+ microglia was observed in the hippocampus 
of OMV-treated APP/PS1 mice (Figures 6H and 6I). This prolif­
eration effect was also validated by IBA1 and Ki-67 co-immu­
nostaining (Figures 6J and 6K), a marker of cell proliferation. 
However, OMV treatment inhibited microglial overactivation 
as shown by the decreased number of IBA1+/CD68+-positive 
microglia (Figures 6L and 6M). In parallel, quantitative 3D 
morphometric analysis of IBA1+ microglia revealed that OMV- 
treated APP/PS1 mice revised the activated microglia into clas­
sically resting phenotypes, characterized by significantly longer 
processes and increased number of segments, branching, and 
terminal points, as well as increased cell volume compared to 
controls (Figures 6N and 6O). In addition to the synaptic remod­
eling, microglia also contribute to Aβ clearance through phago­
cytic and digestive mechanisms.39 Indeed, OMV treatment led 
to an increased trend in the number of microglia surrounding Aβ 
plaques (Figures 6P and 6Q). In contrast, we observed no sig­
nificant effects of OMV treatment on astrogliosis or on astro­
cytic activation in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice 
(Figures 6R–6W). Interestingly, astrocytic endfeet cover the ce­

(Figures 6X–6Z). Astrocytes are critical components of the neu­

integrity of brain barriers. Fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran 
signals indicated that the OMV treatment had obvious effects 
on the integrity of both BBB and blood-CSF barrier (BCSFB)

ise that EcN OMVs are critical regulators of inducing astrocyte- 
microglia-neuron activity and interactions, thereby exerting 
beneficial effects on AD pathology.

AD pathology

been reported to play important roles ranging from vesicle 
biogenesis to bacterial physiology.34,41 Proteomic analysis of

most abundant (Figure 7A) and have been reported to play 
important roles in mediating OMV targeting to host cells and ex­
erting immunomodulatory functions.42,43 To determine the role

DISCUSSION
Growing evidence supports the concept that the gut microbiota- 
derived molecules such as lipopolysaccharide, peptidoglycan, 
and SCFAs directly affect host brain function and AD pathogen­
esis.11,44,45 These bioactive molecules are also present in gut 
microbe-derived OMVs, that is, nano-sized spherical buds of 
the outer membrane produced by both pathogenic and non-path­
ogenic Gram-negative bacteria.33,46 OMVs can transport their 
cargo to proximal and distal cells to exert physiological and path­
ological effects.47–50 In this study, the alterations of microbial 
communities in AD patients are closely correlated with neurolog­
ical function and with a potential exosome-based transport sys­
tem in gut-brain axis signaling. We further investigated the under­
lying mechanisms of multiple roles of EcN and its OMVs in 
reversing AD development, as EcN has been reported to have 
the potential for alleviating the pathology of neurodegenerative 
diseases.24–26 We found that EcN significantly alleviates AD pa­
thology and OMVs play an important role in this process through 
neural and humoral pathways. Mechanistically, EcN OMVs were 
identified as the regulator of astrocyte-microglia-neuron interac­
tions to maintain brain homeostasis, including attenuation of neu­
roinflammation, rescue of tau pathology, and amelioration of syn­
aptic impairment and neuronal loss, which ultimately contributes 
to alleviate cognitive impairments in APP/PS1 mice. In addition, 
we disclosed that the OmpA is essential for EcN OMV targeting 
host cells and exerting immunomodulatory functions. The biolog­
ical context of OmpA within intact OMVs is likely critical—it may 
serve as a key active component that depends on the vesicle’s 
protective packaging and co-delivery for optimal stability, target­
ing, and the functional activity. These findings support the model 
that EcN alleviates AD pathology through secretion of OmpA- 
containing OMVs.

Various gut microbiota-derived OMVs have been described 
with capability to cross the brain barriers and transfer functional 
biomolecules to the brain.51 However, whether the cargo 
released in the interrupted OMVs may somehow reach the brain 
barrier and eventually enter the brain requires further clarifica­
tion. In this study, we showed evidence for the presence of intact 
EcN OMVs, rather than just delivering cargo into the brain. In 
addition, we revealed that OmpA in EcN OMVs is important in 
regulating their translocation, although it is unclear how these 
OMVs cross the biological barriers. After entry into brain, EcN 
OMVs reduced Aβ pathology and regulated locally related im­
mune responses, and eventually improved cognitive function. 
In addition to the circulation system, the vagus nerve is another 
important link between the gut and the brain,52 and OMVs from 
Paenalcaligenes hominis have been shown to penetrate the brain 
via the vagus nerve and cause cognitive impairment.53 Consis­
tently, EcN OMVs also act on the brain through neural pathways, 
but primarily through the humoral pathway. Moreover, our RNA 
sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis revealed a significant impact of 
EcN OMVs on immune and neuronal function pathway genes 
in APP/PS1 mice. The TF analysis suggests that these changes 
are driven by inflammatory response, microglia proliferation and 
differentiation, and astrocyte maturation and neurogenesis, 
which are associated with neurodegenerative conditions.39,54–56
Astrocytes and microglia, both types of glial cells, are key cells 
in the central nervous system (CNS), maintaining homeostasis 
and supporting the function of neurons.36 Recent evidence has 
shown that both glial and neuronal cells can take up and inter­
nalize microbial-derived OMVs and induce an inflammatory 
response.30,47 In line with this observation, we detected that 
EcN OMVs are mainly taken up by neuronal cells, and also by 
glial cells to some extent. The latter induced microglia to transit 
from a reactive phenotype to a resting state, reflected by the in­
crease in dendrite length and cell volume and in the numbers of 
segments, branch points, and terminal points. Meanwhile, the 
EcN OMV uptake leads to the transcriptional reprogramming of 
microglia, which, in turn, downregulates the expression and 
release of key pro-inflammatory mediators, while promoting 
anti-inflammatory effects. In addition to the inflammatory 
responses, the resting microglia showed enhanced Aβ phagocy­
tosis as reported previously.44,57 Moreover, the modest micro­
glial proliferation induced by EcN OMVs may contribute to Aβ 
clearance by increasing the available ‘‘workforce,’’ especially 
since the population of Aβ-associated microglia was also 
elevated. Beyond their role in Aβ clearance, homeostatic micro­
glia play a crucial role in adult neurogenesis by regulating the 
survival of newly generated neurons as well as the elimination, 
formation, and maintenance of synapses.58 In our study, we 
found that EcN OMV administration reduced neurodegeneration 
and synaptic deficits, at least in part due to normalized microglia 
phagocytosis. Indeed, the reactive microglia have been shown 
to be involved in neurodegeneration and excessive synaptic 
pruning59 and resting microglia-neuron contact reduces the ac­
tivities of contacted neurons.60 It is well known that glia cells and 
glial-neuron crosstalk are fundamental to maintaining brain ho­
meostasis and neuronal health.61 In EcN OMV-treated mice, 
we observed an increase in astrocyte-microglia interactions, 
but a decrease in microglia neurons. Therefore, we speculate 
that EcN OMVs may act directly on neuronal cells and inhibit 
the release of ‘‘find-me’’ and/or ‘‘eat-me’’ signals that attract mi­
croglial processes.62 In addition to Aβ accumulation, synaptic 
degeneration is also closely associated with cognitive decline.63
In this respect, we speculate that the EcN OMV-lightened Aβ pa­
thology and synaptic dysfunction are the main contributors to 
the improved memory defects.
All Gram-negative bacteria secrete OMVs that contain compo­
nents of the bacterial outer membrane, one of the main compo­
nents is OMPs.64 Recent research has provided convincing 
evidence for their roles in multiple aspects of bacterial physi­
ology and their interaction with host cells.65,66 Consistent with 
previous report,67 we confirmed that OmpA is the most prevalent 
OMP in the OMVs, a protein necessary for localization and 
immunomodulatory functions.42,43 In terms of efficacy, although 
EcN ΔOmpA−/−OMVs still showed a certain effect in alleviating 
AD pathology, the effectiveness was lower than that of naive EcN 
OMVs, suggesting a significant role played by OmpA in treat­
ment. Notably, the administration of OmpA via i.c.v. injection, 
but not i.v. injection, in APP/PS1 mice could decelerate AD pro­
gression. This was probably due to the clearance of bacterial ef­
fectors in circulation by the host via secreting antibodies, and the 
inherent surface of the lipid phase and physical structure of 
OMVs might protect these effectors from exposure to the clear­
ance mechanisms.68,69 Also, OmpA is a critical structural 
component of EcN OMVs that is necessary for this immunomod­
ulatory effect, as it orchestrates vesicle trafficking and may 
directly interact with inflammatory signaling pathways within 
the target glial cells, thereby reshaping the neuroimmune micro­
environment and mitigating neuroinflammation in AD. In addition 
to proteins, other vesicle components, such as metabolites, also 
contribute significantly to the immunomodulatory properties of 
OMVs.49,50,70 Therefore, further investigation is warranted to 
investigate whether there are other components in EcN OMVs

Overall, EcN OMVs slowed down the AD progression, with 
glial and neuronal cells likely serving as the target cells for EcN 
OMVs. We further revealed the beneficial effects of EcN on brain 
functionality, possibly through the regulation of astrocyte-micro­
glia-neuron interactions by OmpA-containing OMVs. Our find­
ings provide insights into how probiotic-derived OMVs and their 
cargo mitigate AD pathology by modulating immune responses 
and neuroimmune crosstalk in the CNS, thereby establishing a 
mechanistic foundation for developing OMV-based therapies 
aimed at functional recovery in AD.
Limitations of the study
Although we demonstrate that OmpA-containing EcN OMVs 
alleviate AD pathology, the precise molecular targets and 
signaling pathways through which OMVs modulate neuroim­
mune interactions remain elusive. In addition, the mechanisms 
governing OMV translocation across biological barriers into the 
brain parenchyma require additional clarification. While OmpA 
appears critical for OMV function, the specific contributions of 
other vesicle components, such as metabolites and additional 
OMPs, warrant further investigation. Finally, translation of these 
preclinical findings into clinical applications necessitates 
comprehensive toxicological assessments, determination of 
optimal dosing regimens, and evaluation of long-term safety pro­
files to establish the therapeutic feasibility of OMV-based inter­
ventions for neurological disorders.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS
Study cohort
Individuals with AD were diagnosed according to the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association guidelines, and all patients 
had a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score < 25/30 at baseline to be eligible for the study. We recruited a total of AD 18 
patients, including 4 male patients and 14 female patients. We also included 22 healthy controls with an MMSE score >27/30, no 
memory symptoms, no signs of dementia, and no other significant diseases. This group comprised 9 healthy male controls and 
13 healthy female controls. Patients and healthy normal volunteers were between 60 and 90 years of age, had not taken antibiotics 
within two months, and had no serious metabolic diseases. The fecal samples were collected and stored at −80◦C until analysis. 
Informed consent to participate in the study was obtained from all the patients and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the 
Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (reference IIT-O-2024-136).
Bacterial strains and isolation of OMVs
All EcN strains including naive EcN, EcN Cre+/+ and ΔOmpA−/−EcN used in this paper were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) overnight at 
37◦C in shake cultivation at 250 rpm/min. Both Cre gene knockin and OmpA gene knockout in EcN showed no impact on their growth 
(Figure S3F). The OMVs were isolated as described previously with modifications.16 In brief, bacteria-free supernatant was collected 
by two centrifugation steps of at 10,000 g for 15 min at 4◦C. The supernatants were filtered sequentially using 0.45 and 0.22 μm mem­
brane filters. OMVs were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 150,000 x g for 2 h at 4◦C in a Beckman SW32Ti rotor. After removing the 
supernatant, the crude OMVs were resuspended in PBS and ultracentrifugation again at 150,000 x g for 2 h at 4◦C. Then, the purified 
OMVs were suspended in PBS and stored at −80◦C for further use. The protein content of OMVs was assessed by BCA protein assay 
kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
Animals and manipulations
Wild-type C57BL/6J (10–12 weeks, 20–23 weeks), APP/PS1 (20–23 weeks) and Rosa26.tdTomato reporter (10–12 weeks) mice were 
bred at an SPF animal facility. Mice were kept in individually ventilated cages under a 12-h dark/12-h light cycle and received food 
and water ad libitum. Both male and female Rosa26.tdTomato mice were used, and all others were females. The animals were 
randomly allocated to experimental groups and treated with EcN, OMVs and OmpA. These mice were sacrificed at the end of treat­
ment or cognition tests. All animal experiments were performed under the Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the 
National Institutes of Health and were approved by the Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanchang University 
(NCULAE-20250224001).
METHOD DETAILS
Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)
The size and concentration of OMVs were analyzed with NTA using Zetaview system (Particle Metrix, Germany). Five videos of 30 s 
each were recorded for every sample, and the average of five videos was presented as the final size and concentration count for each 
sample. For the machine calibration, standard polystyrene beads of known sizes (100 and 200 nm) were used.
Electron microscopy
Purified OMVs were visualized by negative and immunogold staining TEM as described previously 1,2. In short, samples were 
spotted on a parafilm sheet. Next, formvar/C-coated hexagonal copper grids (EMS G200H-Cu), which were glow discharged for 
10 s, were placed on top of the droplet for 1 min with the coated side of the grid down. The grids were washed 5 times in droplets 
of Milli-Q water, stained with 1% (w/v) uranyl acetate for 10 s and air dried for 24 h before imaging. Visualization of the samples was 
done using a JEM 1400plus TEM (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operating at 80 kV.

Electrophysiological recordings and analysis
Electrophysiology was performed at the Jarvisbio.cn as described previously with modifications.47 Specifically, mice were anesthe­

Y-maze spontaneous alternation test
The Y-maze apparatus was made of gray plastic, consisting of three compartments (3 cm (W) bottom and 10 cm (W) top, 40 cm

5 min. We recorded and analyzed the spontaneous behavioral alternations of the mice using Visutrack (Shanghai, China). A reduction 
in the behavioral alternations corresponds to memory impairment. To exclude the existence of olfactory cues, the apparatus was 
thoroughly cleaned with 20% ethanol after each trial.

Novel object recognition test
The mice were placed in a white, acrylic box (40 × 40 × 40 cm). During the training trial, mice were allowed to explore the arena with 
two identical objects which were placed at two opposite positions within the box at the same distance from the nearest corner. The 
mice were returned to their home cage for 15 min (short-term memory) after the training phase. After that, mice were placed back in 
the same box for 5 min after replacing one of the familiar objects with a novel one. Exploration of the objects by the mice was defined 
as touching the object with their nose or mouth. All trials were videotaped and scored manually using Visutrack (Shanghai, China). 
The percentage preference for the novel object was calculated using the following formula: percentage novel object preferen­
ce = (novel object exploration time/(novel object exploration time + familiar object exploration time)) × 100%. A value > 50% indi­
cates preference for the novel object. To exclude the existence of olfactory cues, the box and the objects were thoroughly cleaned 
with 20% ethanol after each trial.
Statistical analysis
The current study used the Unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test to determine statistical significance between two independent 
groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey test was used to determine statistical significance among multiple groups 
with one independent variable. Graphical data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 and presented as mean ± SEM. A p value of 
<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

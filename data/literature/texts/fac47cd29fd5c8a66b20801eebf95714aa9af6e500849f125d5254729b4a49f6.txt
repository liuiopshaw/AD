Water-soluble metal nanoclusters (MNCs) have received extraordinary attention in both fundamental and
applied ﬁelds due to their ultrasmall size, unique molecular-like properties, rich surface chemistry, benign
biocompatibility, and good stability. Currently, the state-of-the-art research on water-soluble MNCs has
been upgraded from the nanoscale to the molecular level especially in the following aspects: (1) synthesis
of highly luminescent MNCs featuring aggregation-induced emission (AIE), (2) Engineering the ligand
shell of MNCs for controllable surface chemistry and (3) Tracking the reductive growth process of MNCs.
Such molecular-level research progress of water-soluble MNCs in turn facilitated their development in bio-
medical applications. In this Frontier Article, we start our discussion by brieﬂy summarizing the recent
molecular-level research progress of water-soluble MNCs in the above-mentioned three aspects, followed
by our perspectives on these fundamental aspects. Afterwards, the latest advance in biomedical applications
of water-soluble MNCs is discussed. We hope that this Frontier Article could stimulate more studies on the
molecular- or atomic-level understanding and biomedical applications of water-soluble MNCs.
Introduction
Metal nanoclusters (MNCs) are ultrasmall nanoparticles com-
posed of a metal core of <3 nm and an organic ligand shell,
which have attracted much research attention in the past few
decades.1,2 MNCs have discrete electronic transitions because
of the quantum confinement eﬀect and display intriguing
molecular-like
properties
such
as
luminescence,3–7
magnetism,8,9 chirality,10–12 and HOMO–LUMO transition.13–15
Such molecular-like properties of MNCs together with their
intrinsic attributes (e.g., ultrasmall size, rich surface chemistry,
and good stability) make them promising in various fields
including environmental protection,16,17 catalysis,18,19 energy
conversion,20,21 and biomedical science.22–28
The usability of MNCs in these applications is largely dic-
tated by the surface chemistry of their ligand shells.29–31 Based
on ligand attributes, MNCs can be simply classified as hydro-
phobic (i.e., oil-soluble) and hydrophilic MNCs (i.e., water-
soluble).32
While
hydrophobic
MNCs
have
made
many
advances in terms of catalysis,31,33 crystallography,34–40 etc.,
hydrophilic MNCs with benign biocompatibility are particularly
attractive in biomedical applications such as biosensing,16,41,42
bioimaging,43–46 disease diagnosis,28 biomedicine,27,47 and bio-
catalysis.25 In the past few years, tremendous eﬀorts have been
devoted to the methodological design and synthesis of water-
soluble MNCs toward deep understanding of the fundamental
synthetic mechanisms48–50 and investigating physicochemical
properties,51–54 as well as exploring biomedical applications of
MNCs.26,42 From these studies, an interesting phenomenon is
that the research trend is going deep into molecular precision
from the nanoscale level with the aid of high-resolution charac-
terization techniques.51,55–58 Remarkable progress has been
consequently achieved at the molecular level in the following
three aspects for water-soluble MNCs: (1) Synthesis of highly
luminescent MNCs featuring aggregation-induced emission
(AIE),7,59–63 (2) Engineering the ligand shell of MNCs for con-
trollable surface chemistry52–54,64 and (3) Tracking the reductive
growth process of MNCs.50,55–58 Naturally, such advances of
water-soluble MNCs in turn facilitated their development in bio-
medical applications.25,42,47

Synthesis of AIE-featured highly
luminescent MNCs
Acquisition of highly luminescent MNCs is always a major
goal in the field of cluster research, as luminescent MNCs
possess some unique advantages such as excellent biocompat-
ibility and good photostability.16,65 Currently, highly lumines-
cent MNCs have been considered as a complementary lumi-
nescent
probe
for
environmental
and
biomedical
applications.17,42 However, the relatively weak luminescence of
MNCs (usually <5% of quantum yield (QY)) could curtail their
further development in these applications. Therefore, extra
research eﬀort has been dedicated to the design of water-
soluble highly luminescent MNCs in recent years, and certain
exciting achievements have been made.
A series of strategies have been designed to synthesize
highly luminescent MNCs through either tailoring the metal
core composition or modulating the surface ligands or M(I)-
thiolate motifs (here M denotes a metal atom).59–63 Recently,
a novel class of highly luminescent MNCs with an interesting
aggregation-induced emission (AIE)66,67 feature were developed
by Luo et al. via engineering the surface M(I)-thiolate com-
plexes of MNCs at the molecular level (Fig. 1a).7,59 The key
strategy is to immobilize abundant AIE-featured Au(I)-thiolate
complexes on the surface of Au(0) cores to construct unique
Au(0)@Au(I)-thiolate NCs (Fig. 1a). These AIE-featured Au(0)
@Au(I)-thiolate NCs show an intense orange emission at
610 nm with a QY of ∼15% (Fig. 1b), where the strong lumine-
scence originates from the compact shell of Au(I)-thiolate com-
plexes. With the help of high-resolution electrospray ionization
mass spectrometry (ESI MS), the proportion between Au(0)
atoms in the core and the Au(I) species in the Au(I)-thiolate
shell could be estimated. Based on the AIE principle,7,68 the
more compact the shell of the M(I)-thiolate complex in the
MNCs (i.e., higher aggregation degree), the stronger the
luminescence of MNCs delivered.

Theoretically, it is easier to form a denser shell on the MNC
surface with longer M(I)-thiolate motifs, which is proved to be
feasible in synthesizing highly luminescent MNCs with the
AIE feature. On this basis, several smart protocols have been
designed by employing longer M(I)-thiolate motifs to increase
the aggregation degree of the M(I)-thiolate shell of MNCs,60–63
resulting in the formation of a couple of AIE-featured highly
luminescent MNCs. For example, Yu et al. reported the syn-
thesis of AIE-featured Au22(GSH)18 NCs with a QY of ∼10%,
where two RS-[Au-SR]3 and two longer RS-[Au-SR]4 motifs are
deployed to protect the Au8 core (GSH denotes glutathione; SR
denotes the thiolate ligand).60 Dou et al. introduced Ag(I)
species on the surface of weakly luminescent GSH-protected
Au NCs to link short Au(I)-GSH motifs to longer ones.61 This
post-treatment of the Au(I)-GSH motifs made the NC shell
more compact, which lights up the luminescence of MNCs via
the AIE mechanism. More recently, Wang et al. added a Ag(I)
precursor into the reaction system that was initially used to
prepare the Au(0)@Au(I)-thiolate NCs for synthesizing bi-
metallic AuAg NCs.63 During the formation of bimetallic AuAg
NCs, the added Ag(I) species could connect the Au(I)-thiolate
motifs to the NC shell in diﬀerent ways, which leads to a
luminescence enhancement of up to 3.9 times for AuAg NCs
compared to the plain Au NCs. In addition, AIE-featured
highly luminescent Ag NCs and Cu NCs have also been syn-
thesized in a similar way.62,69–71
The progress in the design of AIE-featured highly lumines-
cent MNCs is very impressive, and the luminescence of water-
soluble MNCs is markedly improved by AIE contribution (QY
from ∼0.1% to >10%). Such strong luminescence of MNCs has
motivated the advance of this newly designed luminescent
probe in many luminescence-involved applications including
sensing, imaging, light harvesting, and electronic devices. It
should be mentioned that at the present stage the AIE-featured
MNC family is rather small and it is expected that more MNCs
with stronger luminescence (QYs of >20%) could be reported
in the near future, paving the way to some specific applications
where ultrabright luminescence is particularly required. In
addition, more highly luminescent AIE-featured MNCs with
multi-functionalities (e.g., targeting ability, drug loading/
release capability, and biodegradability) are vastly desired to
meet diverse application needs.
Ligand shell engineering of water-
soluble MNCs for controllable surface
chemistry
The surface chemistry of water-soluble MNCs is vital to their
application performance since the ligand shell of MNCs
makes direct contact with the target substrate (e.g., bacteria
and cells) to form an interface in many applications.24,29,31
Therefore, the application performance of MNCs could be
largely tailored through engineering the ligand shell of MNCs.
While controlling the metal core of MNCs in terms of size and
composition has been well studied in the community,1,2,48
some research attention has been recently paid to engineering
the ligand shell of water-soluble MNCs at the molecular
level.52–54,64
To engineer the ligand shell of MNCs, a primary require-
ment is the accurate control of the functional groups in the
ligand shell of MNCs. Yuan et al. oﬀered a paradigm in this
aspect.64 They employed two types of hydrophilic thiolate
ligands with diﬀerent functional groups (e.g., –COOH, –OH,
–NH2) for the synthesis of water-soluble bi-ligand protected
Au25 NCs via a “NaOH-mediated NaBH4 Reduction” method
(Fig. 2a and b). By varying the feeding ratio of bi-ligands, the
population of such two types of ligands on the surface of Au
NCs could be accurately controlled. Consequently, the number
of functional groups on the NC surface could be tailored at the
molecular level (Fig. 2c). Moreover, the thickness and steric
hindrance of the ligand shell in MNCs can also be finely regu-
lated in this study. The multi-ligand protected MNCs with
precise control of surface functional groups are interesting
because they not only provide a good platform to study their
physicochemical
properties
(e.g.,
stabilities
and
optical
properties),52,54 but also oﬀer ideal research models for explor-
ing interface-related applications,29,31 deepening the funda-
mental understanding of multi-ligand protected MNCs and
expanding their functionalities for broader applications.
Engineering the surface chemistry of MNCs is expected to
be a future research hotspot in the field because this will
endow MNCs with diverse functionalities for various appli-
cations.72 At present, the development in engineering the
ligand shell of MNCs towards surface chemistry control mark-
edly lags behind compared to the prosperous situation in con-
trolling the metal core of MNCs. While the latest research level
is merely limited to the molecular-level control of several types
of functional groups on the surface of MNCs, accurately graft-
ing diﬀerent functional molecules or species on the surface of
MNCs with tunable molecular ratios will be achievable in the

Tracking the reductive growth process
of water-soluble MNCs at the
molecular level
With unremitting eﬀorts for more than 3 decades, high-quality
MNCs of diﬀerent sizes/compositions have been successfully
synthesized. However, understanding the formation mecha-
nism of MNCs at the molecular level (i.e., how the MNCs
gradually grow from M(I)-SR complexes) was still very challen-
ging.55 Specifically, it was not possible to picture the detailed
growth pathway of MNCs without identifying all the intermedi-
ate NC species during the growth, which can be ascribed to
the following facts: (1) The reductive reaction is too fast to
tracking the intermediate NC species and (2) The synthetic
system is too complicated. These situations make the synthetic
process of MNCs more like a black box, which extremely
puzzled the MNC community. Recently, this puzzle has been
addressed by real-time tracking of the reductive growth
process of water-soluble MNCs at the molecular level by
ESI-MS in mild reductive reaction systems.55–58,73
In order to clearly monitor the growth process of MNCs, the
reduction kinetics of MNCs should be slowed down to provide
suﬃcient time for collecting and analysing intermediate
species during MNC synthesis. The Xie group created a mild
reduction environment by deploying a mild gaseous carbon
monoxide
(CO)
reductant
in
the
synthesis
of
Au
NCs
(Fig. 3a).55 The usage of mild gaseous CO could prolong the
reduction period and immediately quench the reaction at a
desired time interval, which enabled the real-time monitoring
of the reduction process by ESI-MS (Fig. 3b). As is shown, a
total of 29 Au(I)-SR complexes and NC intermediate species
were identified and tracked during the whole course of
[Au25(SR)18]−
formation
(Fig.
3b).
All
these
intermediate
species
could
be
expressed
by
a
general
formula
[Aun(SR)mClp]q,
where
the
valence
electron
count
equals
n−m−p−q. Based on detailed analysis, we found that the
growth of Au25(SR)18 follows a stepwise 2e−hopping mecha-
nism (i.e., 0e−→2e−→4e−→6e−→8e−→10e−) within the
CO-reduction system. Afterwards, tracking the growth process
of other MNCs (e.g., Au44 NCs, and alloy NCs) in both CO- and

fundamentals. Specifically, we anticipate that the real-time
monitoring studies would be conducted in the following two

Biomedical applications of water-
soluble MNCs
Water-soluble
MNCs
usually
have
good
biocompatibility
especially when using thiol-containing peptide molecules as
protecting ligands (e.g., glutathione or GSH).74 Besides, the
ultrasmall size, rich surface chemistry, unique chemical pro-
perties, and strong luminescence of MNCs endow them with
versatile applicability in the biomedical field. Therefore, hydro-
philic MNCs are particularly promising in biomedical appli-
cations,
and
several interesting
achievements have been
accomplished in the fields of antibacterial agents,29,47,75–78
cancer radiotherapy,27,79,80 and biocatalysis.25
In recent years, the Xie group worked on the design of
highly eﬃcient MNC-based antibacterial agents against drug-
resistant bacteria.19,29,47,75–77 For example, engineering the oxi-
dation states of the Ag core of Ag NCs with rich Ag(I), GSH-pro-
tected Ag
NCs exhibited
excellent
antibacterial activities
against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria due
to the higher antibacterial activity of Ag(I) than Ag(0).76
Moreover, grafting conventional antibiotics on the NC surface
could also eﬀectively improve the antibacterial performance of
Ag NCs because of the synergistic eﬀect of both antibiotics
and Ag NCs on antibacterial eﬃcacy.78 Besides Ag NCs, Zheng
et al. recently demonstrated that ultrasmall Au NCs (e.g.,
Au25MHA18;
MHA
denotes
the
6-mercaptohexanoic
acid
ligand)
also
have
significant
bacterial
killing
eﬃcacy
(Fig. 4a).77 The antibacterial eﬃcacy of Au NCs is much higher
than those of large sized MHA-protected Au NPs (≈6 nm) and
MHA-Au(I) complexes. The enhanced antibacterial perform-
ance of Au NCs was ascribed to the NC-induced imbalanced
metabolism
(Fig.
4a),
where
upregulating
pro-oxidative
enzymes and down-regulating reductive enzymes concurrently
produce
excess
reactive
oxygen
species
(ROS,
Fig.

Moreover, they unexpectedly
found that more negatively
charged Au NCs could generate more ROS, and thus resulted
in a better bacterial killing eﬃciency.29 This finding is in stark
contrast to the common sense that antibacterial agents with a
more positively charged surface have higher antimicrobial
eﬃcacy, suggesting the complexities of the nanomaterial–cell
interactions.
It was also exemplified that hydrophilic MNCs could be a
good candidate of radiosensitizers for cancer treatment.27,79,80
For example, sub-2 nm Au10(GSH)10 NCs (Fig. 4c, upper panel)
could readily escape from the reticuloendothelial system (RES)
and accumulate in tumours via the enhanced permeability
and retention (EPR) eﬀect, which leads to enhanced cancer
radiotherapy (Fig. 4c, lower panel).27 Last year, Yang and co-
workers introduced ultra-small luminescent Au NCs (acting as
a biocompatible intracellular photosensitizer) into non-photo-

The progress of water-soluble MNCs in biomedical appli-
cations is exciting. Meanwhile, the fact that current studies of
MNCs in biomedical applications are just at the stage of proof-
of-concept is also noticed, and there is still a good way to go
from laboratory studies to clinical assessment. Therefore,
more research eﬀorts are crucially needed, which could on the
one hand deeply probe the undiscovered fundamental knowl-
edge, and on the other hand promote the relevant research to
a higher level. For instance, deeper understanding of the inter-
actions between MNCs and organisms (e.g., clearance pathway
and cytotoxicity) is strongly invited, which could meet the
increasing demands for their in vivo applications. In addition,
we believe that other biomedical applications of MNCs will be
explored (e.g., bacterial sensing and cell apoptosis), and this
will in turn stimulate more interest in designing water-soluble
MNCs with novel functionalities.
Conclusions
To sum up, the past few years have witnessed the rapid
advances of water-soluble MNCs, which upgraded relevant
studies from the nanoscale to molecular level. For example,
water-soluble highly luminescent MNCs with the AIE feature
could be synthesized by modulating the surface M(I)-thiolate
motifs. The surface chemistry of MNCs could be precisely con-
trolled via engineering the ligand shell composition, and the
growth mechanism of MNCs could be disclosed through track-
ing the intermediate NC species in mild reductive growth
systems. Such molecular-level studies not only benefit funda-
mental understanding and methodological design of MNCs,
but also provide rich research models for application explora-
tions. In addition, owing to their good biocompatibility, water-
soluble MNCs have also been demonstrated to be a good can-
didate for biomedical applications, where MNCs performed
excellently as a high-eﬃciency antibacterial agent, a thera-
peutic radiosensitizer, and an intracellular photosensitizer.
However,
we
also
perceived
that
the
molecular-level
research of MNCs is just beginning, and more research
eﬀorts are required to trigger the exponential development of
this novel functional nanomaterial at the molecular level or
even atomic level. In particular, more delicate modulation in
the surface M(I)-thiolate motifs of MNCs is strongly required to
obtain
various
multi-functional
AIE-featured
MNCs
for
meeting diﬀerent application needs. In addition, further
enhancing the luminescence of MNCs to exceed 20% is also
chiefly encouraged in the field. In terms of precisely control-
ling the surface chemistry of MNCs, ligand shell engineering
is an eﬃcient way to endow MNCs with suﬃcient functional-
ities for diverse applications. It is expected that accurately
grafting a number of functional molecules or species on the
surface of MNCs with tunable molecular ratios will be achiev-
able in the near future, which will intensely expand their appli-
cability in applied fields. Regarding the real-time monitoring
of MNCs at the molecular level, besides tracking the growth
process of water-soluble MNCs, such an interesting probing
technique will be helpful in other aspects of MNCs. For
example, it can be used to study the growth mechanism of
hydrophobic MNCs in a two-phase synthetic system; similarly,
it may also be applied to probe the decomposition mechanism
of MNCs at the molecular level. In the aspect of biomedical
applications, we also call for more research contributions to
deepen the investigations on the fundamentals (e.g., clearance
pathway and cytotoxicity issues) in the present applications as
well as to promote the advance of other biomedical appli-
cations of MNCs (e.g., bacterial sensing and cell apoptosis).
On the basis of the above discussion, the development of
water-soluble MNCs has stepped from conventional nanoscale
research
to
molecular-level
study,

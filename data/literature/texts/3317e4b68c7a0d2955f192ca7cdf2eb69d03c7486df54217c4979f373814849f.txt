Silver Ion-Triggered Fabrication of AuAg Bimetallic Aerogel
Superstructures with Tailored Antibacterial Property for
Advanced Anti-Infection Management
Yumeng Xue, Jie Xu, Tianyi Wang, Fangfang Liu, Kangqiang Liang, Qiang Li,
Yunshan Gao, Han Li, Qifei Wu,* and Li Shang*
The clinical application of antibiotic-free, Ag-based antibacterial agents
remains a signiﬁcant challenge due to uncontrolled Ag+-release and limited
long-term antibacterial activity. Herein, a robust antibacterial platform based
on Ag+-triggered self-assembled AuAg aerogel is developed for advanced
infectious wound management. By employing ultrasmall-sized gold
nanoclusters (AuNCs) as building blocks, Ag+ serves as the gelator to actively
interact with AuNCs through multiple types of interactions, including
coordination, metallophilic interactions, and the Anti-Galvanic Reduction
reaction. As a result, novel 3D self-supported porous AuAg bimetallic aerogels
can be controllably fabricated. The obtained AuAg aerogels exhibit tunable
ligament size in the range of 8.5–32.0 nm, adjustable composition, and
controllable antibacterial properties. Mechanistic studies reveal that the initial
concentration of Ag+ plays a critical role in determining the nanostructure
and composition of AuAg aerogels as well as their antibacterial eﬃcacy. A
higher concentration of Ag+ enables a more stable and sustainable release of
Ag+ from AuAg aerogels, leading to long-term and on-demand anti-infection
eﬀects. Consequently, these AuAg aerogels display signiﬁcant anti-infection,
anti-inﬂammation, and pro-regenerative eﬀects for the treatment of infectious
wounds, as demonstrated by the in vivo studies. This work provides a new
approach for reasonable design and ﬂexible manipulation of metal aerogels
for versatile biomedical applications.

Noble metal aerogels, an emerging type of functional mate-

nanostructured noble metals but also oﬀer excellent structural
stability and abundant active sites for interacting with target ob-

release of antimicrobial agents, such as Ag+ ions, making them
promising candidates for in vivo disinfection applications.[21]
However, the precise preparation of noble metal aerogels with
controlled Ag+ release properties for the eﬀective management
of infected wounds has not been fully exploited yet.
In this study, we report the fabrication of AuAg bimetallic aero-
gel by using Ag+ as the inducer and gold nanoclusters (AuNCs)
as the functional building blocks. As illustrated in Scheme 1,
Ag+ triggers the assembly, fusion, and stepwise gelation of L-
penicillamine (LPA)-protected AuNCs via strong interactions
with both surface gold atoms and LPA ligands of AuNCs, leading
to the formation of a 3D porous AuAg bimetallic aerogel. By ad-
justing the Ag+ concentration, the gelation process, microstruc-
ture, and Ag+ release behavior of AuAg aerogels can be ﬁnely
tuned, enabling precise control over the antibacterial activity of
AuAg aerogels for anti-infection and pro-regeneration applica-
tions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the ﬁrst study of de-
veloping metal aerogels for the treatment of infected wounds,
which also provides a new perspective on the design of robust,
antibiotic-free metal nanomaterials for combating chronic infec-
tions in wound treatment.

in the formation of Ag-rich nanostructures during the gelation,

The porous microstructure of the aerogels was characterized
by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning elec-
tron microscopy (SEM). AuAg aerogels prepared with four dif-
ferent concentrations of Ag+ were denoted as AuAg aerogel-
1 (100 μm Ag+), AuAg aerogel-2 (1 mm Ag+), AuAg aerogel-
3 (2 mm Ag+), and AuAg aerogel-4 (5 mm Ag+). As shown in
Figure 2a, all AuAg aerogels exhibited typical 3D hierarchically
self-supported porous structures, providing a large surface area
for interfacial interactions and enabling eﬃcient mass transport
of active components (e.g., Ag+), indicating their great poten-
tial for further biomedical applications. TEM images revealed
that all aerogels possess characteristic microporous structures
composed of hyperbranched networks of ﬁne AuAg nanowires
(Figure 2b). Quantitative analysis of the ligament size showed
that AuAg aerogel-1 prepared with 100 μm Ag+ is fairly uniform
with an average nanowire diameter of 8.5 ± 3.2 nm (Figure 2c).
Such a small ligament size can be attributed to the ultrasmall
size of the building blocks (i.e., AuNCs). As the initial concen-
tration of Ag+ increased, the average diameter of nanowires in-
creased progressively, reaching 32.0 ± 14.4 nm when 5 mm
Ag+ was used (AuAg aerogel-4). Meanwhile, this process was ac-
companied by the gradual formation of an aggregate structure,
likely due to the rapid particle growth caused by strong inter-
action between Ag+ and AuNCs. High-resolution TEM images
shown in Figure 2d,e indicated that both AuAg aerogel-2 and
AuAg aerogel-4 contained crystalline domains with interplanar
distances of ≈0.23 and 0.20 nm, corresponding to the (111) and
(200) planes of face-centered cubic (fcc) Au (Ag), respectively.[26]
It is noteworthy that tremendous grain boundaries, kinks, and
defects along the nanowires could be observed, which are ex-
pected to oﬀer additional active sites for catalysis and biomedi-
cal applications.[27] Then, high-angle annular dark-ﬁeld scanning
transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) imaging and
energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) were used to identify the
spatial distribution of Au and Ag elements, as well as the nanos-
tructure of AuAg aerogels. As shown in Figures 3a and S3 (Sup-
porting Information), both AuAg aerogel-2 and AuAg-aerogel-
4 exhibited porous structures. Au and Ag are uniformly dis-
tributed in AuAg aerogels, with no evident aggregation along
nanowire edges, suggesting a strong interaction between Au and
Ag at the atomic level. This phenomenon indicated that Ag+
not only served as a gelator to initiate the assembly of AuNCs
but also reacted with both the surface and core of AuNCs to
form the alloy structures. The elemental content of Au and Ag
in diﬀerent aerogels was quantiﬁed through inductively coupled
plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) analysis. As
shown in Table S1 (Supporting Information), the ﬁnal compo-
sition of AuAg aerogels depended on the initial concentration
of the gelator. Speciﬁcally, as the initial concentration of Ag+
increased, the Au content decreased while the Ag content in-
creased. For example, the percentage of Au and Ag elements in
AuAg aerogel-1 was measured to be 86.24% and 3.21%, respec-
tively, which changed to 45.67% and 59.86% in AuAg aerogel-4,
respectively.
The chemical composition and valence state of diﬀerent com-
ponents in AuAg bimetallic aerogels were characterized by X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Figure 3b, Au 4f
spectra of all AuAg aerogels can be deconvoluted into Au 4f7/2
and Au 4f5/2. The Au 4f7/2 possesses two major peaks at ≈83.8
and 84.2 eV, indicating the presence of both Au(0) and Au(I)
states (Table S2, Supporting Information). Interestingly, quanti-
tative analysis based on XPS spectra revealed an increase in the
percentage of Au(0) in AuAg aerogels compared to AuNCs, mani-
festing the reduction of Au(I) to Au(0) during the gelation process
(Table S3, Supporting Information). In the XPS spectra of Ag 3d,
characteristic Ag 3d5/2 and Ag 3d3/2 peaks were observed for all
aerogels, as shown in Figure 3c. Further analysis revealed that
the Ag 3d5/2 comprised two peaks at ≈368.3 and 367.8 eV, while
the Ag 3d3/2 comprised two peaks at 373.8 and 374.2 eV, indicat-
ing the coexistence of Ag(I) and Ag(0) states in AuAg aerogels
(Table S2, Supporting Information). Moreover, the proportion of
Ag(I) in AuAg aerogel-1 was 58.50%, which raised to 62.81%,
64.11% and 70.62% in AuAg aerogel-2, AuAg aerogel-3 and AuAg
aerogel-4, respectively (Table S3, Supporting Information). Quan-
titative analysis of the atomic ratio of Ag to Au (nAg/nAu) based on
the integrated peak area and element sensitivity factors showed
that the nAg/nAu was 0.14 for AuAg aerogel-1, which increased
with the rising initial concentration of Ag+ and reached 2.30
for AuAg aerogel-4. These results indicate the signiﬁcant im-
pact of the gelator concentration on the chemical composition of
AuAg aerogels, which was in good consistency with the ICP-OES
results.

to the formation of AuAg bimetallic aerogels.

2.2. Characterization of AuAg Aerogels
The structure and crystallinity of AuAg aerogels were analyzed by
X-ray diﬀraction (XRD). Diﬀraction peaks located at 38.2°, 44.3°,
64.5°, 77.5°, and 81.6° can be assigned to the (111), (200), (220),
(311), and (222) planes of fcc Au (Ag), respectively. This fact is
consistent with the standard fcc lattices of Au (JCPDS No. 04–
0784) and Ag (JCPDS No. 04–0783), indicating the formation
of polycrystalline AuAg structures (Figure 4a).[31,32] The broad
Bragg reﬂections are attributed to the nanoparticulate nature
of the as-prepared AuAg aerogels.[33] In contrast, no diﬀraction
peaks were observed in the XRD pattern of individual AuNCs, re-
ﬂecting their amorphous nature. Although distinguishing fcc Au
from fcc Ag is challenging due to their similar lattice structures,
lattice constants (Au: 0.408 nm, Ag: 0.409 nm), and diﬀraction
patterns, the change in the XRD patterns of the AuAg aerogels
compared to AuNCs conﬁrmed the occurrence of chemical reac-
tions during the gelation process.[34] The structure of AuAg aero-
gels was also characterized by Fourier transform infrared spec-
troscopy (FTIR). As shown in Figure 4b, broad absorption bands
between 3200–3500 cm−1 can be ascribed to the stretching vibra-
tions of O─H and N─H, while peaks located at 2800–3000 cm−1
correspond to the C─H stretching vibration. Notably, compared
to AuNCs, AuAg aerogels displayed similar characteristic adsorp-
tion bands at 1382, 1040, and 1012 cm−1, which are assigned to
the symmetric bending vibrations of the C─H bond in CH3, the
stretching vibrations of a carboxyl group (O═C─OH), as well as
the stretching vibrations of the C─O bond.[35,36] These results in-
dicate the presence of organic residuals derived from the LPA
ligands of AuNCs in the resulting AuAg aerogels. Moreover, the

a mass loss of less than 10%, indicating a rather low content of
organic ligands in the aerogels. This ﬁnding further conﬁrms the
detachment of LPA ligands during the gelation process, which is
consistent with previous reports that cations can strip the sur-
face ligands from the building blocks.[37,38] Following surface
ligand removal, the fusion and aggregation of building blocks
can be further promoted due to the exposure of highly active
sites.
The speciﬁc surface area and porosity property of AuAg aero-
gels were evaluated by N2 physisorption isotherm, which exhib-
ited characteristics of both type II and type IV isotherms, indi-
cating the presence of meso- and macropores throughout the
aerogel framework (Figure 4d–g). Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET)
analysis indicated that the speciﬁc surface area of the aerogels
was 29.0 m2 g−1 (AuAg aerogel-1), 14.9 m2 g−1 (AuAg aerogel-2),
14.3 m2 g−1 (AuAg aerogel-3), and 13.0 m2 g−1 (AuAg aerogel-4).
Obviously, the speciﬁc surface area of the aerogels decreased as
the concentration of Ag+ increased, likely due to the instability of
excessive Ag+ that leads to quick reaction and aggregation as ob-
served in Figure 2b. Consequently, the disordered structures can
block the pores, limiting gas adsorption/desorption. Pore size
distribution results further conﬁrmed the presence of a broad
range of pore sizes (Figure S4, Supporting Information), from
micropores (<2 nm) to mesopores (2–50 nm). Additionally, the
absence of a plateau at high relative pressures (P/P0 = 0.9–1.0) in
the adsorption isotherm implied the co-existence of macropores
(>50 nm), consistent with the SEM observations (Figure 2a).[39]
The presence of abundant and interconnected pores across multi-
ple size ranges enhances the aerogel’s ability to capture and inter-
act with bacteria. Moreover, the reduction in speciﬁc surface area
with increased Ag+ concentration suggests that careful control
over the gelator concentration is critical for optimizing the aero-
gel’s structural and functional properties for further biomedical
applications.
2.3. Antibacterial Activity of AuAg Aerogels

The antibacterial activity of Ag-based nanomaterials is gen-
erally believed to depend primarily on the amount of Ag+
released.[41] Bacteria can produce substances like lactic acid, re-
sulting in a decrease in the pH of the microenvironment that
facilitates the release of Ag+.[42] Once released, Ag+ can adhere
to the negatively charged bacterial membrane through electro-
static interactions and bind to the thiol groups on membrane pro-
teins, inactivating key enzymes. This increases membrane per-
meability and compromises structural integrity, ultimately lead-
ing to membrane rupture. Additionally, Ag+ can penetrate the
bacterial membrane and further disrupt bacterial functions by
damaging DNA, blocking mRNA replication and transcription,
inactivating proteases, and inhibiting enzyme activity, which to-
gether lead to bacterial death.[14,43] In our study, AuAg aerogel-3
and AuAg aerogel-4 with higher Ag contents exhibited signiﬁ-

3. Conclusion
In summary, we have developed an innovative Ag+-induced
self-assembly method to prepare highly porous AuAg aerogels
with tailored and long-term antibacterial properties for infected
wound management. The AuAg aerogels were synthesized by us-
ing Ag+ as a gelator and AuNCs as functional building blocks,
resulting in a 3D fused-nanowire network structure with tun-
able ligament size ranging from 8.5 to 32.0 nm and a speciﬁc
surface area up to 29.0 m2 g−1. Gelation mechanism studies re-
vealed that Ag+ can actively interact with AuNCs through multi-
ple pathways to trigger the subsequent gelation process. Owing to
their highly porous structure and abundant metal nodes, a higher
concentration of Ag+ could lead to the formation of AuAg aero-
gels with a relatively larger nanowire size, higher content of Ag,

4. Experimental Section
Synthesis of AuNCs:
AuNCs were synthesized following the previously
reported method with minor modiﬁcations.[46] Brieﬂy, 60 μL of tetrakis
(hydroxymethyl) phosphonium chloride (THPC, 80% in water) was added
to 235 mL of NaOH solution (6 mm) at 37 °C. The mixture was stirred
for 3 min, followed by the rapid addition of a mixture solution of HAuCl4
(3.35 mL, 50 mm) and LPA (12.5 mL, 0.1 m). The reaction proceeded for
15 h with continuous stirring, during which the initially colorless solution
gradually turned golden yellow. The obtained AuNCs were stored in the
dark at 4 °C before further use.
Fabrication of AuAg Bimetallic Hydrogels and Aerogels:
The solution of
AuNCs was puriﬁed and concentrated via ultracentrifuge ﬁlters (Sartorius,
Vivaspin Turbo, 15 mL, MWCO: 10 kDa) with H2O as the solvent three
times. The puriﬁed and concentrated AuNCs were then re-dispersed in a
certain volume of H2O with a ﬁnal concentration of 2.5 mm, which was
calculated based on the molar concentration of Au according to ICP-OES
analysis. To initiate the gelation of AuNCs, the pH of the AuNC solution
(3.96 mL) was ﬁrst adjusted to 3.0, after which 40 μL of AgNO3 solution
was added. The ﬁnal concentrations of Ag+ were 0 μm, 100 μm, 500 μm,
1 mm, 2 mm, and 5 mm, respectively. After thoroughly mixing with a vortex
mixer, the mixture was kept in the dark at room temperature (25 °C) for
several days. As gelation progressed, ﬂuﬀy black solids gradually formed
and settled at the bottom of the glass vials. The resulting AuAg hydro-
gels were carefully collected and washed with water in a volume at least
15 times greater than that of the hydrogels, followed by stepwise replace-
ment of the supernatant with tert-butanol (ten times, twice per day). The
anhydrous, tert-butanol-containing gels were then freeze-dried to obtain
AuAg aerogels.
Evaluation of Antibacterial Property:
All used containers and materi-
als were sterilized prior to the experiments. The liquid Luria-Bertani (LB)
medium (1% tryptone, 0.5% yeast extract, 1% NaCl) was sterilized at
121 °C under high-pressure steam (0.1 MPa) for 20 min, and then allowed
to cool to room temperature before use. The solid LB medium (1% tryp-
tone, 0.5% yeast extract, 1% NaCl, 1.5% agar) was sterilized in the same
manner as the liquid medium. After sterilization, solid LB medium was
melted in the microwave oven, cooled to 60–70 °C, poured into sterile cul-
ture plates, and left to solidify before use. Two typical bacterial strains,
E. coli (Gram-negative) and S. aureus (Gram-positive), were employed
as models to evaluate the antibacterial performance of AuAg aerogels
through the spread plate method. A monocolony of bacteria was picked
from the solid LB agar plate, inoculated in 5 mL of liquid LB medium,
and then cultured at 37 °C in the shaker (200 rpm) for 12 h. Then, bac-
teria in the logarithmic growth phase were collected by centrifugation at
5000 rpm for 3 min, washed three times with sterile PBS buﬀer (10 mm, pH
7.4), and resuspended in PBS. The bacterial concentration was estimated
by measuring the optical density at the wavelength of 600 nm (OD600).
Finally, the bacterial suspension was diluted to 105 CFU (colony forming
unit) mL−1 by PBS for the antibacterial study. Typically, AuNCs or AuAg
bimetallic aerogels (2 mg) were immersed in 200 μL of bacterial suspen-
sion (105 CFU mL−1) and incubated in the shaker (200 rpm) at 37 °C for
6 h. Then, 100 μL of the bacterial suspension was uniformly spread on LB
agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 18 h. Bacteria suspensions with-
out AuNCs or AuAg aerogels served as the control group. Each group was
carried out with at least four parallel samples to ensure accuracy. CFUs
on the LB agar plates were then counted, and the bacterial inhibition ratio
(%) was calculated using the following equation:
Inhibition ratio % = Ec −Es

The morphological changes of bacteria treated with AuNCs or AuAg
aerogels were observed by SEM. 200 μL of bacterial suspension (108 CFU

then the bacteria were dropped onto a silicon wafer and allowed to dry at
room temperature for further sputter coating before SEM analysis.
Analysis of Ag+ Release Behavior:
To evaluate the in vitro release behav-
ior of Ag+, 50 mg of AuAg aerogel-2 or AuAg aerogel-4 were immersed in
10 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) containing E. coli suspension (108 CFU mL−1) and
incubated at 37 °C in a shaking incubator (200 rpm). At speciﬁc time points
(5 min, 6, 24, 48, and 72 h), 6 mL of Ag+-contained supernatant was ex-
tracted and immediately replaced with 6 mL of fresh E. coli contained PBS.
The extracted supernatant was digested and ﬁltered. The concentration of
released Ag+ was measured through ICP-OES.

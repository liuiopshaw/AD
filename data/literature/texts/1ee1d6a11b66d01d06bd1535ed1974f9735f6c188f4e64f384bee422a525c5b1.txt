Due to their ultrasmall size, low toxicity, strong photoluminescence and ease with which they can be
chemically modiﬁed, luminescent metal (and especially the group IB metal) nanoclusters have attracted
increasing research interest, and have emerged as one of the most attractive nanomaterials. Several
methods, such as one-pot, ligand exchange, and metal exchange methods have been recently explored
to synthesize atomically precise nanoclusters of various sizes and compositions; and the luminescence
intensity of some of these is comparable to or even superior to that of the traditional ﬂuorescent nano-
materials (such as quantum dots), and organic dyes. Speciﬁcally, their high photostability, purity and
atomic precision provide an opportunity for an in-depth understanding into structure–optical property
correlations. This review summarizes recent progress in the ﬁeld, including synthetic protocols, struc-
tural characteristics, luminescence properties, and mechanistic investigations. The prospects for future
development and applications are also covered.

1. Introduction
Precision is a fundamental goal of scientiﬁc research. The recent
exploration of ultrasmall MNCs, typically with diameters of less
than 2 nm [1,2], has not only revealed the exact atomic packing
modes for NCs with speciﬁc number of metal atoms and type(s)
of ligands, but also shed light on the long-standing problems such
as the quantum size effect [3–6], discrete electronic states [7,2],
and the synergistic effect [8,9] between different metal atoms.
Speciﬁcally, atomic precision is an essential prerequisite for
exploring the correlations between the microscopic atomic struc-
tures and the macroscopic physicochemical (such as optical, elec-
trochemical
and
magnetic)
properties,
as
well
as
practical
applications in catalysis [10,11,25] and biomedicine [12,13]. To
this end, the target-oriented synthesis of NCs with speciﬁc func-
tions (or within a conﬁned environment) becomes more applicable
with the aid of the well-designed protocols.
Structurally, ultrasmall NCs ﬁll the gap between discrete atoms
and plasmonic nanomaterials. The accurate elucidation of atomic
packing modes makes it possible to deduce the evolutionary mech-
anism from the metal complex (or cluster precursors) to the nano-
materials [14–16]. In addition, comparisons between a series of
structurally related NCs can inform inherent structure–perfor-
mance relationships, which are fundamentally important for
designing novel property-enhancing strategies. Due to their high
mono-dispersity, low toxicity, and good bio-compatibility, the PL
of the ultrasmall MNCs (mainly the group IB MNCs) has attracted
extensive research interest, and the ﬁeld has seen rapid progress
in recent years [11,17,18].
One of the most attractive categories of atomically precise lumi-
nescent NCs, the group IB (i.e., Au, Ag, and Cu) metal NCs, has been
extensively studied in the past decades [19]. Preliminary studies
have mainly focused on PL of the Group IB metal complexes and
multinuclear (also known as polynuclear) clusters with non-
metal bridges (such as halides, alkynyl, and phosphine ligands).
Some insightful studies have been reported by Hardt, Vogler, Ford,
Zink, Yam, Laguna, Patterson, Balch, Fackler, Che, Omary, Eisen-
berg, Koshevoy, Li, etc., and readers are referred to the excellent
review articles listed in Refs. [20–25]. In contrast to the metal com-
plexes and multinuclear clusters mentioned above, we mainly
focus on the luminescent MNCs, which typically adopt core–shell
structures [1,11,18,25]. In these clusters, compact metal–metal
bonding networks (containing more than ﬁve metal atoms, unless
otherwise speciﬁed) comprise the framework of the metal core,
and the shell is mainly made up of the metal–organic, or organic
ligands motifs.
The atomically precise Au NCs have been extensively reported
in recent years, whereas it was generally accepted that the prepa-
ration of atomically precise Ag or Cu NCs would be much more
challenging, due to their inferior stability compared to that of the
Au NCs [11]. However, with the recent exploration of novel syn-
thetic protocols, both the homometallic Ag [18,26]/Cu [27–29]
NCs and their alloy NCs (with Au, Pd, Pt, etc.) [30] have been suc-
cessfully synthesized and characterized by SCXRD, ESI-MS, XPS,
etc.
In this review article, the MNCs are categorized into two groups,
depending on the number of free electrons: molecular clusters
(Section 3.1) and metal clusters (Sections 3.2–3.5). For a given
NC with the formula of [MxLyDz]q, the number of free electrons
(Ne) is calculated as Ne = x  y  q, where M is the group IB metal
atoms (Au/Ag/Cu), L is the one-electron withdrawing ligands, D is
the two-electron donating ligand, and q is the total charge of the
cluster. Typically, metal clusters bear 2, 4, 8, 16 or 20 free electrons
[18] in contrast, no free electrons are present in the molecular clus-
ters. The number of free electrons is expected to signiﬁcantly inﬂu-
ence the physical–chemical properties of the clusters [65–67].
Traditional, atomically-precise MNCs are generally only weakly
luminescent, and of low quantum yield (QY). However, a system-
atic understanding of structure–optical property relationships
has allowed the development of several strategies such as ligand
engineering and foreign atom doping, which have been used to
improve the PL of the target NCs. To date, QYs of the group IB
NCs of up to 92% [31] and 67% [32] have been reported. Encouraged
by these achievements, we sought to write a review article. This
review mainly covers the synthetic protocols, structural character-
istics,
luminescent
properties,
and
structure–luminescence
correlations of the group IB MNCs with atomic precision, and spec-
ulates as to the prospects for future research in the ﬁeld. For clarity,

2. General synthetic protocols for luminescent nanoclusters
Synthetic methods for ultrasmall MNCs have been greatly
improved in recent decades, which has signiﬁcantly stimulated
the preparation of atomically precise luminescent NCs. These
methods can be broadly categorized into three groups: one-pot
synthesis;
ligand
exchange
methods;
and
metal
exchange
methods.
The one-pot synthesis (also known as kinetically controlled
method or the co-reduction method in some cases) is one of the
most versatile, simple, and straightforward synthetic methods,
and generally comprises the direct mixing of all species continu-
ously, without the necessity to isolate any intermediate (Fig. 1a).
In synthesizing the metal clusters, continuous reduction of oxi-
dized metal salts by organic ligands and a reducing reagent (such
as NaBH4) is frequently used [11,19]. A multitude of NCs, including
monometallic
NCs
(such
as
[Au6(C)(PPh3)6]2+
[36]
and
Ag14(SC6H3
3,4-F2)12(PPh3)8
[37])
and
alloy
NCs
(such
as
[Au7Ag8(C„CtBu)12]+ [38] and [Au3Ag38(SCH2Ph)24X5]2 [39] have
been successfully prepared using this method. In alloy systems,
this method is sometimes called the co-reduction method [38],
since both metal salts are simultaneously reduced.
The ligand exchange approach generally requires a well-
characterized NC to serve as a template (Fig. 1b), from which
ligand etching can result in another monodispersed MNC. The
ligand exchange method has been extensively explored in recent
years [40], and could possibly occur via different modes. The
peripheral
organic
ligands
could
be
partially or
completely
replaced by the newly added ligands in the reaction system
[11,40], while the metal framework could also be possibly
destroyed under certain chemical environments [41]. The ligand
exchange method has been successfully used to prepare the
luminescent NCs such as [Pt1Ag28(SAdm)18(PPh3)4]2+ (via etching
Pt1Ag24(SPhMe2)18 with HSAdm and PPh3) [41].
Recently, metal exchange methods have been widely explored
and used to prepare luminescent alloy NCs [30,40]. Similar to the
ligand exchange reaction, the metal exchange reaction relies on
the reaction of a structurally well-characterized (homometallic or
alloy) NC template with the metal salt of a foreign metal atom.
Depending on the metallic interactions [42,43], the foreign atom
can be incorporated in either the metal core or the exterior struc-
tural motifs (Fig. 1c). Both Galvanic reduction (GR) methods [44–
49] and anti-Galvanic reduction (AGR) method [31,50–52] have
been recently explored. In the GR synthesis, a metal nanoparticle
with relatively more reactive metal atoms is etched/replaced by
the less reactive metal atoms. The AGR is essentially the opposite
of the GR method, because a metal nanoparticle with relatively less
reactive metal atoms is etched/replaced by more reactive metal
atoms. The GR method has been successfully used to prepare lumi-
nescent NCs such as Au1Ag24(SC6H3
2,4-Me2)18
 [48]. Meanwhile, the
AGR method has been used to prepare Au25Ag2(SC2H4Ph)18 with
two Ag atoms depositing on the exterior coordination shell upon
treating the as-prepared Au25(SC2H4Ph)18 with the freshly pre-
pared AgNO3 [50]. Similarly, the series of strongly luminescent
Au6Ag2 [51], Au6Cu2 [52], Au6Ag4 [31], and Au6Ag6 [31] NCs were
also prepared by reaction of the Au6 molecular cluster with the tar-
get silver or copper salt with the AGR method. In addition to the GR
and AGR methods, metal replacement without perturbation of the
original template framework has also been reported. This method
is also known as metal doping in some other papers. For example,
our group recently doped a single Pt atom into the bimetallic

2,4-Me2)18

3. Precise structure and optical properties
3.1. Molecular clusters
3.1.1. M6–8 (M = Au, Ag, Cu) nanoclusters and their alloy derivatives
The Au6 NC [Au6(C)(PPh3)6]2+ (Au6-I) was ﬁrst reported by Sch-
midbaur et al. in 1988 [36]. In their study, Au6-I was synthesized
by the reaction of C[B(OMe)2]4 with AuPPh3Cl in the presence of
excessive CsF. Alternatively, the reaction of Me3SiCHN2 with
[OAu3(PPh3)3]+BF4
 also generated the same Au6-I NC [54]. The
anionic counterions in these two systems are [CH3OBF3] and
BF4
, respectively. SCXRD analysis established that the six gold
atoms are arranged in a slightly distorted octahedral structure
(Fig. 2), and the crystallographic inversion center is occupied by
a carbon atom. In addition, one PPh3 ligand is coordinated to each
of the gold atoms, so that each of the Au atoms bonds in a linear
fashion with P–Au–C(center) bond angle close to 180. According
to a recent study by Jia and Wang, the solid state of Au6-I emitted
green light upon irradiation with UV light. Its kem appeared at
about 540 nm [51], and the emission was quenched in CH2Cl2
solvent.
Using another phosphine ligand dppy, Wang et al. recently syn-
thesized another Au6 NC (i.e., Au6-II) [51]. The synthesis is similar

to that reported by Schmidbaur et al., using the reaction of
[OAu3(dppy)3]+ with Me3SiCHN2 in CH2Cl2 solution. The atomic
structure of Au6-II was determined to be [Au6(C)(dppy)6](BF4)2
by SCXRD. The overall framework and Au–C and Au–Au bond dis-
tances are quite similar to that of Au6-I. Of note, Au6-II is brightly
emissive in the solid state at room temperature (kex = 380 nm,
kem = 495 nm). But like Au6-I, the luminescence of Au6-II is almost
negligible in CH2Cl2 (kex = 370 nm, kem = 430 nm).
By treating Au6-II with two equiv. of AgBF4, Wang et al., pre-
pared the double Ag capped [Au6Ag2(C)(dppy)6]4+ (i.e., Au6Ag2-I)
[51]. SCXRD indicated that the two Ag atoms each capped one of
the two opposite Au3 triangles (Fig. 2). Meanwhile, the six periph-
eral phosphine ligands each acts as a bidentate ligand, with the P
atom binding with one Au atom, and the N atom (on pyridine)
ligating with the Ag atom. Such structural rigidity restricts the
nonradiative relaxation pathways from the excited state, and thus
improves the resulting emission. Indeed, Au6Ag2-I showed bright
emission in the solid state (Fig. 2, kex = 400 nm, kem = 540 nm).
Meanwhile, in striking contrast to the non-emissive phenomenon
of the Au6-I and Au6-II NCs in CH2Cl2 solution, Au6Ag2-I exhibited
strong bright solution luminescence in CH2Cl2 (kex = 300 nm, kem =
625 nm). The QY of Au6Ag2-I was 29% using rhodamine B in abso-
lute ethanol (69% [55]) as the standard. The single lifetime of the
emission was 5.7 ls at room temperature. Speciﬁcally, the CH2Cl2
solution of Au6Ag2-I was still brightly emissive after being kept
in dark for months. In view of the microsecond scale lifetime and
the large Stokes shift, Wang et al., suggested the emission to be
phosphorescence. Herein, the improved structural rigidity induced
by Ag atoms and the Ag–N interactions, and the steric protection
imparted by the peripheral phosphines, greatly slowed the process
of quenching in solvent.
To gain insights into the origin of the emissions and the excited
state dynamics of Au6Ag2-I in solution phase, Wang and Xia et al.,
recently carried out a study which combined femtosecond time-
resolved transient absorption experiments and quantum chemical

also some atomically precise, weakly emissive NCs without
detailed data. For example, Au20 [102], Au28 [103] and Au38 [81]
NCs protected by the hydrophobic phenylethanethiolate were
found to show weak emission at 700–820 nm [82]. Nevertheless,
due to the lack of detailed descriptions, these crystals are excluded
from this review article.

characterize. In this context, DFT calculations have been fre-

4. General Luminescence-enhancement strategies
In these years, several different strategies to stimulate and
enhance PL of the ultrasmall group IB metal NCs have been devel-
oped. These strategies can be roughly categorized into four types:
ligand engineering, metal doping, AIE, and CIEE.
4.1. Ligand engineering
Ligand engineering represents one of the most versatile strate-
gies to regulate the emission intensity, and can be executed by
either an electronic or steric approach. In a recent study by Wu
and Jin on a series of spherical Au25(SR)18 NCs [71], the LMCT type
of electron transition was found to be mainly responsible for the
luminescence. Increasing the electron-donating ability of the
peripheral ligands (via engineering electron donating substituents
or electron rich atoms) and decreasing the electron density of the
metal core (with the premise of maintained framework at multiple
charge states) both achieved PL enhancement. Of note, the intro-
duction of the more electron-donating-ligands also induces signif-
icantly enhanced emission in Au2Cu6 [85] and the rod-like Au25
family of NCs [83], because the luminescence of all of them corre-
spond to the LMCT processes. Here, it is noteworthy that for the
MLCT
(instead of
the
LCMT) type
of
emission,
introducing
electronic-withdrawing group or increasing the electron density
of the metal core will be beneﬁcial to PL. In addition to the elec-
tronic effect, the steric effect of the ligands is also found prominent
for emission regulation. For example, the emission intensity of
Au2Cu6-I protected by the bulky –SAdm ligand is signiﬁcantly
stronger than that of Au2Cu6-II protected by the less bulkier –StBu
ligand [84]. Utilization of the bulky –SAdm ligand signiﬁcantly
contributes to RIV/RIR, and such effect was also suggested to
induce the intense luminescence of the Au8Ag8-I protected by
the alkyne ligands bearing the bulky tBu substituents [32].
4.2. Metal doping
In the metal doping methodology, the metal framework of the
templated NC could be either retained (e.g., Au6-II + AgBF4 ?
Au6Ag2-I [51], [Ag25(SR)18] + AuPPh3Cl ? Au1Ag24-I[48]) or rear-
ranged (e.g., Au11 + AgSR ? Au25xAgx, x  13 [67]; Pt1Ag24-I
+AuPPh3Br ? Pt2Au10Ag13-I [53]). To exclude the potential inﬂu-
ence of the metal framework on emission, herein we focus only
on doping processes where the metal framework is substantially
unperturbed by the foreign metal atoms.

In view of the transformations between the series of Au6 and
Au6MX (M = Ag, Cu; x = 2, 4, 6) molecular NCs reported by Wang
et al., incorporation of the foreign metal atoms occurs via an ‘‘ad-
dition” instead of the ‘‘replacement” mode [31,51,52]. Such metal
addition processes are suggested to be induced by the extra-
coordination ability of the peripheral ligands. The formation of a
strong bonding system outside the original Au6 core signiﬁcantly
enhances the structural rigidity of the aromatic groups on the
phosphine ligand, and thus reduces the nonradiative transitions.
Accordingly, the emission intensity shows positive correlations
with the rigidity of the entire structure.
In addition to the extra rigidity induced by the foreign atom(s),
replacement of the metal atom(s) with foreign metal atom(s)
might also signiﬁcantly affect the electronic structure of the pre-
cursor. For instance, incorporation of the 13th Ag atom into the
rod-like Au25xAgx not only enhanced the structural rigidity, but
also signiﬁcantly perturbed the orbital composition of the HOMO
and LUMO [68] and changes the dynamics of the excited state
[107]. Similarly, the incorporation of Ag atoms in the spherical
Au25 NCs was suggested to increase the energy level of the LUMO,
while hardly affect the energy level of the HOMO, resulting in a
changed HOMO–LUMO gap in Au25xAgx alloy NCs [44]. Despite
the exciting achievements, full details and especially an in-depth
understanding of the relationships between the doping number,
site, and metal type and the geometric and electronic structures
are still lacking.
4.3. Aggregation induced emission (AIE)
AIE has recently emerged as a highly promising strategy to
improve the PL of solid state materials and metal complexes, and
the AIE phenomenon has been successfully introduced in the syn-
thesis of highly luminescent NCs in recent years [108–110]. This
strategy has also been used to prepare ultrasmall NCs with atomic
precision [84,85]. Considering that the key concept for AIE is to
activate the RIR movements, we anticipated that successful execu-
tion of the AIE strategy would rely on the following principles.
Firstly, reduction of the relatively inert metal should be over-
whelmingly favored over that of the active metal complex, so that
the inert metal atom could be selectively assembled as the core
atoms; secondly, the bonding afﬁnity of the different metal atoms
should be sufﬁciently large to ensure the formation of alloy
(instead of the homometallic) NCs; and thirdly, the peripheral
organic ligands should be sterically bulky, so as to reduce the pos-
sibility of the non-radiative transitions in the exterior motifs. With
these principles in mind, we recently achieved an AIE system via
controlled aggregation of an active Cu complex with relatively
inert Au atoms [84] . The non-emissive CuISR and AuIPPh3Cl com-
plexes were successfully transformed into the Au2Cu6-I NC with a
strong red-emission.
4.4. Crystallization-induced-emission-enhancement (CIEE)
All the aforementioned strategies (ligand engineering, metal
doping, and AIE) rely on RIR induced by strong bonding interaction.
However, in many small molecules and macromolecules, non-
covalent interactions (such as hydrogen bonding, cation–p and
p–p interactions), which are mainly intermolecular interactions,
also play an important role in regulating emission intensity
[111]. The Au4Ag13-I NC, the metal core of which is protected by
thiolate and phosphine ligands, unambiguously showed the essen-
tial role of non-covalent CAH  p interactions [87]. Beneﬁting from
the high regularity in the crystalline state, the CAH  p interac-
tions could be well-maintained between each NC and its six adja-
cent structures. To this end, the crystalline states comprise
compact intermolecular non-covalent interactions, which signiﬁ-
cantly restrict the rotatory and vibrational motions of the periph-
eral ligands. In contrast, such intermolecular interactions are
signiﬁcantly disturbed by grinding or solvent collisions in the
amorphous and solution phase, and thus the emission is signiﬁ-
cantly weakened therein. The phenomenon of CIEE is expected to
be widely applicable in various NC systems, since the non-
covalent interactions could be easily modulated by varying the
chemical environments. Nevertheless, as the quenching in solution
happens to group IB clusters as a result of the attack of solvent
molecules or oxygen in many other cases, precise control experi-
ments should be carried out to verify the mechanism of CIEE.
5. Biological and chemical applications of the luminescent
nanoclusters
Compared to traditional luminescent materials, such as quan-
tum dots and lanthanide nanoparticles, the group IB metal NCs
have the advantages of ultrasmall size (easy to be metabolized),
high monodispersity (isotropic physicochemical properties), pre-
cise atomic structure (easy for target-directed chemical modiﬁca-
tion) and low cytotoxicity. Compared to organic dyes, MNCs
show easily-tuneable adsorption–emission characteristics, high
chemical and photostability, and high sensitivity. Therefore, the
luminescent group IB metal NCs have emerged as highly promising
materials for applications in chemical and biological sensing and
imaging. Despite a short history, practical applications of the atom-
ically precise group IB MNCs have been reported.
Recently, Tennikova and Tunik et al., prepared two Au6Cu2

6. Perspective
PL is one of the most attractive physicochemical properties of
the ultrasmall metal NCs, and luminescent NCs with different com-
positions (in both the number/type of the metal atoms and the pro-
tecting ligands) have been explored in the past decade-an
undertaking that has greatly beneﬁted from the continuous explo-
ration of synthetic methods. The precise characterization of com-
ponents and especially the atomic packing mode shed light on
the essential structure–optical property relationships on the
nano-scale, and also motivates the rationale synthesis of novel
NCs with remarkably enhanced emission intensities. Despite the
encouraging achievements, the scope, luminescence properties,
structure–luminescence relationships, and practical applications
of these atomically precise NCs all need to be improved.
With respect to the structure of the luminescent NCs, ultrasmall
molecular and metal clusters have been frequently proposed to
bridge the gap between discrete atoms and plasmonic nanoparti-
cles. Indeed, compact metal–metal bondings are ubiquitous in
nanoparticles, while the metal–organic interactions are fundamen-
tal building blocks in the metal complexes and the multinuclear
clusters (commonly with bridging atoms/functional groups). How-
ever, compared to the vast numbers of metal complexes/multinu-
clear clusters and nanoparticles, only a few metal NCs with speciﬁc
number of metal atoms (such as M25, M29, M41) have been success-
fully prepared as the luminescent materials. Meanwhile, ligand
types are frequently limited to the monodentate thiolate and phos-
phine ligands, while the multidentate ligands which were found to
be beneﬁcial for the luminescence due to the structural rigidity,
have been rarely utilized in preparing the luminescent clusters.
To this end, both the size of the clusters (i.e., with different number
of metal atoms), and the types of the protecting ligands need to be
extended.
So far, the luminescence of most current NCs is still unsatisfac-
tory. The QYs of most reported atomically precise metal NCs are
<10% (Fig. 27), with emission lifetimes generally in the nanosecond
range. In comparison, ultrasmall NCs protected by hydrophilic
ligands (such as peptide, nucleic acid) frequently show much bet-
ter luminescent properties (with much higher QYs and longer life-
time). However, the atomically precise synthesis of hydrophilic
NCs with high purity and low size-dispersity is highly challenging.
Future studies of luminescent metal NCs are expected to focus on
the synthesis of hydrophobic NCs with better emission properties,
atomically precise hydrophilic NCs, and even the NCs with amphi-
philic characteristics. In addition, the chemical modiﬁcation of the
surface ligands with speciﬁc functional groups, and the practical
applications of the reported atomically precise NCs in other ﬁelds
(e.g., chemical sensing and drug delivery) are highly attractive
from both academic and practical viewpoints.
Due to the lack of appropriate samples with comparable struc-
tures, our current understanding of the structure–luminescence
relationships of the MNCs remains quite limited. For example,
MLCT and MLCT have been frequently proposed as the origin of
the ﬂuorescence, enabling the rational improvement of the emis-
sion intensity via tuning the electronic effect of the ligands. How-
ever,
without
careful
experimental
design
or
theoretical
calculations, it remains quite difﬁcult to deduce whether the lumi-
nescence of a novel cluster occurs via MLCT, LMCT, or some other
type of mechanisms. Therefore, in addition to a mechanistic under-
standing of speciﬁc nanoclusters, general structure-luminescence
relationships that can assist in elucidating the routine correlation
of PL with some speciﬁc structural motif(s) might be more applica-
ble. Meanwhile, correlations between the nanoclusters and the

reductants, ligands, and sometimes high pressures and Schlenk

applications of luminescent metal NCs with atomic precision are
still quite limited. As most the reported metal nanoclusters with
atomic precision are hydrophobic, their applications as sensors or
photocatalysts in nonaqueous environments are still to be devel-

NCs rely not only the enrichment of target materials, but also the
improvement on the synthetic methods.

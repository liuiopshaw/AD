ABSTRACT: The threat of bacterial infections, especially drug-resistant
strains, to human health necessitates the development of high-efficient,
broad-spectrum and nonantibiotic nanodisinfectant. However, the effect of
interfacial charge on the antibacterial properties of nanodisinfectant
remains a mystery, which greatly limits the development of highly
antibacterial active nanodisinfectant. Herein, we developed three types of
ultrasmall (d < 3 nm) gold-nanoparticles (AuNPs) modified with 5-
carboxylic(C)/methoxy(M)amino(A)/-2-mercaptobenzimidazole (C/M/
A MB) to investigate their interfacial charge on antibacterial performance.
Our results showed that both the electropositive AMB-AuNPs and
electronegative CMB-AuNPs exhibited no antibacterial activity against
both Gram-positive (G+) and Gram-negative (G−) bacteria. However, the electroneutral MMB-AuNPs exhibited unique
antibacterial performance against both G+ and G−bacteria, even against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA).
Mechanistic investigation revealed a multipathway synergistic bacteriostatic mechanism involving MMB-AuNPs inducing damage to
bacterial cell membranes, disruption of membrane potential and downregulation of ATP levels, ultimately leading to bacterial
demise. Furthermore, two additional electroneutral AuNPs modified with 5-methyl-2-mercaptobenzimidazole (mMB-AuNPs) and 5-
ethoxy-2-mercaptobenzimidazole (EMB-AuNPs) also demonstrated commendable antibacterial efficacy against E. coli, S. aureus, and
MRSA; however, their performance was comparatively inferior to that of MMB-AuNPs. This work provides valuable insights for the
development of high-performance antibacterial nanomaterials.

gold-nanoparticles, bacteriostatic mechanism
1. INTRODUCTION
Bacterial infections, one of the top ten leading causes of death
worldwide, pose an increasingly serious threat to human
health.1,2 The overuse of antibiotics has promoted the
emergence of drug-resistant bacteria, including superbugs,
making it more difficult to treat infections and resulting in
more deaths.3,4 Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop
highly efficient nonantibiotic antibacterial materials.
Among the various emerging antibacterial materials, nano-
materials are undoubtedly a hot topic.5 Many nanomaterials,
such as gold nanoparticles (AuNPs),6 silver nanoparticles,7 and
titanium dioxide nanoparticles8 have been used to prepare
nanoantibacterial agents. AuNPs in particular have attracted
significant attention due to their low toxicity, high stability, and
ease of modification.9−11 In the past decade, both antibiotics
and nonantibiotic drugs have been successfully modified onto
the surface of AuNPs to create excellent nanoantibacterial
materials.12−15 For example, Lai et al. successfully achieved
surface modification of AuNPs with vancomycin which can
effectively inhibit the growth of vancomycin-resistant bac-
teria.16 However, it is important to note that antibiotic-
modified AuNPs may lead to cross-resistance during clinical
treatment due to the same targeting mechanisms as original
antibiotics. Therefore, using nonantibiotic drugs or their
intermediates as modifying ligands might be a safer alternative.
On the other hand, various organic ligands with different
charged group have been successfully modified onto the
surface of AuNP, thereby creating excellent nano antibacterial
regent against different bacteria individually.17−19 However,
the effect of interfacial charge on the antibacterial properties of
gold nanoparticles remains a mystery, which greatly limits the
development of highly antibacterial active gold nanoparticles.
Due to unique biochemical activity of benzimidazole group,
the mercaptobenzimidazole with different charge groups
including electronegative 5-carboxy-2-mercaptobenzimidazole
(CMB), electroneutral 5-methoxy-2-mercaptobenzimidazole
(MMB) and electropositive 5-amino-2-mercaptobenzimidazole

Mechanistic studies on the bactericidal action of MMB-AuNPs

another two electroneutral AuNPs modified with 5-methyl-2-
mercaptobenzimidazole (mMB-AuNPs) and 5-ethoxy-2-mer-

however, their performance was comparatively lower than that
of MMB-AuNPs. These findings provide valuable insights for
designing high-performance nonantibiotic nano antibacterial
agents.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Materials. HAuCl4·3H2O (99.99%), NaOH, NaBH4, ethanol,
methanol and acetic acid were from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent
company. 5-Methoxy-2-mercaptobenzimidazole (MMB), 5-amino-2-
mercaptobenzimidazole (AMB), 5-carboxy-2-mercaptobenzimidazole
(CMB), 5-methyl-2-mercaptobenzimidazole (mMB), 5-ethoxy-2-
mercaptobenzimidazole (EMB) and all antibiotics were from Sigma-
Aldrich. Electron microscopy fixative was from Servicebio. Membrane
potential dye DiSC3(5) and ATP Assay Kit were from BestiBio.
2.2. Bacterial Strains. Standard strains of E. coli (ATCC25922),
S. aureus (ATCC6538), P. aeruginosa (CMCC10104), P. fluorescens
(ATCC13525), Bacillus subtilis (ATCC6633), Bacillus cereus
(CMCC(B)63303) were from Shanghai Bioresources Collection
Center (SHBCC). Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)
was from the Microbiology room of Clinical Laboratory, General
Hospital of Central Theater Command. E. coli, S. aureus, P. aeruginosa,
Bacillus subtilis and MRSA were cultured in LB medium at 37 °C on a
shaking incubator set to 180 rpm, while P. fluorescens and Bacillus
cereus were cultured in nutrient broth medium under the same
conditions.
2.3. Synthesis of CMB-Modified Gold Nanoclusters (CMB-
AuNPs). A mixed aqueous solution of HAuCl4·3H2O (50 mM, 2 mL,
dissolved in water), CMB (0.1 mmoL) and alkaline water (7 mL) was
prepared in a 50 mL round-bottom flask, followed by stirring at 600
rpm for 1 h. Subsequently, 2 mL of sodium borohydride (NaBH4) (6
mg in water) was added to the reaction mixture and continued stirring
at 1200 rpm for another 2 h. Then, the resulting sample was
transferred to a centrifuge tube and subjected to methanol rinsing
under centrifugation conditions (8000 rpm, 10 min) to eliminate any
unreacted materials. This rinsing-ultrafiltration centrifugation process
was repeated thrice. Finally, the purified CMB-AuNPs were
lyophilized using a freeze-drying machine to obtain powdered samples
suitable for subsequent experiments.
2.4. Synthesis of MMB-AuNPs, mMB-AuNPs and EMB-
AuNPs. A mixed solution of HAuCl4·3H2O (50 mM, 1.24 mL,
dissolved in methanol), MMB (100 mg), methanol (13.86 mL) and
acetic acid (3.42 mL) was prepared in a 50 mL round-bottom flask,
followed by stirring at 600 rpm for 1 h. Subsequently, sodium
borohydride (NaBH4) (142 mg) in ethanol (4 mL) was added into
the reaction mixture and stirred at 1200 rpm for an additional 2 h.
The organic solvent was removed using rotary evaporation, and the
sample was then rinsed with alkaline water under centrifugation
conditions (8000 rpm,10 min) to remove the unreacted materials.
This rinsing-ultrafiltration centrifugation process was repeated three
times. After purification, the MMB-AuNPs was lyophilized using a
freeze-drying machine to obtain powdered samples for subsequent
experiments. The synthesis method used for mMB-AuNPs and EMB-
AuNPs followed a similar procedure as that employed for MMB-
AuNPs.
2.5. Synthesis of AMB-Modified Gold Nanoclusters (AMB-
AuNPs). A mixed aqueous solution of HAuCl4·3H2O (50 mM, 1.24
mL, dissolved in methanol), AMB (0.1 mmoL), methanol (8 mL),
acetic acid (1.71 mL) was prepared in a 50 mL round-bottom flask,
followed by stirring at 600 rpm for 1 h. Subsequently, sodium
borohydride (NaBH4) (6 mg) in ethanol (2 mL) was added into the
reaction mixture and stirred at 1200 rpm for additional 2 h. The
sample was transferred into an ultrafiltration centrifuge tube (15 mL,
3 kDa) and washed with water under centrifugation (5000 rpm, 10
min) to remove the small unreacted materials. This washing-
ultrafiltration centrifugation process was repeated three times. After
purification, the AMB-AuNPs was lyophilized using a freeze-drying
machine to obtain powdered samples for subsequent experiments.
2.6. Characterization of Materials. The FT-IR spectra of
AuNPs and the corresponding ligands were acquired using a Bruker,
VERTEX80 V FT-IR spectrometer. The UV−vis spectra of AuNPs
were acquired using a Shimadzu UV-2600 UV spectrophotometer.
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive
spectroscopy mapping images of AuNPs were captured using a JEOL
JEM F200 TEM instrument. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS) measurements of AuNPs were performed on an ESCALAB
250Xi (Thermo Fisher, USA) XPS spectrometer utilizing mono-
chromatized Al Kα radiation. The Zeta Potential of AuNPs was
recorded using Malvern Nano-ZS ZEN3600 Zeta Sizer. The
thermogravimetric (TG) analysis was carried out employing a
Netzsch TG 209 F3 (Germany). Scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) imaging of bacteria was performed using a JEOL JSM-IT800
scanning electron microscope. Transmission electron microscopy
(TEM) imaging of bacteria was conducted utilizing a HITACHI
HT7800 transmission electron microscope. The absorbance or
fluorescence of the samples was recorded using the SYNERGY H1
microplate reader (BioTek USA). Fluorescence images were captured
utilizing a fluorescence microscope (SOPTOP RX50).23
2.7. Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC). The loga-
rithmic-phase bacteria (OD600 ≈1) were diluted 10,000-fold with LB
medium. Subsequently, 100 μL of LB medium was added to each well
of a 96-well plate followed by the addition of 100 μL AuNPs solution
in the first well of each row. AuNPs were logarithmically diluted in
every subsequent row. Then, 100 μL of bacterial suspension was
added into each well containing different concentrations of AuNPs
solutions. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h and the
minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined as the
lowest concentration at which no bacterial turbidity was observed in
all three replicates after incubation. For determination of minimum
bactericidal concentration (MBC), incubation time was extended to
over 24 h.24
2.8. Membrane Potentials of bacteria. The bacteria were
incubated with 8 μM DiSC3(5) for a duration of 30 min, followed by
ion-equilibration using 100 mM KCl. Subsequently, the cells were
treated with MMB-AuNPs for a period of 12 h and the fluorescence
signal was monitored using excitation at 622 nm and emission at 670
nm employing a SYNERGY H1 microplate reader.25
2.9. Membrane Permeability Assay of bacteria. The bacteria
were treated with MMB-AuNPs for 2 h at 37 °C, followed by PBS
washing. Subsequently, the bacteria were stained with PI (200 μM)
for an additional 30 min. Finally, fluorescent images were recorded
using a biological/fluorescence microscope (SOPTOP RX50).26
2.10. Intracellular ATP Levels of bacteria. The bacteria cells
were treated with MMB-AuNPs for 2 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, the
collected bacteria were lysed using a cracking buffer and centrifuged at
12000 rpm for 5 min to obtain the supernatant. The intracellular ATP
levels in the supernatant were determined using an ATP assay kit. The
ATP-detecting solution was added to the supernatant, followed by

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Synthesis and Characterization. The three types of
ultrasmall AuNPs with different charges (electronegative
CMB-AuNPs, electroneutral MMB-AuNPs and electropositive
AMB-AuNPs) were synthesized by using a one-pot method
(Figure 1a). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
images revealed that all three types of AuNPs exhibited
uniform spherical morphology, with an average diameter of
approximately 2.0 ± 0.6 nm (Figure 1b), thereby excluding the
influence of particle size on antibacterial performance. The X-
ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra confirmed the
elemental composition and their valence states of the three
types of AuNPs (Figure 1c). As shown in Figure S1, an analysis
of the XPS spectra indicated that all three AuNPs contained
four elements: C, N, S, and Au, and the MMB-AuNPs and
CMB-AuNPs also included O elements, corresponding to the
ligand surrounding the AuNPs. Furthermore, UV−vis
absorption spectra shown in Figure S2 demonstrated a
significant enhancement in absorption for all three types of
AuNPs within the range of 400−600 nm while retaining
characteristic absorption peaks from their original ligands,
providing evidence for MMB, CMB and AMB functioning as
ligands surrounding the AuNPs. Additionally, Fourier trans-
form infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was employed to
determine how ligands were linked to the gold core. Figure
1d compares to the FT-IR spectra of three types of
mercaptobenzimidazoles, the disappeared of stretching vibra-
tion peak of sulfhydryl group (-SH) around 2570 cm−1 in the
corresponding AuNPs, indicating that all three types of ligands
were modified onto the AuNPs via Au−S bonds. The zeta
potentials (Figure S4) of CMB-AuNPs, MMB-AuNPs and
AMB-AuNPs at the same pH (PH ≈5) were −26.9, 0, and
17.1 mV. Overall, these characterizations validate the
successful synthesis of three types of AuNPs with similar size
but varying interfacial charges.

To investigate the antibacterial activity of MMB-AuNPs
against drug-resistant strains, methicillin-resistant Staphylococ-
cus aureus (MRSA), a typical drug-resistant strain, was
employed as a model for assessment. MRSA is a prevalent
and highly virulent bacterium in clinical practice, exhibiting
robust resistance to multiple antibiotics.28−30 Aztreonam and
Erythromycin, two commonly used antibiotics, were utilized as
controls. The inhibitory effect of MMB-AuNPs on MRSA was
compared with that of Aztreonam and Erythromycin. As
shown in Table S2, both Aztreonam and Erythromycin
exhibited an MIC of 32 μg mL−1 against MRSA, confirming
the presence of drug-resistant ability in MRSA strains. Notably,
the MIC of MMB-AuNPs (4 μg mL−1) was significantly lower
than that of Aztreonam and Erythromycin, suggesting potent
antibacterial activity of MMB-AuNPs against MRSA. More-
over, the antibacterial effects of MMB-AuNPs, Aztreonam and
Erythromycin were further compared by quantifying colony
forming units (CFUs) on agar plates and calculating
corresponding antibacterial ratios using turbidity colorimetry
methods. As shown in Figure 2a−h, both CMB-AuNPs and
AMB-AuNPs exhibited no significant differences in CFU

128 μg mL−1. Similarly, both Aztreonam and Erythromycin
exhibited no significant differences in CFU counts compared
to the blank control at a concentration of 8 μg mL−1, indicating
negligible antibacterial efficacy against MRSA at this
concentration. In contrast, the CFUs of MRSA treated with
MMB-AuNPs decreased in a dosage-dependent manner, with
no CFU observed when treating MRSA with 8 μg mL−1 MMB-
AuNPs.

3.3. Antibacterial Mechanism. The antibiotic mechanism
of MMB-AuNPs was investigated using E. coli, S. aureus and
MRSA as model bacteria. First, the propidium iodide (PI) dye
was employed to monitor the permeability of the bacterial
membrane in E. coli, S. aureus and MRSA treated with MMB-
AuNPs, as it exhibits enhanced fluorescence upon binding to
DNA or RNA. It is worth noting that PI fluorescence is only
enhanced when the cell membrane is damaged, as it cannot
penetrate an intact membrane. As shown in Figure S6, the
fluorescence intensity of E. coli, S. aureus and MRSA treated
with MMB-AuNPs was significantly increased compared to the
control group, indicating that MMB-AuNPs caused damage to
the bacterial membrane.
Then, the morphological changes in bacteria treated with or
without MMB-AuNPs were examined through scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) and TEM. The SEM images
(Figure 3a) revealed severe wrinkling and significant collapse,
even direct rupture, of the surface of E. coli, S. aureus and
MRSA treated by MMB-AuNPs, accompanied by a substantial
release of cellular contents. The TEM images (Figure 3a)
further demonstrated damage to the bacterial membrane and
loss of internal components of E. coli, S. aureus and MRSA
treated by MMB-AuNPs. To further investigate the distribu-
tion of MMB-AuNPs, the gold content on both the cell
membrane and within cells were quantified using TEM energy-
dispersive spectroscopy. As shown in Figure 3b, the extensive
localization of Au on both the E. coli and S. aureus cell envelope
and within its intracellular compartments suggested that
MMB-AuNPs can across the bacterial wall and membrane
into the bacterial interior, which may disrupt the exchange of
matter and energy between bacteria and their external
environment, ultimately leading to bacterial death.
Subsequently, the membrane potential and intracellular ATP
levels of E. coli, S. aureus and MRSA treated with MMB-AuNPs
were quantified to further investigate the underlying
biochemical mechanism by which MMB-AuNPs induce
bacterial death. The cyanine dye DiSC3(5), which exhibits
enhanced fluorescence in response to increase of membrane
potential, was utilized for monitoring the membrane potential

proposed, wherein MMB-AuNPs induce structural damage to
bacterial cell membranes, disrupt membrane potential, and
downregulate ATP levels, ultimately culminating in bacterial
demise (Figure 4e).
To further validate the proposed hypothesis, we prepared
two additional types of electroneutral AuNPs modified with 5-
methyl-2-mercaptobenzimidazole (mMB-AuNPs) and 5-
ethoxy-2-mercaptobenzimidazole (EMB-AuNPs), aiming to
validate the unique antibacterial performance of AuNPs
modified with electroneutral mercaptobenzimidazole ligands.
As shown in Figure 5a-c and Figure S7, both mMB-AuNPs and
EMB-AuNPs demonstrated similar sizes to MMB-AuNPs,
while the mMB and EMB ligands were also surface-modified
onto AuNPs through Au−S bonds, resembling the modifica-
tion observed in MMB-AuNPs. Importantly, both mMB-
AuNPs and EMB-AuNPs demonstrated dose-dependent
antibacterial activity, with an MIC value of 64 μg mL−1
against E. coli, S. aureus, and MRSA (Figure 5d-f and Figures
S13−15), which was higher than that of MMB-AuNPs. This
result suggested that the antibacterial performance of MMB-
AuNPs was better than that of both MB-AuNPs and EMB-
AuNPs.
4. CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, we have successfully synthesized three AuNPs
modified with mercaptobenzimidazole ligands with different
charges and investigated their interfacial charge on anti-
bacterial performance. Both the electropositive AMB-AuNPs
and electronegative CMB-AuNPs showed no antibacterial
activity against both G+ and G−bacteria. While the
electroneutral MMB-AuNPs exhibit the unique antibacterial
performance against both G+ and G−bacteria, even against
methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Mecha-
nistic investigation revealed a multipathway synergistic
bacteriostatic mechanism involving MMB-AuNPs inducing
damage to bacterial cell membranes, disruption of membrane
potential and downregulation of ATP levels, ultimately leading
to bacterial demise. Additionally, two other electroneutral
mMB-AuNPs and EMB-AuNPs also demonstrated commend-
able antibacterial efficacy against E. coli, S. aureus, and MRSA;
however, their performance was comparatively inferior to that
of MMB-AuNPs. This work provides valuable insights into the
development of high-performance antibacterial nanomaterials.

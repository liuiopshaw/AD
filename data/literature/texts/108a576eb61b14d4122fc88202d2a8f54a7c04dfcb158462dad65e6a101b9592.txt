A B S T R A C T   
The expanding production and use of nanomaterials in various fields caused big concern for human health. 
Oxidative stress is the most frequently described mechanism of nanomaterial toxicity. A state of oxidative stress 
can be defined as the imbalance of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant enzyme activities. 
Although nanomaterials-triggered ROS generation has been extensively investigated, little is known regarding 
the regulation of antioxidant enzyme activities by nanomaterials. This study used two typical nanomaterials, 
SiO2 nanoparticles (NPs) and TiO2 NPs, to predict their binding affinities and interactions with antioxidant 
enzymes catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD). The molecular docking results showed that CAT and 
SOD had different binding sites, binding affinity, and interaction modes with SiO2 NPs and TiO2 NPs. The binding 
affinities of the two NPs to CAT were more potent than those to SOD. Consistently, the experimental work 
indicated NPs adsorption caused the perturbation of the second and tertiary structures of both enzymes and thus 
resulted in the loss of enzyme activities.   
1. Introduction 
The rapid growth of nanotechnology has been achieved in the past 
two decades, which led to plenty of nanomaterial applications in in­
dustry, agriculture, food, biomedicine, etc (Hochella et al., 2019; Nel 
et al., 2006). Not surprisingly, it was observed that the intentional and 
unintentional release of nanomaterials into the ecosystem, thus, may 
pose risks to the environment, lives, and human beings. However, the 
knowledge of the health effects of nanomaterials exposure is still 
insufficient. Consequently, the effort to determine health risks associ­
ated with nanomaterials exposure needs to be strengthened. 
Current scientific findings summarized the routes of nanomaterial 
exposure, including inhalation, oral, or dermal. After nanomaterials 
enter the body, they may pass through biological barriers, migrate be­
tween different organs, and penetrate cell membranes due to their small 
size (Yang et al., 2021). Once nanomaterials interact with cells, they 
may disrupt cellular structures, damage cellular biomacromolecules (e. 
g., protein, DNA), compromise organelle function, and ultimately de­
creases cell survival and proliferation (Lewinski et al., 2008). Mecha­
nistic studies usually linked nanomaterials-induced cytotoxicity with the 
overwhelming reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation due to nano­
materials’ high surface free energy (Nelissen et al., 2020). ROS damage 
is a consequence of redox imbalance, which can be divided into two 
aspects, increased ROS and decreased antioxidant defense. Previous 
studies illustrated that nanomaterials exposure dysregulates the activ­
ities of two important antioxidant enzymes, catalase (CAT) and super­
oxide dismutase (SOD) (Ahamed et al., 2010; Ali et al., 2014; Huang 
et al., 2018; Jiang et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2020; Valerio-Garcia et al., 
2017). Nevertheless, the alteration of enzyme activities may be related 
to their molecular structure, and gene regulation (Liu et al., 2020), 
whether the direct interaction of nanomaterials with CAT and SOD will 
affect the conformation and activity of the enzymes and whether it can 
disrupt the intracellular redox status needs further investigation.

SiO2 NPs and TiO2 NPs is closely related to the oxidative stress induced

2019). 5 nm TiO2 NPs caused a decrease in SOD and CAT enzyme ac­
tivities in the liver (Domingo M G et al., 2022). In the current study, we 
used two widely used nanomaterials, namely SiO2 NPs and TiO2 NPs, as 
model compounds, combined with molecular docking and experimental 
approaches to investigate the mechanism of NPs interaction on the in­
hibition of CAT and SOD activities. Molecular docking results showed 
that CAT and SOD enzymes have different binding sites, binding affinity, 
and interaction modes with SiO2 NPs and TiO2 NPs. The binding affinity 
and interaction force were also size-dependent. The larger the size of the 
nano-ligand, the stronger the binding affinity and interaction force with 
CAT and SOD. Hydrophobicity played a pivotal role in the interaction 
between the two NPs and CAT. Electrostatic interaction was the main 
mode of interaction between the two NPs and SOD enzyme. Meanwhile, 
the experimental results also confirmed that CAT and SOD were differ­
ently adsorbed on the surface of SiO2 NPs and TiO2 NPs. Conformation 
changes and enzymatic activity modulation of these two enzymes were 
found upon SiO2 NPs and TiO2 NPs incubation. These findings provided 
a basis for understanding how the structure and function of antioxidant 
enzymes are affected by NPs in a biological context and offered a new 
explanation of NPs-caused toxicity. 
2. Experimental section 
2.1. Molecular docking 
(SiO2)72 and (TiO2)72 clusters were used as models of SiO2 NPs and 
TiO2 NPs, respectively. The spherical clusters with a diameter of 7 nm 
for the two NPs were constructed by repeating their unit cell for the 
simulation study (Mehdizadeh et al., 2019; Mousavi et al., 2019). The 
NPs models were designed and optimized by Material Studio (MS) 
software, the Cartesian coordinate of atoms were consulted from the 
previous studies (Auvinen et al., 2015; Munde et al., 2017), and atomic 
charges were computed using the Gasteiger Hückel charge model (Liu 
et al., 2017). The X-ray crystallographic 3D structure of the CAT enzyme 
(PDB IDs: 3RGP) and SOD enzyme (PDB IDs: 1SPD) were obtained from 
the protein data bank. The prediction of protein conformation was used 
by PROCHECK v.3.5 program (AbdelGawwad et al., 2020). HEX 8.0.0 
software was used to run a molecular docking study (Fardanesh et al., 
2019; Mousavi et al., 2019; Sabziparvar et al., 2018; Shariati et al., 
2018). CHIMERA 1.1.4 and CHIMERA X software were used to calculate 
and display attainable docking modes and binding sites of antioxidant 
enzymes and NP clusters (Mousavi et al., 2019; Sabziparvar et al., 2018). 
2.2. Chemical and reagents 
SiO2 NPs (size of 5–15 nm and size of 100 nm) were purchased from 
Sigma-Aldrich Inc. (St. Louis, MO, USA). TiO2 NPs (size of 5–10 nm) 
were purchased from Aladdin Industrial Inc. (Shanghai, China). Catalase 
(CAT, from the bovine liver) was bought from Solarbio Science & 
Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Superoxide dismutase (SOD, from 
porcine blood) was supplied by Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd 
(Shanghai, China). The BCA protein assay kit was obtained from Ding­
guo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Monoclonal primary an­
tibodies against CAT and SOD were obtained from Abways 
Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The CAT assay kit was 
purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, 
China). The SOD assay kit was supplied by DOJINDO Co., Ltd. 
(Shanghai, China). Goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP-conjugated secondary 
antibody was supplied by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). 
All other chemicals used were of the highest commercial grade. 
2.3. Preparation of NPs-enzyme complexes 
SiO2 NPs and TiO2 NPs were dispersed with double steaming water, 
CAT enzyme and SOD enzyme were dissolved with PBS buffer solution 
(1.44 g Na2HPO4, 0.24 g KH2PO4, 8.0 g NaCl, 0.2 g KCl), respectively. 
SiO2 NPs and TiO2 NPs dispersions were mixed with CAT and SOD 
enzyme solutions in equal volume, respectively, and incubated at 37 ◦C 
for 1 h, the final concentrations of both NPs and antioxidant enzymes 
were 0.5 mg/mL. Then, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 
min, the supernatant was discarded, and the sedimentation was recon­
stituted in a loading buffer (1 M Tris⋅HCl, 10% SDS, 50% glycerin, 0.5% 
bromophenol blue, 5% β-mercaptoethanol). 
2.4. TEM characterization of NPs-enzyme complexes 
The negative staining method was used to examine the structure and 
morphology of SiO2 NPs, TiO2 NPs, and NPs-enzyme complexes. A drop 
of sample solution (30 μL) was adsorbed to a glow-discharged carbon- 
coated copper grid and stayed for 10 min. Excess solution was removed, 
allowing the grid to air dry, and stained with freshly prepared uranyl 
acetate. The size and shape of SiO2 NPs, TiO2 NPs, and NPs-enzyme 
complexes were observed using transmission electron microscopy 
(TEM) (JEM-1200EX, JEOL Ltd). The average primary diameters of NPs 
and NPs-enzyme complexes were estimated using particle size analysis 
software (Nano Measurer). 
2.5. Zeta potential and hydrodynamic size characterization 
NPs and NPs-enzyme complexes were dispersed in purified water at a 
concentration of 0.5 mg/mL and sonicated for 5 min before use. Zeta 
potential and hydrodynamic sizes of NPs and NPs-enzyme complexes 
were detected with a Zetasizer (NanoZSE, Malvern Instruments, Mal­
vern, UK). 
2.6. Western blotting analysis 
CAT and SOD were dispersed in PBS buffer solution and incubated 
with pristine SiO2 NPs or TiO2 NPs at 37 ◦C for l h, respectively. Un­
bound and loose-binding antioxidant enzymes were separated from NPs- 
enzymes complexes by centrifugation (12,000 g for 10 min) and 
extensive washing. The precipitation represented antioxidant enzymes 
located on the surface of NPs, while the supernatant represented the 
unbound and loose-binding antioxidant enzymes. Collected samples 
were reconstituted in loading buffer (1 M Tris⋅HCl, 5% β-mercaptoe­
thanol, 10% SDS, 50% glycerin, 0.5% bromophenol blue), boiled at 
100 ◦C for 5 min. An equal volume of the sample was loaded on a 12.5% 
sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) 
gradient gel, then separated by electrophoresis at 120 V for 60 min, then 
transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked 
with 5% nonfat dry milk at room temperature for 2 h. Then, the mem­
branes were incubated with a specific primary antibody at 4 ◦C over­
night and an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature 
for 1.5 h. The membranes were visualized with an electro­
chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate system (Beyotime, Shanghai, 
China).

Different concentrations (1, 5, 10, 20, 40 mg/mL) of SiO2 NPs or TiO2

2.10. Enzyme activity assay 
After the incubation of antioxidant enzymes with SiO2 NPs for 1 h, 
then, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min, the superna­
tant was discarded, and the sedimentation was reconstituted in loading 
buffer. The enzyme activities of NPs-enzyme mixtures were measured 
according to the manufacturer’s instructions. CAT activity was deter­
mined using a CAT assay kit according to the ammonium molybdate 
spectrophotometric method. The reaction of CAT decomposes H2O2 can 
be stopped by ammonium molybdate. The remaining H2O2 reacts with 
ammonium molybdate to generate a faint yellow complex. CAT activity 
can be calculated by measuring the absorbance of the reaction mixture 
at 405 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular De­
vices). SOD activity was measured by utilizing highly water-soluble 
tetrazolium salt that produces a formazan dye upon reduction with 
the superoxide anion. The activity of SOD was calculated by measuring 
the absorbance of the reaction mixture at 450 nm using a microplate 
reader. 
2.11. Statistical analysis 
Data were collected from at least three independent experiments and 
presented as the mean ± SD. The statistical significance of differences 
was performed by one-way ANOVA followed by least significance dif­
ference (LSD) multiple comparison tests using SPSS 19.0 software. A 
value of p < 0.05 was considered significant. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Model construction and docking of two antioxidant enzymes with 
nanoclusters 
Here, molecular docking was used to predict the binding affinity and 
binding site between NPs and proteins. (SiO2)72 cluster and (TiO2)72 
cluster were used as molecular docking ligands, CAT and SOD enzymes 
were used as docking receptors (Fig. S1). In order to ensure the reli­
ability of molecular docking, the conformations of the two receptor 
proteins were predicted. The Ramachandran map results showed that 
the proportion of amino acid residues in the most favored and allowed 
regions of CAT and SOD reached 98.9% and 96.6%, while the reference 
value was 90% (Fig. S2), indicating the stable and complete conforma­
tion of selected receptor proteins. The binding sites, the nearest inter­
acting residues (cutoff distance of 4 Å), and binding energies of two 
antioxidant enzymes with (SiO2)72 cluster and (TiO2)72 cluster were 
shown in Fig. 1 and Table S1, respectively. Docking data clearly showed 
that different types of nanoclusters bind to differentiated sites of CAT. 
(SiO2)72 cluster is located near the A chain (blue) of CAT (Fig. 1A), while 
(TiO2)72 cluster is mainly located near the C chain (yellow-green) 
(Fig. 1C). However, the two nanoclusters shared the same binding site to 
SOD (Fig. 1B and D). The binding energies of (SiO2)72 and (TiO2)72 
clusters to CAT were −371.44 and −352.06 E-value, and the binding 
energies of (SiO2)72 and (TiO2)72 clusters to SOD were −313.51 and 
−280.52 E-value, respectively (Table S1). These results suggested that 
CAT and SOD enzymes displayed a higher affinity toward (SiO2)72 
cluster than (TiO2)72 cluster. Compared with SOD enzyme, the affinity 
between CAT enzyme and (SiO2)72 cluster was more substantial. The 
nearest interacting residues for clusters with enzymes were listed in 
Table S1. We further surveyed the amino acid residues involved in the 
formation of the intermolecular hydrogen bond. Specifically, seven 
hydrogen bonds were formed in the complex of (SiO2)72@CAT (Pro-373. 
A, Cys-376. A, Tyr-378. A were involved); however, (SiO2)72@SOD, 
(TiO2)72@CAT and (TiO2)72@SOD only have six, one and two hydrogen 
bonds, respectively (Table S2). 
3.2. Analysis of interaction patterns of antioxidant enzymes with 
nanoclusters 
To further analyze the interactions between nanoclusters and two 
antioxidant enzymes, the contact volume, surface area, surface hydro­
phobicity, and electrostatic potential of the binding sites for the docking 
complexes were calculated (Fig. 2). The contact volume of the binding 
pockets of (SiO2)72@CAT and (TiO2)72@CAT were 4857 Å3 and 3857 
Å3, while (SiO2)72@SOD and (TiO2)72@SOD were 3828 Å3 and 3654 Å3, 
indicating that the more extensive contact range and superior combi­
nation of CAT enzyme to (SiO2)72 and (TiO2)72 (Fig. 2A). Similar trend 
was found in surface areas. Fig. 2B demonstrated the hydrophilic (cyan) 
and hydrophobic (yellow) binding sites of antioxidant enzymes to two 
nanoclusters. It was found that the surface hydrophobicity (yellow) of 
binding sites between two nanoclusters and CAT enzyme were obviously 
distributed, while the surface hydrophilicity (cyan) of binding sites be­
tween two nanoclusters and SOD enzyme was mainly observed. Mean­
while, the main active residues of interaction were presented in Fig. S3, 
as shown in Fig. S3 A and C. The main active residues involved in 
binding were generally hydrophobic (purple), while some hydrophilic 
residues (green). Fig. S3 B and D showed that the main active residues 
involved in binding were hydrophilic charged residues (red and blue) 
and uncharged residues (green). We could predict that the combination 
of these two nanoclusters with CAT enzyme had an obvious hydrophobic 
effect in addition to intermolecular hydrogen bond, while the

3.3. Simulated docking of antioxidant enzymes with spherical NPs 
In order to compare the effect of NPs models with different sizes, 
Spherical clusters with a diameter of 7 nm for the two NPs were con­
structed by repeating its unit cell for the docking study (Fig. 3). Docking 
data in Table S3 showed that the calculated binding energies of spherical 
SiO2 NPs with CAT enzyme and SOD enzyme were −842.60 and 
−465.21 E-value, respectively, revealing a greater binding affinity of 
CAT enzyme for SiO2 NPs. Compared with SOD enzyme, there were 
more nearest interacting residues between SiO2 NPs and CAT enzyme, 
indicating that the binding of SiO2 NPs to CAT enzyme was more 
obvious and stable (Table S3, Fig. 3A and C). Meanwhile, as shown in 
Table S3, Fig. 3B and D, the number of the nearest interacting residues 
between TiO2 NPs and CAT enzyme was also significantly more than that 
between TiO2 NPs and SOD enzyme, and the binding affinity between 
TiO2 NPs and CAT enzyme (−532.20 E-value) was also more substantial 
than that of SOD enzyme (−444.81 E-value). The calculation results in 
Table S3 also showed that the binding energy of spherical SiO2 NPs to 
CAT enzyme was more substantial than that of TiO2 NPs of the same size. 
According to the cutoff distance was 4 Å, the interaction residues and 
hydrogen bonds between the two antioxidant enzymes and SiO2 NPs 
were also more than those of TiO2 NPs. The docking results again 
verified that the binding ability of SiO2 NPs to the two antioxidant en­
zymes was higher than that of TiO2 NPs, which was consistent with the 
results in Fig. 1. 
In addition, two docking data clearly indicated that different types 
and sizes of nanoclusters changed the binding site of antioxidant en­
zymes. It also showed that the large-size spherical NPs were more 
capable of interacting with CAT and SOD enzymes and had stronger 
binding affinity than the small-size nanoclusters. The affinity of spher­
ical SiO2 NPs to CAT enzyme was the highest among all docking com­
plexes, and the number of intermolecular hydrogen bonds (up to 33) and 
the contact of the nearest residues were the most (Table S3 and Fig. S4). 
From the perspective of the interaction residues, it was preliminarily 
predicted that in addition to the apparent hydrophobic interaction be­
tween spherical SiO2 NPs and CAT enzyme, the hydrogen bond and 
electrostatic interaction were also obvious, which also supported vdW 
interactions between hydrophobic residues (Fig. S4A).

adsorbed enzymes versus different c/c ratios of antioxidant enzymes/ 
NPs. As shown, when the concentration of the enzyme was fixed, the 
adsorption capacity (%) increased with the increasing concentration of 
NPs. Both SiO2 NPs (Fig. 4A versus Fig. 4B) and TiO2 NPs (Fig. 4C versus

conclusion in Fig. S5. Moreover, the adsorption capacity of SiO2 NPs was 
higher than that of TiO2 NPs in the case of CAT (Fig. 4A versus Fig. 4C), 
while both NPs adsorbed a similar amount of SOD (less than 20% at the 
highest c/c ratio, i.e., SOD: NPs = 1:40). We next verified whether there 
were enough adsorbates in the NPs-enzyme complex. When we fixed the 
concentration of SiO2 NPs, the adsorption percentage decreased pro­
portionally with the increase of adsorbates, which confirmed the excess 
of adsorbates (Fig. S8). Interestingly, we found that the adsorption ca­
pacity of amino-modified SiO2 NPs (SiO2–NH2 NPs) for CAT was lower 
than that of unmodified SiO2 NPs (Fig. S9), although it seemed that 
positively charged NPs should be more easily adsorb CAT in pH buffer 
above its isoelectric point (pI = 5.4). Previous studies also confirmed 
that the positively charged NPs were not favorable to the adsorption of 
protein molecules (Aramesh et al., 2015; Sakulkhu et al., 2015; Tenzer 
et al., 2013). Next, we investigated the influence of different tempera­
tures (4 ◦C, 25 ◦C and 37 ◦C) on the adsorption capacity of SiO2 NPs for 
CAT. The result showed that the temperature had a negligible effect on 
the adsorption capacity of SiO2 NPs (Fig. S10). Particle size may play a 
role in adsorption, and we thus embarked on an adsorption study of CAT 
on 100 nm SiO2 NPs. As shown in Fig. S11, we also observed a 
concentration-dependent CAT adsorption, and the adsorption capacity 
was comparable to 5–15 nm SiO2 NPs. 
3.5. Conformational changes of the antioxidant enzymes adsorbed to NPs 
The function (or activity) of a protein is highly dependent on its 
structure. Previous studies indicated that changes in the secondary 
structures of proteins could be detected by CD spectroscopy (Deng et al., 
2011; Shang et al., 2009). We studied the impact of SiO2 NPs on the 
conformation of the absorbed protein. First, the CD spectra of CAT and 
SOD in the absence and presence of continuously increasing concen­
trations of SiO2 NPs were tested, and the secondary structure contents 
were calculated according to the previously published methods (Whit­
more and Wallace, 2008). The results are shown in Fig. 5 and Table S6. 
After the addition of NPs, the CD spectra of CAT gradually shifted to 
lower ellipticity values around 192, 208, and 220 nm, which are the 
characteristic CD peaks of α-helix and β-sheet, presenting the π-π* and 
n-π* amide transition of the peptide chain (Wei and Ge, 2013).

Obviously, the α-helix content of the CAT decreased and the β-sheet 
increased with the increasing content of SiO2 NPs. However, the α-helix 
and the β-sheet content of the SOD did not change upon SiO2 NPs 
adsorption. Interestingly, the turn and random coil of CAT and SOD 
were not obviously changed. Overall, these results indicated that the 
binding of SiO2 NPs induced the secondary structural changes of CAT. 
Next, we investigated the binding parameters of CAT that adsorbed on 
SiO2 NPs. Fluorescence quenching measurement was previously used to 
examine the interactions between the quencher and fluorescent sub­
stance, which revealed the binding mechanism of the fluorescent sub­
stance and quencher (Hao et al., 2018; Mousavi et al., 2019; Sun et al., 
2014). As shown in Fig. S12, the fluorescence intensity of CAT was 
decreased with the titration of SiO2 NPs. These fluorescent spectra yield 
a linear plot with a binding constant (Ka) of 3.0605 M-1. The result 
indicated that the binding site (n) was 0.6257, which suggested that 
approximately two CAT molecules bind to one SiO2 NPs. In addition, the 
fluorescence spectrum was also used to evaluate the changes in the 
tertiary structure of CAT caused by SiO2 NPs. CAT has four polypeptide 
chains containing twenty-four Trp and eighty Tyr residues, and the 
maximum fluorescence emission peak relies on exposure to the envi­
ronment of Trp and Tyr residues (Attar et al., 2009). The maximum 
fluorescence emission peak shifted to red wavelength with an increase of 
SiO2 NPs concentrations, indicating the change of tertiary structure of 
CAT. Specifically, this result implied the interaction between SiO2 NPs 
and CAT changed the conformation of CAT enzyme, and the microen­
vironment of its amino acid residues (Jash and Kumar, 2014). 
3.6. The influence of SiO2 NPs adsorption on antioxidant enzyme 
activities 
We speculated that the effect of SiO2 NPs adsorption on CAT activ­
ities was due to the apparent secondary and tertiary structure changes 
(Dogac and Teke, 2013; Pudlarz et al., 2018). As shown in Fig. 6, after 
incubation at 37 ◦C for 1 h, SiO2 NPs dose-dependently inhibited the 
enzymatic activity of CAT with the increasing of SiO2 NPs concentration, 
in comparison with free CAT. Interestingly, we found an excellent linear 
correlation between CAT adsorption and the loss of CAT activity 
(Fig. S13, R2 = 0.9551). Consistently, we also observed similar CAT 
activity loss upon 100 nm SiO2 NPs adsorption (Fig. S14 and Fig. S15). 
However, the activity of SOD was not obviously affected, which was 
consistent with the previous finding of low efficiency of SOD binding. 
4. Conclusion 
The ability of proteins to interact with ligands to form molecular 
complexes is an important way for proteins to perform their biological 
functions and plays an important role in various life activities (Lill, 
2011). The purpose of molecular docking research is to accurately 
identify the position and posture of ligands at protein binding sites and 
predict the affinity between ligands and proteins (Shariati et al., 2018). 
We explored the interactions of SiO2 NPs and TiO2 NPs with CAT and 
SOD enzymes by molecular docking. Different types and sizes of nano­
clusters had different binding sites, binding affinity and interaction 
modes with antioxidant enzymes. It was depicted that intermolecular 
hydrogen bond and hydrophobic interaction were the main contributing 
forces in the spontaneous interaction of NPs with CAT enzyme. The main 
binding force of NPs with SOD enzyme was hydrophilic interaction such

These results showed that the direct interaction among molecules and 
conformational changes were the critical factors for the changes in the 
antioxidant enzyme activity when CAT and SOD enzymes were directly 
exposed to the SiO2 NPs or TiO2 NPs at the molecular level. To the best of 
our knowledge, our study, for the first time attributed the loss of anti­
oxidant enzyme activity to NPs-driven adsorption, which provided a 
new explanation of oxidative stress related to NPs exposure.

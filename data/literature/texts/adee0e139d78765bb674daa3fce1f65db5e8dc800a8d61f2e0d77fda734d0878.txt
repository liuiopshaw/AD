A B S T R A C T   
Hyaluronan is being investigated extensively as a biocompatible and biodegradable material for use in 
biomedical applications. While the derivatization of hyaluronan broadens its potential therapeutic use, the 
pharmacokinetics and metabolization of the derivatives must be thoroughly investigated. The fate of 
intraperitoneally-applied native and lauroyl-modified hyaluronan films with varying degrees of substitution was 
investigated in-vivo employing an exclusive stable isotope-labelling approach and LC-MS analysis. The materials 
were gradually degraded in peritoneal fluid, lymphatically absorbed, preferentially metabolized in the liver and 
eliminated without any observable accumulation in the body. Hyaluronan acylation prolongs its presence in the 
peritoneal cavity depending on the degree of substitution. The safety of acylated hyaluronan derivatives was 
confirmed via a metabolic study that revealed its degradation into non-toxic metabolites, i.e. native hyaluronan 
and free fatty acid. Stable isotope-labelling with LC-MS tracking comprises a high-quality procedure for the 
investigation of the metabolism and biodegradability of hyaluronan-based medical products in-vivo.   
1. Introduction 
Hyaluronan (HA) is used widely in many therapeutic fields due to its 
biocompatibility, 
biodegradability, 
nontoxicity 
and 
non- 
immunogenicity. However, with concern to certain therapeutic appli­
cations, the limitations of HA consist of its high level of hydrophilicity, 
rapid dissolution in body fluids and rapid elimination from the body 
depending on the Mw and the dose administered (ˇSimek et al., 2021). 
The chemical modification of HA with hydrophobic side-chains or co­
valent cross-linking with, e.g. glutaraldehyde, divinyl sulfone or car­
bodiimides (Schant´e et al., 2011) represents a common approach to 
overcoming its high degradation rate. HA derivatives provide inter­
esting biomaterials for a variety of medical, pharmaceutical, food and 
cosmetic applications, including e.g. injectable products, implantable 
scaffolds and drug delivery systems (Fallacara et al., 2018). One of its 
most attractive properties, in addition to prolonged degradability and 
the physical properties of the final compositions, comprises the com­
plete elimination of the respective derivative and its degradation into 
harmless metabolites once it has fulfilled its function in the organism. 
The various ways in which to achieve this include the modification of 
the HA with hydrophobic substances that occur naturally in the body, 
such as fatty acids, one of which, the lauroyl-modification of HA (lau- 
HA), we recently described in Chmelaˇr et al. (2019). Acylated HA de­
rivatives have been studied in the context of a number of medical ap­
plications e.g. injectable nanocarriers for the treatment of cancer and 
neurodegenerative disorders (Park et al., 2010; Pepe et al., 2020; 
Smejkalov´a et al., 2014), ophthalmologic applications (Huerta-´Angeles 
et al., 2020) and the treatment of acne (Pavlík et al., 2022). However, 
despite the wide range of acylated HA derivative applications, little is 
known of their pharmacokinetics, metabolization and the potential 
toxicity of their degradation products. Depending on the level of HA 
modification, its derivatization may lead to increased cytotoxicity, thus

necessitating its thorough testing prior to application (Huerta-´Angeles 
et al., 2018). Whereas the pharmacokinetics of intravenously adminis­
tered oleyl-modified HA has recently been reported in ˇSimek et al., 
2019, the acylated HA metabolization mechanism has not yet been 
studied systematically. 
This study concerns the investigation of the in vivo fate of intra­
peritoneally administered HA and lau-HA, for the purposes of which we 
employed recently developed specific LC-MS methodology to trace HA 
and its derivatives in the body without the need for fluorescent or 
radioisotope labelling. Moreover, the study introduces a stable isotope- 
labelling strategy for establishing the metabolization of exogenous HA. 
A thin film was chosen as the evaluated form of lau-HA since several 
medical applications involve the application of HA derivatives in the 
form of films or textile materials (Huerta-´Angeles et al., 2018). The 
intraperitoneal administration route was selected since it is highly 
relevant in terms of the potential use of HA and its derivatives as anti­
adhesive barriers to intra-abdominal adhesions (Chmelaˇr et al., 2019) or 
as peritoneal dialysis supplements (Breborowicz et al., 2002). Addi­
tionally, preparation of thin films from HA or its derivatives via solution 
casting is the simple procedure preventing any undesirable modulation 
of the material composition (degradation or chemical modification). The 
aims of this study were to investigate the degradability of intraperito­
neally administered lau-HA and to confirm the metabolization of acyl­
ated HA into non-toxic metabolites, which makes up a crucial factor in 
terms of its safe use in medical applications. 
2. Material and methods 
2.1. Preparation of the isotope-labelled acylated HA and free-standing 
lauroyl-HA films 
The lau-HA was prepared via the esterification of the hydroxyl 
groups on the HA backbone using a symmetric lauric acid anhydride 
(ˇSmejkalov´a et al., 2012). The 13C-labelled lau-HA was prepared ac­
cording to the same procedure, however the HA was substituted by 13C- 
labelled HA prepared biotechnologically from D-glucose (U-13C, 99 %) 
(Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, USA) (ˇSimek et al., 2021). The 
deuterated lauric acid (≥98 %, Merck, Germany) was first converted to 
symmetrical anhydride using benzoyl chloride for the synthesis of the 
deuterated lau-HA. The resulting derivates, with their respective degrees 
of bound lauryl (DS; expressed as the weight %) and the molecular 
weights of the native HA backbone were as follows – lau-HA I (11.2 %, 
330 kDa), lau-HA II (13.0 %, 320 kDa), HA-(2H)C12 (13.0 %, 300 kDa), 
(13C)HA-C12 (13.8 %, 273 kDa), (13C)HA-(2H)C12 (14.2 %, 273 kDa). A 
similar procedure was used for the synthesis of 13C-palmitoyl modified 
octasaccharide comprising one 13C-palmitic acid (≥99 %, Merck, Ger­
many) per HA octasaccharide (Contipro, Czech Republic), with glucur­
onic acid at the non-reducing end. The Mw of HA and lau-HA and its 
distribution were determined by SEC-MALLS as described previously 
(ˇCoˇzíkov´a et al., 2017). 
The free-standing HA (Mw 330 kDa) and lau-HA films were prepared 
via solution casting and characterized according to Chmelaˇr et al. 
(2019). The resulting films evinced a weight per area of 2.3 mg cm−2. 
1.5 × 1.5 cm samples were cut from these films with weights of 5.2 mg 
± 10 % and areas of 2.25 cm2 ± 10 % for the in vivo experiments. The 
thickness of films was 16.6 μm ± 12 %. Ethylene oxide was used for the 
sterilization of the samples. The molecular weight distribution of HA and 
lau-HA derivatives is presented in (S.Fig. 1), other characteristics of thin 
films and input derivatives are summarized in S.Table 1. 
2.2. Pharmacokinetic studies 
Male C57BL/6J mice, 11–14 weeks of age and with weights of 20–25 
g, were obtained from the Laboratory of Animal Breeding and Experi­
mental Facility of the Faculty of Medicine (Masaryk University, Brno, 
Czech Republic). The experiments were approved by the Animal Care 
Committee of the Czech Academy of Sciences (protocol no. 9/2019) and 
were fully in accordance with the EU and NIH Guide for the Care and Use 
of Laboratory Animals. 
Following the operation procedure described in Chmelaˇr et al. 
(2019), the HA or lau-HA films were placed into the peritoneal cavity on 
the small intestine near the cecum. The tested materials comprised films 
of native HA (330 kDa), lau-HA I and lau-HA II. The mice were sacrificed 
at several time points following operation via the withdrawal of blood 
under deep isoflurane anesthesia. The time points for the native HA film 
group were 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, 120 h; for the lau-HA I films 0.5, 1, 1.5, 
2, 3, 5, 7, 10, 15, 25, 30, 35 days and for the lau-HA II films 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 
20, 30, 40, 50, 60 days. Three animals were sacrificed at each time point. 
The isotopically labelled lau-HA in the form of lyophilizate was 
applied intraperitoneally for the metabolization studies of the acylated 
HA. The tested materials comprised unlabelled lau-HA II, HA-(2H)C12, 
(13C)HA-C12, and (13C)HA-(2H)C12. The sampling time points for the 
lau-HA films were set at the 5th and 10th post-operative days. Three 
animals were sacrificed at each time point. 
With respect to the study of the metabolization of the acylated HA, 
100 μl of 13C-palmitoyl-modified HA octasaccharide (7.53 mg ml−1) was 
intravenously administered to four mice, which were subsequently 
sacrificed at 2 and 8 h following administration. The samples were 
assayed in duplicates. Blood was collected via cardiac puncturing and 
the plasma was separated by means of centrifugation for 15 min at 
3000g. All the biological specimens were subsequently stored at −80 ◦C 
pending their analysis. 
2.3. In vitro metabolism of acylated hyaluronan via HepG2 cells 
Human HepG2 hepatocyte carcinoma cell lines were cultured in 
DMEM high glucose medium supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine 
serum (Capricorn Scientific, Germany) and 1× penicillin/streptomycin 
(Biosera, France). The cell lines were cultured according to the manu­
facturer's instructions. The cells were seeded on a 6 well plate (500,000 
cells/well) for 6 days (37 ◦C, 5 % CO2, 95 % humidity) for the modified 
HA uptake and metabolization experiments. On day 1, the cells were 
incubated in 2 ml of the cultivation media. On day 2, the cultivation 
media from day 1 was aspirated, and the cells were treated with 2 ml of a 
solution consisting of acyl-HA (unlabelled HA-C12, HA-(2H)C12, (13C) 
HA-C12, (13C)HA-(2H)C12 and (13C)palmitoyl-modified HA octa­
saccharide) samples dissolved in full cultivation media to 250 μg ml−1. 
After 5 days of cultivation, the media were collected and frozen (−80 ◦C) 
as was the sterile PBS which was used for the washing of the cells (4 
times, 1 ml). The cells were lysed with the addition of 0.5 ml of chilled 
methanol accompanied by 15 min of incubation at −80 ◦C. The cells 
were then scraped off and the content of the wells transferred to test 
tubes, vortexed and centrifuged (4 ◦C, 5 min at 6000g). The analytes 
from the pellets were extracted two times with the addition of 250 μl of 
ice-cold methanol. The supernatants acquired following centrifugation 
were pooled together. 
2.4. Analysis of the native and lauroyl-modified hyaluronan 
Solid residual film and tissue samples were diluted prior to the 
analysis with a homogenization buffer (100 mM PBS with 10 mM EDTA, 
pH 7.4) and homogenized using a Precellys 24 homogenizer (Bertin 
Technologies, France). The native HA and lau-HA were quantified ac­
cording to the modified method described by ˇSimek et al., 2019; satu­
rated HA disaccharide (30 μg ml−1) and decanoyl-modified HA (6 μg 
ml−1; Contipro, Czech Republic) were used as the internal standards. 
The same procedure was employed for the analysis of the Mw fractions 
collected from size exclusion chromatography (2.6) with the omission of 
the protein degradation step. 
The analysis of the native HA was performed on an Agilent 1290 
(Agilent, USA) chromatographic system coupled with a TQ 6495 (Agi­
lent, USA) triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer according to ˇSimek

2.5. Molecular weight of the hyaluronan and lauroyl-hyaluronan and its 
distribution 
The Mw distributions of the HA and lau-HA in plasma, urine, liver 
and kidney homogenates (100 mg ml−1), as well as the residual film (10 
mg ml−1), were determined by means of size exclusion chromatography. 
An Alliance e2695 HPLC system (Waters, USA) equipped with a fraction 
collector III (Waters, USA) was used for the conducting of the analysis. 
Two series-connected Shodex OHpak SB-806M HQ (300 × 8 mm, 13 
μm) and SB-807 HQ (300 × 8 mm, 35 μm) (Showa Denko K. K., Japan) 
columns were used for the HA analysis. 100 μl of each sample was 
injected and the columns were eluted with 0.15 mol⋅l−1 sodium chloride 
at 1 ml min−1 and 40 ◦C. Twenty fractions (1 ml each, 8th–28th minutes 
of elution) were collected from all the samples. The HA in each fraction 
was quantified applying the LC-MS method described above (2.5). 
Two series-connected PolySep-GFC-P linear (300 × 7.8 mm) and 
PolySep-GFC-P 2000 (300 × 7.8 mm) (Phenomenex, USA) columns were 
used for the lau-HA analysis. 100 μl of each sample was injected and the 
columns were eluted with 0.05 mol⋅l−1 NaH2PO4.2H2O:2-propanol (3:1) 
mixture at 1 ml min−1 and 40 ◦C. Twenty fractions (1 ml each, 5th–25th 
minutes of elution) were collected from all the samples. The lau-HA in 
each fraction was quantified applying the LC-MS method described 
above (Section 2.5). 
2.6. Analysis of the native and lauroyl-modified hyaluronan 
octasaccharide 
20 μl of internal standard solution (13C-labelled HA oligosaccharides, 
30 μg ml−1, and palmitoyl-modified HA decasaccharide, 6 μg ml−1; 
Contipro, Czech Republic) and 400 μl of chilled (−80 ◦C) methanol was 
added to 100 μl of the cell pellet suspension and liver homogenate (250 
mg ml−1). The samples were vortexed for 30 min at 4 ◦C and incubated 
for 2 h at −80 ◦C. The samples were then centrifuged (10 min, 15,000g, 
4 ◦C) and the supernatant was dried under a stream of nitrogen. The 
dried residues were then redissolved in 100 μl of water for the native 
octasaccharide analysis or in 100 μl of an isopropanol/water mixture 
(6:4, v/v) for the lauroyl-modified octasaccharide analysis. 
An Acquity UPLC chromatographic system was coupled with a Xevo 
TQ-XS triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters, UK) equipped with 
an electrospray ionization source operating in the negative ion mode. 
The chromatographic separation of the unmodified HA oligosaccharides 
was performed using an Atlantis Premier BEH C18 AX column (50 × 2.1 
mm, 1.7 μm) (Waters, UK) at 60 ◦C. The mobile phases consisted of 10 
mM ammonium acetate (pH 5.0) and acetonitrile. The flow rate was set 
at 0.4 ml min−1 and the following acetonitrile gradient was applied: 5 % 
in 0–0.5 min, 5–60 % in 0.5–5.0 min, 60 % in 5.0–6.0 min, 60–5 % in 
6.0–6.3 min and 5 % in 6.3–8.0 min. The injection volume was 5 μl. The 
electrospray parameters were as follows: voltage 3 kV, source temper­
ature 150 ◦C, desolvation temperature 500 ◦C, sampling cone voltage 60 
V, cone gas flow (N2) 150 l⋅h−1, desolvation gas flow (N2) 1000 l⋅h−1, 
nebulizer gas flow (N2) 7.0 bar. 
An Acquity UPLC Glycan BEH Amide HILIC column (130 Å, 1.7 μm, 
2.1 × 150 mm) (Waters, UK) was used for the LC-MS analysis of the 
lauroyl-modified octasaccharide. The mobile phases consisted of 10 mM 
ammonium formate (pH 4.4) and acetonitrile. The flow rate was set at 
0.3 ml min−1, the column temperature was 25 ◦C and the following 
aqueous phase gradient was applied: 0–1 min 20 %, 11 min 60 %, 12–17 
min 95 %, 18–22 min 20 %. The electrospray parameters were as fol­
lows: spray capillary voltage 2.8 kV, source temperature 150 ◦C, des­
olvation temperature 500 ◦C, sampling cone voltage 70 V, cone gas flow 
(N2) 150 l⋅h−1, desolvation gas flow (N2) 1000 l⋅h−1, nebulizer gas flow 
(N2) 7.0 bar. 
2.7. Metabolomic analysis 
The metabolomic analysis was performed according to a method 
described in (ˇSimek et al., 2021). Methanolic extracts (according to 
Section 2.7) were analysed using an Acquity I-class chromatographic 
system (Waters, UK) connected to a Synapt G2-Si mass spectrometer 
(Waters, UK). An Acquity C18 HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) 
(Waters, UK) at 22 ◦C was used for the chromatographic separation. The 
mobile phases consisted of 0.1 % formic acid in water and 0.1 % formic 
acid in 95 % acetonitrile. The flow rate was 0.45 ml min−1 and the 
following 95 % acetonitrile gradient was applied: 0 % in 0–2 min, 0–15 
% in 2–4 min, 15–45 % in 4–10 min, 45–100 % in 6.0–6.3 min, 100 % in 
13–20 min and 0 % in 25–35 min. The injection volume was 3 μl. The 
electrospray parameters were as follows: spray capillary voltage −1.8 
kV, source temperature 120 ◦C, desolvation temperature 500 ◦C, sam­
pling cone voltage 20 V, source offset 40 V, cone gas flow (N2) 20 l⋅h−1, 
desolvation gas flow (N2) 800 l⋅h−1, nebulizer gas flow (N2) 6.0 bar. The 
data was collected in the 50–1200 m/z negative full scan mode at a scan 
rate of 2 s−1. The isotopic analysis and the isotope-labelled metabolite 
assignments were performed using the El-MAVEN data processing en­
gine (Elucidata, India). 
2.8. Analysis of the free fatty acids 
The sample processing approach was the same as that used for the 
oligosaccharide analysis (Section 2.7). The analysis of the free fatty acids 
was performed using a Vanquish H chromatographic system connected 
to an Exploris 240 orbitrap type mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher 
Scientific, USA). 
The chromatographic separation was performed using an Acquity 
UPLC Protein BEH C4 column (300 Å, 1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 50 mm) 
(Waters, UK) at 30 ◦C. The mobile phases consisted of 10 mM ammo­
nium acetate (pH 5.0) and 95 % acetonitrile. The flow rate was set at 0.5 
ml min−1, and the following acetonitrile gradient was applied: 0–0.5 
min 10 %, 1 min 45 %, 4 min 35 %, 4.5–6.5 min 100 %, 7–8 min 10 %. 
The injection volume was 5 μl. The electrospray parameters were as 
follows: RF lens 70 %, spray capillary voltage 2.5 kV, sheath gas (N2) 60 
AU, aux gas (N2) 15 AU, sweep gas (N2) 2 AU, ion transfer tube 350 ◦C, 
vaporizer temp 400 ◦C. The data was collected in 100–350 m/z negative 
full scan mode with a resolution of 30,000. 
2.9. Data analysis 
Pharmacokinetic data was evaluated in Microsoft Excel. To calculate 
values of AUC trapezoidal rule was used for integration. To evaluate rate 
constants for HA and lau-HA absorption and elimination Bateman 
function was used. For data fitting, Solver Excel add-in program and 
VBA macros were used. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Biodegradability of the intraperitoneally administered lau-HA and 
native HA films 
Aimed at confirming the biodegradability of the intraperitoneally 
administered lau-HA, a thin native HA film (330 kDa) and two lau-HA

films with differing DS (lau-HA I, 330 kDa, DS 11.2 % and lau-HA II, 320 
kDa, DS 13.0 %) were placed into the peritoneal cavity on the small 
intestine near the cecum of C57Bl/6 mice. The peritoneal cavity was 
visually inspected at predetermined time points for the integrity of the 
films, and the HA/lau-HA content was determined in the residual films, 
organs and body fluids using an LC-MS method. The native HA film was 
observed to have dissolved rapidly in the peritoneal fluid, and no re­
sidual material was observed after just 4 h (Fig. 1A). The rapid elimi­
nation of the HA film from the peritoneal cavity was also evident from 
the HA plasma concentration time profile, which indicated the instant 
absorption of the HA into the bloodstream (Fig. 1B). However, the 
dissolution of the lau-HA films was observed to be significantly slower. 
Both the lau-HA I and II films remained in the form of a solid hydrogel 
for three days, following which the lau-HA films began to disintegrate 
being detectable only in some mice (1–2 out of 3) at time points after 5 
days after implantation (Fig. 1A). After 15 days, the lau-HA I film was 
observed to have completely resorbed, with only <1 % of the adminis­
tered material remaining in the peritoneal cavity. The dissolution of the 
lau-HA II film was slower; after 30 days, 8 % of the administered ma­
terial remained in the peritoneal cavity. However, after 60 days, it was 
determined that both the lau-HA films had been eliminated from the 
peritoneal cavity. By way of comparison, an intraperitoneally adminis­
tered film made from a mixture of native HA and carboxymethylcellu­
lose was previously observed to have resorbed from the peritoneal cavity 
within seven days (Burns et al., 1997). The data thus suggests that the 
chemical modification of HA with lauroyl sidechains ensures biode­
gradability following its intraperitoneal application whilst preserving 
the integrity of the HA film for several days. 
3.2. Absorption of the HA and lau-HA from the peritoneal cavity into the 
blood circulation system 
The LC-MS analysis of the various organs and body fluids considered 
was subsequently performed aimed at the investigation of the organ 
distribution of the lau-HA and the identification of the lau-HA degra­
dation products. The native HA film dissolved rapidly in the peritoneal 
fluid and the HA was absorbed into the bloodstream (Fig. 1). The 
bioavailability of the intraperitoneally administered HA (330 kDa) was 
73 % of that of intravenously administered HA (370 kDa) (S.Fig. 3), as 
described in (ˇSimek et al., 2021). 23 % of the administered dose (Y) was 
seen to be present in the plasma as soon as at the first sampling point (4 
h). The maximum concentration of HA in the plasma was observed 12 h 
following implantation, at which time its concentration in the plasma 
was 1437.9 μg ml−1 (Y 43 %). These results are in agreement with those 
obtained from the intraperitoneal infusion of a dialysis solution sup­
plemented with 2 mg of high-Mw HA (1.8–2.4 MDa) to rats, i.e. the cmax 
was also observed at 12 h (Breborowicz et al., 2001). 
The absorption of the dissolved lau-HA from the peritoneal cavity 
was significantly lower, and the peak plasma concentrations were 
determined at just 37.6 and 19.9 μg ml−1 (Y 1.0 % and Y 0.8 %) for the 
administered lau-HA I and lau-HA II films, respectively. The modifica­
tion of the HA with lauroyl affected not only the extent of the absorption 
from the peritoneal cavity but also the absorption kinetics (Table 1). The 
absorption rate constant for the lau-HA I was observed to be almost 
twice as high as that for the lau-HA II, which resulted in significantly 
different calculated plasma peak concentration times of 95 h for the lau- 
HA I and 170 h for the lau-HA II. A higher DS results in the increased 
hydrophobicity of the film and decreased solubility in peritoneal fluid, 
which leads to the prolonged stay of the film in the peritoneal cavity and 
the slower absorption of lau-HA into the systemic circulation. 
The higher DS also affected the elimination kinetics from the 
bloodstream. Lau-HA was seen to be present in the bloodstream for 35 
and 60 days following the implantation of the lau-HA I and lau-HA II 
films, respectively, while the concentration of HA in the plasma returned 
to normal levels just five days following the implantation of the native 
HA film. In comparison, the calculated terminal half-lives in the plasma 
for the lau-HA were 5–10 times higher than for intravenously adminis­
tered oleyl-HA with a lower Mw (15 kDa, DS 10 %) (ˇSimek et al., 2019).

3.3. Distribution of the HA and lau-HA in the organism 
Due to its large area and the presence of mesothelial cell microvilli, 
the peritoneum is a very effective entry site to the systemic circulation 
for intraperitoneally administered substances. While small solutes (Mw 
< 5–20 kDa) are removed from the peritoneal cavity via the system of 
capillaries that lead to the portal vein, macromolecules (Mw > 30 kDa) 
are transported primarily via the lymphatic system (Al Shoyaib et al., 
2019). 
Aimed at identifying the HA and lau-HA transport pathways, the Mw 
distributions of the HA and lau-HA in the administered films, film resi­
dues, plasma, liver and kidney were determined by means of gel chro­
matography. The Mw distribution results of the lau-HA in the 
administered films, film residues, plasma and urine were observed to be 
similar (Fig. 2), thus suggesting the absence of the occurrence of 
degradation/depolymerization in the peritoneal cavity and the plasma. 
Therefore, these results support the hypothesis that HA and lau-HA are 
transported from the peritoneal cavity to the blood circulation system in 
their intact forms via the lymphatic system. 
The HA and lau-HA in the bloodstream were distributed mainly to 
the liver and the kidneys (Fig. 3), in a similar way as is intravenously 
administered HA and oleyl-HA (ˇSimek et al., 2019, 2021). However, 
most of the intraperitoneally administered HA was present in these lo­
cations due to the perfusion of blood to these organs (S.Table 2, S.Fig. 4). 
This was, however, not the case of the lau-HA since its hepatic and renal 
concentrations were significantly higher than in the blood circulation 
system (S.Table 3, S.Table 4). The concentration of lau-HA per organ 
weight in the spleen was at least 2–3 times higher than in the liver for 
both DS. The spleen contains only 6 % of the blood volume of the liver 
(Hall et al., 2012), thus the lau-HA may also be distributed within the 
splenic extravascular space, which is related to its lymphatic flow 
filtration function, i.e. the main lau-HA absorption pathway from the 
peritoneal cavity. 
Delayed elimination was observed in the lungs and adipose tissue as 
a result of the increased hydrophobicity of the lau-HA. Data from one of 
our previous studies showed that Mw exerts no impact on the retention of 
native HA in the lungs (ˇSimek et al., 2021), which indicates that this 
phenomenon should be attributed to the hydrophobicity of lau-HA and 
its possible binding to the surfactants present in the lungs (Veldhuizen 
et al., 1998). This observation is in agreement with the adipose tissue 
data, which indicated enhanced lau-HA levels even at those time points 
at which the lau-HA in the plasma had already decreased. In contrast, no 
native HA was deposited in the adipose tissue, probably due to its high 
degree of hydrophilicity. 
Approximately 7–10 % of the administrated lau-HA and native HA 
was deposited in the organs located in the abdomen at their maximum 
concentration times. It was assumed that these organs, e.g. the stomach 
and small intestine, were coated with a solubilized polymer since the 
elimination kinetics of the HA and lau-HA from these organs correlated 
exactly with the elimination of the films from the peritoneal cavity. This 
was also reflected in the enhanced amounts of HA and lau-HA discov­
ered in the contents of the peritoneal organs; these samples were 
probably contaminated with the solubilized film present on the surface 
of the organs during sampling. 
Intraperitoneally administered HA and lau-HA do not accumulate in 
the body; >99 % of the administered amounts were observed to have 
been eliminated from the body after 3.7, 18.6 and 32.7 days following 
the administration of the native HA, lau-HA I and lau-HA II films, 
respectively (calculated from Bateman's function (S.Fig. 5)). In com­
parison, intravenously administered oleyl-HA and high-Mw HA at 
similar doses were observed to have been eliminated within 72 h (ˇSimek 
et al., 2019).

(Fig. 4). 15.1 % of the administered HA was eliminated in the urine and 
1.44 % in the feces within 5 days. The excreted amount of lau-HA was 
approximately ten-times lower than that of the HA; 1.5 % of the

3.5. Metabolism of the acylated derivatives of HA 
The metabolization of HA derivatives is thought to occur via several 
pathways, the most favorable of which in the case of acylated HA 
derivatives comprises metabolization into native HA and free fatty acids 
that occur naturally in the body. In order to test this hypothesis, the 
individually acylated HA oligosaccharide (AN8-C16) 13C-labelled on the 
palmitate group was synthesized and its metabolism investigated in vivo 
(intravenous application) and in vitro (HepG2 cells). The acylated 
octasaccharide was chosen because it has known structure (S.Fig. 6) and 
low Mw allowing direct mass spectrometry detection. The length of 
octasaccharide was selected because it is recognized by CD44 the main 
receptor for HA (Misra et al., 2015). Since 13C-lauric acid was not 
available commercially, 13C-palmitic acid, which exhibits similar phar­
macokinetic behavior, was selected for the synthesis. 
Following the cultivation of the HepG2 cells in the presence of AN8- 
C16, a mere 1 % of the administered amount was present intracellularly 
and approximately 33 % was present in the culture medium in the intact 
form (Fig. 5). The remainder of the administered amount was present in 
the medium in the form of native octasaccharide (AN8) (52.7 %) and free 
palmitic acid (14.2 %), thus confirming the ability of hepatic cells to 
cleave acyl from HA. Metabolites of 13C-labelled palmitic acid (lauric 
acid, myristic acid, oleic acid, palmitoyl carnitine) were also observed,

Following the intravenous administration of AN8-C16 to C57BL/6 
mice (50 mg kg−1), only 8 % of the administered AN8-C16 was observed 
in the blood 2 h after administration and 5 % after 8 h. Cleaved AN8 and

served to confirm the cleavage of the acyl modification from the HA 
backbone in vivo (Fig. 5). In addition, the untargeted metabolic analysis 
revealed that the liver and plasma contained AN8-C16 hydroxylated on 
the palmitoyl moiety. Thus, acylated derivates can also be metabolized 
in the form of intact molecules without prior cleavage by hyaluronidases 
or esterases. However, since the activity of this alternative pathway was 
>20-fold lower than that of the acyl cleavage pathway, it can be 
assumed that these hydroxylated metabolites are subsequently cleaved 
to native HA and hydroxylated fatty acids, which also occur naturally in 
the body. 
Aimed at confirming the metabolization pathway for intraperitone­
ally administered lau-HA and at identifying the location of its occur­
rence, lau-HA with the heavy isotopes on the acyl (2H) and HA (13C) 
backbone was synthetized, characterized (S.Fig. 7) and administered 
intraperitoneally C57BL/6 mice. The animals were sacrificed after 5 and 
10 days and concentrations of the lau-HA in the plasma, liver, residual 
film and urine were determined from the quantification of either the 
native (ΔAN2) or modified (ΔAN2-C12) unsaturated disaccharides 
(ˇSimek et al., 2019). 
The ratio of ΔAN2-C12/ΔAN2 in the residual films was similar to 
that determined in the plasma and the liver (Fig. 6A), thus indicating the 
similar metabolization rate for the modified and native parts of the lau- 
HA. A different ratio for the urine correlates with the fact that polar 
compounds are preferentially eliminated via glomerulus filtration. In 
the liver, a small decrease in ΔAN2-C12/ΔAN2 ratio signifies a slightly 
higher rate of deesterification compared to native HA metabolization. 
These data are consistent with the high turnover of native HA reported 
in the liver (Necas et al., 2008) and correlates with data on intravenously 
administered oleyl-HA (ˇSimek et al., 2019), that is, the metabolization of 
the modified and unmodified parts of the oleyl-HA occurred at similar 
rates. The unchanged ΔAN2-C12/ΔAN2 ratio in the residual films in the 
peritoneal cavity also implied that despite the presence of esterases and 
lipases in the peritoneal fluid (Day & Harris, 1960), lau-HA is metabo­
lized only after its absorption into the systematic circulation and its 
distribution to the liver. This was further supported by the unchanged 
Mw of lau-HA in the peritoneal cavity (Fig. 2). In addition, the metab­
olomic analysis of the liver confirmed that the de-esterification of lau- 
HA occurs in the liver (Fig. 6B) and that lau-HA is depolymerized and 
further metabolized into monosaccharides in a similar way as is native 
HA (ˇSimek et al., 2021) (Fig. 6C). Interestingly, no metabolization into 
hydroxylated lau-HA was observed, which served to confirm that the 
activity of this pathway is relatively minor. 
4. Conclusion 
Thin films made from synthetically prepared HA modulated via 
acylation with lauroyl is biodegradable. It was proven that HA and lau- 
HA are transported via the lymphatics from the peritoneal cavity to the 
systematic circulation. HA and lau-HA do not accumulate in the body 
and were observed to have been completely eliminated from the body 5,

The bacteria redox balance between oxidizing and reducing species plays a critical role in bacterial activ-
ities, and the disruption of this homeostasis offers a ﬂexible antibacterial strategy to combat bacterial
multidrug resistance. Here, the ligand exchange strategy of Au NCs was ﬁrst developed to construct an
oxidative stress ampliﬁer. We cleverly utilized the reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation ability of his-
tidine (His)-stabilized Au NCs. Cinnamaldehyde (CA) was modiﬁed on the surface of Au NCs through an
aldimine condensation reaction, and the modiﬁcation of CA on the surface of Au NCs further accelerated
ROS generation. Meanwhile, the strong Au-S interaction between CA-Au NCs and thiols facilitated the
ligand exchange of surface histidine-cinnamaldehyde (His-CA) with thiol molecules, causing the con-
sumption of thiols in bacteria and the release of His-CA, which thus ﬁnally resulted in efﬁcient bacterial
cell death. CA-Au NCs showed excellent antibacterial effects on methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aur-
eus (MRSA), including 48-h bioﬁlm removal and the treatment of a pig skin wound infection model, rep-
resenting a promising antibacterial agent for clinical applications.

1. Introduction
The invention of antibiotics provided an important weapon for
human beings to ﬁght against pathogenic bacterial infection; how-
ever, widespread bacterial multidrug resistance has become a seri-
ous problem due to the abuse and misuse of antibiotics in recent
decades [1,2]. Although great effort has been made to develop
new antibiotics, it is quite a struggle to catch up with multidrug-
resistant (MDR) bacteria [3,4]. The ever-widening gap between
antibiotics and MDR bacteria is a risk to human health [5–7];
therefore, it is urgently necessary to develop effective coping
strategies to combat MDR bacterial infections in the future.
Noble metal nanoclusters (NCs) are composed of several to doz-
ens of atoms, and the core size is usually less than 2 nm, which is
between
metal
atoms
and
plasmonic
nanoparticles,
with
molecular-like properties and discrete electronic energy levels
[8–11]. The distinct optical and chemical properties have gained
metal NCs extensive applications in biomedical ﬁelds such as sens-
ing, imaging and therapy [8–11]. Moreover, the antibacterial per-
formance of metal NCs has also gained increasing attention in
recent years [2,3,12–15]. Studies have revealed that Ag NCs of a
smaller size (less than 2 nm) exhibit brighter antibacterial effec-
tiveness than larger scale silver nanoparticles [3,13,15]. Nonethe-
less, the antibacterial properties of Ag NCs depend heavily on the
release of Ag+ ions from their surface, which will inevitably cause
toxicity and side effects to living host cells when combating patho-
genic bacteria [12,16]. This security issue deserves our attention
because it may restrict the clinical applications of Ag NCs. Relative
to Ag NCs, Au NCs have a higher biocompatibility [2,12], which is
attributable to the well-known inertness and stability of gold
[17]. Therefore, ultrasmall Au NCs (below 2 nm) might be an excel-
lent candidate to construct intelligent antibacterial systems as they
have demonstrated great potential in antimicrobial applications
[2,12,18].
The rich active ingredient in traditional herbs could be a huge
treasure trove for developing natural antibiotics [19–21]. Cortex
cinnamomi is a widely used spice and traditional herbal medicine
in some countries. Cinnamaldehyde (CA) is a representative com-
ponent of cortex cinnamomis [22,23], which exhibits a favorable
biological function in antibacterial applications [24–27]. Cin-
namaldehyde as a natural antibacterial agent has been categorized
as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the Food and Drug
Administration (FDA) [23], while the practical applications of cin-
namaldehyde are rather restricted by its inherent shortcomings,
including its strong odor, hydrophobicity, and high volatility. The
derivatization of cinnamaldehyde has been proven to be a feasible
answer to this problem by chemical modiﬁcation, such as the reac-
tion of amino acids with cinnamaldehyde to form Schiff base com-
pounds [22,23]. Safe natural antibiotics may be expected to replace
some artiﬁcial antibiotics and withstand some antibiotic-resistant
bacteria. At present, researchers often load or bind traditional
antibiotics to nanomaterials to treat bacterial infections [3,28].
However, with the abuse of antibiotics, bacteria have developed
resistance to many antibiotics. Because traditional herbs are rich
in active ingredients, the combination of nanomaterials and some
herbal extracts, such as cinnamaldehyde and berberine, might pro-
vide a new opportunity to tackle the growing bacterial resistance.
As an organism, bacteria must regulate their multiple internal
balances to maintain their activities, such as metal homeostasis
[29] and redox balance [30]. Therefore, one practical sterilization
strategy is to break the internal balance of bacteria and expand
their internal imbalance state. Low-molecular-weight thiols play
signiﬁcant roles in maintaining the redox balance between oxidiz-
ing and reducing species in bacterial cells [31,32]. The depletion of
thiols will cause rapid accumulation of reactive oxygen species
(ROS) in bacterial cells, thus giving play to antibacterial effects.
However, there is currently a lack of effective strategies to target
the bacterial redox balance. The ligand exchange reactions of Au
NCs may be an effective strategy that can occur when the ligands
have stronger afﬁnity for Au than the stabilizing ligands of Au
NCs [33]. For example, histidine (His)-stabilized Au NCs can
undergo ligand exchange reactions when encountering thiol
groups due to stronger Au-S interactions than Au-N interactions
[33,34]. Feng et al. found that His-stabilized Au NCs could exert
antiviral effects on the pseudorabies virus by inhibiting the replica-
tion process [35]. In this work, the ligand exchange strategy of gold
nanoclusters was developed to construct an efﬁcient oxidative
stress ampliﬁer (CA-Au NCs) for achieving antibacterial effects.
We ﬁrst synthesized His-stabilized Au NCs via Au-N interactions
using His as a reductant and stabilizing ligand, and then CA was
modiﬁed on the surface of Au NCs through a typical aldimine con-
densation reaction. The ability of ultrasmall Au NCs to generate
reactive oxygen species (ROS) was utilized and further accelerated
by the functionalization of CA on the surface of the Au NCs. At the
same time, the strong Au-S interaction between the CA-Au NCs and
thiols
promoted
ligand
exchange
of
the
surface
histidine-
cinnamaldehyde (His-CA) groups with thiol species. Therefore,
enhanced ROS generation and signiﬁcant thiol depletion offer
ampliﬁed oxidative stress in bacteria, which thus disrupts the
redox balance of bacteria and eventually induces bacterial death.
The CA-Au NCs system exhibited multiple antibacterial mecha-
nisms, including the disruption of membrane integrity, the dissipa-
tion of membrane potential, the improvement on membrane
permeability. The synergistical effect of Au NCs and CA leads to
the simultaneous enhanced ROS generation and signiﬁcant thiol
depletion, and the release of His-CA ligand as well, which eventu-
ally contribute to the signiﬁcant improved antimicrobial perfor-
mance.
CA-Au

new ideas for combating multidrug-resistant bacterial infections.

2.3. Synthesis of Au NCs and CA-Au NCs
Au NCs were prepared according to a typical procedure with
minor modiﬁcations [34,36,37]. Brieﬂy, HAuCl4 (5 mL, 10 mM)
was added to a histidine solution (15 mL, 0.1 M), and after mixing
evenly, the solution was kept away from light at room temperature
for at least 12 h. Cinnamaldehyde (CA)-functionalized Au NCs were
prepared by a typical aldimine condensation reaction. The above
Au NC solution was adjusted to pH 10 with 1 M NaOH solution,
and excess cinnamaldehyde (0.25 mL) was then dissolved in
10 mL of ethanol and added dropwise to the Au NC solution. The
resultant mixture was stirred at room temperature for 10 h and
then kept at 4 C for more than 24 h. Next, a rotary evaporator
was used to remove ethanol, and extra cinnamaldehyde was lea-
ched through a 0.22-lm organic ﬁlter membrane. A 72-h dialysis
procedure was required to further purify the CA-Au NCs (molecular
weight cut-off (MWCO): 500–1000), and afterward, the solution
was concentrated by rotary evaporation. The resultant solution
was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the super-
natant was ﬁltered through a 0.22-lm aqueous ﬁlter membrane
to remove larger particles. Finally, the sample was quantiﬁed to
~1 mM (based on Au atoms) by ICP-MS. The yield of CA-Au NCs
is about 19.3%. The ﬁnal product was stored at 4 C for further use.
2.4. Bacterial culture and antibacterial test
Methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA, CMCC(B)
26003) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA,
ATCC 43300) were cultured in broth medium (0.5% w/v tryptone,
0.5% w/v sodium chloride, 0.3% w/v beef extract, pH 7.0–7.2) in a
shaker at 37 C and 200 rpm. The cultured bacteria growing in log-
arithmic phase were washed several times and then diluted
to ~ 5  106 colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU mL1) with
phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 20 mM, pH 7.0). Afterwards,
900 mL of the bacterial suspension was mixed with 100 mL of the
Au NC/CA-Au NC solution (with a ﬁnal level within 10–80 mM on
the basis of Au atoms) or the control group (ultrapure water) in a
2-mL round bottom centrifuge tube and further incubated at
37 C and 200 rpm for 2 h. Afterwards, the bacterial suspension
was diluted 1000 times using deionized water, and then, 100 mL
was taken and spread on a broth agar plate. The colonies were then
counted after incubating for 15 h in an incubator at 37 C. Bacterial
viability was calculated according to the following equation:
Bacteria viabilityð%Þ ¼ CFUtreat=CFUcontrol  100%:
2.5. Bacterial membrane damage
Hoechst 33342 and propidium iodide (PI) were used to evaluate
membrane damage in the bacterial cells. Log phase MSSA cells
were incubated without or with 30 mM Au NC/CA-Au NC solutions
at 37 C and 200 rpm for 2 h. Then, 5 mg mL1 Hoechst 33342 and
10 mg mL1 PI were added to each bacterial suspension and incu-
bated for 15 min in the dark. After washing several times with
PBS, ﬂuorescence imaging was performed on the sample slides
using a BX53M upright ﬂuorescence microscope.
2.6. SEM and TEM analysis
Cultured MSSA growing in logarithmic phase was collected by
centrifugation (8000 rpm, 5 min), washed twice with PBS, and then
diluted to ~ 5  106 CFU mL1 with PBS. Then, 30 lM of Au NCs/CA-
Au NCs or control solutions (PBS) was added to the bacterial sus-
pension and incubated in a shaker at 37 C and 200 rpm for 2 h.
The treated bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation,
washed twice with PBS, and then ﬁxed with a 2.5% glutaraldehyde
solution for 4 h. Finally, the bacterial cells were dehydrated with
sequential concentrations of ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%,
and 100%). The treated bacterial suspension was then dropped
onto a piece of clean aluminum foil and allowed to dry naturally
for 24 h before being used for SEM analysis to observe the bacterial
morphology. Moreover, the treated bacterial suspension was
dropped onto a TEM copper grid, allowed to dry naturally, imme-
diately negatively stained with 2% sodium phosphotungstate solu-
tion for 1–2 min, naturally dried for 24 h and used for TEM analysis
to observe the morphology of the bacteria.
2.7. Change in the membrane potential
3,30-Dipropylthiadicarbocyanine iodide (DiSC3(5)) was applied
to test the change in membrane potential [2]. MSSA growing in log-
arithmic phase was collected and resuspended in HEPES buffer
(10 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 20 mM glucose, and 100 mM KCl). Then,
5 mM DiSC3(5) dye was added to the bacterial suspension and incu-
bated at 37 C for 1 h in the dark. After washing three times with
HEPES, 30 lM of Au NCs/CA-Au NCs or control solutions (PBS) were
added to the bacterial suspension and incubated for 2 h at 37 C.
Finally, the ﬂuorescence of the solution was measured at an excita-
tion/emission wavelength of 622/670 nm by an ELISA microplate
reader.

2.8. ROS generation analysis
20,70-Dichloroﬂuorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) dye was applied

2.9. Thiol levels analysis
Thiols are an important antioxidant that can protect cells from
reactive oxygen species. Thiols levels were detected using the Ell-
man method. MSSA growing in the logarithmic phase was treated
for 2 h with 30 lM Au NCs/CA-Au NCs or control solutions (PBS).
The treated bacteria were washed twice, resuspended in PBS, and
lysed using a JY92-II ultrasonic cell crusher. Thiols were then quan-
tiﬁed using the Thiol Detection Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bio-
engineering Institute, China). The absorbance at 405 nm reﬂected
intracellular thiol levels.

2.12. Mature bioﬁlm clearance test
The effect of the Au NCs/CA-Au NCs on 48-h bioﬁlm removal
was studied. First, 200 lL of Log-phase MRSA cells (~5  107 CFU
mL1) were cultivated in a 96-well cell culture plate for 48 h in a
37 C incubator to form mature bioﬁlms. After the mature bioﬁlm
was formed, the original bacterial suspension was removed from
each pore; then, the bioﬁlm was washed twice with PBS. Subse-
quently, the bioﬁlm was incubated with different concentrations
of Au NCs/CA-Au NCs (0, 2.5, 5, 10, 30, 50, and 80 lM) in broth
medium for 24 h at 37 C and then washed twice with PBS to
remove the residual material. Thereafter, 100 lL of ﬂuorescein
diacetate (FDA) solution (20 mM) was added to each well and incu-
bated for 1 h at 37 C away from light to stain the living bacteria in
the bioﬁlm. A separate FDA solution was used as a blank sample.
The ﬂuorescence (F) of each solution was then measured using
an ELISA microplate reader at excitation/emission wavelengths of
488/530 nm. The scavenging effect on the mature bioﬁlm was eval-
uated according to the following equation:
Bacterial viabilityð%Þ ¼ ðFtreat  FblankÞ=ðFcontrol  FblankÞ  100%
Among them, identiﬁcation of bioﬁlm formation was deter-
mined by crystal violet staining. Brieﬂy, after the bacteria were cul-
tured for 48 h in a 37 C incubator, the medium was discarded, and
the cells were washed twice with PBS. Then, the bioﬁlm was ﬁxed
with 400 lL of methanol for 5 min and dried for 20 min at room
temperature after removing methanol. Afterwards, the bioﬁlm
was stained with a 0.1% crystal violet solution for 5 min. The dye
was then discarded, and the bioﬁlm was washed with ultrapure
water until the solution was colorless. Finally, the morphological
structure of the bioﬁlm was observed using the BX53M upright ﬂu-
orescence microscope.
Moreover, confocal ﬂuorescence microscopy was used to study
the scavenging effect of the Au NCs/CA-Au NCs on the 48-h bio-
ﬁlms. The bioﬁlm was ﬁrst treated without or with the 50 lM Au
NCs/CA-Au NCs for 24 h at 37 C. Then, a 10-mg mL1 Hoechst
33342 and 20-mg mL1 PI solution were added to the treated bio-
ﬁlm and incubated for 20 min at 37 C to stain live/dead bacteria.
3D ﬂuorescence imaging was then performed using a confocal laser
scanning microscope with a 20  objective lens.
2.13. Pig skin wound infection model
A pig skin wound infection model was applied to evaluate the
therapeutic effect of the Au NCs/CA-Au NCs. Brieﬂy, pig skin with
a thickness of ~2.0 mm was cut into three pieces of the same area
(~2.0  2.0 cm2), and then, the same rectangular wound (~1  0.
5 cm2) was cut out of each piece. The obtained pig skin was steril-
ized under an ultraviolet lamp. Next, the wound was infected with
10 lL of logarithmic growth MRSA (~5  106 CFU mL1) for 1 h. The
wounded pig skin was then treated without or with Au NCs/CA-Au
NCs (10 lL, 30 lM) every 12 h. The color of pig skin was observed
and recorded every 24 h. The pig skin tissues were ground and
made into a 10% tissue homogenate four days later. The bacterial
colonies were then counted by the agar plate dilution method,
and the results were expressed as CFU per milliliter tissue
homogenate.

and introduces multiple C@C bonds, interfering with the charge
transfer from the original histidine ligand to the gold atom. More-
over, the shoulder peak at ~500 nm is attributed to the formation
of C@N bonds in Schiff bases [41]. Therefore, the change in
UV–vis absorption and ﬂuorescence spectra indicates the success-
ful functionalization of CA.
The FT-IR spectra of Au NCs and CA-Au NCs are shown in Fig-
ure S3. For the His-stabilized Au NCs, the peak at ca. 3429 cm1
belongs to the stretching vibration of OAH, and the two character-
istic peaks appearing at ca. 3009 and ca. 2881 cm1 are attributed
to the stretching vibration of NAH [35]. The peak at ca. 1635 cm1
belongs to the stretching vibration of C@O. The abovementioned
peaks indicate the existence of amino and carboxyl groups on the
surface of the Au NCs. The presence of amino groups provides reac-
tion sites for the modiﬁcation of CA on the Au NCs. In the spectrum
of CA-Au NCs, the peaks at ca. 3009 and ca. 2881 cm1 disappear,
and the peaks at 1601 and 1562 cm1 are attributed to the stretch-
ing vibrations of C@O and C@N, respectively [41], demonstrating
the formation of Schiff bases induced by an aldimine condensation
reaction between the aldehyde groups of CA and the amino groups
of Au NCs.
There are various thiol species in different bacteria, which are
derived from cysteine (Cys) in most cases, such as glutathione
(GSH), bacillithiol (BSH), mycothiol (MSH), and coenzyme-A
(CoASH) [32]. Here, the interaction between CA-Au NCs and thiols
was investigated using GSH as a thiol model. Fig. 2c illustrates the
ﬂuorescence change of CA-Au NCs in the presence of GSH. The ﬂu-
orescence intensity of CA-Au NCs increased gradually as the con-
centration of GSH increased from 0 to 0.8 mM. As the AuS bond
is more stable than the AuN bond [33,34], the histidine-
cinnamaldehyde (His-CA) on the surface of CA-Au NCs was
replaced by the added GSH, and the enhanced ligand exchange
with increased GSH thus contributes to the increasing ﬂuorescence
of CA-Au NCs. To verify this, XPS spectra of CA-Au NCs before/after
the presence of GSH were recorded. As demonstrated in Fig. 2d, the
N 1 s peak of the CA-Au NCs is centered at 399.4 eV, primarily attri-
butable to the amine nitrogen in the imidazole ring (=CNH) and
imine nitrogen (C@N). Fig. 2d shows the N 1 s peak of Au NCs
appearing at a higher position of 400.4 eV, ascribed to the presence
of the amine nitrogen of the –NH2 group. This suggests that the
functionalization of CA on Au NCs greatly reduces the number of
amino groups on CA-Au NCs. After the addition of GSH, distinct
twin peaks for CA-Au NCs are observed at 398.9 and 400.1 eV, indi-
cating the successful binding of GSH and partial desorption of His-
CA. The ligand exchange is further supported by the appearance of
the S 2p peak at 162.8 eV upon the addition of GSH, which indi-
cates that GSH was bound to the surface of the CA-Au NCs through
the formation of Au–S bonds (Fig. 2e). Moreover, the Au 4f peak
became much sharper after GSH addition (Fig. 2f), which further
corroborates the above results.

3.3. Antibacterial mechanism
To elucidate the antibacterial mechanism of CA-Au NCs, a series
of studies were conducted, including Hoechst 33342/PI staining,
membrane potential measurement, ROS and thiol detection, and
SEM/TEM observation using MSSA as the model bacteria. Log-
phase MSSA was ﬁrst treated with the same concentration of Au
NCs and CA-Au NCs (30 lM based on Au atoms) for 2 h. The cell-
permeant nuclear dye Hoechst 33342 was employed to stain all
bacterial cells, both alive and dead. The DNA-binding ﬂuorescent
dye PI was utilized to monitor cell membrane damage, as it can
penetrate cells with damaged membranes to bind with DNA
[3,42]. Fig. 4 shows the Hoechst 33342/PI staining results in which
the blue and red ﬂuorescence illustrate the total bacterial popula-
tion and bacteria with damaged membranes, respectively. The bac-
teria in the control group showed scarcely any membrane damage,
and slight membrane damage occurred in the Au NC-treated group.
In contrast, the bacterial cells treated with CA-Au NCs show signif-
icant membrane damage, suggesting that CA-functionalized Au
NCs are more liable to damage bacterial cell membranes, which
was attributed to the synergistic effect of CA and Au NCs.
To further corroborate the above observations, the morpholog-
ical changes of MSSA were recorded via SEM and TEM. As shown in
Fig. 5, the original MSSA is the typical spherical shape with smooth
and intact cell membranes, while the cell membrane integrity suf-
fered varying degrees of destruction after Au NC and CA-Au NC
treatment. SEM images revealed that the morphology of MSSA
after Au NC treatment became wrinkled and that some bacterial
cells were ruptured. More severe disruption of membrane integrity
was observed for the CA-Au NC-treated group in which more bac-
terial cells ruptured and a large amount of viscous intracellular
contents leaked out, resulting in the fusion of cells into one whole
piece (Fig. 5a). As seen in the TEM images (Fig. 5b), the outline of
MSSA treated with Au NCs became blurred, and the cell wall was
destroyed. However, MSSA treated with CA-Au NCs presented a
more serious state in which the remaining bacterial debris was
observed, and its surface was riddled with holes. In addition, the
inner membrane potential of MSSA treated with Au NCs/CA-Au
NCs was also measured using the membrane potential-sensitive
dye DiSC3(5). DiSC3(5) is self-quenched inside the phospholipid
bimolecular layer and will be released into the solution to induce
ﬂuorescence enhancement when the membrane is depolarized,
and the ﬂuorescence intensity is proportional to the degree of
membrane potential dissipation [43]. Unsurprisingly, CA-Au NC

In brief, compared to Au NCs, CA-Au NCs exhibit a more signiﬁcant
inﬂuence on bacterial cell membrane properties in terms of mem-

Bhatia et al. reported that AgAu NPs could increase oxidative
stress and attack the bacterial cell membrane, proteins, and DNA
by producing ROS, thus killing bacteria [44]. To further elucidate
the biochemical processes inside bacteria induced by CA-Au NCs,
the ROS and thiol levels of MSSA treated with CA-Au NCs were mea-

ligand exchange. The accelerated production of ROS accompanied
by the depletion of thiols offers ampliﬁed oxidative stress in bacte-
rial cells and then metabolic disorders, thereby effectively inducing
the death of bacterial cells. Multipath antibacterial mechanisms
suggest that CA-Au NCs have the potential to serve as novel antibac-
terial drugs to tackle increasing bacterial resistance.
3.4. Biosafety evaluation
Biocompatibility is essential for the practical application of
antibacterial drugs; therefore, MTT assays and hemolysis tests were
performed to evaluate the cytotoxicity of the Au NCs/CA-Au NCs. As
shown in Fig. 6a, b, and c, the Au NCs/CA-Au NCs showed little or
almost no toxicity to HeLa cells/MCF-7 cells/HaCaT cells at concen-
trations ranging from 2.5 to 80 lM, and a cell viability higher than
88% was obtained. Furthermore, hemolysis tests indicate that Au
NCs/CA-Au NCs do not cause obvious damage to human red blood
cells, and the hemolysis rate is lower than the internationally recog-
nized standard (5%) [45] even if the concentration reaches 160 lM
(Fig. 6d). Therefore, Au NCs/CA-Au NCs might be practical in
blood-contact applications, such as wound dressings. In summary,
CA-Au NCs exhibit excellent biocompatibility with negligible cyto-
toxicity to HeLa cells, MCF-7 cells, HaCaT cells, and human red blood
cells, suggesting that the proposed strategy is useful for developing
antibacterial applications in clinical practice.

4. Conclusions
We constructed an oxidative stress ampliﬁer (CA-Au NCs) to
ﬁght against multidrug-resistant bacterial infection by targeting
the bacterial redox balance with elevated reactive oxygen species
(ROS) levels and concurrently depleted thiol levels in bacteria.
CA-Au NCs show multiple antibacterial mechanisms, including dis-
ruption of membrane integrity, increased membrane permeability,
membrane potential dissipation, and ampliﬁed oxidative stress.
The antibacterial property of CA-Au NCs was signiﬁcantly better
than that of the original Au NCs, and the complete killing of MRSA
and MSSA can be easily achieved at low antibacterial reagent
levels. The successful removal of the MRSA bioﬁlm and the treat-
ment of the pig skin wound infection model indicate that CA-Au
NCs have the potential to ﬁght against multidrug-resistant bacte-
rial infection. In this work, a gold nanocluster surface ligand
exchange strategy was ﬁrst developed to construct an oxidative
stress ampliﬁer (CA-Au NCs) for combating multidrug-resistant
bacterial infection. Compared to the previously reported gold clus-
ters for bacteria killing only by increasing ROS levels [2,12], in this
method, the consumption of thiol levels in bacteria and the release
of the histidine-cinnamaldehyde (His-CA) ligand on the surface of
CA-Au NCs due to the occurrence of the ligand exchange reaction
also greatly contribute to the antimicrobial effect. This proposed
strategy cleverly combines the properties of herbal extracts (CA)
and the properties of His-stabilized Au NCs, including weak Au-N
interactions and ultrasmall sizes, which further expands the appli-
cation of gold nanoclusters in the antibacterial ﬁeld and provides
new ideas for combating multidrug-resistant bacterial infections.

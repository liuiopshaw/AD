Mitochondrial dysfunction elevates cellular NADH/NAD + ratios, inducing 
reductive stress that impairs biosynthesis and cell viability. However, the 
mechanisms by which cells buffer excess NADH to maintain redox homeo-
stasis remain unclear. In this study, we identify a mitochondrial-cytosolic 
metabolic circuitry involving pyruvate carboxylase (PC), malate dehydroge-
nases (MDH1/2), and malic enzyme 1 (ME1) that mitigates NADH overload 
by coupling anaplerotic flux with NADH oxidation. Under reductive stress 
induced by electron transport chain (ETC) dysfunction, oxaloacetate 
derived from PC is converted to malate by MDH1/2, which is then oxidized 
by ME1, transforming both cytosolic and mitochondrial NADH into cytosolic 
NADPH. Although cellular NADPH is typically associated with antioxidant 
defense and biosynthesis, our experiments show that TPNOX, which can ox-
idase NADPH to NADP + , restores proliferation under ETC inhibition, and this 
rescue is entirely dependent on the mitochondrial-cytosolic metabolic cir-
cuitry. This finding highlights that the primary function of the circuit is to 
maintain NADH homeostasis rather than to generate NADPH. Notably, 
glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) enhances ME1 activity inde-
pendently of its catalytic function by acting as a scaffold, thereby prevent-
ing net NADPH production while facilitating the conversion of NADH to 
NADPH. Disruption of the PC-MDH1/2-ME1 pathway through PC inhibition 
sensitizes cells to complex I inhibitors and/or glutaminase blockade, syner-
gistically suppressing tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo. These findings 
uncover a redox-buffering strategy that redirects NADH into NADPH produc-
tion, revealing a metabolic vulnerability in redox-adapted cancer cells.
INTRODUCTION
Redox balance is a fundamental determinant of metabolic homeostasis, with 
NADH/NAD + ratios critically regulating glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) 
cycle, and biosynthesis. 1–5 Under normal physiological conditions, mitochon-
drial respiration efficiently oxidizes NADH to regenerate NAD + , sustaining meta-
bolic reactions and ATP production. 1,6 However, when electron transport chain 
(ETC) activity is compromised by hypoxia, mitochondrial dysfunction, onco-
genic transformation, or pharmacological inhibition, NADH accumulates in 
both the cytosol and mitochondria, leading to reductive stress that threatens 
cell viability by disrupting central carbon metabolism, redox signaling, and 
anabolic capacity. 1–3,7
To mitigate this threat, cells engage multiple adaptive responses to restrain 
NADH production and facilitate its clearance. 1,3 Metabolic reprogramming un-
der reductive stress involves suppressing NADH-generating pathways, 
including downregulation of the TCA cycle, and rerouting glucose metabolism 
toward lactate fermentation, which not only regenerates NAD + independently 
of mitochondria but also sustains ATP production without exacerbating redox 
imbalance. 1,8 More recently, reductive stress has been shown to trigger glycerol 
biosynthesis and secretion, a cytosolic pathway that consumes NADH, albeit at 
the cost of carbon substrates and ATP. 9 Additionally, cells activate glutamine-
dependent lipid biosynthesis as a compensatory electron sink under reductive 
stress, but this pathway primarily consumes NADPH rather than NADH, 3,10–12 
highlighting the compartmentalized redox challenge imposed by ETC 
dysfunction.
The inability to effectively dissipate excess NADH contributes to pathologies 
characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction and redox imbalance. In cancer, 
metabolic rewiring and hypoxic microenvironments often result in elevated
NADH levels, promoting reductive stress that fuels genomic instability and treat-
ment resistance. 3,13 Similarly, ischemic injury, acute alcoholic liver injury, fatty 
liver disease, mitochondrial myopathy, and neurodegenerative diseases involve 
impaired mitochondrial respiration and NADH accumulation, underscoring the 
clinical relevance of redox homeostasis in diverse disease contexts. 7,14–18 
Efficient conversion of NADH into NADPH would simultaneously relieve 
reductive pressure and bolster antioxidant and biosynthetic capacity. While 
the mitochondrial nicotinamide nucleotide transhydrogenase (NNT) catalyzes 
NADH-to-NADPH conversion, its activity is membrane-potential dependent 
and restricted to the mitochondrial matrix, limiting its utility under ETC inhibition 
and offering no cytosolic redox relief. 19 Consequently, a gap remains in our un-
derstanding of how cells coordinate NADH-to-NADPH conversion across sub-
cellular compartments, especially within the cytosol where biosynthesis and 
reductive stress intersect.
Here, we identify a mitochondrial-cytosolic metabolic circuitry involving pyru-
vate carboxylase (PC), malate dehydrogenases (MDH1/2), and malic enzyme 1 
(ME1) that enables cells to dissipate excess NADH by generating NADPH. This 
pathway is activated under ETC inhibition, linking anaplerotic carbon flux to 
cytosolic redox detoxification. We further show that glucose-6-phosphate dehy-
drogenase (G6PD) enhances ME1 activity through a non-enzymatic mecha-
nism. Disruption of this circuit compromises redox balance and sensitizes can-
cer cells to ETC and glutamine metabolism inhibitors, highlighting a 
therapeutically targetable vulnerability in redox-adapted tumors.
MATERIALS AND METHODS 
Cell culture and treatments
HeLa, SK-Hep1, 143B, and HEK293T cells were obtained from the American Type Culture 
Collection (ATCC) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco) sup-
plemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) 
at 37 ◦ C in a humidified 5% CO 2 atmosphere. To minimize the interference of exogenous 
pyruvate on cellular NADH levels, NADH-related experiments were performed using pyru-
vate-free DMEM (Gibco). All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of my-
coplasma contamination, and no commonly misidentified cell lines were used in this study.
Measurement of NADH/NAD + and NADPH/NADP + ratios
Intracellular NADH/NAD + and NADPH/NADP + ratios were measured using NAD/NADH-
Glo and NADP/NADPH-Glo assays (Promega) following the manufacturer’s protocols. 
Briefly, 5 × 10 4 cells were seeded in 24-well plates overnight and incubated with the treat-
ment medium. Cells were washed with PBS and lysed with 200 μL of ice-cold PBS/bicar-
bonate buffer (1:1) with 1% Dodecyl Trimethyl Ammonium Bromide (DTAB). For the acid 
treatment, 50 μL of the cell lysate was mixed with 25 μL of 0.4 N HCl and incubated at 
60 ◦ C for 15 min. For the base treatment, the same volume of lysate was incubated at 
60 ◦ C for 15 min. Neutralization was achieved by adding 25 μL of 0.5 M Trizma base 
(acid treated) or 50 μL of HCl/Trizma solution (base treated). Neutralized samples 
(10 μL) were transferred to 384-well white plates and mixed with 10 μL of freshly prepared 
detection reagent. After 30 min of gentle shaking at room temperature, luminescence was 
measured using a Synergy H1 Hybrid Multi-Mode reader (BioTek).
Cell viability assays
Cells were seeded in triplicate into 24-well plates (5 × 10 4 or 1 × 10 4 cells per well). The 
next day, cells were treated with indicated compounds for the specified duration. Then cells 
were trypsinized and the viable cells were counted using Muse Cell Analyzer (Luminex 
Corporation).

Tumor xenografts in nude mice
All of the xenograft experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use 
Committee (IACUC) at Capital Medical University in accordance with the principles and pro-
cedures outlined in the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Labora-
tory Animals. Female nude mice (4–5 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River 
Laboratory Animal Technology. The mice were housed in a pathogen-free animal barrier 
facility. The housing conditions were maintained at a temperature of 19 ◦ C–23 ◦ C, humidity 
of 40%–70% and a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. The maximal tumor burden permitted by the 
ethics committee was less than 10% of the mouse’s body weight, and this limit was not 
exceeded. A total of 5 × 10 6 HeLa cells or 8 × 10 6 SK-Hep1 cells were injected subcutane-
ously into mice. Once the tumor reached 100–200 mm 3 , the mice were assigned randomly 
into different groups. Metformin was dissolved in PBS and administered by oral gavage at 
100 mg kg − 1 daily or every other day. ZY444 and CB839 were dissolved in 10% dimethyl 
sulfoxide and 90% corn oil; ZY444 was administered by intraperitoneal injection at 
2.5 mg kg − 1 daily or every other day, while CB839 was administered orally at 150 mg 
kg − 1 on the same schedule. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume 
(mm 3 ) = length (mm) × [width (mm)] 2 × ½.
Quantification and statistical analysis
The results of all the experiments were generated from at least three independent rep-
licates unless noted otherwise in the figure legend. All results are presented as mean ± 
standard deviation (SD). Plots and statistical analysis were performed using Prism 10 soft-
ware (GraphPad). Statistical significance was calculated as appropriate using a two-tailed 
Student’s t test or two-way ANOVA as indicated in the figure legends.
RESULTS
G6PD promotes ME1 activity independently of its enzymatic activity 
Cytosolic NADPH is primarily generated by G6PD, ME1, and IDH1. 20 To 
assess their roles in redox regulation, we knocked down each enzyme in 
HeLa cells using small interfering RNAs (siRNAs). Silencing G6PD or ME1 signif-
icantly impaired cell proliferation, whereas IDH1 knockdown had no effect 
(Figures S1A–S1C). Notably, G6PD-depleted cells exhibited marked sensitivity 
to mitochondrial ETC inhibition by antimycin A and phenformin (Figure S1D), 
a phenotype not observed with ME1 or IDH1 knockdown (Figures S1E and S1F). 
To further validate these findings, we generated CRISPR knockout (KO) lines 
of each enzyme. G6PD KO phenocopied the siRNA results, with impaired growth 
and enhanced susceptibility to ETC inhibition, both of which were rescued by re-
expression of wild-type G6PD (Figures 1A–1C). In contrast, ME1 KO suppressed 
proliferation but did not affect ETC inhibition sensitivity (Figures S1G and S1H), 
and IDH1 KO had no detectable effects on either parameter (Figures S1I 
and S1J).

redox ratios. G6PD loss reduced the NADPH/NADP + ratio and concurrently

We previously demonstrated that both wild-type G6PD and naturally occur-
ring G6PD mutants found in patients with G6PD deficiency are capable of 
rescuing cell proliferation and redox balance in G6PD-deficient cells. 21 In this 
study, we employed a catalytically inactive G6PD-R198P mutant 21 and found 
that both the wild-type and R198P variants were able to restore the NADPH/ 
NADP + and NADH/NAD + ratios (Figures 1A–1D), further supporting a non-enzy-
matic role for G6PD in maintaining redox homeostasis.
Considering that inactive G6PD maintained NADPH/NADP + homeostasis, we 
next tested whether G6PD regulates ME1 or IDH1 activity. G6PD KO reduced 
ME1 enzymatic activity without affecting IDH1 (Figures 1E and S1M). Strikingly, 
both wild-type G6PD and R198P restored ME1 activity, and overexpression of 
either protein in 293T cells enhanced ME1 activity (Figures 1E and 1F), with 
no effect on IDH1 (Figure S1N).
In vitro assays using purified proteins confirmed that both G6PD and R198P 
directly enhances ME1 activity (Figures 1G and 1H). Co-immunoprecipitation 
experiments further demonstrated that G6PD interacts with ME1 at both the 
endogenous and exogenously expressed levels (Figures 1I–1K). Moreover, 
pull-down assays using purified His-tagged G6PD or G6PD-R198P and FLAG-
tagged ME1 proteins revealed a direct physical interaction between G6PD 
and ME1 (Figure 1L). To further define the interaction interface between 
G6PD and ME1, we performed truncation mutant analyses based on the known 
structural domains of ME1 and G6PD (Figure S1O). Our results show that ME1 
interacts only with full-length G6PD, as none of the G6PD truncation mutants 
retained binding capacity (Figure S1P), indicating that the structural integrity 
of G6PD is essential for its interaction with ME1.Truncation analysis of ME1 re-
vealed that G6PD binds to the N-terminal region of ME1. Specifically, interaction 
with G6PD was observed in the ME1 truncation containing amino acids 1–271, 
whereas deletion of the first 121 amino acids completely abolished the interac-
tion (Figure S1P). These findings collectively demonstrate that G6PD promotes 
ME1 activity through direct interaction, independently of its dehydrogenase 
activity.
ME1 maintains NADH/NAD + balance via NADPH-generating metabolism 
Our previous findings revealed that G6PD exerts multiple functions indepen-
dent of its enzymatic activity, broadly regulating metabolic homeostasis. 21 
Consequently, G6PD-KO cells are highly susceptible to NADH accumulation-
induced reductive stress under various stress conditions. 21 Here, we challenged 
G6PD-KO HeLa cells with ETC inhibitors, antimycin A, or phenformin and found 
that treatment with α-ketobutyrate, 23 a substrate that can be reduced by lactate 
dehydrogenase (LDH) and thereby lowers the NADH/NAD + ratio, significantly 
improved cell viability (Figures S2A–S2C). To further modulate the NADH/ 
NAD + ratio, we employed a doxycycline-inducible system to express LbNOX, 2 
an engineered enzyme that transfers electrons from NADH to oxygen, gener-
ating NAD + and water (Figures 2A and 2B). LbNOX expression also markedly 
enhanced the survival of G6PD-deficient cells under ETC inhibition 
(Figure 2C). This makes the G6PD-deficient cell line a suitable model for inves-
tigating the regulation of reductive stress.
Given that G6PD regulates ME1 activity and ME1 deletion leads to elevated 
NADH/NAD + ratios (Figures 1E–1H and S1K), we next investigated the role of 
ME1 in the cellular response to reductive stress. We ectopically expressed 
ME1 or IDH1 in G6PD-KO HeLa cells, with G6PD re-expression serving as a con-
trol (Figure 2D). Similar to G6PD, ME1 overexpression significantly improved cell 
viability under ETC inhibition (Figure 2E) and mitigated the increase in the 
NADH/NAD + ratio and the decline in the NADPH/NADP + ratio (Figure 2F). In 
contrast, IDH1 overexpression had minimal effects on these parameters 
(Figures 2D–2F). Consistent results were observed in another cell line, SK-
Hep1, where ME1 overexpression rescued cell viability, reduced the NADH/ 
NAD + ratio, and restored the NADPH/NADP + ratio in G6PD-deficient cells under 
ETC inhibition (Figures S2D–S2F). We also examined whether other malic en-
zymes or NADPH-generating enzymes, including ME2, ME3, and IDH2, could 
affect the proliferation of G6PD-KO cells under ETC inhibition. Our results 
showed that overexpression of none of these enzymes restored proliferative ca-
pacity (Figures S2G–S2H), indicating that they are unable to functionally substi-
tute for ME1 in this context.
To dissect whether ME1-mediated rescue depends on restoring NADH/NAD + 
or NADPH/NADP + balance, we expressed doxycycline (Dox)-inducible TPNOX 
(Figure 2G), a synthetic oxidase that transfers electrons from NADPH to oxy-
gen. 24 Interestingly, TPNOX often led to a reduction in the NADH/NAD + ratio 
without significantly affecting the NADPH/NADP + ratio, likely due to an as-yet-
undefined metabolic pathway that converts NADH into NADPH. 24 Consistently, 
TPNOX expression in HeLa/G6PD-KO cells did not significantly reduce NADPH/ 
NADP + ratio (Figure S2I) but attenuated ETC-inhibition-induced NADH/NAD + 
elevation (Figure 2H), as confirmed by the genetically encoded cytosolic 
NADH/NAD + biosensor SoNar 25 (Figure 2I). Importantly, TPNOX significantly 
enhanced the survival of G6PD-KO cells under ETC inhibition (Figure 2J). A 
similar outcome was achieved with EcSTH-LbNOX, another NADPH oxidase, 15 
which not only lowered the NADPH/NADP + ratio but also decreased the NADH/ 
NAD + ratio under ETC inhibition (Figures S2J–S2L).
Strikingly, siRNA-mediated depletion of ME1 abrogated the ability of TPNOX 
to rescue both the elevated NADH/NAD + ratio and the impaired viability of 
G6PD-deficient HeLa or SK-Hep1 cells under ETC inhibition (Figures 2K–2M 
and S3A–S3C). De novo fatty acid synthesis requires a substantial amount of 
NADPH. By employing the inhibitor ND646 to restrict NADPH consumption in 
cells, we observed a significant reduction in the rescue effect of ME1 on the 
impaired viability of G6PD-deficient HeLa and SK-Hep1 cells under ETC inhibi-
tion (Figures 2N and 2O). These findings suggest that ME1 contributes to redox 
homeostasis not merely by simply providing cellular NADPH but through a 
metabolic mechanism that couples NADPH production to NADH oxidation. 
Thus, ME1 plays a key role in alleviating NADH reductive stress by functionally 
linking NADPH generation to the maintenance of NADH/NAD + balance.
ME1 cooperates with MDH1/2 to mitigate NADH reductive stress
Malate, the substrate for cytosolic ME1, is generated by mitochondrial MDH2 
and cytosolic MDH1 (Figure 3A). To determine whether ME1 activity relies on 
upstream malate supply, we silenced MDH1 in G6PD-KO HeLa cells. Knock-
down of MDH1 markedly attenuated the TPNOX-mediated decrease in the 
NADH/NAD + ratio under ETC inhibition, as detected by the SoNar biosensor 
(Figures 3B and 3C). MDH1 depletion also impaired TPNOX-mediated rescue 
of cell proliferation under these conditions (Figure 3D). Interestingly, similar ef-
fects were observed upon knockdown of MDH2, suggesting that both cytosolic 
and mitochondrial malate dehydrogenases are required for the redox-buffering 
function of ME1 (Figures 3E–3G). These findings were corroborated in SK-Hep1 
G6PD-KO cells, where knockdown of either MDH1 or MDH2 similarly impaired 
TPNOX function (Figures S3D–S3I).
To evaluate the role of this pathway under basal conditions, we knocked 
down ME1, MDH1, or MDH2 in wild-type HeLa, SK-Hep1, and 143B cells. All 
three perturbations resulted in elevated SoNar signals, indicating increased 
NADH/NAD + ratios and underlying a conserved role in redox homeostasis 
(Figures 3H and S4A–S4F). We further found that TPNOX conferred protection 
against ETC inhibition in wild-type HeLa and SK-Hep1 cells. However, this pro-
tective effect was significantly blunted by knockdown of ME1, MDH1, or MDH2 
(Figures 3I, 3J, and S5A–S5D), indicating that TPNOX function relies on an 
intact MDH1/2-ME1 metabolic axis.
Together, these results demonstrate that the oxidation of NADPH by TPNOX 
creates a metabolic “pull” that increases ME1-mediated malate consumption. 
This elevated ME1 flux stimulates MDH1/2 activity to regenerate malate, lead-
ing indirectly to NADH oxidation. ME1 and its upstream malate-generating en-
zymes, MDH1 and MDH2, form a functional metabolic pathway that is essential 
for buffering cellular NADH, maintaining NADH/NAD + homeostasis, and miti-
gating reductive stress.

PC supports cytosolic and mitochondrial NADH redox homeostasis
To evaluate the role of PC in redox regulation, we overexpressed PC in G6PD-
KO HeLa cells. Similar to G6PD expression, PC expression robustly rescued cell 
viability under various stress conditions, including ETC inhibition (Figures 5A, 
5B, and S7A). PC overexpression also attenuated the increase in NADH/NAD + 
ratio caused by ETC inhibition, but its effect on NADPH/NADP + levels was min-
imal (Figure 5C). Conversely, silencing PC impaired the ability of TPNOX to 
rescue G6PD-KO HeLa and SK-Hep1 cells under ETC inhibition (Figures 5D,
5E, S7B, and S7C). This requirement for PC was similarly observed in wild-
type HeLa and SK-Hep1 cells (Figures 5F, 5G, and S7D), underscoring the impor-
tance of PC in countering reductive stress.
PC depletion by siRNA in wild-type HeLa cells led to a significant increase in 
the NADH/NAD + ratio and elevated SoNar signal (Figures 5H, 5I, and S7E), 
consistent with an induction of reductive stress. Similar effects were observed 
in SK-Hep1 and 143B cells, where PC knockdown increased NADH/NAD + ratio 
and SoNar signal but did not alter the NADPH/NADP + ratio (Figures S7F–S7H). 
Pharmacological inhibition of PC using ZY444 26 also dose-dependently 
increased NADH/NAD + ratios without affecting NADPH/NADP + in HeLa and 
SK-Hep1 cells (Figures 5J and S7I), further confirming PC’s role in buffering 
excess NADH.

DISCUSSION
Impairment of the ETC leads to NADH accumulation in both cytosol and mito-
chondria, causing reductive stress that disrupts glycolysis, biosynthesis, and 
antioxidant defense. 2,3,31 We identify a dual-branched, PC-driven mechanism 
that channels excess NADH into NADPH production via malate metabolism, 
coupling mitochondrial anaplerosis to cytosolic redox detoxification 
(Figure 7). PC converts pyruvate to OAA in mitochondria, after which OAA exits 
through two routes. In one branch, MDH2 reduces OAA to malate using mito-
chondrial NADH; malate is exported and oxidized by ME1 to generate 
NADPH, converting mitochondrial NADH equivalents into cytosolic NADPH. In 
the other, GOT2 transaminates OAA to aspartate for export; cytosolic GOT1 re-
generates OAA, MDH1 reduces it to malate using cytosolic NADH, and ME1 ox-
idizes malate to produce NADPH. Together, these pathways allow both mito-
chondrial and cytosolic NADH pools to fuel ME1, increasing NADPH when 
reductive stress is most severe. The resulting NADPH supports antioxidant 
and anabolic reactions, thereby relieving NADH burden.
This dual architecture provides two advantages. The MDH2-malate-ME1 axis 
directly consumes mitochondrial NADH, preserving NAD + to sustain residual 
oxidative phosphorylation and dehydrogenase activity. 32 The GOT2-GOT1/ 
MDH1-malate-ME1 shuttle removes excess cytosolic NADH generated by 
ETC blockade and converts it into NADPH to maintain antioxidant and biosyn-
thetic capacity. In both cases, NADH is redirected into adaptive reducing power 
that supports survival.
We further show that G6PD enhances ME1 activity through a non-catalytic 
scaffolding function (Figure 1). By promoting efficient malate oxidation, G6PD 
sustains continuous NADH-to-NADPH flux. Loss of this interaction impairs acti-
vation of the PC-MDH1/2-ME1 pathway and renders cells susceptible to reduc-
tive collapse.
Although PC has been implicated in ME1-dependent NADPH production dur-
ing oxidative stress, 27,32 our findings redefine the PC-ME1 axis as an NADH 
disposal system functioning as a metabolic transhydrogenation-like cycle 
that converts mitochondrial and cytosolic NADH into NADPH. This circuit is 
driven by the need to maintain NADH/NAD + homeostasis under reductive 
stress, with NADPH consumption—physiological (biosynthesis and antioxidant 
defense) or experimental (eg, TPNOX-mediated oxidation)—providing the pull 
that sustains flux (Figure 7). Thus, its dominant role is redox balancing rather 
than net NADPH accumulation. G6PD optimizes this cycle by scaffolding 
ME1 into a high-activity complex while suppressing its own NADPH-producing 
activity, ensuring efficient malate oxidation and uninterrupted NADH clearance. 
Disruption of this regulation markedly compromises the PC-MDH1/2-ME1 axis. 
Therapeutically, this dual PC pathway represents a metabolic vulnerability. 
PC inhibition blocks both malate export routes and sensitizes cancer cells to 
complex I and glutamine metabolism inhibitors in vitro and in vivo (Figure 6).

Because many cancers exhibit elevated NADH/NAD + ratios, they may depend 
on the PC-ME1 network for redox homeostasis. Targeting this axis may there-
fore impair NADH clearance and antioxidant capacity, undermining tumor 
viability.
In summary, we define a two-branched, PC-dependent malate shuttle that 
converts NADH into NADPH, linking mitochondrial anaplerosis with cytosolic 
redox homeostasis. This mechanism supports survival under respiratory stress 
and represents a tractable target for combination cancer therapies. Future 
studies should determine how this circuit is regulated across tissues and stress 
contexts and how it integrates with other redox-buffering systems.

SUMMARY
Despite extensive efforts dedicated to developing therapies for Alzheimer’s disease (AD), we still lack effec-
tive approaches capable of curing or halting its progression. Mounting evidence highlights the critical roles of
dysfunctional microglia, which promote the abnormal accumulation of harmful protein deposits and initiate
aggressive neuroinflammation, contributing to both the onset and advancement of AD. Hereby, we develop a
brain-targeted ceria nanoparticle (T-CeNP) that exhibits both superoxide dismutase and catalase mimicking
activities for AD therapy. The functionalization of advanced glycation end products (RAGE)-targeting peptide
facilitates the blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration of T-CeNP, prevents the formation of β-amyloid (Aβ) fi-
brils, and boosts Aβ degradation by microglia. By integrating the multifunctional RAGE-targeting ligand
and bioactive CeNPs, T-CeNP can effectively cross the BBB, quench elevated reactive oxygen species,
attenuate microglial activation, disrupt Aβ aggregation, and promote its clearance in an AD mouse model,
thereby slowing the progression of AD.
INTRODUCTION
For decades, Alzheimer’s disease (AD) research has centered on
two potential key neuropathological hallmarks: the accumulation
of β-amyloid (Aβ) as plaques and the development of tau protein-
induced neurofibrillary tangles in the brain, as outlined by the Aβ
cascade hypothesis and the tau hypothesis. However, recent
limited benefits in delaying AD progression by targeting Aβ and
tau proteins in clinical studies have challenged the traditional
belief, highlighting that the presence of Aβ accumulation or tau
pathology alone may not be enough to cause dementia.1,2
Ongoing studies suggest that abnormal protein deposition
and folding are considered pathological features rather than
the pathogenesis of AD, while taking full consideration of
their detrimental and aggravating effects on AD progression.

The disrupted brain environment in Alzheimer’s disease (AD)—marked by chronic
inflammation and the buildup of abnormal protein deposits—is increasingly recognized as a central feature
of the disorder. Growing evidence from both laboratory and clinical studies suggests that dysfunctional mi-
croglia, the brain’s resident immune cells, are key drivers of disease progression, rather than passive re-
sponders to initial damage. Herein, we developed nanoengineered ceria nanoclusters (T-CeNP) interacting
with microglia and pioneered their applications in mitigating the progression of dementia. T-CeNP is a hybrid
of multifunctional enzyme CeNPs with ultra-small particle size and the advanced glycation end products
(RAGE)-targeted peptide. These two essential components act both independently and synergistically,
providing substantial penetration of the blood-brain barrier, rapid and sustained relief from neuroinflamma-
tion, reduction of β-amyloid (Aβ) fibril burden, and modulation of activated microglia, thereby mitigating or
halting the progression of AD.

constitutes another crucial factor in the progression of AD.3–5
Microglia, the resident macrophages of the brain, continually
and dynamically monitor for pathogens and cell debris while
providing maintenance for the brain tissue.6,7 In the early stages
of AD, activated microglia recognize and bind to Aβ fibrils through
their cell-surface receptors, subsequently engulfing them through
phagocytosis. However, as AD progresses, the phagocytic abili-
ties of microglia become altered and impaired. In the late stages
of AD, the accumulation of Aβ fibrils overwhelms the microglias’
capacity to clear them, resulting in hyper-activation among micro-
glial cells. This hyper-activation promotes inflammatory re-
sponses, leading to the production of neurotoxic reactive oxygen
species (ROS) and pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor ne-
crosis factor alpha (TNF-α), a phenomenon termed neuroinflam-
mation. Apart from phagocytosis and degradation of Aβ, microglia
meticulously orchestrate the interaction with Aβ plaque, which
profoundly manipulates the accumulation of toxic Aβ and the
development of AD. In the early stages of Aβ plaque formation,
characterized by small plaques, microglia actively gather and pro-
liferate around the amyloid plaques, displaying the typical
morphology of activated microglia. These surrounding microglia
form a protective barrier and perform various functions on plaque
composition and toxicity. These functions include but are not
limited to limiting the outward expansion of plaques, promoting
the physical compaction of Aβ into less toxic spherical plaques,
and preventing the continuous accumulation and growth of new
Aβ onto existing plaques. Ultimately, these actions help decrease
the
interaction
of
Aβ
with
nearby
vulnerable
neurons.8,9
Conversely, as AD advances, the reduced and compromised
coverage of microglia around plaques leads to the formation of
enlarged protofibril Aβ hotspots, exacerbating neurotoxicity and
neuronal dystrophy. As a result, dysfunctional microglia contribute
to the abnormal deposition of Aβ, the formation of toxic Aβ pla-
ques, and neuroinflammation, ultimately damaging neurons and
triggering neuronal injury signals that further activate microglial
cells, perpetuating the damaging cycle. The ongoing progression
of this cycle leads to further loss of neurons and exacerbates AD
progression10 along with their coordinated dysregulated neuroin-
flammatory microenvironment, especially the presence of unfa-
vorable Aβ fibrils, which are two critical components in this pro-
cess.
Noticeably,
recent
human
genome-wide
association
studies (GWASs) have identified that the majority of risk genes
for AD are preferentially or exclusively expressed in microglia,11
further highlighting the crucial role of these cells in AD. Therefore,
multifunctional intervention strategies and methodologies are ur-
gently needed to simultaneously reduce Aβ fibrils, attenuate mi-
croglial activation, and neutralize the Aβ fibril-dominated microen-
vironment for the manipulation of AD.
Here, we reported the development of a brain-targeted ceria
nanoparticle (CeNP)-based hybrid nanocluster (T-CeNP) de-
signed for the combination therapy of AD (Figure 1A) by
leveraging the advantages of nanomedicine, including targeted
cargo delivery and intrinsic multifunctional properties.12 The su-
per-small CeNPs, which exhibit both superoxide dismutase
(SOD)- and catalase (CAT)-mimicking activities and scavenge
noxious intracellular ROS with outstanding antioxidant and
anti-inflammatory capacities,13–18 were hybridized into the ma-
trix of poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles. The hy-
bridization would not only preserve the intrinsic properties of
CeNP, which helped attenuate the activation of microglia and
provocative neuroinflammation,19 but also optimize their phar-
macokinetics by increasing solubility, reducing agglomeration,
and extending the circulation time. Additionally, a targeting
ligand (peptide, CKLVFFAED) for the receptor for advanced gly-
cation end products (RAGE) was incorporated onto the surface
of the hybridized nanocluster to create AD brain T-CeNP.
RAGE, a receptor responsible for binding and transporting solu-
ble Aβ across the blood-brain barrier (BBB) into the brain via
transcytosis, was overexpressed in the BBB of AD patients.20–23
This characteristic made RAGE a promising target for shuttling
therapeutic molecules or particles into the brain, and it has
been explored for potential AD treatment.24 Furthermore, the
RAGE-targeting peptide, derived from Aβ, enabled T-CeNP to
recognize intracerebral soluble Aβ and participate in its aggrega-
tion, forming a complex of Aβ plaque and T-CeNP.25,26 This
complex was readily identified and endocytosed by resident
microglia. Apart from digesting the Aβ on the surface of the com-
plex by microglia, the encapsulated CeNPs also quench the
elevated ROS and attenuate the activation of microglia. These
two essential components, CeNPs and the RAGE-targeting pep-
tide of T-CeNP, act independently and synergistically, providing
rapid and sustained relief for neuroinflammation, reduction of Aβ
fibril burden, and modulation of activated microglia. As a result,
serving as a nanohybrid treatment system with a notable capac-
ity for modulating the brain microenvironment, T-CeNP demon-
strates the effectiveness of a multi-module strategy in mitigating
the progression of AD and preserving the learning and cognitive
capabilities of AD animals (Figure 1A).
RESULTS
Fabrication and characterization of CeNP-embedded
PLGA nanoparticles
To overcome the inevitable inter-particle agglomeration of
CeNPs in physiological solutions and extend their biological
application, the usages of CeNPs in vivo were usually embedded
in well-dispersed nanocarriers or coated with a stabilizer.27–29
Herein, we, for the first time, hybridize ultra-small sizes CeNPs
into the matrix of PLGA, a United States Food and Drug Admin-
istration (FDA)-approved biodegradable copolymer for drug de-
livery, to fabricate a nanocluster, T-CeNP. The cerium oxide-
loaded PLGA nanoparticle was prepared using an emulsion
method, as shown in Figure 1A; Scheme S1. Poly(lactide-co-
glycolide)-b-poly(ethylene glycol)-maleimide (PLGA-PEG-Mal)
was mixed with PLGA to introduce functional groups for further
modification. The resulting cerium oxide-loaded PLGA nanopar-
ticles were purified following our published method.30 After that,
the RAGE-targeting ligand, CKLVFFAED peptide, was added to
yield RAGE-targeted CeNP (T-CeNP). The non-targeted CeNP
(N-CeNP) was constructed similarly to T-CeNP except for re-
placing the targeting peptide with 2-mercaptoethanol. The tar-
geted empty nanoparticles (T-PLGA) and non-targeted empty
nanoparticles (N-PLGA) were prepared similarly to T-CeNP and
N-CeNP, respectively, excepting no CeNP was embedded.

RAGE-targeted nanoparticles (T-PLGA and T-CeNP)
penetrate the BBB in vitro
RAGE is known to interact with Aβ and is responsible for the
influx of circulating Aβ into the brain, which detrimentally contrib-
utes to the etiology and pathogenesis of amyloid angiopathy and
neuroinflammation observed in AD. The increased expression of
RAGE in the BBB of AD has been well substantiated and exten-
sively explored as a target for the theragnostic of AD.34–37 Here-
in, we leveraged the CKLVFFAED peptide, a sequence derived
from Aβ with high affinity for RAGE but negligible cytotoxicity,
to functionalize our nanocluster for AD brain targeting.38 To eval-
uate how inflammation affects the expression of RAGE receptors
on the BBB, human cerebral microvascular endothelial cells
(hCMEC/D3) were employed. Immunocytochemistry revealed
that the treatment of BV-2 conditioned medium (CMBV-2) slightly
upregulated RAGE expression in these cells (Figure 2F). In
contrast, the co-culture of conditioned medium collected from
LPS-treated BV-2 cells (CMBV-2
+
LPS) significantly boosted
RAGE expression. To evaluate the effect of RAGE receptor
expression on the cellular uptake of nanoparticles, hCMEC/D3
cells were co-incubated with T-CeNP, N-CeNP, T-PLGA,
and N-PLGA in the regular culture medium, CMBV-2, or
CMBV-2 + LPS, and analyzed by confocal microscopy. Figure 2G
showed stronger red signals in cells treated with T-CeNP than
that of N-CeNP, suggesting the modification of RAGE-targeting
ligand boosted the uptake of T-CeNP in hCMEC/D3 cells. A
similar difference was also observed in the empty PLGA nano-
particles, evidenced by stronger red signals for cells treated
with T-PLGA than those of N-PLGA (Figure 2H) in both condi-
tioned media. More importantly, the co-culture of CMBV-2 + LPS
only induced better nanoparticle uptake for T-PLGA, not
N-PLGA. Combining the results shown in Figures 2F–2H, we
can conclude that, in an inflammatory condition, the cellular up-
take of T-CeNP by hCMEC/D3 cells can be significantly
improved by the upregulated RAGE receptors. Since an inflam-
matory condition was included in the study while T-CeNP has
anti-inflammatory effects, T-CeNP was replaced with T-PLGA
for the BBB-penetrating study to decrease the complexity of
the system. The BBB in vitro model was developed from an
hCMEC/D3 cell-based transwell system. In all cases, the perme-
abilities (Pe) for lucifer yellow were less than the reported
1.33 × 10−3 cm/min, which is an established criterion for an
in vitro BBB monolayer integrity.38 Results shown in Figure 2I re-
vealed that the transcytosis of T-PLGA through a BBB was sub-
stantially higher than that of N-PLGA in a typical culture environ-
ment. Surprisingly, when CMBV-2 + LPS was introduced, the
transcytosis of T-PLGA was enhanced significantly compared
with that in CMBV-2 (Figure 2J) and reached a level similar to
that in a transwell without a BBB monolayer at 24 h, indicating
the excellent BBB-penetrating efficacy of the targeted nanopar-
ticle. With the help of a transwell BBB model, we further validated
that T-CeNP effectively entered the SH-SY5Y cells after pene-
trating the BBB (Figure S7).

and neuronal damage and loss due to the unremitting insult from
chronic inflammatory response and neurotoxic material, such as

brils (Figure 3B) and reduce H2O2-induced ROS (Figure 2B), we
further explored whether the above protective effects could be

that N-CeNP could rescue N2a cells from the damage of H2O2 in a

free CeNP. The decreased red signal in Figure 3G revealed that
T-CeNP could effectively protect N2a cells from the toxicity of
Aβ fibrils. The neuroprotective effect of T-CeNP was further
confirmed by the cell viability assay (Figure 3H).

To investigate the expression of RAGE in AD mice and its capac-
ity to transport nanoparticles (Figure 4A), the brain tissue sec-
tions of 4-months-old 5XFAD (AD transgenic) and C57BL/6
(wild type [WT] normal) mice were analyzed by fluorescent immu-
nohistochemistry. Figure S9 revealed that RAGE expression
(green signals) in the brain of AD mice is much higher than that
in normal ones. To probe whether RAGE is expressed in the
BBB, anti-CD31 antibody was employed to detect the endothe-
lium cells on the brain blood vessel. As expected, the presence
of overlapping green (RAGE) and red (CD31) signals were de-
tected within the brain blood vessels of AD mice, but not in
normal mice (Figure 4B), validating the overexpression of
RAGE in the BBB of AD animals, distinguishing it from the normal
counterparts. These results are consistent with previous find-
ings.44,45 As the influx transporter of circulating Aβ into the brain,
RAGE has been demonstrated to upregulate in the AD brain’s
BBB, microglia, and neurons due to the perturbative microenvi-
ronment. This upregulation detrimentally contributes to Aβ depo-
sition, neuroinflammatory responses, and neuronal damage.45
On the flip side, by fully leveraging RAGE, specific strategies
achieve targeted drug delivery to the AD brain by mimicking
the abnormal transportation of Aβ.35,46
To investigate whether T-CeNP can utilize the AD mouse over-
expressed RAGE penetrating its BBB into the brain (Figure 4A),
Cy5-labeled T-CeNP and N-CeNP were intravenously injected

T-CeNP improves the nesting, memory, and cognitive
skills of AD mice
AD is a chronic, progressive neurodegenerative condition char-
acterized by symptoms that often remain concealed in its early
stages. The presence of mild cognitive impairment symptoms
typically indicates progression to the later stages of AD, where
irreversible damage to the brain has already occurred.47,48 To
investigate the therapeutic effect of T-CeNP for AD, 5XFAD
mice, a typical mouse model of AD, at the age of 4 months old
were employed and randomly divided into three groups (n = 7)
and intravenously injected with saline (control), T-CeNP, and
N-CeNP (1 mg/kg) twice per week for 2 months as shown in
Figure 4E. C57BL/6 mice given saline were used as a normal
group. Considering nesting is a natural behavior of rodents for
heat conservation, reproduction, and shelter, and this behavior
would be progressively deteriorated along with the progress of
AD in mice,49,50 the assessment of nest construction capacity
was performed once per week for the duration of treatment.
Figures 4F and 4G revealed that the nesting skill for the mice in
the control and N-CeNP treatment groups declined dramatically
within 2 months of the study. Surprisingly, as the treatment
continued, the animals in the T-CeNP treatment group showed
progressively improved nesting construction capacity compared
with the control and N-CeNP treatment groups (p < 0.001), indi-
cating a persistent and progressive therapeutic effect of
T-CeNP.
To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of mice after
different treatments, a Morris water maze assay was carried
out to study their spatial learning and memory ability by standard
protocols. The performance of the mice was recorded through
videos and analyzed by EthoVision XT15 software to determine
the path length, latency to reach the platform, and swim speed
for each mouse during the test. Interestingly, after two months
of treatment, the trace (time) of T-CeNP-treated AD mice almost
located in the targeted quadrant (Figure 4H), which was different
from that shown in control and N-CeNP-treated AD mice, while
similar to that shown in the normal group. Furthermore,
T-CeNP-treated AD mice showed a similar learning capacity to
normal mice (Figure 4I), evidenced by the decreased escape la-
tency time during the training session. In contrast, no change
was observed in the control and N-CeNP-treated AD groups.
In addition, the increased number crossing the targeted platform
and time in the targeted quadrant for the T-CeNP-treated AD
mice suggested their improved memory and cognitive ability
compared with that of the control and N-CeNP groups
(Figures 4J and 4K).

T-CeNP reduces Aβ deposition and attenuates the
activation of microglia and astrocytes in the brain of AD
animals
To evaluate the effect of T-CeNP on the Aβ deposition in the AD
brain, thioflavin S was employed to stain the Aβ plaques.
Figures 6A, 6B, S12A, and S12B revealed that the Aβ plaques
in the hippocampus and cortex of AD mice were reduced after
T-CeNP treatment, which was also validated with the reduced
soluble Aβ burden in the brain of T-CeNP-treated AD mice
through ELISA assay (Figure S13). In contrast, there was no
noticeable Aβ burden reduction observed in the N-CeNP-
treated group. It was noted that both increased deposition and
formation of super-neurotoxic Aβ plaques were closely impli-
cated in the delinquent microglia, such as compromised phago-
cytosis ability and low efficiency of microglial consolidation and
compaction. These implications in Aβ plaques spurred our inter-
est in delineating the state of microglia in the brain of different
treatments. Typically, microglia maintain the homeostasis of
the central nervous system (CNS) through phagocytic clearance
of intracerebral anomalous substances. Under AD pathological
conditions, microglia would be gradually activated and con-
verted to DAM, characterized by decreased capacities in engulf-
ment and degradation of aberrant substances and increased
secretion of neuroinflammation.51 Microglia activation was usu-
ally indicated by the upregulation of IBA-1, a commonly used
marker of intracellular protein related to the reorganization of
the
microglia

and
phagocytosis

effectively attenuate the activation of microglia in the hippocam-
pus and cortex regions of AD mice, indicating the attenuated
activation
and
functional

of
microglia.

treatment. As we expected, due to its limited BBB penetration
capacity, N-CeNP treatment did not affect microglia activation.
Moreover, we also investigated whether T-CeNP would ven-
ture into the accommodation of astrocytes, the most abundant
type of glial cells in the brain microenvironment. The activation
of astrocytes was indicated by the increased expression of glial
fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). Interestingly, it was found that
although the treatment of T-CeNP only yielded a limited effect
on decreasing intracerebral expression of GFAP (this limitation
was even prominent in the hippocampus region of AD mice),
T-CeNP could effectively alter the morphology of astrocytes
from hypertrophic to normal status (Figure 6E, 6F, and S15),
which would increase intimate contact between astrocytes and
plaque, limit plaque growth, and attenuate plaque-related
dystrophic neurites.52
T-CeNP protects neurons from death and shows
excellent safety in 5XFAD mouse model
Neuron cells are susceptible to intracerebral Aβ plaques and
neuroinflammation-induced toxicities. The neuropathological
change, including death and loss, is a critical hallmark of AD pa-
tients and accounts for most clinical symptoms. Herein, the
neuron densities in the hippocampus and cortex of different
treatment groups were examined with the help of anti-NeuN anti-
body. Figures 6G and 6H revealed an obvious neuronal hypocel-
lularity in the hippocampus and cortex regions of AD control
mice compared with normal ones. In contrast, the treatment of
T-CeNP significantly attenuated neuron loss and retained their
density in AD mice, which might be attributed to the reduced
Aβ plaque burden and attenuated microglia and astrocytes acti-
vation (Figures 6A–6F).
Considering the accumulation of T-CeNP in other organs
(Figure 4D) and its long-term administration (Figure 4E), the
in vivo chronic toxicities of T-CeNP were further investigated.
No weight loss was observed during the two months of
T-CeNP treatment (Figure S16). In addition, histological analysis
confirmed the biosafety of the T-CeNP treatment, evidenced by
no structural change observed in the major organs, including the
heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney (Figure S17). We further as-
sessed the potential acute toxicity of T-CeNP at 100× the treat-
ment dose and did not observe noticeable systemic toxicity
based on blood biochemical index and body weight (Figures 7
and S18). Thus, T-CeNP is a safe and effective tool for treat-
ing AD.
DISCUSSION
Till now, most treatments for AD are focused on the clearance of
Aβ plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles in the brain. Unfortu-
nately, clinical trials have shown that therapies targeting only
one of these potential causes only modestly slows AD progres-
sion. For example, aducanumab, lecanemab, and donane-
mab—humanized monoclonal antibodies targeting distinct Aβ
epitopes or conformations—have each received regulatory
approval in 2021, 2023, and 2024, respectively. All three drugs
have demonstrated the ability to reduce cerebral amyloid plaque
burden. However, the magnitude of clinical benefit remains
modest, and all three are associated with significant adverse

perplexing outcomes further reified the refractoriness of AD.
While the exact reasons for the limited success of these
drugs remain unclear, several plausible possibilities exist.
First, the BBB presents a significant challenge; many bio-
macromolecule-based drugs, such as antibodies, have limited
ability to penetrate the brain parenchyma and perform their in-
tended functions. Second, targeting the clearance or generation
of abnormal proteins can disrupt metabolic homeostasis, poten-
tially triggering compensatory mechanisms that undermine the
therapeutic effect. Moreover, most drugs tested so far have sin-
gular functions and responsibilities, lacking the ability to address
the complex and multifaceted brain environment effectively.
These factors highlight the complexity and mystery surrounding
AD mechanisms. As it stands, all available treatments for AD are
palliative, primarily focused on managing symptoms rather than
altering the disease’s progression. There is a pressing need for
therapeutics that target alternative pathological mechanisms to
halt or reverse the underlying pathology of AD.
Recent research indicates that microglial overactivation and
dysfunction, leading to disturbances in the brain’s microenviron-
ment, such as chronic neuroinflammation, could play a critical
role in the initiation and progression of AD. Targeting these
mechanisms presents a promising therapeutic strategy. Micro-
glia are integral to interactions within the brain’s microenviron-
ment, and their dysregulation can contribute to several neuro-
pathological hallmarks of AD. These include the deposition of
Aβ aggregates, the propagation of abnormally folded tau protein,
the accumulation of ROS, and the disruption of trophic support
homeostasis. Consequently, many studies have demonstrated
that
targeting
microglia
by
modulating
their
phenotype,
enhancing Aβ phagocytosis, and altering their metabolism can
alleviate cognitive impairment in AD mouse models.55
These nanotechnology-based therapeutics are particularly
notable for their multifunctional capabilities, achieved through
both their inherent physicochemical properties and strategic
design.56,57 Numerous preclinical nanomedicine platforms have
been developed to modulate dysfunctional microglia,58–60 micro-
glia-associated abnormal protein aggregation,26,61 and inflamma-
tory reactions62,63 by delivering therapeutic payloads or serving as
active therapeutic agents themselves. These approaches have
shown promising results in alleviating both pathological and
cognitive symptoms in AD mouse models. For instance, intracere-
bral administration of RMC/pApoE2 lipoplex in APP/PS1 mice
eliminated approximately 86.9% of Aβ plaques in the hippocam-
pus. This intervention also restored synaptic integrity and neuronal
connectivity by modulating the inflammatory microenvironment,
reprogramming aberrant microglial activity, and enhancing Aβ
clearance through both macroautophagy and insulin-degrading
enzyme (IDE)-mediated pathways.60 However, the clinical transla-
tion of such approaches faces substantial barriers, including the
complexity of formulation and the invasiveness of direct intracere-
bral delivery. Additional limitations—such as restricted therapeu-
tic specificity, limited permeability across the BBB, and subopti-
mal intracerebral biodistribution—further constrain the practical
utility of these promising nanomedicines.
To address the above limitations, we have developed a biomi-
metic and multifunctional hybrid nanocomplex composed of a
RAGE-targeting peptide, CeNP seeds, and a PLGA matrix.
This combination aims for a comprehensive, synergistic therapy
for AD. Each component of the nanocomplex has definite and in-
dividual functions, but together, they generate a comprehensive
and synergistic therapy for AD by targeting overactivated micro-
glia and priming the microglia-disrupted microenvironment.
CeNP, which possesses both SOD- and CAT-mimicking activ-
ities, can scavenge toxic intracellular and extracellular ROS,
acting as self-regenerating antioxidative and anti-inflammatory
agents. CeNPs have shown efficacy in treating various diseases,
including cancer and AD.64 However, the precise mechanisms
by which CeNP improves AD outcomes remain unclear, aside
from their role in reshaping the disease-associated microenvi-
ronment. For example, CeNP has been shown to reprogram can-
cer-associated fibroblasts to normal fibroblasts, suppressing
cancer metastasis and inducing M1-to-M2 macrophage polari-
zation, alleviating inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis.29,65
These findings suggest that CeNP might similarly regulate the
intracerebral microenvironment of AD, particularly the resident
microglia. Nevertheless, the high hydrophobicity and poor BBB
penetration of unmodified CeNP limits its in vivo application in
brain-related diseases. To enhance biocompatibility while main-
taining bioactivity, we used FDA-approved biodegradable and
biocompatible PLGA as the reserve matrix for the CeNPs. Given
that the RAGE receptor is overexpressed in the BBB under in-
flammatory conditions, particularly in AD brain tissue, we func-
tionalized the CeNP-embedded PLGA nanoparticles with a
RAGE-targeting ligand to create T-CeNP. This modification
ingeniously mimics and leverages the abnormal peripheral Aβ
transport to the AD brain. T-CeNP exhibited excellent stability
in physiological environments and retained its CAT- and SOD-
mimicking activities. Furthermore, T-CeNP was biocompatible
and demonstrated significant antioxidant and anti-inflammatory
effects, evidenced by reduced ROS and TNF-α production
induced by LPS and Aβ. Notably, T-CeNP robustly preserves mi-
croglial homeostasis by preventing the transition from the anti-
inflammatory M2 phenotype to the pro-inflammatory M1 state
in response to Aβ, a pivotal driver of neuroinflammatory imbal-
ance in AD. By maintaining the equilibrium between M1 and
M2 polarization, T-CeNP may effectively modulate microglial
function, characterized by distinct cytokine profiles, gene
expression patterns, and signaling pathways, thereby offering
a compelling therapeutic avenue for addressing AD pathogen-
esis.66,67 Accredited to derivation from Aβ and intrinsic high-af-
finity to Aβ of the RAGE-targeting ligand, T-CeNP not only
prevented the formation of Aβ fibrils by forming T-CeNP/Aβ ag-
gregates but also enhanced Aβ clearance and degradation by
microglia. Interestingly, the presence of Aβ facilitated the cellular
uptake of T-CeNP by microglia in turn. As a result, T-CeNP effec-
tively protected neuronal cells from the damage caused by
elevated H2O2 and Aβ-induced toxicity. Leveraging the overex-
pression of RAGE receptors in AD brain (especially on the
BBB), the multifunctionality of the RAGE-targeting ligand and
the inherent properties of CeNP, T-CeNP was successfully tar-
geted to the brain parenchyma, reducing Aβ fibril deposition
and significantly mitigating neuroinflammation in the 5XFAD AD

dysfunctional microglia in the deteriorating AD, rather than
the original orchestration. We herein developed nanoengi-
neered ceria nanoclusters (T-CeNP) interacting with microglia
and pioneered their applications in mitigating the progression
of dementia. T-CeNP is a hybrid multifunctional enzyme

sclerosis, Huntington’s disease, spinal cord injury, and post-

conditions.

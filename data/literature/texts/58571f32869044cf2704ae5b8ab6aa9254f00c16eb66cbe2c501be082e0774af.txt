ABSTRACT: With the booming antimicrobial drug resistance
worldwide, traditional antibacterial agents (e.g., antibiotics) are
usually powerless against superbug. Targeting antibacterial
pathways different from traditional antibiotics could be an
effective approach to treating wounds with a resistant bacterial
infection. In this work, an antibacterial polymersome was
developed to physically induce bacterial membrane damage and
interfere with bacterial metabolism. First, we synthesized an
antibacterial poly(ε-caprolactone)-block-poly(glutamic acid)-
block-poly(Lys-stat-Phe) copolymer, which was then self-
assembled into polypeptosome with the amplification of surface
positive charges to disrupt bacterial membranes. In addition,
the polypeptosome was further decorated with photocatalytic
bismuth sulfide (Bi2S3) nanoparticles as a photocatalyst to interfere with reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH)
conversion. Specifically, near-infrared light generated free electrons from Bi2S3 nanoparticles could effectively interfere with
NADH homeostasis to induce antibiotic-resistant bacteria death, as verified by transcriptome sequence analysis. Moreover,
effective healing of antibiotic-resistant bacteria-infected wounds of mice was achieved with a spray of polypeptosome
dispersion. Overall, we provided a fresh strategy to integrate bacterial membrane damage and metabolism interference
functions within antibacterial polymersomes for healing antibiotic-resistant bacteria-infected wound.
KEYWORDS: infected wound, antibacterial, polymersome, Bi2S3, NADH
1. INTRODUCTION
Infected wounds are very common in everyday life, posing a
threat to human health and life because recurrent infections are
difficult to heal. Currently, the most common clinical
treatments for bacterial infections are still antibiotics.1,2
Unfortunately, bacteria can easily develop antibiotic resistance
due to the widespread use of antibiotics, which reduces the
efficacy of antibiotics and even leads to high mortality rates.3,4
Thus, developing antibacterial strategies, such as nanomateri-
als, dressings, and patches, is significant in the treatment of
diseases associated with bacterial infections, including infected
wounds.5−11
Traditional antibacterial mechanisms such as inhibiting
enzyme activity or disturbing cellular structural function have
been widely targeted but gradually ineffective due to bacterial
efflux mechanisms. Targeting energy metabolism which is
essential for all physiological activities of bacteria could be an
effective strategy against drug-resistant bacteria.12−14 As
nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+/NADH) homeo-
stasis is essential for maintaining regular energy metabolism
within bacteria with and without antibiotic resistance, such a
characteristic makes NAD+/NADH a deeply investigable target
for antibacterial therapies.15,16 Compared to traditional
approach using inhibitors of NADH dehydrogenase, an
exogenous approach could be more precise and effective to

applied noninvasive techniques in the clinic, near-infrared light
with satisfactory tissue penetration and negligible phototoxicity
has also been utilized for wound healing, such as photo-
dynamic and photothermal treatments.18,19
Photocatalytic antimicrobial therapy is a photosensitive
reaction accompanying subsequent biological effects.20 This
process involves excitation of the photosensitizer with laser
radiation of a specific wavelength at the site of bacterial
infection, which generated electrons to react with intracellular
or extracellular substances to achieve an antibacterial
effect.21,22 As a member of the bismuth-based semiconductor
group, Bi2S3, having a low direct band gap, wide absorption,
and good photoconductivity, can utilize NIR light effectively to
initiate a series of photocatalytic reactions. It can generate
electrons in the conduction band and valence band under near-
infrared laser excitation, which provides the possibility as a
photosensitizer.23,24 Therefore, constructing Bi2S3-based bio-
materials for exacerbating metabolic imbalance through cutting
off nutrient supply, triggering reactive oxygen species (ROS)
production, and disturbing NADH homeostasis, etc. has
emerged as a promising strategy for healing antibiotic-resistant
bacterial infected wounds.
Besides photocatalytic antimicrobial therapy, antimicrobial
peptides, a component of innate immunity, are one of the
promising alternatives to antibiotics.25 However, most
antimicrobial peptides are susceptible to degradation by
proteases in the physiological environment.26−29 Fortunately,
such problems can be effectively addressed by embedding
antimicrobial peptides into polypeptide-based polymersome
(polypeptosome).30,31 Polymersomes are hollow nanospheres
which are self-assembled from amphiphilic polymers.32−34 The
diversity of polymers endows polymersomes with excellent
versatility, which makes them promising for drug delivery,
cancer therapy, and antibacterial applications.35 Compared
with molecularly solubilized polypeptide and conventional
drug encapsulation, the self-assembly behavior of polypeptides
can locally amplify the positive charge density on the surface of
polypeptosomes. Its attachment to the bacterial surface
through charge attraction causes bacterial death and achieves
higher bacterial inhibition.36,37 This enhanced contact killing
mechanism is a physical antibacterial approach similar to that
of natural antimicrobial peptides.

that can in situ deposit bismuth sulfide (Bi2S3). As shown in

2.2. In Vitro Biofunctionality of APs@Bi2S3. 2.2.1. Cyto-
toxicity of APs and APs@Bi2S3. The cytotoxicity of APs and
APs@Bi2S3 against human normal liver cells (L02 cells) was
assessed by using the CCK-8 assay. Cytotoxicity was quantified
as relative cell viability, which is the ratio of viable cells in the
experimental group added with samples to those of the control
group without samples. The relative cell viability of L02 cells
cocultured with APs and APs@Bi2S3 at different concen-
trations was shown in Figure S11. When the concentration of
APs and APs@Bi2S3 was less than 250.0 μg mL−1, the relative
cell viability was >90%, and no obvious toxicity was observed.
When the concentration of the APs and APs@Bi2S3 was over
0.50 mg mL−1, it showed minor cytotoxicity, but the cell
viability still remained more than 80%. These results
demonstrated that APs and APs@Bi2S3 had good cytocompat-
ibility. The cytotoxicity of APs@Bi2S3 with NIR irradiation was
further investigated. As shown in Figure 2C, no significant
changes were observed in the cell viability of L02 cells
cocultured with APs@Bi2S3 under 808 nm laser irradiation
when compared with that without laser irradiation. These
results indicated that APs@Bi2S3 had a low cytotoxicity against
normal cells without or with NIR irradiation. As shown in
Figure S12, the L929 cells were also co-incubated with 0.25
mg/mL APs@Bi2S3 before and after NIR light irradiation for
12 h. Subsequently, the cells were also stained with live−dead,
and no significant cytotoxicity was found compared to the
control group results which are consistent with the cytotoxicity
we measured with CCK-8.

2.3. In Vitro Antibacterial Activity of APs and APs@
Bi2S3. The in vitro antibacterial activity of APs and APs@Bi2S3
with or without 808 nm laser irradiation was assessed. Two
representative bacterial strains, Escherichia coli (E. coli,
ATCC35218, Gram-negative) and methicillin-resistant Staph-

adopted to test the broad-spectrum antibacterial activity,
respectively. After cocultured with different concentrations of
APs and APs@Bi2S3 with or without 808 nm laser irradiation,
the viable bacteria were inoculated to LB agar plates for cell
counting, and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)
values were obtained at the same time. Figure 3A shows the
digital photos of E. coli and MRSA bacterial colonies after being
treated with the same concentration of APs and APs@Bi2S3
without (APs@Bi2S3 group) or with 808 nm laser irradiation
(APs@Bi2S3+NIR; 0.20 mg mL−1, the corresponding Bi2S3

significant difference between these two groups, which

contributed little extra antibacterial activity to the APs. The
cell counting and cell viability are recorded in Figure 3B,C,
which also supported the above conclusion. The number of
living bacterial cells was reduced by 50%, and the bacterial cell
viability still remained 40−60% after treatment with APs or
APs@Bi2S3 of 0.20 mg mL−1. In fact, APs or APs@Bi2S3
exhibited significant antibacterial activity only when the

excellent antibacterial activity. Overall, the APs showed a
synergic antibacterial effect with photocatalytic Bi2S3 nano-

2.4. Transcriptome Sequence Analysis and Antibac-
terial Mechanism Exploration. To decipher the potential
antibacterial targets and the corresponding antibacterial
mechanisms, we analyzed the dynamic transcriptome of
APs@Bi2S3 + NIR treated MRSA by RNA sequencing. After
treatment without or with APs@Bi2S3 + NIR, a total of 2637
genes were detected in MRSA gene expression profiles. Among
them, 476 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were
statistically significant (ln(treated/control) > 1 or ln(treated/
control) < −1), displayed by the volcano plot analysis (Figure
4A). There were 278 up-regulated genes (red) and 198 down-
regulated genes (blue). Meanwhile, the volcano plots with
more details and the denser part of log10(Pajust) < 10 have
been shown in Figure S13. The correlation analysis indicated
that the two groups were comparable (Figure 4B). Meanwhile,
a heat map of the hierarchical clustering of differentially
expressed genes is shown in Figure 4C, demonstrating the
expression levels of each gene in different groups.
To further clarify the effect of APs@Bi2S3 + NIR on the
biological function and gene pathway of MRSA, we conducted
Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and
Genomes enrichment analysis (Figure 4E,F). It was discovered
that 511 differentially expressed genes were gathered in 20
Gene Ontology in terms of molecular function, biological
process, and cellular component. In particular, the main
differential genes for molecular function are the trans-
membrane transporter protein activity. The major differences
in biological processes are centered on the ATP biosynthetic
process. In the cellular components, the major differential
genes were protein complexes. This result suggests that APs@
Bi2S3 can disrupt bacterial membranes and affect both cellular
and metabolic processes in bacteria. Subsequently, the Kyoto
Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses
grouped differentially expressed genes into different pathways,
revealing functional information and relationships of these
differentially expressed genes. Some of these include oxidative
phosphorylation, ribosome, and naphthalene degradation.
These pathways play an important role in the antibacterial
process of APs@Bi2S3 and indicate the presence of related
biological features such as disturbed energy metabolism and
redox levels within the bacteria.
As shown in Figure 4D, the disruption of redox balance in
bacteria may be one of the causes of bacterial death. In
particular, NADH plays a key role in maintaining intracellular
redox homeostasis. We found that adh1 and adhE genes were
significantly down-regulated, suggesting that APs@Bi2S3
irradiated by NIR not only oxidizes intracellular NADH to
NAD+ but also inhibits the up-regulation of related genes
within the bacteria, thereby suppressing NADH production in
normal metabolic pathways. Additionally, APs@Bi2S3 are
assembled from antimicrobial peptides, whose surfaces are
enriched with positive charges that can kill bacteria by
disrupting their membranes. The disruption of the membrane
tends to affect the expression of ATP genes and ATP synthesis
pathways. As shown in the Figure 4G, the genes related to ATP
synthesis and transmembrane transport were significantly
down-regulated.
2.5. In Vivo Healing Ability of APs@Bi2S3. The wound
healing ability of APs@Bi2S3 was evaluated on an MRSA
infected mice model. Two symmetrical wounds (5 mm
diameter) were inflicted on the back of every mouse on day
1. Then, the wounds were inoculated with MRSA (∼108 CFU
mL−1) subsequently. The mice with infected wounds were
randomly divided into three groups (n = 5 for each group). On
day 3, the infected wounds in each group were treated with 20
μL of the following dispersions: (i) PBS; (ii) APs@Bi2S3; (iii)
APs@Bi2S3 + NIR.
Afterward, the mice in each group were treated with these
dispersions every 2 days. The wounds were photographed, and
the area of wounds was recorded every day to investigate the
wound healing process. As presented in Figure 5A, the PBS
group showed the slowest healing rate, the APs@Bi2S3 group
was in the middle, and the APs@Bi2S3 + NIR group showed
the fastest healing rate. The wounds treated with APs@Bi2S3 +
NIR almost closed on day 9, but the wounds treated with PBS
still remained open on day 13. As a comparison, the wounds
treated with APs@Bi2S3 without laser closed on day 13, but
there were still visible scars on the back of the mice. The
relative wound area was recorded and summarized in Figure
5B,C. The wounds in the PBS group still remained 30% open
area on day 13, while most wounds in the APs@Bi2S3 + NIR
group closed on day 9 and recovered on day 11. In addition,
the wounds in the PBS group suffered festering and the wound
area even became larger during the first 7 days. The exotoxins
and enzymes of MRSA can further destroy healthy tissue
around the wound, leading to localized tissue necrosis. Thus,
compared to the traditional Staphylococcus aureus infected
wounds,8 the combination of MRSA resistance and persistence
of the infection can trigger the formation of chronic wounds
and diffuse abscesses. Although the wounds in the APs@Bi2S3

3. CONCLUSION
We proposed a photocatalytic antimicrobial therapy based on
Bi2S3-decorated polypeptosomes with NADH dehydrogenase-
like activity for the treatment of MRSA infected wounds. The
PCL-b-PGA-b-P(Lys-stat-Phe) copolymer was synthesized and
then self-assembled into polypeptosomes with in situ
decoration of ultrasmall Bi2S3 nanoparticles to obtain a
Bi2S3-decorated polypeptosome (APs@Bi2S3). Bi2S3 nano-
particles in APs@Bi2S3 generate free electrons under near-
infrared light that could effectively interfere with NADH
homeostasis and produce ROS to induce antibiotic-resistant
bacteria death. Transcriptome sequence analysis was used to
confirm the antibacterial mechanism. In addition, APs@Bi2S3
exhibited good cytocompatibility with normal cells without
and with NIR irradiation. In vivo study further demonstrated
that APs@Bi2S3 with NIR irradiation was able to efficiently
eradicate bacteria and promote the healing of MRSA infected
wounds, without causing obvious side effects. Moreover, such a
polypeptosomes-based platform can be expanded to connect
many other multimeans combined antibacterial strategies, such
as antibiotic delivery, photodynamic therapy, and sonodynamic
therapy.
4. METHODS
4.1. Synthesis of PCL54-b-PGA6-b-P(Lys15-stat-Phe9) Copoly-
mer. PCL54-NH2 macroinitiator, Bz-Glu-NCA, Z-Lys-NCA, and Phe-
NCA monomers were synthesized according to our previously
reported methods,42−44 with yields of ∼97%, 93%, 92%, and 93%,
respectively. The 1H NMR spectra of PCL54-NH-Boc and PCL54-NH2
were shown in Figures S2 and S3.
The PCL54-b-PBLG6-b-P(Lys15-stat-Phe9) copolymer was synthe-
sized by the successive ring-opening polymerization of Bz-Glu-NCA,
Z-Lys-NCA, and Phe-NCA using PCL54-NH2 as a macroinitiator in
one pot. In a glovebox, PCL54-NH2 (1.0 g, 0.175 mmol) was firstly
dissolved in anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF) (4.0 mL). A
solution of freshly prepared Bz-Glu-NCA (0.3690 g, 1.403 mmol) in
anhydrous DMF (2.0 mL) was then added. The mixture was stirred at
room temperature for 24 h. After that, Phe-NCA (0.3370 g, 1.755
mmol) and Z-Lys-NCA (1.025 g, 3.510 mmol) were dissolved in
anhydrous DMF (20.0 mL), which was then added to the above
reaction mixture. The reaction was continued for another 48 h. After
reaction, the product was then precipitated in a large excess of diethyl
ether by three times and dried under vacuum. The 1H NMR of
PCL54-b-PBLG6-b-P(Z-Lys15-stat-Phe9) was shown in Figure S4.
Yield: ∼75%.
The final PCL54-b-PGA6-b-P(Lys15-stat-Phe9) copolymer was
obtained after a deprotection process. PCL54-b-PBLG6-b-P(Z-Lys15-
stat-Phe9) copolymer (0.5 g) was dissolved in 33 wt % HBr/
CH3COOH solution (5.0 mL) and stirred for 4 h. Acetic acid and
HBr were neutralized by adding excess triethylamine to the reaction
solution. After that, additional N,N-dimethylformamide (20 mL) was
added. The solution was then transferred to a dialysis tube (molecular
weight cutoff: 3.5 kDa) and dialyzed against deionized water for 2
days. A white powder was obtained after lyophilization. The 1H NMR
spectrum of PCL54-b-PGA6-b-P(Lys15-stat-Phe9) is shown in Figure
S5. Yield: ∼81%.
4.2. Preparation of Antibacterial Polypeptosomes (APs).
The amphiphilic copolymer was assembled into polypeptosomes by
the solvent switching method. PCL54-b-PGA6-b-P(Lys15-stat-Phe9)
copolymer (5.0 mg) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (5.0 mL).
Due to the large number of hydrogen bonds in the polymer in the
process of lyophilization, a few drops of trifluoroacetic acid were
added for rapid dissolution. Then, deionized water (10.0 mL) was
added dropwise with a peristaltic pump under constant agitation at
500 rpm. The dispersion was then transferred to a dialysis tube
(molecular weight cutoff: 14.0 kDa) and dialyzed against deionized
water for 2 days to remove the organic solvent, resulting in an
aqueous polypeptosome dispersion.
4.3. Preparation of Bi2S3-Decorated Antibacterial Poly-
peptosomes (APs@Bi2S3). The bismuth sulfide nanoparticle-
decorated polypeptosomes were prepared by in situ depositing Bi2S3
nanoparticles on the polypeptosomes. An aqueous solution of
Bi(NO3)3·5H2O (1.57 mL, 1.0 mg mL−1) precursor was added to
polypeptosomes dispersion (5.0 mL, 0.50 mg mL−1) and stirred for 1
h to allow bismuth ions to fully enrich on or around the surface of the
polypeptosomes. Then, a Na2S·9H2O solution (0.888 mL, 2.0 mg
mL−1) was rapidly added to the mixture. The reaction was continued
for another 15 min. The obtained bismuth sulfide-decorated
polypeptosomes were then dialyzed against deionized water
(molecular weight cutoff: 14.0 kDa) for 24 h to remove impurities.
4.4. CVC Determination. The CVC is defined as the critical
concentration of the polymer that forms polypeptosomes. First,
pyrene (6.0 mg, 30 μmol) was dissolved in 50 mL of acetone as a
probe for monitoring polypeptosome formation. Then, 10 μL of
probe solution was added to the centrifuge tube, and acetone was
evaporated at room temperature. Second, 1.0 mL of polypeptosome
dispersion was added to the centrifuge tube containing the probe
solution. Third, the sample was stirred for 12 h and the fluorescence
intensity of each sample dispersion was measured using a Lumina
fluorescence spectrometer. Finally, a linear fit was performed for the
ratio of fluorescence intensity (393 nm/376 nm) at different
concentrations, and the intercept point of the two lines corresponded
to a CVC of 19.5 μg/mL.
4.5. Photocatalytic Antibacterial Tests. The plate counting
method was used to measure the minimum inhibition concentration
(MIC) values. Bacterial saline solution (100.0 μL) was added to the
96-well plate (∼106 CFU mL−1). APs or APs@Bi2S3 saline dispersions
(100.0 μL) preprepared at different concentrations were added to the
96-well plates. Each well was irradiated with an 808 nm NIR laser (1.0
W/cm2) for 10 min and cultured for another 4 h. Then the cocultured
medium (100.0 μL) was successively diluted 10-fold each time to a
certain concentration. After that, the diluted medium (100.0 μL) was
evenly spread on LB agar. The LB agar plates were allowed to culture
at 37 °C for 48 h, and the number of colonies was counted and
recorded (the effective counting of colonies on agar plates was
between 30 and 300). MIC is defined as the lowest concentration of
antimicrobials that kills 90% of bacteria.

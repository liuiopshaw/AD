1. Introduction
Traditional bacterial infections are controlled by commercially
available antibiotics such as streptomycin, tetracycline, penicillin, and
vancomycin through their effects on bacterial cell wall synthesis,
translational machinery, and DNA replication. (Kohanski et al., 2010)
Antibiotics not only save patients’ lives but also play an indispensable
role in medicine and surgery. Administration of antibiotics can prevent
or treat infections in patients who have had complex surgeries and in
patients receiving long-term chemotherapy treatment or suffering from
chronic diseases such as diabetes, renal disease, and rheumatoid ar-
thritis (Fischbach and Walsh, 2009). However, the rapid development
of resistant bacteria due to continuous mutation endangers the efficacy
of antibiotics (Laxminarayan et al., 2013; Davies and Davies, 2010).
The antibiotic resistance crisis is mainly due to the overuse and misuse
of these medications (Aslam et al., 2018). Bacteria with multidrug re-
sistance (MDR) against many antibiotics can also develop through the
production of enzymes to degrade or inactivate the antibiotic as well as
through modifications or alterations of bacterial efflux pumps, targeting
binding sites, and entry ports. (Blair et al., 2014) Moreover, low eco-
nomic incentives as a result of the expensive and long process of anti-
biotic innovation and commercialization have slowed the development
of new antibiotics in the pharmaceutical industry (Simpkin et al.,
2017). With the declining efficiency of currently available chemother-
apeutic drugs due to increasing drug resistance against antibiotics,
there is an exigent need to develop alternative treatment strategies.
To overcome bacterial resistance to antibiotics, nanomaterials have
been a stepping stone toward combating antimicrobial resistance.
(Rudramurthy et al., 2016; Baptista et al., 2018; Gupta et al., 2019;
Kumar et al., 2018) Owing to having a smaller dimension than that of
bacteria, a large surface area to volume ratio and specific surface and
chemical properties, metal-based nanoparticles such as those of silver
(Ag), gold (Au), titanium dioxide (TiO2), copper oxide (CuO), and zinc
oxide (ZnO) allow strong interactions with bacteria and exhibit desir-
able antimicrobial activity. (Zheng et al., 2018a, b; Uyguner Demirel
et al., 2018; Ananth et al., 2015; Sirelkhatim et al., 2015) Unlike an-
tibiotics, metal nanoparticles act against bacteria through several dif-
ferent mechanisms such as disruption of the bacterial membrane,
transmembrane electron transfer, and respiratory processes, damage of
cellular components such DNA, proteins and enzymes, and induction of
oxidative stress (Gold et al., 2018; Shaikh et al., 2019; Edris et al.,
2017). As a result of the multifunctional mode of antibacterial action,
metal nanoparticles usually possess broad-spectrum antibacterial ac-
tivity, and the development of resistance is unlikely (Shaikh et al.,
2019; Lakshminarayanan et al., 2018). Although many metal nano-
particles exhibit effective antibacterial activities, the cytotoxicity of
released metal ions from nanoparticles that lack appropriate template
and capping ligands limits their usage (Ganguly et al., 2018). In addi-
tion, these nanoparticles are prone to aggregation in high ionic strength
solutions and are passivated by biomacromolecules and some specific
molecules such as aminothiols in biological fluids, thereby diminishing
their antibacterial activity (Loza et al., 2014).
Metal nanoclusters (NCs) such as Au NCs, Ag NCs and Cu NCs have
attracted immense attention due to electron confinement within the
nanostructure at ultrasmall sizes comparable to the Fermi wavelength
of the electron, which results in electronic energy states that exhibit
molecule–like properties such as photoluminescence, molecular chir-
ality, intrinsic magnetism and redox behavior. (Higaki et al., 2019; Su

proteins in the absence or presence of reducing agents initiate the nu-

in situ functionalized on the particles during the formation of NCs.

that with large-sized gold nanoparticles (Au NPs, > 2 nm) (Zheng et al.,

ultrasmall Au NCs have strong interactions with bacteria, inducing a
metabolic imbalance of the bacterial cells after internalization of these
materials. Furthermore, the internalized Au NCs were shown to induce
the production of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), which
also contributes to their antibacterial ability. The same group has re-

antimicrobial activity.(Zheng et al., 2016) Their study reveals that the
synergistic interaction of the intrinsically antibacterial activity of dap-
tomycin (causing bacterial membrane damage) and Ag NCs (triggering
ROS production) greatly improved the bactericidal capability. The ex-
pensive precious metal (Au and Ag) may limit the usage of antibacterial
Au NCs and Ag NCs, even though they have shown promising anti-
bacterial capability. Recently, tannic acid-capped Cu NCs have been
reported to exhibit antibacterial activity by damaging the cellular
membrane, however, these NCs are only effective for inactivating gram-
positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Bacillus
subtilis (Xia et al., 2019). Thus, the development of inexpensive metal
NCs with broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity remains an attractive
challenge.
In this work, we have synthesized bovine serum albumin (BSA)-
templated antibacterial Cu NCs (∼2 nm) capped with 1,3-propane-
dithiol (PDT). Scheme 1A shows the one-pot synthesis of the PDT/
BSA–Cu NCs through conjugation of PDT onto the Cu NCs templated
with BSA. BSA induces the nucleation and controlled growth of Cu NCs
via its amino acid residues such as cysteine, histidine, and tyrosine. (Xie
et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2014; Bhunia et al., 2019) PDT, is an organic
compound with two thiol (–SH) groups at each end, which can also
serve as a lipophilic carrier to improve the internalization of Cu NCs
into bacteria (Domenico et al., 1997). In contrast to PDT and BSA–Cu
NCs, PDT/BSA–Cu NCs exhibited superior bactericidal effects against
non-multidrug-resistant (non-MDR) bacteria such as E. coli, Salmonella
enteritidis (S. enteritidis), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), and S.
aureus as well as multidrug-resistant bacteria such as methicillin-re-
sistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). The photoluminescence and
aqueous dispersibility, as well as the antibacterial activity of the Cu
NCs, are highly dependent on the PDT ligand on the surface of the
BSA–Cu NCs. Furthermore, carbon fiber fabrics (CFFs) modified with
PDT/BSA–Cu NCs also exhibited phenomenal antibacterial activity,
demonstrating that Cu NCs is a potential antibacterial coating for textile
materials.
2. Experimental
2.1. Synthesis of BSA–Cu NCs and BSA/PDT–Cu NCs
A 2.0 mL solution containing BSA (50 μM), CuSO4 (2 mM), ascorbic
acid (50 mM), and NaOH (100 mM) was incubated at 70 °C for 60 min.
The heating resulted in a color change of the solution from light yellow
to brown, indicating the formation of BSA–Cu NCs. After the reaction,
BSA–Cu NCs (2.0 mL) was cooled to room temperature and then stored
at 4 °C when not in use. The synthesis of PDT/BSA–Cu NCs is almost the
same as those of BSA–Cu NCs, except that PDT (0.4–3.6 mM) is used in
the synthesis of the Cu NCs.
2.2. Characterization of Cu NCs
UV–vis absorption and photoluminescence spectra of the Cu NCs
were recorded using a double-beam UV–vis spectrophotometer (Cintra
10e, GBC, Victoria, Australia) and a Cary Eclipse fluorescence spec-
trophotometer (Varian, Palo Alto, CA, USA), respectively. The photo-
luminescence lifetimes of the Cu NCs were analyzed using a photo-
counting PicoHarp 300 system (PicoQuant, Berlin, Germany) with a
diode laser as a light source emitting at 375 nm. High-resolution
transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images of as-prepared Cu
NCs were captured using an FEI Tecnai–G2–F20 instrument equipped
with a Philips Tecnai G2 dispersive X-ray spectrometer. TEM samples
were prepared by adsorbing 100 μL of purified sample onto a carbon-
coated grid for 1 h, then, the excess sample was removed from the grid,
which was dried at room temperature for the measurements. X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements of BSA–Cu NCs and
PDT/BSA–Cu NCs samples were carried out with ES-CALAB 250 spec-
trometer (VG Scientific, East Grinstead, UK) having Al Kα X-rays as the
excitation source. Sample solution (150 μL) was drop coated onto Si
substrate followed by overnight vacuum drying. To correct the binding
energies, carbon (C) 1 s peak at 284.6 eV was used as a standard.
Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra for BSA–Cu NCs and PDT/
BSA–Cu NCs were obtained using Nicolet iS5 FTIR spectrometer
(Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Field-emission scanning
electron microscopy (FESEM, Hitachi S-4800) and an Oxford Inca en-
ergy dispersive X-ray (EDX) system (Oxford, UK) were used to observe
the morphology of CFFs and to determine the composition of Cu in
functionalized CFFs. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra were recorded
using a J-815 spectrometer (JASCO, Tokyo, Japan) with a 1 cm path
length cuvette at ambient temperature.
2.3. Bacterial culture
E. coli, S. enteritidis, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and MRSA bacteria were
grown separately in Luria–Bertani medium (LB broth). Individual co-
lonies were isolated and inoculated in LB broth. The bacterial cultures
were then incubated at 37 °C until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600
nm) reached 1 (optical path length = 1 cm). Each bacterial culture
(1 mL) was centrifuged at a relative centrifugal force (RCF) of 5000 × g
at 25 °C for 15 min and washed with phosphate-buffered saline [PBS,
containing 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, and 2.0 mM
KH2PO4, adjusted to pH 7.4 using HCl (pH 7.4)] twice prior to further
use. All the solutions were dispersed in sodium phosphate buffer
(10 mM, pH 7.4) at 37 °C at 200 rpm for 1 h. Then, 200 μL of the desired
dilution from the E. coli [1.0 × 109 colony forming unit (CFU) mL–1]
suspension was spread on the surface of LB agar plates and allowed to
solidify for the colony formation assay.
2.4. Antibacterial assays
The minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) are reported as the
minimum concentration of nanomaterials used to kill > 90 % of the
total bacterial population. The MICs were determined using the broth
microdilution method. E. coli, S. enteritidis, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus and
MRSA bacteria (104 CFU mL−1) were incubated separately with PDT,
BSA–Cu NCs or PDT/BSA–Cu NCs in PBS solution at 37 °C under
shaking at a speed of 250 rpm for 1 h. Then, 100 μL of diluted inoculum
from the bacterial suspension was spread on the surface of LB agar

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Synthesis and characterization of BSA–Cu NCs
The BSA–Cu NCs are obtained through the reduction of Cu(II)
(2.0 mM) by ascorbic acid (50 mM) in the presence of BSA (50 μM)
templates. The reduction of Cu(II) by ascorbic acid via a two-electron
transfer reaction forms a Cu(0) species and yields dehydroascorbic acid
(DHA). (Tu et al., 2017) The pH plays a crucial role in the synthesis of
BSA–Cu NCs because it influences not only the reducing strength of
ascorbic acid but also the charge and conformation of the template
protein (BSA) (Xie et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2014; Bhunia et al., 2019).
Therefore, we investigated the reaction at several pH values. We did not
observe any Cu NCs by TEM characterization at pH ≤9.0, and the
photoluminescence spectra suggest the formation of Cu(I)–BSA com-
plexes with emission wavelengths at approximately 400–600 nm (Fig.
S1A). (Zhong et al., 2015; Dixon and Egusa, 2018) In contrast, the Cu
NCs with sizes of 2.3 ± 0.4 nm (from 100 counts) were formed at pH
11.0
(Fig.
S1B),
and
their
broad
and
long-wavelength
photo-
luminescence emission (600–850 nm) is consistent with that of most
reported Cu NCs. (Wang et al., 2014; Basu et al., 2019; Wang et al.,
2018; Zhao et al., 2015) We believe that the strong reducing strength of
ascorbic acid and partial denaturation of BSA at pH 11.0 mainly con-
tribute to the formation of stable BSA-templated Cu NCs.(Dixon and
Egusa, 2018)

(Wang et al., 2016) In addition, the PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs having a dense
capping-ligand shell of PDT on the Cu NC surface reduces collision- and

(Tseng et al., 2016, 2014; Chevrier et al., 2018; Bootharaju et al., 2018;

from the Cu(I)–S shell into the core, resulting in a weaker photo-
luminescence intensity. (Pyo et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2014).

It is well known that silver ions (Ag+), as well as silver nano-
particles (Ag NPs), are effective nonantibiotic biocides. (Zheng et al.,
2018a) By taking advantage of their ultrasmall size and high stability,
we further demonstrated the long-term antibacterial activity of PDT3.6/
BSA–Cu NCs against E. coli. For comparison, aliquots of E. coli (104 CFU
mL–1, 100 μL) suspension were introduced separately onto LB agar
plates in the presence of PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs ([Cu] = 0.5 μM), Ag+
(0.5 μM) or Ag NPs (∼15 nm in diameter, [Ag] = 0.5 μM), which were
then incubated separately for 24, 48 and 72 h and then grown at 37 °C
for another 24 h (Fig. S9). Compared to that observed for the untreated
group (975 ± 23 CFU), negligible colonies were observed for all
treated groups in 24 h incubated agar plates (Fig. S9A). At the end of
72 h, Ag+-treated (680 ± 57 CFU) and Ag NP-treated (420 ± 12 CFU)
groups lost ∼70 % and ∼43 % of their bactericidal activity (Fig. S9B),
respectively, whereas PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs (30 ± 6 CFU) lost only 2.9
% of their activity. The large decreases in the bactericidal activity of
Ag+ and Ag NPs are mainly because they are prone to oxidation.
(Schultz et al., 2018) The PDT capping the BSA-templated Cu NCs
minimizes the oxidation-induced decay of antimicrobial activity. The
excellent antibacterial efficiency of PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs with long-term
stability demonstrates the synergistic effect of BSA and PDT for stabi-
lizing Cu NCs.

synthetic conditions are the same as those for PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs. The
obtained Cu NCs are denoted as GSH/BSA–Cu NCs, DDT/BSA–Cu NCs,
MSA/BSA–Cu NCs, LA/BSA–Cu NCs, and DTT/BSA–Cu NCs when using
GSH, DDT, MSA, LA, and DTT for the synthesis of Cu NCs, respectively.

of PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs (Fig. S10), respectively. Our results revealed
that thiol ligands play a vital role in the antibacterial properties of Cu
NCs. We noted that BSA–Cu NCs modified with short carbon chain (e.g.,
MSA) or dithiol (e.g., LA and DTT) ligands exhibit superior antibacterial
capability. (Heuer-Jungemann et al., 2019) The PDT ligand, with both
short carbon chain and dithiol characteristics, imparted the PDT3.6/
BSA–Cu NCs with the best antibacterial activity. The short carbon chain
and dithiol characteristics of PDT with a neutral charge can facilitate
ligand chelation with Cu(I) species which is again in consistence with
the XPS data, confirmed the presence of oxidized Cu species in on
PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs, thereby increasing the thiol density on the NC

3.3. Antibacterial mechanism
Copper ions play vital roles as electron acceptors/donors in many
natural enzymes and can effectively participate in Fenton-like reactions
leading to the generation of various ROS, such as hydrogen peroxide
(H2O2), hydroxyl radicals (·OH), superoxide anion radicals (O2·–), and
perhydroxyl radicals (HOO·). (Phaniendra et al., 2015) We evaluated
the ability of PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs to produce ROS by using the 2′,7′-
dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) assay. Bacteria are
composed of several cellular esterases that cause the deacetylation of
DCFH-DA, which is then oxidized by ROS to produce green fluorescent
2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCF). We observed that PDT3.6/
BSA–Cu NC-treated E. coli induced the generation of higher ROS levels
relative to those for PDT- and BSA–Cu NC-treated NCs (Fig. 3). Electron
spin resonance (ESR) spectra using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide
(DMPO) as a spin trap reveal that ascorbyl free radicals were only
formed in the sodium phosphate buffer solution (10 mM, pH 7.4) con-
taining PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs (Fig. 4). This phenomenon can be ascribed
to the PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NC-induced high concentration of ROS further
reacting with the remaining ascorbate, leading to the formation of Asc·
free radicals. Ascorbate readily donates electrons to highly reactive free
radicals, such as O2·–, ·OH, and HOO· radicals, and ascorbate is itself
converted into a less reactive and long-lifetime Asc· radical. (Roginsky
and Stegmann, 1994; Romo et al., 2019) Therefore, we suggest that the
strong antibacterial activity of PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs is mainly due to
membrane disruption through physical interaction between the Cu NCs

3.5. Antibacterial PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs-coated carbon fiber fabrics
Many textile materials are prone to act as carriers for bacterial in-
fection due to their ability to support the facile adherence and growth
of pathogenic microbes. (Callewaert et al., 2014) Therefore, anti-
microbial textiles have attracted much interest because of their great
potential for reducing the transmission of infection in medical and
healthcare environments. Simply applying an antibacterial coating on
textiles not only protects the textiles and wearers themselves but also
mitigates infectious diseases (Nwanya et al., 2019; Budama et al., 2013;
Abramova et al., 2013). Recently, much attention has been drawn to
carbon fiber textiles owing to their very high strength-to-weight ratio,
tensile strength, compressive strength, and toughness, as well as ex-
cellent heat, corrosion, and fatigue resistance. The above results have
demonstrated that PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs possess both excellent anti-
bacterial activity and minimal cytotoxicity. Therefore, the PDT3.6/
BSA–Cu NCs can potentially be developed as an effective antibacterial
composite to protect public health. In this study, we fabricate PDT3.6/
BSA–Cu NC-coated carbon fiber fabrics (CFFs) by a direct dipping
method. The CFF samples (1 cm × 1 cm) are soaked in a PDT3.6/
BSA–Cu NC (10 μM in terms of Cu) solution for 1 h and then washed
with pure water. The scanning transmission electron microscopy/en-
ergy-dispersive X-ray (STEM-EDS) mapping of Cu indicates that the
PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs are homogeneously distributed on the CFFs (Fig.
S15). We further determined a value of 1.3 μg Cu per cm2 CFFs in the
PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NC-coated CFFs by using ICP-MS. We also noted that
the PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NC-coated CFFs are very stable, demonstrating that
less than 1 % of the Cu was released after incubating the PDT3.6/
BSA–Cu NC-coated CFFs in DI water for 24 h. The high stability of the
PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NC-coated CFFs can be ascribed to the strong interac-
tion between the surface BSA molecules on the Cu NCs and the gra-
phite-like structure of the carbon fibers. The antibacterial activity of the
CFFs was evaluated according to the modified Japanese Industrial
Standards (JIS) L1902 method. The PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NC-coated CFFs
show excellent antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus
(Fig. 6), whereas the pristine CFFs exhibit negligible antibacterial
ability. The PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NC-coated CFFs can inactivate > 98 % of E.
coli or S. aureus. The direct contact of bacteria with the adsorbed
PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs causes the inactivation of bacteria, which is mainly
attributed to the strong antibacterial ability of the modified CFFs. Our
results suggested that CFFs coated with PDT3.6/BSA–Cu NCs can be an
effective clothing material to protect from microbial infection and to
serve as an antibacterial dressing for various medical applications.

and Jianping Xie*

ABSTRACT: Bulk gold (Au) is known to be chemically inactive.
However, when the size of Au nanoparticles (Au NPs) decreases to
close to 1 nm or sub-nanometer dimensions, these ultrasmall Au
nanoclusters (Au NCs) begin to possess interesting physical and
chemical properties and likewise spawn diﬀerent applications when
working with bulk Au or even Au NPs. In this study, we found that it
is possible to confer antimicrobial activity to Au NPs through precise
control of their size down to NC dimension (typically less than 2
nm). Au NCs could kill both Gram-positive and Gram-negative
bacteria. This wide-spectrum antimicrobial activity is attributed to
the ultrasmall size of Au NCs, which would allow them to better
interact with bacteria. The interaction between ultrasmall Au NCs
and bacteria could induce a metabolic imbalance in bacterial cells
after the internalization of Au NCs, leading to an increase of intracellular reactive oxygen species production that kills
bacteria consequently.
KEYWORDS: gold nanoclusters, silver nanoclusters, antimicrobial agents, metal nanoparticles, reactive oxygen species
N
anotechnology advancement has brought much
excitement to the material science ﬁeld.1,2 The
excitement is not unwarranted as material scientists
are able to precisely control the physicochemical properties
down to atomic precision to yield advanced materials with
extraordinary properties, such as nanoparticles (NPs) with
quantum size eﬀects.1,2 Among the tunable physicochemical
properties, the size-dependent properties of NPs receive the
most attention. The exploration of control size has resulted in
particular applications of NPs, such as the surface-enhanced
Raman scattering (SERS)-based analysis, green catalysts,
superior magnetic resonance imaging agent, etc.3−6 Controlling
NPs’ size also allows the material scientists to modulate their
biochemical and biophysical properties, including cell uptake,
biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, cytotoxicity, targeting eﬃ-
ciency, etc.7−13 It is also possible to push the NPs’ eﬀectiveness
further by reducing the size of metal NPs to a value comparable
to the Fermi wavelength of electrons (∼1 nm), producing the
ultrasmall metal nanoclusters (NCs).14−16 Ultrasmall metal
NCs (with core sizes less than 2 nm) hold discrete electronic
states and characteristic geometric structures, which oﬀer them
several intriguing molecular-like properties, such as well-deﬁned
molecular structure, quantized charging, HOMO−LUMO
transition, molecular magnetism, molecular chirality, and strong
luminescence.17−23 An interesting example is that gold (Au) is
highly catalytic in the form of Au NCs. It was reported that
Au55 NCs (consisting of 55 Au atoms) showed eﬃcient
catalytic activity for selective oxidation of styrene by dioxygen,
whereas even slightly larger Au NPs (>2 nm) were completely
inactive.24
In nanomedicine applications, several recent studies have
shown that further decreasing the size of silver (Ag) NPs to the
NC range could produce enhanced antimicrobial agents with
wide-spectrum applicability that allows us to remain ahead of
the antimicrobial resistance.25,26 Nevertheless, the enhanced
antimicrobial eﬀectiveness was not accompanied by desirable
biocompatibility in the mammalian cell model, raising safety
concerns for its clinical application.27 This is expected as the
antimicrobial activity of Ag NCs is mainly derived from the
generation of Ag+ ions, which are potentially toxic to both the
targeted bacteria cells and the host human cells alike. Clearly,
there is a need for an alternative wide-spectrum antimicrobial
agent with higher biocompatibility (over Ag NCs) to ensure the
clinical translation.
Unlike silver, gold, the “noblest” among the metals, is inert,
highly stable, and would not easily dissociate into ions.28 These
noble qualities contribute to the widely accepted notion of Au
NPs as being highly biocompatible in mammalian system, both
in vitro and in vivo.29 This biocompatibility in mammalian cells
is also observed when the size is further reduced to the NC
range. For example, utilizing the in vitro colorectal cell model,
we observed no cytotoxic and genotoxic eﬀect on the cells
exposed with Au NCs.30 Interestingly, Au NCs were found to
instead boost cell metabolism and overall cell proliferation. In
animal studies, ultrasmall Au NCs showed improved tumor
uptake and high renal clearance.31,32 It is also this inertness that

many in the ﬁeld including us assumed that Au would not be a
viable antimicrobial material. Instead, Au NP-based antimicro-
bial strategies evolved around grafting known antimicrobial
compounds, such as ampicillin, antimicrobial peptides, and
cationic or zwitterionic ligands, on the surface of large Au NPs,
presumably using Au NPs as passive drug carriers.33−37
Now, since an enhanced antimicrobial activity was observed
when the size of the Ag NPs was reduced to the NC range, we
hypothesized that through size control, Au NPs, when reduced
down to Au NCs, might begin to exhibit some special biological
properties, such as antimicrobial activity, contrary to the
popular assumption of inertness. Here, we demonstrated that
ultrasmall Au NCs possess surprisingly high wide-spectrum
antimicrobial activity, which is however absent in their larger
Au NP counterparts. In addition, we elucidated the possible
mechanism that drives the antimicrobial activity of ultrasmall
Au NCs.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
As size is the decisive factor in our hypothesis, it is pivotal for
the Au NPs used in this study to have diﬀerent sizes while
keeping their respective surface groups the same. As such, we
utilized 6-mercaptohexanoic acid (MHA) as protecting ligand
in the synthesis of both Au NPs and NCs. As shown in Figure
1a, the UV−vis absorption spectrum of Au NPs shows one
absorption peak at ∼520 nm, which is the characteristic surface
plasmon resonance peak of small Au NPs (typically <20 nm).38
The red solution of Au NPs also identiﬁes the presence of small
Au NPs. The size of the as-synthesized Au NPs was analyzed by
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figure 1a inset and
Figure S1a), and according to the particle size distribution
(Figure S1b) of the Au NPs, the size of the as-sythesized MHA-
protected Au NPs was determined to be ∼6.0 ± 3.0 nm.
Changes in the physicochemical properties of Au NPs could
also be reﬂected from their optical properties. The UV−vis
absorption spectrum of the as-prepared Au NCs (Figure 1b)
shows four distinct absorption peaks at 440, 552, 670, and 760
nm. These peaks correspond well to the characteristic
absorption peaks of reported thiolate-protected Au25 NCs
(NCs contain 25 Au atoms).22 Moreover, the Au NCs do not
show any peak at ∼520 nm, which indicates that the as-
prepared Au NCs were homogeneously formed without any Au
NP byproduct. As shown in the inset photo of Figure 1b, the
Au NC solution is reddish-brown. This solution color is also
indentical to that for thiolated Au25 NCs. In addition, from the
inset TEM image of Figure 1b, the size of Au NCs was found to
be less than 2 nm. As a result, the surface to volume ratio of Au
NCs to Au NPs is approximately 3-fold. The molecular formula
of the as-prepared Au NCs was determined to be Au25MHA18.
This was evident from the electrospray ionization mass
spectrometry (ESI-MS) analysis depicted in Figure S2, in
which three sets of peaks at m/z 1262, 1514, and 1892 were
observed. These peaks correspond to [Au25(MHA)18 −5H]6−,
[Au25(MHA)18 −4H]5−, and [Au25(MHA)18 −3H]4−,
respectively. The detailed assignments are presented in the
middle and bottom panels of Figure S2. For the structure of
Au25MHA18, it has a centered icosahedral Au13 as the core. This
core is protected by 12 Au(I) and 18 thiolate ligands, which are
divided into six pairs of [SR−Au(I)−SR−Au(I)−SR] motifs
(SR represents thiolate ligand).39 Compared to the Au NPs,
our data suggest that the Au NCs possess better-deﬁned
structure and size. Consequently, the Au NCs could be
expected to behave in a more molecular-like manner when
compared to the Au NPs.
In good agreement with the above data, the hydrodynamic
diameter of Au NCs was ∼4 nm, whereas that of the Au NPs
was ∼10 nm (Figure S3). The ζ-potential values of Au NCs
and NPs were determined to be −30.6 ± 2.5 and −29.5 ± 1.6
eV, respectively. The similar ζ-potential value was expected as
both Au NPs and NCs are protected by the same thiolate
ligand. In addition, we also prepared Au(I)−MHA complexes
that will be utilized as a reference group later in the study. The
Au(I)−MHA complexes were produced by mixing the Au(III)
ions and MHA in the absence of reducing agent. As shown in
Figure 1c, the complexes do not show any absorption peak, and
the color of the solution remains colorless, further conﬁrming
the absence of Au NCs or NPs in solution.

To prove our hypothesis, we treated Gram-positive,
Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) with the same concentration
(0.1 mM on the basis of Au atoms) of Au25MHA18 (Au NCs)
and Au−MHA NPs (Au NPs). We utilized SYTOX green
nucleic acid stain to diﬀerentiate the viable and nonviable cells,
allowing us to assess the killing eﬃcacy of each treatment
group. As SYTOX green is inherently cell-impermeant, it would
penetrate cells with a compromised membrane, which
essentially are the dead cells. SYTOX green then stains the
dead cells’ nucleus, leaving cells with intact bacterial membrane
unstained. In addition, a cell-permeant nuclear stain Hoechst
33342 could stain the total bacteria population (alive and dead)
for normalization. Our ﬂuorescence imaging (Figure 2)
suggests a stark diﬀerence in the killing eﬃcacy among the
diﬀerent treatment groups. In particular, after exposure of S.
aureus to Au NCs for 2 h, a majority of the S. aureus population
were positively stained with the SYTOX green, indicating the
occurrence of signiﬁcant membrane damage. Our semi-
quantitative analysis suggests that the Au NCs have eﬀectively
killed ∼95% of the S. aureus. In contrast to the eﬀective killing
eﬀect of the Au NCs, the as-prepared Au NPs only induced
minimum killing eﬀect on S. aureus, which was only ∼3% of the
S. aureus population. Similarly, for the Gram-negative type
Escherichia coli (E. coli), Au NCs could kill a majority of the
bacteria population. We detected that approximately ∼96% of
bacteria population were killed after 2 h treatment, whereas
larger sized Au NPs could only kill ∼2% of the bacteria
population (Figure S4). It is possible that the diﬀerence in the
antimicrobial eﬃcacy between the Au NCs and Au NPs was
caused by their diﬀerent cell uptake proﬁle. As such, we
measured the internalization of both particle groups and found
there was no signiﬁcant diﬀerence on the amount of Au NCs
and Au NPs being uptaken by the cells (Figure S5). In addition,
the diﬀerent antimicrobial eﬃcacy could also stem from the
inherent size diﬀerence between the Au NCs and Au NPs,
which in turn resulted in the cells being dosed with diﬀerent
particle number. Through our meta-analysis (Figure S6), we
estimated that ∼15-fold higher concentration of Au NPs was
needed in order to reach the same particle number dosing as
the 0.1 mM of Au NCs used in this study. As such, we
introduced 2 mM of Au NPs (on the basis of Au atoms, which
is 20 times higher than the Au NC concentration used) to treat
the bacteria. We detected marginal increase on the Au NPs’
antimicrobial eﬃciency even at this high dose. Figure S6
showed that Au NPs could only achieve ∼8% killing eﬀect of
the S. aureus. Overall, these results further showcase the Au
NCs’ enhanced antimicrobial eﬃciency.
In order for the NPs to elicit antimicrobial eﬀect, they would
have to interact with the bacterial target. As such, their surface
landscape, which is embodied in the type of the surface ligand
used and the density of ligand on the surface, plays the major
role in determining the eﬃcacy of NP-based antimicrobial
agents. As both Au NPs and NCs are protected by the same
ligand (MHA), we could expect that the type of the ligand used
would not be the contributing factor to their diﬀerent
antimicrobial properties. To completely exclude the ligand
eﬀect, MHA ligand was introduced to the bacteria. We noted
that the MHA ligand alone did not show antimicrobial activity
(Figure S7), conﬁrming that the diﬀerent antimicrobial eﬀect
was not derived from the surface ligand.
The drastic size diﬀerence between the Au NCs and NPs also
suggests that they possess diﬀerent MHA ligand density on
their surface. Compared to their Au NP counterpart, the Au
NCs with a higher surface to volume ratio would possess a
higher MHA to Au ratio on their surface (per particle). This
will result in high localized carboxylic groups on the Au NC
surface. Therefore, we utilized poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) to test
whether the localized carboxylic groups around the Au NCs are
the cause for their bacteria killing eﬀect. The result (Figure S7)
showed that the PAA elicited no killing eﬀect on the S. aureus,
suggesting that the localized carboxylic groups around the Au
NCs are not the major contributors for their enhanced killing
eﬀect.
Moreover, our analysis of Au25 structure (Figure 1) indicates
that the surface of Au NCs possesses a high density of Au(I)−
SR motifs. Therefore, the diﬀerence in the ligand arrangement
could be another reason for the enhanced antimicrobial eﬃcacy
of Au NCs. To verify the role of the localized Au(I)−SR, we
introduced the same concentration (Au atoms basis) of Au(I)−
MHA complexes (Au(I) complex) to the S. aureus and E. coli. If
the antimicrobial eﬀect of Au NCs is dictated by the high
density of Au(I)−SR on their surface, these free-ﬂoating
Au(I)−MHA complexes would also eﬃciently kill the S. aureus
and E. coli. However, the Au(I)−MHA complexes showed
minimum killing eﬀect, as can be seen in Figure 2 and Figure
S4. Our semiquantitative analysis also corroborated this ﬁnding
as only ∼5% of the S. aureus population and ∼3% of E. coli
population were killed by treatment with Au(I)−MHA
complexes. Taking all these results into account, we deduced
that the enhanced antimicrobial eﬀect of Au NCs was not
merely determined by the ligand identity or its density on the
surface but by the overall ultrasmall Au NCs, as an entire entity
and compound.
We further examined whether the killing eﬃcacy of Au NCs
could also be observed in other bacterial species. Interestingly,
we discovered the broadness of Au NCs’ antimicrobial activity.
They are able to eﬃciently kill Gram-positive bacteria, S.
epidermidis and Bacillus subtilis, and Gram-negative bacteria, E.
coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. As shown in Figures 3 and S4,
the ﬂuorescence images suggest that a majority of the bacteria

NCs against each of the bacteria strains, we quantiﬁed the half-

eﬀectiveness to the traditional antimicrobials in the market
further highlights their potential as one of the alternative
antimicrobial agents. Moving forward, further studies need to
be done to ameliorate the concern over the overall price
production of the Au NCs that exceed these traditional
antibiotics and allow bench-to-bed translational application of
these NCs.

eﬃcacy to their capability to modulate the reactive oxygen
species (ROS) production that eventually kills the bacterial
cell.42 As such, we asked whether the antimicrobial eﬃcacy of
Au NCs was driven by the ROS production. As shown in Figure
4a, we noted that Au NCs induced about 3-fold increase in the
intracellular ROS production when compared to the control
group. In good agreement with our antimicrobial data in Figure
2, Au NPs did not induce obvious intracellular ROS production
in S. aureus (Figure 4a). Likewise, Au(I) complex, MHA ligand,
and PAA were unable to elicit intracellular ROS generation
(Figure S9), demonstrating good agreement with the
antimicrobial performance of the respective control com-
pounds. Further extending our investigation to other bacterial
strains, we noted that the Au NCs induced signiﬁcant increase
on the ROS production. The Au NC treatment induced about
2−3-fold increase of intracellular ROS as observed consistently
on four diﬀerent bacterial strains (Figure S10). Overall, these

ROS is typically produced as the consequence of cellular
metabolism. As such, we further investigated the eﬀect of Au
NCs on the metabolism of S. aureus, and this information
allows us to pinpoint the underlining mechanism that results in
the ROS generation and the subsequent antimicrobial eﬀect.
Our Genechip microarray analysis suggests that multiple genes
regulating the S. aureus metabolism were perturbed by the
treatment of bacteria with Au NCs (Figure 4b; the detailed
abbreviation of genes can be found in Table S1). In particular, a
cluster of genes responsible for the expression of metabolic
enzyme that promotes the oxidation process was signiﬁcantly
up-regulated following the Au NC treatment. For instance, we
noted 1.6-fold up-regulation of dmpI gene that encodes 4-
oxalocrotonate tautomerase. This enzyme is responsible for
oxidative catabolism of toluene, o-xylene, 3-ethyltoluene, and
1,2,4-trimethylbenzene into their intermediates of the citric acid
cycle, producing ROS as its enzymatic byproduct.43 The
signiﬁcant up-regulation of genes encoding the oxidative
metabolic enzymes suggests that Au NCs have induced more
pro-oxidative enzymes to be intracellularly expressed in S.
aureus. Exacerbating the matter further, a number of genes
encoding the enzymes that could counterbalance this oxidative
metabolic reaction were suppressed upon the treatment with
Au NCs. We noted 1.8-fold down-regulation of ilvC gene
encoding ketol acid reductoisomerase, which requires ROS to
reduce (R)-2,3-dihydroxy-3-methylbutanoate to (S)-2-hydroxy-
2-methyl-3-oxobutanoate.44 Furthermore, Gapdh, the known
intracellular ROS scavenger, was reduced by 2.48-fold, which
could inhibit the cells’ capability to reduce the abundant ROS.45
These results clearly suggest that the Au NCs have created a
metabolic imbalance in the cells by simultaneously promoting
the pro-oxidative enzymes up-regulation while suppressing
reductive enzymes, leading to the overall accumulation of
intracellular ROS. Contrasted with the lackluster bacterial
killing performance of Au NPs, we observed lesser perturbation
to the metabolic pathways in the Au NP-treated cells. The
metabolic gene expression levels of the Au NP-treated group
showed lesser expression level change when compared to the
Au NC group (Figure 4b).
To further establish the importance of cellular metabolism in
the antimicrobial activity of Au NCs, we reduced cell
metabolism to its minimum by decreasing the exposure
temperature to 4 °C. If the metabolic activity of the bacteria
cell is important to the antimicrobial performance of Au NCs,
reducing the metabolic activity would considerably reduce the
killing eﬀect of Au NCs. Indeed, reducing the metabolic activity
of the cells could signiﬁcantly suppress the ROS production
induced by Au NCs (Figure S11a). We noted that the ROS
production induced by Au NCs was reduced by 200% in the
metabolically inactive S. aureus when compared to their
metabolically active counterparts (Figure 4a). Consequently,
halting the metabolic activity of the bacteria has signiﬁcantly
reduced the killing eﬀect of Au NCs (Figure S11b). We
observed ∼97% of the cells were still viable following the
treatment with Au NCs on the metabolically inactive S. aureus.
Active metabolism is necessary for the Au NC internalization to
the cells. As such, we asked further whether the signiﬁcant
reversal in Au NCs’ antimicrobial eﬃciency in the inactive S.
aureus was mainly caused by the reduction in their internal-
ization to the cells. Indeed, our ICP-OES data (Figure S11c)
suggest that the uptake of Au NCs into metabolically inactive
bacteria (4 °C) was reduced by ∼70% when compared to
normal condition (37 °C). It is worth noting that even at
reduced internalization level, there would be 0.37 μg/mg of Au
NCs inside the inactive S aureus. As such, if Au NC
internalization was the main factor of their antimicrobial
mechanism, we would expect to witness a proportional
reduction in the antimicrobial killing eﬀect and not the drastic
reversal of the antimicrobial eﬃcacy that we observed in Figure
S11b. Overall, these results suggest that cell metabolism and
internalization work hand-in-hand in modulating the anti-
microbial activity of Au NCs. The sweet spot in the
temperature-dependent activity agrees well with striking the
bacteria by Au NCs when they are metabolically most active.
In addition, the modulatory role of the cellular machinery
(i.e., the gene expression as well as the ROS production)
strongly suggests that the internalization of Au NCs is required
for the Au NCs to confer their antimicrobial properties. Taken
together, our experimental data suggest that the Au NCs
internalized by bacteria cells could induce the intracellular
metabolic imbalance that results in an increase of intracellular
ROS production and the subsequent killing eﬀect.
Besides the inﬂuence of Au NCs toward cellular metabolism,
they could also cause damage to bacterial membrane, as shown
in Figure 4b. It was found that the genes related to membrane
integrity, autolysin (Alt and SA1898), were up-regulated,
indicating that there are some damages on the bacterial
membrane, such that the bacterial membrane becomes
compromised. This assertion on membrane damage as a
consideration is further supported by our SYTOX data, which
directly relies on membrane integrity (Figure 2). Consequen-
tially, the genes related to surface membrane structure were all
down-regulated; that is, SA0129 gene encoding the cell wall
surface anchor family protein was 1.92-fold reduced, suggesting
that the damaged bacterial membrane could not be repaired.
This result is in line with the ﬂuorescence imaging data (Figure
2), where the SYTOX green stained and identiﬁed those
bacteria with damaged membrane after treatment with Au NCs.
Moreover, Au NCs also inﬂuenced the genes involved in
transcription and translation (down-regulated, Figure 4b). For
example, tnpA gene, which encodes transposon Tn554 related
to resistance to erythromycin and spectinomycin, was 6.58-fold
decreased.46 This suggests that after treatment with the Au
NCs, bacteria lose their innate ability to resist further antibiotic
attack, therefore making them easier to be killed. Perhaps from
that logic, it would be feasible to supplement current antibiotics
regimes with the Au NCs developed in this study to further
lower the antibiotic resistance.
For any antimicrobial formulation to achieve bench-to-bed
translation, it has to be very safe to the human host. To ensure
the Au NCs’ safe utilization, we investigated their cytotoxic
eﬀect on human cells. We noted that Au NCs did not elicit
cytotoxicity toward human intestinal mucosa epithelial cell line
(NCM460) (for the oral route) and human microvascular
endothelial cells (HMVEC) (for the intravenous route) at the
same concentration used on the bacteria (Figure S12). Further
dose increase (5 times higher than the initial dose) did not
result in any obvious toxic eﬀect on the tested human cell types,
as well. This is consistent with our previous report that neither
cell death nor DNA damage was observed following Au NC
treatment on the NCM460 cells.30

CONCLUSION
In conclusion, we have demonstrated that it is possible to
confer antimicrobial activity to Au NPs, which have long been
considered as inert, by way of controlling their sizes to the NC
range. In particular, these sub-2 nm sized Au NCs showed
antimicrobial eﬀect that was absent from their larger counter-
part, Au NPs (>2 nm). Au NCs could kill both the Gram-
positive and Gram-negative bacteria, showing their potential as
a wide-spectrum bactericide. This wide-spectrum antimicrobial
activity is attributed to the Au NCs’ ultrasmall size, allowing
them to possess high surface to volume ratio. Consequently,
when the Au NCs were internalized, this active surface could
induce a metabolic imbalance in the cells, which leads to an
increase of intracellular ROS production, conferring bacteria
killing eﬀect as the end result.

Synthesis of Au NCs, Au NPs, and Au(I) Complex. MHA-
Protected Au NCs. The synthesis of Au NCs followed a reported
method.22 In a typical synthesis, freshly prepared aqueous solutions of
HAuCl4 (20 mM, 0.25 mL) and MHA (10 mM, 1 mL) were mixed in
water (3.35 mL), leading to the formation of white Au(I)−MHA
complexes. After that, an aqueous NaOH solution (1 M, 0.3 mL) was
added to dissolve the mixture. A freshly prepared NaBH4 solution
(112 mM) was obtained by dissolving 43 mg of NaBH4 in 2 mL of
NaOH solution (1 M), followed by the addition of 8 mL of ultrapure
water. After that, 0.1 mL of NaBH4 solution was added into Au(I)−
MHA complexes, and the Au NCs were collected after 3 h. After
synthesis, a stirred ultraﬁltration cell (model 8010, Millipore
Corporation, USA) with a semipermeable membrane of 3000 Da
molecular weight cutoﬀwas used to purify Au NCs in solution.
MHA-Protected Au NPs. Freshly prepared aqueous solutions of
HAuCl4 (20 mM, 0.25 mL) and MHA (10 mM, 0.1 mL) were mixed
in water (4.65 mL), leading to the formation of white Au(I)−MHA
complexes. After that, an aqueous NaOH solution (1 M) was added to
dissolve the mixture and to bring the pH to ∼12. In the last step, 0.05
mL of 112 mM NaBH4 solution (dissolving 43 mg of NaBH4 in 2 mL
of 1 M NaOH solution and then adding 8 mL of ultrapure water) was
introduced, and the Au NPs were collected after 3 h. After synthesis,
ethanol was used to wash the Au NPs in solution.
Au(I)−MHA Complexes. Freshly prepared aqueous solutions of
HAuCl4 (20 mM, 0.25 mL) and MHA (10 mM, 0.5 mL) were mixed
in water (4.25 mL), leading to the formation of white Au(I)−MHA
complexes. After that, an aqueous NaOH solution (1 M) was added
until all the complexes were dissolved in water.

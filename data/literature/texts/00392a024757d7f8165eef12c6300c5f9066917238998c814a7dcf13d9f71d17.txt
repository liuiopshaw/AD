The interactions between inorganic-based nanomaterials (NMs) and biological membranes are among
the most important phenomena for developing NM-based therapeutics and resolving nanotoxicology.
Herein, we introduce the structural and functional effects of inorganic-based NMs on biological mem-
branes, mainly the plasma membrane and the endomembrane system, with an emphasis on the interface,
which involves highly complex networks between NMs and biomolecules (such as membrane proteins
and lipids). Signiﬁcant efforts have been devoted to categorizing and analyzing the interaction mecha-
nisms in terms of the physicochemical characteristics and biological effects of NMs, which can directly
or indirectly inﬂuence the effects of NMs on membranes. Importantly, we summarize that the biological
membranes act as platforms and thereby mediate NMs-immune system contacts. In this overview, the
existing challenges and potential applications in the areas are addressed. A strong understanding of
the discussed concepts will promote therapeutic NM designs for drug delivery systems by leveraging
the NMs-membrane interactions and their functions.

1. Introduction
Nanomedicine is a rapidly developing science and the wide-
spread use of nanomaterials (NMs) in biomedicine is mostly based
on their nanometer size, which allows them to easily pass through
biological barriers (such as the blood–brain barrier and placental
barrier) to reach target tissues, organs and cells [1,2]. To date,
the most well-studied NMs including inorganic NMs (e.g., metallic
and carbon-based nanoparticles), lipid-based NMs (e.g., liposomes)
and polymeric NMs (e.g., chitosan and dendrimers) have shown
great
potential
in
the
ﬁeld
of
nanomedicine
[3,4],
where
inorganic-based NMs are not only highly efﬁcient carriers but also
therapeutic agents. Although many studies have revealed that
inorganic-based NMs can induce intracellular biological events
after internalization [5], a systematic review is needed about the
structural and functional effects of NMs on biological membranes,
which are not only the most important platforms for catalysis and
cellular metabolism processes but can also form barriers or plat-
forms for NMs functions.
In this review, to comprehensively summarize different types of
inorganic NM-membrane interactions, biological membranes are
classiﬁed into two main categories, the plasma membrane (PM)
and the endomembrane system, which also includes the nuclear
membrane and vesicle membranes. Although these biological
membranes all consist of a phospholipid bilayer, speciﬁc lipids,
proteins and other biomolecules endow different membranes with
unique functions. For example, the PM acts as a communication
bridge between the extra- and intracellular environment and it is
the ﬁrst cellular interface that NMs contact [6]. After NMs pass
through the PM, they continue to act on organelle membranes,
which separate the cellular compartments and provide sites for
the catalytic reaction of enzymes. Furthermore, organelles coordi-
nate and communicate with one another via vesicular trafﬁcking
pathways and membrane contact sites (MCSs) [7], which are also
targeted by NMs. Most importantly, alterations in the function
and structure of biological membranes are likely crucial factors
that contribute to NMs-induced cellular responses, including but
not limited to nanotoxicological effects [8,9]. This review is the
ﬁrst step to comprehensively illuminating the interaction between
NMs and cellular biological membranes, covering toxic and physi-
ological membrane alterations and unearthing potential mecha-
nisms that might provide valuable information to guide the
design and application of safer and more efﬁcient nanomedical
technologies.
2. The PM as the ﬁrst gateway that interacts with NMs
The PM acts as a permeability barrier and serves as a platform
for various functional complexes, including receptors, ion channels
and lipid rafts. These complexes, which are composed of proteins,
lipids and carbohydrates, are involved in cell–cell interactions, sig-
nal transduction, material transport and other cellular functions.
The PM is the ﬁrst site of contact and usually the target of NMs;
thus, alterations in the PM result in various cellular responses. In
this review, NM-PM interactions are mainly categorized as effects
on (i) PM physiology, (ii) PM proteins and (iii) PM lipids.
2.1. Effects on membrane physiology
The morphology/topography and ﬂuidity/mobility of the PM are
dynamic and ﬂexible in the physiological state, and maintenance of
integrity is a prerequisite for the function of the PM. When NMs
target the PM or pass through the PM (also known as uptake of
NMs), the physiological properties or integrity of the PM are

altered, and this in turn can have diverse impacts on cellular
reactions.
The roughness and stiffness of the PM have been reported to be
affected by NM-PM interactions. Treatment with silver (Ag) or gold
(Au) nanoparticles (NPs) or graphene can increase the roughness
and stiffness of the PM, leading to various membrane-related
effects such as contraction of lamellipodia, decreased cellular
adhesion [10,11], and membrane rufﬂing [12–14]. Furthermore,
the elasticity of the PM was found to be reduced after interaction
with cerium oxide (CeO2) NPs [15]. These depressive effects on
membrane physiological properties can further induce membra-
nous deformation and collapse, leading to a reduction in cellular
height or loss of cell–cell contact inhibition [13,16,17]. However,
positive effects of NMs with speciﬁc functionalizations on mem-
brane physiological properties have also been reported. Wang
et al. [18] reported that D-penicillamine-coated quantum dots
(QDs) soften the PM, rendering it more ﬂexible to allow passive
penetration of the QDs without affecting the overall membrane
integrity. Additionally, PEGylated graphene oxide (GO) shows
diminished membrane-cutting capability and enhances membrane
mobility and diffusion within the membrane, thereby promoting
cell migration [19]. It can be suggested that the NM-PM interaction
process will inevitably affect the physiological properties of the
PM, resulting in diverse inﬂuences on membrane dynamics and
cellular responses. Utilizing these effects properly will be valuable
for therapeutic nanomedical applications.
Loss of PM integrity after NMs treatment is another common
phenomenon and results in efﬂux of cellular contents and
decreased cell viability. Studies that used lactate dehydrogenase
(LDH) release as an indicator of PM integrity loss found that many
types of NMs increase LDH release in a dose- or time-dependent
manner [16,20–22]. Moreover, NMs such as Ag NPs [23], titanium
dioxide (TiO2) NPs [24], and graphene family nanomaterials (GFNs)
[25,26] can destroy PM integrity and lead to leakage of intracellular
components, highlighting the potential of such NMs in anticancer
and antibacterial applications. In terms of the structure of the
PM, injury to membrane integrity is closely related to NMs-
induced degradation of membrane components, which leads to cell
autolysis [25] or to the formation of pores ranging in size from tens
of nanometers to a few micrometers through membrane fragments
detachment (Fig. 1a) [13,27]. Furthermore, because they can simu-
late the relationship between NMs and membranes, model mem-
branes and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have been
applied to expand theoretical research and to identify the intrinsic
mechanism underlying alterations in PM integrity [28,29]. Accord-
ing to MD simulations, Au NPs form hydrophilic pores that extend
through the entire upper leaﬂet of the bilayer membrane, which
increases NP penetration and leads to water inﬂux, resulting in cell
swelling and even PM rupture (Fig. 1b) [30]. Nonetheless, many
studies have not identiﬁed temporary or permanent alterations
in PM integrity after exposure to NMs, which has led to confusion
about their recovery possibilities and possible uses.
2.2. Effects on membrane proteins
Membrane proteins, including both transmembrane proteins
and those that attach loosely to one side of the PM, serve as recep-
tors,
transport
proteins
(e.g.,
ion
channels/aquaporins)
and
enzymes and play key roles in cellular signal transduction, sub-
stance exchange and other cell biological reactions [31]. In current
studies, these proteins have been regarded as important targets of
NMs.
NMs can bind speciﬁcally to the domains of membrane recep-
tors and thereby trigger recruitment of intracellular molecules to
the PM, resulting in membrane receptor-mediated biological
effects such as endocytosis and inﬂammation [5]. For example,
NMs such as silica (SiO2), TiO2 and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) can
bind directly to diverse membrane receptors (SR-A1, MARCO,
CD36, and others), leading to inﬂammasome activation and
interleukin-1b (IL-1b) secretion [32]. However, these receptor-
binding effects depend largely on the characteristics of the NMs,
including their surface coating, size and shape, all of which directly
determine the types of ligands with which the NMs interact [33].
Additionally, NMs can indirectly activate or alter the expression
of membrane receptor proteins. GO has been reported to increase
the level of transforming growth factor-b (TGF-b) receptor, which
later activates downstream TGF-b-Smad2/3 signaling that drives
epithelial-mesenchymal transition in tumor cells [17]. Other stud-
ies have indicated that GO [16,19,34], multi-walled carbon nan-
otubes (MWCNTs) [35] and SiO2 NPs [36] can activate or alter
the expression of integrin proteins and later activate intracellular
FAK/Src-related pathways, ultimately regulating cellular functions
such as proliferation and cytoskeletal reorganization.
As one of the most important types of membrane transport pro-
teins, ion channels exhibit unique pore structures, thus providing
pathways for ions to cross the PM. In a previous summary, we indi-
cated that NMs can impact the structure and function of ion chan-
nels in three main ways: (i) by directly physically blocking the
channel pore; (ii) by changing the ﬂexibility and/or ﬂuidity of the
PM and thereby indirectly affecting ion channel permeability;
and (iii) by shedding ions, resulting in ion antagonism (Fig. 2)
[37]. Through these mechanisms, NMs can alter ion currents and
ion exchange, thus inﬂuencing intra- and extracellular ion metabo-
lism and altering the membrane potential [11]. Importantly, these
effects may ultimately lead to physiological or pathological
changes in the nervous system and in cardiac and muscle tissues
[38–40].
Membrane enzymes, another type of membrane protein, act as
catalysts of cell biological reactions. NMs are capable of activating
membrane enzymes through cellular signaling pathways, thus
modulating a series of associated cellular processes. For example,
reduced GO can activate NADPH oxidases (NOXs) in neurons by
increasing the translocation of NOX cytoplasmic subunits to the
PM, leading to overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS),
and initiation of protein kinase C (PKC) signaling was shown to
be the likely key factor in this process [41]. Additionally, NMs
can directly inhibit the function of membrane enzymes. FOF1-
ATPase is an ATPase located on the PM and on the membranes of
intracellular organelles and is responsible for energy conversion
in cells via H+ transport [42]. Ag and Fe3O4 NPs have been reported
to reduce the activity of FOF1-ATPase through direct interaction,
thereby dissipating H+-coupled K+ transport and the proton gradi-
ent across the bacterial PM, changing membrane permeability and
resulting in antibacterial effects [43,44]. Although ATPase is a sen-
sitive target for some types of NMs, the magnitude of the antibac-
terial effect can vary due to the different membrane structures of
gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. More importantly,
membrane-bound enzymes are diverse and play various roles in
cellular metabolism, and the current understanding of the interac-
tions between NMs and membrane enzymes and the subsequent
cellular reactions is still insufﬁcient and lacks speciﬁcity.
2.3. Effects on membrane lipids
In addition to proteins, membrane lipids, including phospho-
lipids, glycolipids, and sterols, form the lipid bilayer [31]. Progress
in lipidomics has made it possible to achieve a more comprehen-
sive understanding of the complex and interrelated changes in
NM-PM lipid interactions. One of the major ﬁndings of such studies
is that NMs induce changes in the expression and distribution of
PM lipids. It was reported that GO upregulated the expression of
phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylinositol and cholesteryl

esters and downregulated the expression of phosphatidylserine in
cortical neurons, while few-layer graphene reduced the expression
of phosphatidylcholine but increased that of triacylglycerols [45].
Similar to these effects, GFNs can increase membrane cholesterol
levels and thereby participate in regulation of Ca2+ signaling via
the membrane stromal-interacting molecule 1/calcium release-
activated calcium channel protein 1 (STIM/Orai1) complex in
astrocytes [45] or P2Y receptors in neurons [53]. Besides, certain
types of NMs trigger translocation of phosphatidylserine from
the inner PM leaﬂet to the outer leaﬂet upon cellular apoptosis
[46–48]. These alterations in PM lipid expression and distribution
primarily occur due to indirect modulation by NMs; for example,
activation of certain intracellular pathways and resulting changes
in membrane lipid metabolism may occur during NMs treatment.
However, the signal transduction pathways associated with lipid
metabolism are complicated, and how NMs modulate these path-
ways to regulate PM lipids is still obscure.
Membrane lipids also contribute to ﬁlling in structural defects
and to the formation of lipid rafts (cholesterol and sphingolipid-
rich domains) in the PM [31,49]. This suggests that NMs-induced

3. NMs interact with organelle membranes in eukaryotes
Compared with eukaryotic cells, prokaryotes have few membra-
nous organelles in the cytoplasm; therefore, this chapter primarily
discusses the interaction between NMs and organelle membranes
in eukaryotic cells. Organelle membranes divide the cell into vari-
ous functional and structural compartments, and they work
together to perform crucial cellular functions, such as synthesis
and transport of proteins and transduction of intracellular signals.
The sophisticated communication and cooperation between these
compartments depend heavily on vesicle trafﬁcking and MCSs
[7]. Reviewing the way in which NMs interact with the endomem-
brane system (Fig. 3) help to reveal the intracellular journey of
NMs and explore their potential application prospects.
3.1. Vesicular and lysosomal membranes
After contact with the PM, most NMs are translocated to endo-
cytic vesicles and interact with their membranes. Generally, NMs
that enter the cell via clathrin-mediated endocytosis end up in
lysosomes, and this may not be suitable for NPs modiﬁed with
materials that are susceptible to lysosomal degradation. However,
caveolin-dependent mechanisms can sometimes help NMs escape
lysosomal degradation, a fact that has been exploited in nanocar-
rier design [5]. After their escape into the cytoplasm, NMs encoun-
ter other organelle membranes [65], resulting in changes in the
structure and function of these membrane-bound organelles and
subsequently inducing various cellular responses.
3.1.1. Vesicular membranes
The vesicular membrane originates from the PM or from orga-
nelle membranes via membrane budding to form vesicles (such
as endosomes, phagosomes, and synaptic vesicles). After being
swallowed via phagocytosis, macropinocytosis or micropinocyto-
sis, the NMs contained in vesicles are delivered to phagosomes,
macropinosomes and early endosomes [6]. The intact membranes
of these vesicles ensure that NMs remain inside and are trans-
ported smoothly to the lysosomes for degradation. This process
protects cells from the adverse effects that might be caused by
NMs and promotes the application of NMs designed to target lyso-
somes. However, numerous studies have indicated that NMs can
interfere with the stability and integrity of vesicular membranes
by inducing osmotic rupture (the proton-sponge effect), inner
polymer swelling or the formation of membranous pores and
thereby escape from endosomes/phagosomes [5,6,31,65].
For
example, oxidized carbon NPs can form pores on endosome mem-
branes, resulting in leakage of loaded agents and cytoplasmic local-
ization of the NPs [66,67]. Similar results were observed for
platinum-based magnetic NPs [68] and Au NPs [69]. Furthermore,
vesicle membrane functions can also be affected. Negatively
charged Au NPs are capable of blocking the binding between the
subunits of vacuolar V-ATPase, which makes the endosome

3.1.2. Lysosomal membranes
Most NMs that remain inside endosomes are delivered to lyso-
somes, where membrane integrity and stabilization are prerequi-
sites for maintaining the acidic environment and lysosomal
degradative capacity [74]. However, NMs can destabilize or induce
pore formation in the membrane, thereby increasing lysosomal
membrane permeabilization (LMP) and resulting in release of lyso-
somal contents such as degradative enzymes (e.g., cathepsins) [75–
78]. The lipid composition of the lysosomal membrane has an
important membrane stabilizing effect, and lipid defects increase
LMP [79], suggesting that NMs can regulate LMP through their
effects on membrane lipids. Similarly, Yu et al. [80] reported that
rutile TiO2 NPs, which have a higher afﬁnity for lipids than anatase
NPs, adhere to the lysosomal inner membrane and increase LMP.
As mentioned above, some NMs can directly bind and extract lipids
from membranes, and this is likely the underlying mechanism,
which still needs to be veriﬁed.
Furthermore, NMs can indirectly modulate lysosome metabo-
lism through various routes. First, the proton pump on the lysoso-
mal membrane participates in maintaining the acidic environment
of lysosomes, which is conducive to the dissolution of ion-
shedding NMs (e.g., CuO [81], ZnO [82] and rare earth oxides
(REOs) [75]), leading to lysosomal proton consumption and alter-
ations in membrane protein expression [83,84], ultimately causing
luminal alkalization. Additionally, some released ions show high-
afﬁnity binding to free lysosomal phosphates and can even strip
the phosphate groups from phospholipids in the lysosomal mem-
brane [75], resulting in loss of membrane integrity. In addition to
ion-shedding NMs, other NMs, such as Au NPs, have been reported
to decrease the activity of vacuolar V-ATPase, the main proton
pump that maintains the acidic pH of lysosomes, by dissociating
its subunits, thereby causing lysosomal alkalization [85]. The
above results show that NMs can affect the structure of ATPases
located on endolysosomal vesicles and block their function. The
most likely reason for this blocking effect is the direct competitive
binding of NMs to the subunits of ATPase, thus decreasing the
binding between the V1 and V0 proteins, but more exploration
should be conducted.
Similar to the vesicle fusion reviewed above, lysosome fusion
and ﬁssion-based output participate in membrane exchange
between lysosomes and the PM, endosomes and autophagosomes.
For example, repair of PM damage requires recruitment of lyso-
somes to fuse with the PM and replace the disrupted portion of
the membrane, and autophagy requires autophagosomes to deliver
the substrates they contain to lysosomes [74]. Many recent studies
have indicated that NMs block the fusion between lysosomes and
autophagosomes [83,85,86]. In these cases, the ability of the
deacidiﬁed lysosomes to fuse with autophagosomes was sup-
pressed, leading to blockage of autophagic ﬂux and autophago-
some accumulation. Intriguingly, ubiquitin-related proteins, such
as galectin-3 and ULK, can be recruited to the disrupted lysosome
and cause the autophagosome to undergo phagocytosis in a pro-
cess that is termed lysophagy [74]. This is a selective autophagic
process that recycles impaired lysosomes, and it may operate nor-
mally to some extent during NMs treatment [83]. Importantly, the
detailed modulatory effects of NMs on lysophagy remain a com-
plex issue that requires more in-depth exploration. Furthermore,
because the lysosomal membrane plays an important role in the
membrane repair system, whether NM-induced lysosome disrup-
tion affects this system is not yet understood.
During delivery and degradation of internalized NMs, the NMs
can escape endosomes and lysosomes by disturbing the membrane
stability and integrity of these organelles, thereby enabling some
NMs to release drugs or target other cellular compartments; how-
ever, this process is not beneﬁcial for controlling the biological
risks associated with the use of NMs. Some NMs can also target
lysosomes and promote lysosome-mediated cell death by inducing
lysosome alkalization and increasing LMP, which is of great signif-
icance for enhancing the anticancer effect of NMs [87].
3.2. Nuclear membranes
The nuclear membrane, also known as the nuclear envelope,
consists of inner and outer membranes. On these membranes,
nuclear pore structures are formed by nucleoporin proteins. Gener-
ally, nuclear pore complexes (NPCs) have an outer diameter
of ~ 80–120 nm and an inner diameter of ~ 40 nm [88] and are
important in mediating nucleocytoplasmic material exchange,
including active transport of large biomolecules and passive diffu-
sion of small molecules. Thus, NPCs provide a possible pathway for
uptake of NMs, such as Au NPs [89], Ag NPs [90] and graphene QDs
[91], and this process is closely related to the function and size of
NPCs. It appears that there are three possible ways for NMs to
translocate into the nucleus: (i) NMs smaller than 9 ~ 39 nm can
passively diffuse into the nucleus through the NPCs, while larger
NMs can enter the nucleus through active transport [92–94]; (ii)
the active transport of NMs can be facilitated by nuclear localiza-
tion signals (NLSs) [93,95]; and (iii) NMs can pass through the
membrane into the nucleus during cell division [96,97]. For exam-
ple, after binding to cytoplasmic importins to trigger NLS, mSiO2
NPs with diameters of up to 50 nm were found to enter the nucleus
through NPCs [98]. However, Au NPs conjugated to NLS (Au
NPs@NLS) accumulated in the perinuclear region but were not
translocated into the nucleus, probably because the size of the
aggregated Au NPs exceeded the threshold [69]. Therefore, the
speciﬁc characteristics of NMs, as well as the aggregation effect,
should be considered when applying NMs to enter the nucleus.
Moreover, the effects of NMs located in the perinuclear region
on the nuclear membrane are important. Lammel et al. [12]
reported that GO and carboxyl graphene caused nuclear membrane
invagination, although the integrity of the membrane remained
unchanged. Similar results were observed for high-aspect-ratio
NMs such as CuO nanowires (Fig. 4a) [99]. In addition to morpho-
logical changes, aggregation of the abovementioned Au NPs@NLS
in the perinuclear region increased nuclear membrane stiffness,
evidenced by the upregulated expression of lamin A/C, a mem-
brane protein that plays a key role in mediating nuclear membrane
stiffness and stabilization. The increased expression of proteins
that stabilize the nuclear architecture indicates that such close

The nucleus is the control center of the cell and is the main

3.3. Mitochondrial membranes
Mitochondria are the key organelles in control of ATP synthesis,
cellular redox balance and calcium homeostasis. They are double-
membrane organelles with an outer and inner mitochondrial
membrane (OMM and IMM, respectively) containing cristae that
are formed by folds in the IMM. The OMM contains embedded
ion channels and porins and plays a key role in the transport of
material, while the IMM is the site of mitochondrial respiratory
complexes I-IV (also known as the respiratory electron transport
chain, ETC) and ATP synthase, the major enzymes in energy meta-
bolism [104]. Numerous studies have observed that NMs can target
mitochondria and can even be translocated into mitochondria
[41,105,106]. During targeting and translocation, NMs can interact
with the mitochondrial membrane, inducing a series of responses
in mitochondrial outer membrane permeability, mitochondrial
permeability transition (MPT), mitochondrial membrane potential
(MMP) and ROS and ATP production [107].
The swelling of mitochondria and the destruction or even disap-
pearance of cristae are types of damage originated from the inter-
action between NMs and the mitochondrial membrane [108,109].
These mitochondrial conformational changes appear to be related
to the induction of an osmotic pressure imbalance between the
OMM and IMM or MPT pore openings, an imbalance that is usually
accompanied by a decrease in MMP [110,111]. Speciﬁcally, NMs-
induced MPT pore opening enables K+ and H+ inﬂux and increases
mitochondrial membrane ﬂuidity, thus leading to mitochondrial
swelling, and the underlying mechanism is probably the high-
afﬁnity binding of NMs to membrane proteins (e.g., adenine
nucleotide
translocase)
[110,112].

targets of NMs, and the responses of mitochondria to NMs con-

antibacterial efﬁciency, and other processes. Recent studies have
conﬁrmed that targeting of mitochondrial membranes by NMs
can lead to oxidative stress damage and abnormal energy metabo-

and recycle depolarized mitochondria. In addition, a shuttle system
that disposes of mildly damaged mitochondria operates via
mitochondria-derived vesicles. These double-membrane vesicles

to endolysosomes for degradation and release [118,121], although
the detailed molecular mechanism remains unclear. Whether NMs
can trigger this process when mitochondrial damage occurs is an
interesting topic that needs further exploration.
3.4. ER membranes and ER-mediated MCSs
The ER is a single, large, continuous membrane system that
starts at the nuclear envelope and displays two basic types of mor-
phology: interconnected tubules and sheets [122,123]. Studies per-
formed to date have helped assign tubules to rough ER and sheets
to smooth ER, which together function in the synthesis and trans-
port of proteins and lipids [124]. Importantly, the ER does not oper-
ate independently but forms MCSs with multiple membrane
systems, including the PM and organelle membranes; these MCSs
are correlated with the functional demands of the cell, such as
the exchange of lipids and Ca2+ and the ﬁssion and movement of
organelles [123,125]. Therefore, alterations in ER morphology and
membrane dynamics are not only linked to ER homeostasis but
also play key roles in regulation of cellular function [126].
Studies have indicated that NMs can induce morphological and
functional alterations in the ER membrane that can later induce ER
stress (accumulation of unfolded proteins in the ER) [127]. ER
membrane expansion, swelling, vacuolization and even rupture
are the most typical morphological alterations observed after
treatment with NMs [20,46,47,128]. The unfolded protein response
is activated to manage ER stress. If the problems are not solved by
the three known ER transmembrane proteins PERK, IRE1 and ATF6,
ER-mediated apoptosis might be initiated, mainly by activating
mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)-caspase 3/12 signaling
[46,127]. Thus, ER stress is considered a common nanotoxicological
response, but the revealed mechanism appears to be superﬁcial.
Whether treatment with NMs decreases the rate of protein folding
or blocks the clearance of unfolded proteins, thereby inducing ER
stress, or whether NMs that have been translocated to the ER
[47,129] directly intervene in the abovementioned processes is
worth studying. Moreover, ER-phagy, a selective autophagic
machinery for ER quality control [130], can be activated after
NMs exposure [20], delivering the excess ER membrane generated
during stress to lysosomes for clearance. The detailed dynamic pro-
cesses and the relevant molecules associated with various NMs
treatment are poorly understood.
The ER lumen houses the major Ca2+ store in mammalian cells
and releases Ca2+ through ion channels such as ryanodine recep-
tors and inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor (IP3R) channels.
Some internalized NMs can activate phospholipase C (PLC), leading
to higher-afﬁnity binding between IP3 and IP3R, which promotes
Ca2+ ﬂux from the ER [128,131]. This Ca2+ release is likely the
key mechanism through which NMs cause disturbances in intracel-
lular Ca2+ distribution and subsequent biological effects. Recently,
the concept of ER-mitochondrion contact sites, also known as
MAMs, has opened new avenues of research because the IP3R at
these sites transmits Ca2+ from the ER to mitochondria [123,132].
NMs, such as Ag, have been reported to increase mitochondrial
Ca2+ levels originating from the ER by extending the length of
MAMs and with the help of IP3R [128]. The ER-mediated mito-
chondrial Ca2+ overload pathway is also thought to be the principal
factor in cell apoptosis induction [46,133]. Moreover, ER-PM con-
tacts (EPCs) are also inﬂuenced by NMs treatment and are involved
in regulation of ER calcium homeostasis. Therein, STIM1, an ER Ca2+
depletion sensor, can translocate to EPCs and activate Orai1 cal-
cium channels on the PM, leading to inﬂux of extracellular Ca2+
into the ER [131,134], a common method of Ca2+ supplementation
of the ER. However, NMs such as upconversion NPs [135] and GO
[45] can directly modulate the structure of STIM1 to stimulate
Orai1 and can activate the STIM1/Orai1 complex by increasing
the amount of cholesterol in the PM. Both of these pathways func-
tion without ER Ca2+ depletion and thus may cause ER Ca2+ over-
load, leading to ER stress. Therefore, the existence of EPCs
provides a viable approach by which NMs can modulate ER func-
tion without entering cells.
Because the ER membrane is an important target of NMs, cellu-
lar protein/lipid synthesis and calcium homeostasis are susceptible
to their action, which subsequently inﬂuences kinds of cellular
metabolic and physiological responses, including muscle contrac-
tion, neuronal transmission and cell motility [126,127]. Although
many studies have explored the interaction effects between NMs
and the ER membrane, some have mainly focused on alterations
in NMs-induced ER stress-related pathways but have given less
attention to the effects of physical contact with NMs. Additionally,
how NMs interact with ribosomes, which are important organelles
located on the rough ER membrane, and thereby affect protein syn-
thesis is still an intriguing issue that has not been fully explored.
ER-mediated MCSs form networks that communicate with and
transfer materials (e.g., transmission of Ca2+ and transfer of pro-
teins and lipids) to other organelles between adjacent bilayers
rather than by vesicular transport, which leads to more efﬁcient
material transfer between organelles (Fig. 5) [123,132]. For exam-
ple, EPCs participate in the delivery of newly synthesized lipids
from the ER to the PM and in the return of lipid metabolites from
the PM to the ER for recycling [132,136]. One thing is certain: ER-
mediated
MCS-dependent
calcium
homeostasis
and
material
transport make MCSs potential targets for drug intervention in
related disorders. Moreover, whether MCSs form a pathway
through which NMs can be transported to adjacent organelles is

3.5. Golgi apparatus membranes
The Golgi apparatus collects and dispatches protein products
received from the ER and is another important part of the
endomembrane system. Golgi membranes are typically organized
as a stack of ﬂattened cisternae that dynamically direct trafﬁcking
pathways for retrieval and recycling of Golgi and ER components
and receive cargo from the PM and endosomes [137], including
endocytosed NMs [138]. Although a few studies have indicated
that internalized NPs can damage the Golgi membrane by increas-
ing the number of membrane fragments, leading to high levels of
immature protein accumulation on the PM [139], detailed informa-
tion on the interactions between NMs and the Golgi membrane is
still lacking. Moreover, speciﬁc enzymes are anchored on the indi-
vidual stacked membranes of the Golgi, allowing enzymatic reac-
tions to occur during the process of cargo modiﬁcation and
transportation to its cellular destination [140]. Thus, whether
NMs directly or indirectly participate in these enzymatic reactions
and thereby regulate cellular metabolism is an unknown issue.
Recently, NMs that target the Golgi have been designed as anti-
cancer agents because of the importance of the Golgi in cell meta-
bolism [141]. For example, Mocan et al. [142] used Au NPs as a
nanocarrier for serum albumin based on the targeted effects of
NPs on the Golgi and ER in hepatoma cells. They found that pho-
tothermal treatment of the nanocarrier system initiated Golgi
apparatus dysfunction and structural collapse, promoting apopto-
sis of cancer cells. Thus, the Golgi is a valuable target, but various
types of inorganic NMs could exert either promoting or depressing
effects, which should be veriﬁed in speciﬁc cases. Furthermore, if
the purpose of treatment is to regulate cellular metabolism rather
than to induce cell death, how to speciﬁcally target the desired
sites on the Golgi is another issue that should be addressed.

types: point-to-plane, line-to-plane and plane-to-plane [151,152].

membrane effects of 2D NMs compared to NMs of other shapes.
4.1.2. Effects of charge
Differences in surface charge (cationic, neutral, anionic or zwit-
terionic) have great biological impacts on the electrostatic interac-
tions between NMs and the PM. Given that the PM is negatively
charged overall, cationic NMs have a stronger afﬁnity for the PM
than their negative/uncharged counterparts based on the law of
opposite charge attraction [5,61,157]. Thus, cationic NMs exhibit
more potent modulatory effects on membrane structure and func-
tion, but the mechanisms through which this occurs are very
diverse. For example, the induction of hydrophilic pores in the
PM by cationic Au NPs is primarily due to the strong attractions
between the terminal groups of cationic ligands and the phosphate
groups of lipids. This attraction also reorients NPs to adapt to the
surrounding membrane lipids [30], which likely further promotes
penetration of the membrane by the NPs. Moreover, electrostatic
interactions between cationic NPs and other membrane compo-
nents also occur. Binding of NMs to the negatively charged
polysaccharide portions of LPS has been suggested to not only
increase the bacteria-killing efﬁcacy of NMs [61] but also enable
LPS detection in the medical industry [158]. In addition to their
charge properties, the charge density of NPs also contributes to
NM-membrane interactions; the levels of membrane penetration
and disruption increase as the charge density of the NPs increases
in different ways. Speciﬁcally, the degree of NM penetration pri-
marily increases with a charge density dependence at cationic cov-
erages of < 50%, while membrane disruption begins to increase
rapidly at higher charge densities (greater than60% cationic cover-
age) in the case of Au NPs [30]. These charge effects on the mem-
brane
have
already
beneﬁted
speciﬁc
NMs
in
antibacterial
applications. Compared with negatively charged NPs, positively
charged Ag or Au NPs show more potent antibacterial effects
because of the intimate interactions between these NMs and the
PM [159].
Additionally, the charge effect inﬂuences the uptake efﬁciency
of NMs. The uptake rate of cationic Au NPs was reported to be ﬁve-
fold greater than that of their anionic counterparts because half of
the cationic Au NPs diffused into cells by generating holes in the
PM, whereas anionic and neutral NPs were internalized only via
endocytosis [160]. Furthermore, the intracellular accumulation of
cationic NMs in endosomes or lysosomes probably results in an
inﬂux of protons and chloride (the proton sponge effect), causing
swelling and eventual rupture of the vesicles [161]; and this could
be regarded as an effective pathway of escape from the endolyso-
somal system. However, current studies suggest that electrostatic
interactions between charged NMs and the PM are complicated
and that they extend far beyond the principle of Coulomb’s law
because some NMs with negative surface charges can also efﬁ-
ciently pass the negatively charged PM and accumulate within
cells [12,162]. Therein, one determinant factor might be the char-
acteristics of the cells that are being treated, such as the presence
of neuronal spiking activity that can attract negative CdSe/ZnS NPs
[163]. Therefore, the electrical properties of the target cells should
be considered when designing the charge properties of NMs.
4.1.3. Surface hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity

hydrophilic NMs and biological membranes differ from the interac-
tions of hydrophobic NMs with the same membranes, resulting in
varying degrees of cellular uptake and translocation efﬁcacy [164].
Hydrophilic NMs are generally water soluble and dispersible and
appear to display higher membrane interaction effects. However,
MD simulations have revealed that hydrophobic NPs diffuse more
readily into the membrane and embed stably in the membrane

4.1.4. Ion-shedding NMs
Ion shedding, an important property of some metal-based NMs,
such as ZnO, Ag and REO NPs, is characterized by the solubilization
and release of metal ions into the extra- and intracellular environ-
ments, and these released ions play a key role in NMs-induced
membrane effects. For example, the released ions from Ag or Cu
NPs disrupt the bacterial membrane and produce a sterilizing
effect [159]. Additionally, some NPs show pH-dependent dissolu-
tion ability in acidic vesicles (mainly lysosomes, late endosomes
and autolysosomes) that contributes to vesicular membrane alter-
ations. Ion shedding by NMs within lysosomes can lead to lysoso-
mal alkalization through proton consumption, accompanied by
altered expression and distribution of lysosomal membrane recep-
tors. Our previous study found that treatment with ZnO NPs
decreases the expression of VAMP7, an important protein located
in lysosomes that promotes luminal Zn2+ clearance [83,169]. The
downregulation
of
VAMP7
further
exacerbated
ZnO
NPs
dissolution-induced lysosomal Zn2+ overload, thereby disturbing
lysosomal ion homeostasis and leading to an increase in LMP.
Additionally, rare earth ions derived from REO NPs ion shedding
in lysosomes are capable of stripping phosphates from the sur-
rounding lysosomal lipid bilayer [75]. More importantly, the ions
released into the cytoplasm can target other membrane compo-
nents and affect membrane-mediated processes. For example,
Zn2+ can either depress or stimulate Ca2+ inﬂux through Cav2.3
voltage-gated Ca2+ channels on the PM, thereby regulating neu-
ronal excitability [170].
The abovementioned studies have revealed that ion-shedding
NMs affect the membrane due simultaneously to the dissolution
process and the release of ions and that the observed alterations
occur mainly in the PM and the lysosomal membrane. Determining
whether speciﬁc cell types contribute to ion dissolution and iden-
tifying the resulting responses is important. For example, hepato-
cytes are the main storage vault for iron and contribute to
sustaining iron homeostasis in the body. Thus, when a hepatocyte
is exposed to iron oxide NPs, will the resident ions work synergis-
tically with or against exogenous ions? What roles will the biolog-
ical membrane system play in the overall process?
4.1.5. Surface topography
Recent studies have suggested potential links between the sur-
face topography of NMs and the reactions they induce in biological
membranes [12,63]. When cells are located on a nanoarray with a
given topographical structure, the structure of their PMs will be
altered to ﬁt the geometry of the surface. When cells are grown
on nanoarrays fabricated using porous silicon nanoneedles, PM
invagination has been reported to occur around the needle struc-
ture, along with a surrounding accumulation of clathrin-coated
pits and caveolae. At the same time, the nanoneedle interface
induces membrane rufﬂing and increased membrane roughness
without affecting membrane integrity [171]. An inﬂuence of sur-
face topography was also observed in 0D NMs interacting with
the membrane. For instance, the pore structure of mSiO2 NPs can
increase the surface area that is available for contact with the
PM; therefore, mSiO2 NPs do less morphological damage to the
PM than solid SiO2 NPs [47]. The intrinsic mechanism of this effect
is likely related to the fact that the increased surface area of mSiO2
NPs increases the afﬁnity of the NPs for the PM and membrane
components
and
alters
the
membrane
deformation
energy
required for internalization, thereby reducing the physical disrup-
tion that occurs during their direct interaction.
Intriguingly, 2D NMs have been reported to self-fold, forming
wrinkles on the surface during synthesis and application [172],
and the wrinkled 2D structure (mostly existing in phosphorene
nanosheets or graphene nanosheets) represents a generated topog-
raphy that has speciﬁc interaction effects on membranes, such as
extraction of phospholipids from the PM [27,52]. Wrinkled phos-
phorene was shown not only to extract membrane phospholipid
molecules but also to allow them to distribute regularly in a zigzag
pattern on the surface of the NM. Moreover, the surface topogra-
phy (‘‘armchair long side” or ‘‘zigzag long side”) determined the
orientation of later phosphorene insertion into the PM [53].

most current studies have focused on how speciﬁc surface modiﬁ-
cations alter NMs-induced PM effects, mainly because the PM is
usually the ﬁrst site of contact. Whether the reserved coating
induces similar or dissimilar effects on the endomembrane system
deserves more attention and exploration.
4.1.7. Formation of protein corona
When NMs encounter biological ﬂuids, including blood or cell
culture medium, the biomolecules contained can rapidly (<30 s)
cover the surface of NMs to form a biomolecular-NM complex
[180]. The formation of a protein corona on the NM surface can
alter the original physicochemical properties (such as size and sur-
face charge) of NMs and confer on them a new biological identity
because the corona is the structure that actually contacts the mem-
brane system; therefore, it affects the cellular targeting, biodistri-
bution and toxicity of NMs [181,182].
First, the protein corona can decrease the contact area of NMs
with the PM by wrapping the NM surface and thus altering its abil-
ity to adhere to the PM [5,33,183]. The presence of a protein coat-
ing has been reported to increase the thickness of GO and reduce
its available surface area, thereby limiting GO insertion into the
PM by slowing the lipid extraction dynamics [184]. Multiple stud-
ies have also focused on how the protein corona affects the nanos-
cale size of NMs and thereby changes their contact with the PM
and their further uptake pathways [33,185,186]. Moreover, the
protein corona can normalize the surface charge of NMs, evidenced
by the negative zeta potential detected after protein coating,
because most proteins are negatively charged [180,187]. This can
either inhibit or promote NM-biological membrane interactions,
depending on the afﬁnity of the coating proteins for the membrane
system. In addition to the size and charge effects, protein adsorp-
tion is also a type of ‘‘natural functionalization” [181] that miti-
gates
NMs-induced
membrane
destruction
and
dysfunction
through (i) quenching excessive free radical generation, especially
in the case of GFNs [188], and thereby alleviating the induced
membrane lipid peroxidation [54]; (ii) decreasing the rate of NM
agglomeration and the diffusion rate of the assembled particles
[189], thereby slowing the contact between bare NPs and the
membrane structure; and (iii) providing protection from metal
ions released by ion-shedding NMs [190]. All the above mitigations
can signiﬁcantly reduce the incidence of cytotoxicity.
In addition, the protein corona can shield modiﬁcations pre-
loaded on the NM surface, thereby reducing the opportunity for
the original modiﬁcations to bind to the desired membrane com-
ponents and ultimately limiting their biological effects. For exam-
ple, transferrin-coated SiO2 NPs lost their ability to bind to
transferrin receptors on the PM in the presence of serum proteins,
mainly due to protein corona formation [191]. A decrease in NM-
membrane receptor binding contributes to reduced endocytosis
of NMs through receptor-mediated pathways [167,184]. However,
protein corona effects on NM-membrane interactions do not
always occur for three possible reasons: (i) NMs coated with a pro-
tein corona may still have free areas that interact with the mem-
brane [192]; (ii) protein adsorption is a highly dynamic process,
and the adsorbed proteins can incidentally detach [193]; and (iii)
intracellular matrix and lysosomal degradation can disassemble
or degrade the protein corona from the NM surface [194], re-
exposing the buried NMs. Protein coronas for NMs have been
developed for decades, and controlling protein adsorption by mod-
ifying NPs with diverse functional proteins and tailoring appropri-
ate NPs that favor selective endogenous peptides and proteins to
generate promising therapeutics have already beneﬁted nanome-
dicine [182]. Intriguingly, studies of lipids, another common type
of biomolecule in biological ﬂuids and biologic membranes, have
indicated that lipid coronas can also form on NMs [195]. The for-
mation of lipid coronas probably leads to more beneﬁcial interac-
tions between NMs and membranes. This issue is interesting,
although limited information has been reported.
4.1.8. Other properties of NMs
The crystal phase differences exhibited by NMs also contribute
to the induced membrane effects because differences in catalytic
performance are closely related to the crystal phase. Anatase
TiO2 NPs have been reported to have higher afﬁnity for proteins,
while rutile NPs have higher afﬁnity for phospholipids [80], and
these afﬁnities determine the effects of the NPs on membrane
structure and result in corresponding membrane effects. Addition-
ally, the elasticity of NMs is another factor that mediates the
mechanical status of NM-membrane interactions. Generally, rigid
NMs retain their morphology during cellular uptake, while soft
NMs are usually deformed due to the forces associated with the
speciﬁc ligand-receptor interaction and membrane wrapping, lead-
ing to decreased receptor binding ability and decreased rates of
endocytosis. Additionally, a much higher elastic deformation
energy of the membrane is required when soft NMs deform, and
the bending energy must be overcome to complete internalization
[196,197].
In summary, modulating the physicochemical properties of
NMs to control the contact pattern between NMs and biological
membranes is an important method of modifying NMs for the
desired purpose. Obviously, it is not sufﬁcient to pay attention to

4.2. Indirect NM-biological membrane interaction mechanisms
4.2.1. Cytoskeleton-directing NM-membrane interactions
The cytoskeleton is a cross-linked network structure that
includes actin ﬁlaments, microtubules, and intermediate ﬁlaments.
Within this network, actin ﬁlament elements interact intimately
with the PM at a wide variety of cellular locations, regulating
membrane-mediated cell functions that include migration, adhe-
sion and deformation. Microtubules are present throughout almost
the entire cell matrix and are responsible for trafﬁcking of intracel-
lular membranous organelles to and from the PM, and intermedi-
ate ﬁlaments interact with the PM at sites of cell–cell and cell-
matrix adhesion at desmosomes and hemidesmosomes, thereby
regulating cellular morphology through membrane attachment
and resistance to mechanical stress [199]. Therefore, alterations
in cytoskeletal elements underlie the regulation of membrane sys-
tem structure and function by NMs [38].
NMs can disturb normal cytoskeletal polymerization/depoly
merization dynamics and thus indirectly modulate biological
membranes. NMs induce disruption of actin polymerization, and
loss of binding between ﬁlamentous actin and actin-binding pro-
teins can further lead to reduced PM ﬂuidity or even PM collapse
[16,200]. Activation of membrane receptors (e.g., integrins) that
further initiate downstream cascades (the FAK-Rho-ROCK and
FAK/Src pathways) participates in the regulation of actin dynamics,
thereby changing membrane behavior. Moreover, actin ﬁlaments
participate in anchoring and fusion of vesicles with the PM, and
NMs can depress synaptic vesicle exocytosis [41] and the uptake
cycle [62] by disturbing the homeostasis of actin dynamics.
Besides, actin dynamics also contribute to NMs-induced nuclear
membrane deformation and regulate force transmission between
NMs and the nuclear envelope [201]. Under actin ﬁlament disrup-
tion via latrunculin, nanopillar-induced nuclear membrane defor-
mation was greatly reduced (Fig. 6a-c) [202]. However, the
underlying mechanism through which NMs disturb actin dynamics
is complex. NMs with unique 2D structures are able to insert into
the interstrand gaps of actin tetramers and break them via van der
Waals interactions [203]. Excessive ROS generation, which can
directly oxidize actin ﬁlaments, increases coﬁlin activation and
inhibit formation of the actin nucleation complex (the ARP2/3
complex), is usually reported as another factor [41,204]. Moreover,
in addition to the abovementioned integrin-FAK-related pathways,
other membrane receptors, such as G protein-coupled receptors
and receptor tyrosine kinases, can also regulate the activity and
recruitment of actin regulatory proteins via intracellular Rho
GTPase signaling and thereby modulate actin dynamics and mem-
brane effects [205]. Given the close relationship between actin and
the membrane, whether NMs can trigger these pathways to indi-
rectly modulate membrane structure and function is an interesting
topic worth studying.
In addition to their effects on actin dynamics, NMs can inhibit
the polymerization and aggregation of microtubule proteins and
thereby block microtubule-modulated cellular functions such as
endomembrane fusion [83,206]. While investigating the mecha-
nism of NMs-induced microtubule destabilization, MD simulations
revealed that the inhibitory effects of fullerene modiﬁed with OH
were mainly attributable to the formation of hydrogen bonds
between the NPs and tubulin heterodimers [207]. Moreover, when
graphene participates in strong dispersive interactions on the
microtubule surface, it can pull one dimer away from another
through a ‘‘tug-of-war” mechanism at the hydrophilic interface of
two neighboring tubulin proteins [208]. Extracellular signal-
regulated protein kinase (ERK)-MAPK-GSK-3b signaling is one of
the key signal transduction pathways that regulates microtubule
kinetics, and it is activated during treatment with various NMs
[82]. Whether this pathway contributes to alterations in NMs-
induced microtubule dynamics and thereby changes cellular func-
tions still lacks detailed research. Moreover, despite the important
role of intermediate ﬁlaments, whether NMs can affect their
dynamics remains obscure.
Posttranslational modiﬁcation (PTM) of cytoskeletal elements is
a newly detected target of NMs that also induces biological mem-
brane alterations. For example, ZnO NPs induce acetylation of
microtubules in PC12 cells via the a-tubulin acetyltransferase 1
(aTAT1) pathway, and membrane fusion between lysosomes and
autophagosomes is promoted during the overexpression of micro-
tubule acetylation (Fig. 6d) [83]. PTMs are one of the factors that
affect the properties of the tubulin/actin polymer and regulate
cytoskeleton-mediated cellular function; therefore, further studies
are required to explore the role of these modiﬁcations in NMs-
induced biological effects. More importantly, the underlying mech-
anism through which NMs induce PTM of cytoskeletal elements is
still unknown, but such modiﬁcations could be a useful way of
mediating NMs-induced alterations in biological membranes. Tak-
ing microtubule acetylation as an example, it has been reported
that TGF-b-activated kinase 1 (TAK1) directly phosphorylates
aTAT1 at Ser237, thereby critically enhancing its catalytic activity,
increasing the level of microtubule acetylation and further enhanc-
ing microtubule stability [209]. Such a mechanism could be uti-
lized to promote microtubule-related cellular functions, such as
intracellular transportation.
4.2.2. Roles of redox status
ROS are natural byproducts of the cellular metabolism of oxy-
gen and have important roles in cell signaling and homeostasis.
However, NMs-induced ROS overproduction is responsible for oxi-
dation of membrane phospholipids and protein amino acid side
chains and for protein oxidative carbonylation, thus leading to
increased membrane permeability and eventually to membrane
rupture [55]; this improves the antibacterial efﬁciency of some
types of NMs [23,210]. Mitochondrial ETC dysfunction is recog-
nized as the most important origin of NMs-induced ROS, while
NOXs are another common source that has begun to attract atten-
tion [22,41]. The protein kinase B (AKT), ERK, and PKC pathways
can all phosphorylate NOXs cytoplasmic subunits (e.g., p47phox
and p67phox), thereby promoting their translocation to the mem-
brane, where they form active NOX complexes and induce O2– gen-
eration [211]. Although many studies have shown that NMs can
activate the AKT, ERK and PKC cascades [82], there is still a lack
of reliable evidence as to which of these cascades is the speciﬁc
upstream cause of NMs-induced NOXs activation. Moreover, the
excessive ROS production induced by NMs can damage the mito-
chondrial membrane itself through oxidation of the membrane
components described above and can lengthen the opening of
the MPT pore, thereby reducing MMP and inducing further ROS
release through a mechanism termed ROS-induced ROS release
[111,212].
Unlike the NMs-induced cellular ROS production reviewed
above, some NMs themselves release reactive radicals such as
the carbon radicals released by GO, which can oxidize membrane
lipids during the interaction between NMs and the PM [55,56].

5. Biological membranes as the platform mediating NMs-
immune system contact
Based on the widespread application of and exposure to NMs,
it is inevitable to consider the important role of the immune sys-
tem, because NMs are often ﬁrst picked up by and retained in
immune cells after entering the body. Although many current
studies have focused on the interaction between NMs and the
immune system, an in-depth understanding of how NMs either
speciﬁcally interact with or avoid recognition by the immune sys-
tem from the view of NMs-membrane interactions can supple-
ment the application and reduce the risk of toxicity of NMs.
Immune cells, which are the basic components of the immune
system, recognize and present antigens and produce wanted/un-
wanted suppressive or stimulatory immune responses on the
basis of membrane surface molecules (including antigens, recep-
tors, major histocompatibility complex (MHC) I and II, and others)
[222]. Therefore, the roles of the biological membrane system, the
key platform that participate in NMs-mediated inﬂammation,
immunogenic effect, complement activation and immunosuppres-
sion, are discussed.
5.1. Inﬂammation
Induction of inﬂammation and production of cytokines are the
main characteristics of immune system activation and are also
considered common nanotoxicological effects. As mentioned in
the
previous
sections,
membrane-adsorbed
and
membrane-
inserted NMs interact with membrane ligands to activate integrins,
Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and other membrane proteins, which
may result in activation of downstream cascades (e.g., MAPK,
JAK/STAT) and in NF-jB translocation into the nucleus; this, in
turn, upregulates the synthesis and secretion of inﬂammatory
cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-12 and TNF-a) and macrophage polarization
into pro-inﬂammatory M1 phenotypes [19,155,223,224]. In addi-
tion to binding to receptors exposed on the outer leaﬂet of the
PM, internalized NMs can activate membrane proteins located on
the inner leaﬂet of the bilayer, especially PLC, thereby triggering
calcium mobilization by cooperative Ca2+ channels and Ca2+ stor-
age through a network that involves IP3R, the STIM-Orai1 complex
and calcium release-activated calcium channels. Elevated intracel-
lular Ca2+ participates in regulation of the immunological activities
of macrophages, including promoting the secretion of inﬂamma-
tory cytokines [131].
The endomembrane system also participates in mediating the
inﬂammatory response. NMs that disrupt and escape the endolyso-
somal system can induce cathepsin release, thereby promoting
NLRP3 inﬂammasome and IL-1b production [21,75]. The underly-
ing mechanism is probably that the released cathepsins increase
the production of prostaglandin and activate inﬂammation via an
Atg-7-dependent mechanism [225,226]; but this should be veriﬁed
for NMs. In addition to the lysosome-inﬂammation pathway, dam-
aged mitochondria [227] and ER stress [228] are also regarded as
major sources of upstream inﬂammatory signals. For example,
damaged mitochondria with parkin accumulated on the OMM
recruit and bind to p62 (an autophagy receptor) for mitophagy,
and p62 can further activate NF-jB via its TRAF6 binding motif,
increasing the expression and release of IL-1b [227]. Therefore,
these studies suggest that NMs-induced endomembrane alter-
ations could potentially modulate cellular inﬂammation develop-
ment. However, few nanotoxicological studies have regarded
membrane effects as an effective approach for modulating NMs-
induced immune responses.
5.2. Immunogenicity of NMs
Certain nanoparticles have been demonstrated to be immuno-
genic and to serve as adjuvants to increase the immunogenicity
of weak antigens and beneﬁt vaccine efﬁcacy [229]. In addition
to being applied as vaccine adjuvants, many other NMs have been
applied as carriers, where they either act as targeting components
or as active pharmaceutical ingredients. Therefore, the impact of
NMs is complicated and varies according to different application
scenarios. On the one hand, the stimulated immune response
and antigenicity of loaded cargos and NMs-based carriers could
go against the therapeutic purpose or induce an overactive
immune response, such as a hypersensitivity reaction. On the
other hand, some types of NMs have emerged as a potential
weapon in cancer immunotherapy because they can stimulate
immune recognition of tumors and thereby improve the therapeu-
tic efﬁcacy under the immunosuppressive tumor microenviron-
ment [230,231]. Taking iron oxide NPs as an example, these NPs
were shown to additively attract macrophages and exhibit strong
afﬁnity to their PMs, thus leading to greater uptake of NPs. Inter-
nalized NPs further accelerated the polarization of recruited
macrophages to the M1 type, and this induced not only a pro-
inﬂammatory TH1-type response at the tumor site but also release
of H2O2, providing iron to generate OH via the Fenton reaction
and thereby causing apoptosis of tumor cells and inhibition of
tumor growth [223,232].
Based on the foregoing results, NMs can act as effective antigens
in vaccine and drug delivery systems. Therein, the underlying
mechanism is closely related to NMs-membrane interactions: (i)
certain NMs activate immune effects, such as cellular inﬂamma-
tion, as discussed previously, an effect that is dependent on the
interaction between NMs and membrane inﬂammation receptors,
and can collaboratively enhance the immune response to antigens;
(ii) the interactions between NMs and the PM can promote inter-
nalization of NM-antigen complexes; and (iii) internalized NMs
that are capable of escaping the endolysosomal system also protect
antigens from degradation in lysosomes, thereby resulting in
increased presentation of the peptides to cellular MHC for further
T cell-mediated immune responses [67,233,234]. Thus, selection
of an appropriate NM based on the membrane properties of
immune cells is beneﬁcial for enhancing the effects on NMs-
induced immune response activation and improving the efﬁciency
of the antigen delivery system.

5.3. Complement system activation
The complement system is a group of approximately 30 pro-
teins produced by the liver and present in plasma and exerts
opsonization functions in both innate and adaptive immunity upon
being activated. Recently, some studies have indicated that NMs
treatment can activate the complement system, which brought
NMs

from
systemic
circulation
via
antigen-

mechanism is that certain complement proteins, such as C3a, have
a high binding afﬁnity with the NP surface and drive the complex
to interact with the complement receptor on the PM, inducing its
recognition and internalization by APCs and ﬁnally leading to acti-
vation of the complement cascade and severe hypersensitivity
reactions [237,238]. In most cases, complement activation should
be avoided to minimize the clearance rate of drug delivery vehi-
cles. Thus, much effort has been made to design NMs with surface
properties (e.g., modiﬁcation and charge) that decrease the binding
capacity of NMs and further mitigate activation of the complement
cascade [236].
In addition to the plasma complement, immune cells can con-
stitutively express complement, named intracellular complement
(IC) [239]. Recently, Ilinskaya et al. [240] reported a novel mecha-
nism of NMs-induced complement activation independent of the
plasma complement. Speciﬁcally, NMs, due to their membrane
hole-poking capacity, can induce self-opsonization through leak-
age of IC and binding with cell membrane receptors and then acti-
vate the complement cascade. As discussed above, many NMs
show potent membrane damage effects based on their special
NM-membrane interaction patterns, likely endowing these NMs
with the capacity to induce IC leakage. Thus, the characteristics
of NMs or indirect biological factors that direct NMs-membrane
interactions, should be important determining factors for modulat-
ing NMs-mediated IC activation.
5.4. Immunosuppression
Immunosuppression is a reduction in the activation or efﬁcacy
of the immune system. The ability of NMs to induce immunosup-
pression, which is characterized by release of anti-inﬂammatory
cytokines (e.g., IL-10 and TGF-b), suppressed activation of involved
pathways, release of proinﬂammatory cytokines, recruitment of
regulatory immune cells (especially M2 macrophages, T and B regs,
and myeloid-derived suppressor cells) and inhibition of the allergic
response, is a promising complement for nanomedicine [241,242].
To date, inorganic-based NMs have been used to depress the
immune state in ulcerative colitis [243], rheumatoid arthritis
[244] and hemolytic anemia [245,246] with therapeutic effects.
Therein, the biological membrane system acts as the necessary
platform to provide receptors for binding, to mediate the activation
of downstream signaling and molecule production and release and
ﬁnally to regulate the migration of and contacts between immune
cells.
NMs have been reported to suppress immune responses by
altering the structure and function of biological membranes and
by affecting the expression and activity of related proteins. For
example, MWCNTs can upregulate the expression of COX2, which
is located on the nuclear membrane and is a key enzyme in the
production of the inﬂammatory mediators PGE2 and IL-10, thus
causing T cell dysfunction and depressing systemic immunity
[247]. Moreover, TLR2 plays a bidirectional role in mediating the
innate immune response, and the TLR2-mediated MAPK pathway
promotes IL-10 production [248]; this might be the molecular
mechanism
responsible
for
NMs-induced
anti-inﬂammatory
effects [249]. Although studies have indicated that NMs can sup-
press immune responses, limited information is available on the
link between NMs and biological membranes in these processes.
Whether the structure and function of biological membranes can
provide targets to extend the application of NMs in modulating
the immune response under conditions that lead to aberrant acti-
vation of the immune system still needs further investigation.
Above all, the NMs-induced immune response is mainly depen-
dent on interactions between NMs and immune cells, in which the
biological membrane is the key platform. Moreover, the physico-
chemical properties of NMs have been reported to play a crucial
role in modulating NMs-induced immune effects [250]; whether
these properties modulate the immune response through biologi-
cal membrane pathways is an intriguing but unknown issue.
Therefore, based on the close link between biological membranes
and the immune response, the information on NMs-membrane
interactions summarized above could contribute to development
of potential applications of inorganic-based NMs to the immune
system.
6. Applications and future perspectives
6.1. Membrane effects-based nanomedicine
NMs have been widely designed and applied in biomedical
research and used in clinical practice as drug delivery systems,
imaging agents and scaffolds or implants. During their application,
the interaction between NMs/nanocarriers and cellular biological
membrane systems is a crucial factor that signiﬁcantly affects the
desired output of the NMs/nanocarriers.
Among the novel drug delivery systems investigated, lipid-
based NMs, polymeric NMs, and inorganic-based NMs are common
nanocarriers that can be modiﬁed to carry drugs, antibodies and
vaccines. Some of them have been approved for clinical trials or
have already entered clinical practice [1,251]. Although NMs can
be used as nanocarriers, the interactions between NMs and biolog-
ical membranes and the mechanisms underlying these interactions
are not consistent. (i) Liposomes, the most widely applied lipid-
based
NMs,
are
biomimetic
vesicles
composed
of
a
self-
assembling concentric lipid bilayer and an aqueous core domain.
The structure of their membrane is analogous to that of the cell
membrane, and they can adsorb to or fuse with the PM and with
endocytic vesicles to release loaded drugs into the cytoplasm.
However, because of their aqueous core, they have a low capacity
to encapsulate lipophilic drugs; moreover, they are often leaky and
unstable in biological ﬂuids [252–254]. (ii) The types of available
polymeric NMs are diverse, and these NMs can easily be internal-
ized via ligand-mediated endocytosis and phagocytosis without
affecting the structure or physiology of the PM. Nevertheless, a
few studies have observed direct penetration or insertion of den-
drimers into the PM under high-dose treatments [255,256]; such
insertion could cause lipid desorption and further increase the size
of existing defects or create new holes in the lipid bilayer [257].
Thus, both lipid-based and polymeric NMs are considered as
membrane-friendly NMs that exhibit few membrane effects under
most circumstances. However, these NMs usually act as nanocarri-
ers and do not have any intrinsic therapeutic effects, which
restricts their application range.
Compared with liposomes and polymeric NMs, inorganic-based
NMs not only enhance the efﬁcacy of loaded drugs but also inde-
pendently act as contrast agents for anatomical and functional
imaging and as treatment strategies by mediating cell death and
other effects [251] (Table 1). All of these nanomedicine applica-
tions are based on the interaction between NMs and biological
membrane systems, and they are systematically summarized in
this review to provide a more in-depth understanding. The ability
to affect membrane structural and functions such as integrity,

6.2. Nanotoxicology and membrane restoration
However, if destructive NM-biological membrane interactions
occur in normal cells or the effects go far beyond the desired
extent, then cytotoxicity and even cell death may occur. Most of
the detected nanotoxicity proﬁles, including ROS generation and
imbalanced ion homeostasis, can be attributed to the summurized
NM-membrane interactions. In addition, most nanomedical appli-
cations, such as drug delivery and therapy that applies NMs,
require NMs to function in a closed environment with an intact
membrane. Therefore, it is of great importance to strengthen the
understanding and utilization of cellular membrane repair systems
to limit and prevent the destructive effects and promote the design
and fabrication of NMs.
The membrane repair system is complicated and far from
clearly understood. Membrane resealing can be mediated by the
endolysosomal membrane or endosomal sorting complex required
for transport (ESCRT) machinery. ESCRT-mediated vesicle budding
has been proposed to repair small wounds (<100 nm) in the PM,
lysosome, nucleus and other organelles [268,269]. Ca2+ inﬂux after
PM injuries larger than 100 nm can recruit intracellular vesicles,
mainly lysosomes, to fuse with the PM at the sites of injury and
act as a ‘‘patch” to ﬁll the wound and reduce the membrane tension
to facilitate resealing (Fig. 7) [268,270]. Although few studies have
addressed this topic, the role of membrane repair systems in nano-
medicine should not be ignored because they allow NMs to
translocate to the desired cellular compartment with good biocom-
patibility. Furthermore, when rapid, abundant or large-area mem-
brane defects occur, which are frequent during exposure to some
NMs, the membrane repair system cannot operate well, thereby
suppressing the therapeutic effects of NMs to some extent.
Thus, taking the membrane repair mechanism into considera-
tion in NMs design and fabrication is probably a favorable method
for extending NMs applications. Furthermore, activation of the
autophagy
system
(including
macroautophagy
and
selective
autophagy for organelles) after NMs exposure can effectively
degrade internalized NMs, membrane debris and damaged orga-
nelles to protect cells. However, as reviewed in the section on lyso-
somal membranes, some kinds of NMs are capable of increasing
LMP, inducing lysosomal alkalization or disturbing the fusion pro-
cess between autophagosomes and lysosomes, leading to NMs
accumulation in or escape from the endolysosome system and
blockade of autophagic ﬂux. Therefore, in most cases, preservation
of lysosome function and normal autophagy are also important
issues in NMs-based diagnosis and therapy.
6.3. Biological membrane-based NMs design to modulate the immune
response
The effects of NM-membrane interactions not only beneﬁt the
treatment of the pathological states but also modulate the immune
response to strengthen therapeutic efﬁcacy. Therefore, how to
achieve the desired immune response or to avoid unwanted
immune activation, NM-membrane interactions could be a prof-
itable point that should be considered. Speciﬁcally, (i) to improve
the safety of NMs application, unwanted NMs-induced inﬂamma-
tion activation and an overactivated immune response could be
avoided or reduced in view of the NM-membrane interaction
effects. For example, modiﬁed NMs could be fabricated to reduce
the binding effects between NMs and membrane proteins, which
are involved in recognition and internalization by immune cells
and activation of inﬂammatory cascades [271]. (ii) Decreasing
NMs immunogenicity could not only reduce the immune toxicity
of NMs but also contribute to intensifying NMs carrier features,

A new concept of cell membrane-coating technology that lever-

developed for reducing the immune response of engineered
nanocarriers [273,276], with the primary advantages of lowered

was ﬁrst demonstrated using red blood cell PM to cover a NP core
in a right-side-out orientation, resulting in a core–shell structure
with a proper functioning membrane [277,278]. Recently, other
blood cells (platelets and white blood cells), cancer cells, and stem
cells that have unique membrane functionalities have also been
investigated as membrane sources [276,279] that could serve in
NMs-immune system contact. For example, NPs coated with plate-
let membranes (PNPs) possess a unilamellar membrane coating
functionalized with immunomodulatory and adhesion antigens
associated with platelets and can evade the immune system and
bind to sites that naturally recruit platelets, such as damaged vas-
culature [280]. In addition to the cell membrane, bacterial mem-
branes could also be utilized to coat NPs (BM-NPs) and act as
antibacterial vaccines. Intriguing results indicated that administra-
tion of BM-Au NPs could induce a more rapid activation and mat-
uration of dendritic cells in vivo, as well as stronger IFN-c and IL-
17 production, indicating an ability to generate Th1- and Th17-
biased cell responses against the source bacteria [281]. In conjunc-
tion with the complicated membrane structure, we suggest that
future studies should focus on membrane components, including
membrane proteins (e.g., ion channels and enzymes) and lipids,
which could enable exploitation of many new and exciting
applications.

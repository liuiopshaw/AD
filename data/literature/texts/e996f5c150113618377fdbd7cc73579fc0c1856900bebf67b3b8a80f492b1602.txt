ABSTRACT: The development of modular strategies for
programming self-assembled supramolecular architectures
with distinct structural and functional features is of immense
scientiﬁc interest. We reported on the intrinsic antibacterial
capability of anionic amphiphilic gold nanoclusters (GNCs)
capped by para-mercaptobenzoic acid, which was closely related
to the protonation level of terminal carboxylate groups. By
using of the metal−ligand coordination-driven and solvent
evaporation-induced self-assembly, we constructed GNCs-based
mixed-metal metal−organic network (MM-MON) ﬁlms on
titanium disks as antibacterial nanocoatings. Taking the
reasonable utilization of tetravalent metal ions M4+ (Ti, Zr,
Hf; hard Lewis acid) and bactericidal divalent metal ions M2+
(Cu, Zn; borderline acid) co-incorporated metal−carboxylate coordination bonds, the MM-MON ﬁlms exhibited superior
stability due to the robust M4+−O bonds and M2+ releasing behavior resulting from the labile M2+−O coordinating. Together,
the MM-MON ﬁlms integrated the bacteria-responsive character of GNCs, exceptional chemical stability, and greatly
enhanced antibacterial activity, ultimately killing adherent bacteria and initiating a self-defensive function. In a rat model for
subcutaneous implant-associated infection, the MM-MON nanocoating showed an approximately 2 and 1 log lower multidrug-
resistant Staphylococcus aureus implant and tissue colonization, respectively. The generalizable modular strategy of the GNC−
metal networks is amenable to facilitate the functionalization of metal surfaces for combating implant-associated infections.
KEYWORDS: gold nanocluster, antibacterial, implant-associated infections, metal−organic frameworks, metal−ligand bonds
A
tomically precise gold nanoclusters (GNCs) have
received widespread attention due to their distin-
guished molecule-like structures1 and quantized
charging,2 which provide an ideal platform for versatile
applications in catalysis,3,4 ﬂuorescence sensing,5 and bio-
medical targeting.6,7 With the rapidly growing database of core
structures of GNCs due to crystallographic analysis, stunning
progress has been achieved to fabricate GNCs-based self-
assembled supramolecular architectures.8 For example, the Xie
group reported aurophilic interactions-directed self-assembly
of GNCs into one-dimensional (1D) nanowires.9 Besides, the
assembly of thiolated nanoclusters directly linked by SbF6−
anions and outer metal atoms into framework material was
reported.10 It has long been recognized that the electronic
properties and geometric conﬁguration are governed mainly by
the core structure and metal−ligand interface, but of equal
importance is that the solubility and chemical reactivity result
almost exclusively from the tunable organic ligand layer.
Recently, we developed the method enabling discrete
Au22(SG)18 (SG, glutathione thiolate) nanoclusters to be
self-assembled into supramolecular spherical assemblies via the
engagement of hydrogen bonding among glutathione ligands.11
In recent years, with the burgeoning demands for multi-
functional hierarchical structures in catalysis,12 sensing,13 drug
delivery,14 and other ﬁelds,15 the research of multicomponent
materials with modular and hierarchical arranged structures has
attracted great attention.15 In particular, metal−organic
frameworks (MOFs), built of metal nodes coordinated with

highly tunable hierarchy and diversity.16 Though powder
MOFs have been widely used in gas storage and catalysis,17 an
enormous challenge facing the adoption of MOFs is the poor
processability due to their high crystalline nature. Additionally,
signiﬁcant eﬀort has been devoted to fabricating mixed-metal
MOFs to take advantage of the complexity and the synergism
of diﬀerent metal ions, but most of them are still in their
infancy.18 The diﬃculty consists in the use of metal ions that
must have the same Lewis acidity and coordination geometry,
in which the metals were randomly distributed in most cases.19
According to the principles of MOFs, the multitopic character
of GNCs liganded with aliphatic carboxylic acids is expected to
enable metal ions coordinated with carboxylate ligands to
construct coordination-directed supramolecular architectures.
The cationic compounds- (such as chitosan and quaternary
ammonium) and lysozyme-capped GNCs have been used as
antimicrobial agents based on the strong electrostatic
interactions between cationic groups and negatively charged
bacterial cell membranes, or the bacteriolysis mechanism by
lysozyme.20−22 We propose the anionic amphiphilic GNCs
may exhibit an antibacterial activity with minimal eukaryotic
cell cytotoxicity. Nowadays, antimicrobial resistance and
implant-associated infections (IAIs), as two major topics of
bacterial infections, have painted a grim picture of the global
public health crisis.23 Bacterial adhesion, bioﬁlm formation,
and antimicrobial resistance are the main causes of IAIs, which
lead to the failure of the host immune response.24 Until now,
titanium and its alloys have been widely used as dental,
cardiovascular, and orthopedic implants for their favorable
mechanical features and good biocompatibility.25 Although
various antifouling- and bactericidal-based approaches have
been developed for antibacterial surface modiﬁcation of
titanium implants,26 certain inherent disadvantages do exist,
including lower diagnostic capabilities and the resistance to
chronic antibiotic therapies.27 Therefore, the development of
new, more complex bioactive molecules and self-defensive
antibacterial coatings is thus imperative.28
Herein, we demonstrated the variable antibacterial capa-
bilities of para-mercaptobenzoic acid (pMBA)-capped GNCs,
which was closely related to the protonation state of ligands.
Furthermore, the construction of GNCs-based mixed-metal
metal−organic network (MM-MON) ﬁlms as an adaptive
antibacterial coating was achieved. The combining of a hard-
acid tetravalent metal ion and a second softer divalent metal
ion at diﬀerent coordination bonds, which complies with the
principles of reticular chemistry, enables a controlled softer
metal dissolution without jeopardizing chemical stability of the
networks. Thus, the GNCs and MM-MON ﬁlms were featured
with a suitable set of modular and adjustable properties: (i)
adjustable hydrophobic/hydrophilic surface and antibacterial
activity of GNCs by tuning the protonation/deprotonation
state of ligands, (ii) modular metal−carboxylate coordination
bonds by using metal ions of diﬀerent softness/hardness, and
(iii) adjustable antibacterial eﬃciency by adjusting the
compositions and ratios of mixed-metal ions. We expect the
fabrication methodology of GNC−metal networks could be
helpful in the development of a next-generation of adaptive
antibacterial surface modiﬁcations.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Antibacterial Action of Anionic Amphiphilic GNCs.
First, pMBA-capped GNCs with an average core size around
1.8 nm were prepared (Figure 1a). On the basis of the
amphiphilic structural feature of ligand pMBA, which is
composed of a hydrophobic benzene ring and a terminal
hydrophilic aromatic carboxylic acid, the macroscopic
amphiphilic nature of the GNCs depends on the proto-
nation/deprotonation state of the ligand layer (Figure 1b,c).
As indicted from the previous report,29 the fully protonated
GNCs are soluble in short alcohols, while the partially and fully
deprotonated GNCs are water-soluble. As shown in Figure S1,
the GNCs in partially deprotonated and fully protonated states
had an almost identical UV−vis spectrum, indicating the
stability of the gold core in the protonation/deprotonation
process. In this study, Gram-positive (Gram+) methicillin-
resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Gram-negative
(Gram−) extended-spectrum β-lactamases Escherichia coli
(ESBL E. coli) were used as model bacteria to investigate the
antibacterial activities of GNCs. As shown in Figure S2, GNCs
exhibited dose-dependent antimicrobial activity toward MRSA
and ESBL E. coli at pH 7.4. Moreover, the GNCs are presumed
to possess a pH-dependent antibacterial capability via tuning
the hydrophilic/hydrophobic performance of the ligands
(Figure 1d). In order to better quantify the protonation/
deprotonation state of the ligands at diﬀerent pH values, we
measured the pH-dependent Fourier transform infrared
(FTIR) spectra of diﬀerent GNCs in the pH range of 3.0−
12.8 in solid phase, in which the protonation state in solution is
preserved when GNCs are dried. The GNCs were fully
protonated at pH 3.0 in aqueous solution and precipitated
from water, while the GNCs were fully deprotonated at pH
12.8. As shown in Figure S3, the peaks around 1585 cm−1 are
assigned to the phenyl-ring vibration of pMBA ligands.29 With
the pH values increased, the intensity of the symmetric
stretching mode of the delocalized −COO−group of pMBA
ligands at 1396 cm−1 is distinctly enhanced because of the
increased rate of −COO−/−COOH.29 The proliferation of
bacteria was signiﬁcantly inhibited by GNCs in the case of pH
5.8 compared to that in pH 7.4, while the bacteria without
GNCs incubation showed similar growth curves in the two
diﬀerent pH conditions (Figure 1e,f).

Tetravalent Metal Ion-Based Homometallic MON
Films and Their Antibacterial Performance. Inspired by
the surface-supported MOF thin ﬁlm,35 the mixture of
tetravalent metal ions (Ti4+, Zr4+, Hf4+) and fully protonated
GNCs in ethanol was deposited onto a solid support to
produce cross-linked GNC−metal three-dimensional ﬁlms by
virtue of coordination-driven and solvent evaporation-induced
self-assembly (Figure 4a). The linkage chemistry and surface
electronic state of the GNC−metal ﬁlms were employed by in
situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Notably, the
coordination interactions were evidenced by the distinct
changes in O 1s spectra. In the fully protonated GNCs, the
O 1s peak could be deconvolved into two components
corresponding to the C−OH (533.4 eV) and CO (531.8
eV) species (Figure 4b).36 After the coordination with Ti4+,
three peaks centered at 533.4, 531.8, and 530.3 eV in the high-
resolution O 1s spectra can be assigned to the oxygen atoms in
C−OH, CO, and O−Ti−O, respectively.37 Similarly, in the
O 1s spectra of Zr- and Hf-based homometallic MON ﬁlms,
the O−Zr−O and O−Hf−O species are centered at 530.2 and
530.1 eV,36,38 respectively. However, the most prominent
change is that all three homometallic Ti-, Zr-, and Hf-MON
ﬁlms exhibit a very subtle peak of coordinated-free carboxylate
groups along with the emergence of a large proportion of O−
M4+−O (M = Ti, Zr, Hf) species, signifying the successful
coordination of M4+ with the carboxylate groups. It is found
that the relative population of the O−M4+−O in these ﬁlms
decreases with the ranking order as Ti (46.4%), Zr (33.2%),
and Hf (24.7%). The binding energies of Ti 2p, Zr 3d, and Hf
4f with spin−orbit splitting doublets corroborate the oxidation
states of Ti4+, Zr4+, and Hf4+, similar to the reported Ti-, Zr-,
and Hf-based MOFs (Figure 4c and Table S2).
Then, the surface morphologies and thicknesses of as-
prepared silicon wafer-supported ﬁlms were investigated by
SEM. As shown in Figure 4d, all the as-prepared MON ﬁlms
exhibited a dense structure with uniform ﬁlm morphologies
and a thickness between about 100 and 200 nm. Furthermore,
the time-dependent dynamic water contact angle measure-
ments were conducted. Instantaneous water contact angles
displayed certain hydrophobic properties of the as-prepared
ﬁlms due to the protonated ligands, while the decreased values
after 5 min indicated the porous character of the metal−ligand
networks (Figure 4e). In the present study, the in vitro
antimicrobial activity of the Ti-, Zr-, and Hf-MON ﬁlm-coated
Ti disks against MRSA, one of the leading bacteria involved in
IAIs, was investigated via studying their adhesion on the
surface of the Ti disks. As shown in Figure S9, the poor
antibacterial activity of the tetravalent metals-based ﬁlm should
be attributed to the robust M4+−O bonds, which are less prone
to hydrolysis in aqueous conditions.
Heterobimetallic MM-MON Films and Their Anti-
bacterial Performance. We hypothesized that the limita-
tions of strong M4+−O bonds might be circumvented by
combining a hard and second softer metal ion (Cu2+ or Zn2+)
with intrinsic antibacterial activity to obtain the balance of
chemical stability and dissolution of a bactericide for the
antibacterial activity enhancement. According to Pearson’s
hard−soft acid−base principle,39 the metal−ligand coordina-
tion bond strength is determined by the polarization of
carboxylate ligands (hard Lewis base) and metal ions (Lewis
acid). The ionization potential (In) (electron acceptability)
reﬂects the polarizability of the metal ions and the hardness of
Lewis acid in the order of Ti4+ > Zr4+ > Hf4+ > Cu2+ > Zn2+
(Figure 5a and Table S3),40 which is in accord with the
population of M4+−O revealed in the deconvoluted O 1s
spectra of Ti-, Zr-, and Hf-MON ﬁlms. Furthermore, the
metal−ligand coordination bonds strength was estimated by
density functional theory (DFT) calculations to reveal the
metal ion and pMBA interactions. As shown in Figure 5b and
Table S4, tetravalent metal ions (hard acid) have a much
stronger binding aﬃnity with carboxylic groups than divalent
metal ions (borderline acid), which is in line with the tendency
of ionization potential.
Next, we explore this concept by using heterobimetallic
coordination bonds of mixed-metal ions. After the co-
incorporation of Cu2+ or Zn2+ with M4+, the valence state of
Cu or Zn in the MM-MON ﬁlms was investigated by XPS. The
deconvoluted XPS spectra of Cu 2p show two subpeaks for
each of the Cu 2p1/2 and Cu 2p3/2 levels (Figure S10). The left
subpeaks (green lines) observed at 934.5 and 954.3 eV are
characteristic of the Cu2+, while the right subpeaks (blue lines)
observed at 932.5 and 952.4 eV can be attributed to the Cu+,
which are reduced from a fraction of Cu2+.41 Furthermore, two
accompanying shakeup satellite peaks corroborated the
presence of a coordination interaction in the form of Cu2+.42
The similarly divided subpeaks of Cu 2p suggested their
electronic similarities. However, as shown in Figure S10, no Zn
2p was detected in the M4+ and Zn2+ co-blended MM-MON
ﬁlms, resulting from the fast dissolution of Zn2+ due to the
weakest and a relatively longer Zn−O bond (Table S4), thus
bearing the worst coordination ability. Then, a question arose,
are the mixed-metal ions randomly distributed or uniformly
distributed in the MM-MON ﬁlms, which are mostly found
with randomly distributed behaviors in mixed-metal MOFs?19
To examine the distribution of mixed-metal ions, HAADF-
STEM analysis together with elemental mapping was
conducted. When the mixture of GNCs-M4+-Cu2+ in ethanol
was deposited onto the carbon ﬁlm-coated copper mesh TEM
grid and allowed to evaporate in the air, it dries into a MM-
MON ﬁlm. As shown in HAADF-STEM images, heavy atoms
Au are observed brighter, but lighter atoms are diﬃcult to
observe, since the atomic number of Au (Z = 79) is much
larger than the other constituent elements (Ti: Z = 22, Zr: Z =
40, Hf: Z = 72, Cu: Z = 29). As shown in Figure 5c and
Figures S11 and 12, EDX mappings of the MM-MON ﬁlms
illustrated that Cu and hard metal (Ti, Zr, Hf) species regularly
distributed along the GNCs, further verifying the coexistence
of a mixed-metal−ligand coordination bond.
Furthermore, a synchrotron grazing-incidence wide-angle X-
ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurement was carried out to
study the molecular packing and crystallinity of the
homometallic MON and heterobimetallic MM-MON ﬁlms.

CONCLUSION
In summary, we demonstrated the adjustable antibacterial
activity of pMBA-capped GNCs. We propose the anionic
amphiphilic GNCs can disrupt the structural integrity of the
phospholipid bilayer, increase membrane permeability, and
then evoke the leakage of essential intracytoplasmic contents.
The antibacterial activity is closely related to the protonation
level of pMBA ligands, and with more ligands protonated
under a lower pH environment, weakened electrostatic
repulsion and enhanced hydrophobic interactions synergisti-
cally reinforce the disruption of the bacterial membrane by
GNCs. Furthermore, we have rationally designed a series of
GNC-based metal−carboxylate coordination bond-directed
ﬁlms on a solid surface as antibacterial nanocoatings. By
combining of robust M4+−O bonds and inferior Cu2+−O
bond, the heterobimetallic MM-MON ﬁlms enable the
controlled dissolution of Cu2+, while the structural integrity
is retained. The in vitro and in vivo results demonstrated the
bacteria-triggered release character and contact-killing capa-
bility of the self-defensive MM-MON ﬁlm nanocoating.
Therefore, the modular nature of the GNC−metal networks
will be amenable to facile antibacterial nanocoatings of metal
surfaces and oﬀers preventive and therapeutic beneﬁts for the
development of next-generation antibacterial surface modiﬁ-
cation.
EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
Materials. Gold(III) chloride trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O, 99%)
and sodium borohydride (98%) were obtained from Sinopharm
Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The chemical
compounds para-mercaptobenzoic acid (90%), titanium(IV) chloride
(99.99%), zirconium(IV) chloride (99.9%), hafnium(IV) chloride
(99.5%), copper(II) chloride dihydrate, and zinc chloride anhydrous
were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).
Extended-spectrum β-lactamase Escherichia coli (ESBL E. coli, ATCC
35218), methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA, ATCC
43300), Staphylococcus epidermidis (SE, ATCC12228), and Pseudo-
monas aeruginosa (PA, ATCC15442) were obtained from the
Department of Microbiology Laboratory, Shanghai Jiao Tong
University Aﬃliated Sixth People’s Hospital. LIVE/DEAD BacLight
Bacterial Viability Kit was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc
(Waltham, MA, USA). All solutions were prepared with ultrapure
water (resistivity 18.2 MΩ.cm) using a Milli-Q water system.
Synthesis of pMBA-Capped Gold Nanoclusters. The synthesis
of GNCs was based on a previous report.45 Aqueous solutions of 294
mM HAuCl4 and 100 mM pMBA methanol solution were combined
with water and methanol to give 3 mM HAuCl4, 6 mM pMBA, and
50% (v/v) methanol. After the formation of white Au(I)-pMBA
complexes in the mixture (100 mL), an aqueous NaOH solution (1
M, 0.5 mL) was added to dissolve the mixture. A freshly prepared
solution of 150 mM NaBH4 in water was added to the mixture in a
3:1 ratio of NaBH4:Au together with another 0.5 mL NaOH (1 M).
After that, the above reaction solution was stirred quickly overnight.
The ﬁnal product was precipitated by the addition of NaCl (to 10
mM) and ethanol (to 50%) until the solution became turbid and
centrifuged at 8000 g for 5 min. The pellet was allowed to dry and
redissolve in water, obtaining water-soluble partially deprotonated
GNCs. The fully protonated GNCs were prepared by adding a large
excess of 1 M HCl. For example, the pH of obtained water-soluble
GNCs (1 mL, 4 mg/mL) was adjusted to about 10.0 by using 1 N
NaOH, then about 2 mL of HCl (1M) was added to the above GNCs
aqueous solutions, causing immediate precipitation from the water.
The obtained black precipitate was centrifuged, decanted, and rinsed
three times with deionized water to remove excess HCl and NaCl.
Afterward, the fully protonated GNCs were dissolved in ethanol.
Bacterial Culture and Antibacterial Analyses. The antibacte-
rial activity of gold nanoclusters was assessed with Gram-positive
methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA, ATCC 43300) and
Gram-negative extended-spectrum β-lactamases Escherichia coli
(ESBL E. coli ATCC 35218) bacteria, which were provided by the
Department of Microbiology Laboratory, Shanghai Sixth People’s
Hospital. In brief, a monocolony of MRSA or ESBL E. coli on the
sheep blood agar (SBA) plate was dipped into 5 mL of fresh trypticase
soy broth (TSB) medium and cultured to a mid-log growth phase at
37 °C under 200 rpm. Then the obtained bacterial suspensions were
diluted in TSB (pH 7.4) to 1−2 × 106 CFU/mL based on the optical
density of the suspension at 600 nm (OD600). Subsequently, the
above bacterial solution of 900 μL was introduced to the sterile test
tube, and 100 μL of GNCs (ﬁnal concentrations of 12.5−200 μg/mL)
was then added to tubes and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Then the
bacteria were diluted and spread on SBA plate for the CFU analysis
and with the following equation to evaluate antibacterial eﬀects:

Preparation of GNCs-Based Metal−Organic Network Films.
GNCs-based MON ﬁlms were formed by dropping the mixtures of
fully protonated GNCs and metal ions in ethanol on the surface of
titanium disks, silicon wafers, or TEM grids. For the preparation of
Ti-, Zr, or Hf-based homometallic MON ﬁlms, the fully protonated
GNCs, TiCl4, ZrCl4, and HfCl4 were dissolved in ethanol,
respectively. As an example, 1 mL of fully protonated GNCs (4
mg/mL, about 0.5 mM pMBA ligand) and 50 μL ZrCl4 (100 mM) in
ethanol with the blend mole ratio of pMBA:Zr4+ = 1:1 was
ultrasonically mixed for 2 min. Next, the as-prepared GNCs-Zr
suspension was deposited onto the surface of the silicon wafer or
titanium disks with a coverage density of 63.67 μL/cm2. The
suspension was then allowed to evaporate to dryness under room
temperature (RT). Notably, the suspension ultimately dried into a
blue ﬁlm on the silicon wafer in a few minutes and left to continue
drying overnight. Similar procedures were performed to prepare the
Ti-MON and Hf-MON ﬁlms. For the preparation of Ti−Cu-, Zr−
Cu-, or Hf−Cu-based MM-MON ﬁlms, the fully protonated GNCs,
TiCl4, ZrCl4, HfCl4, and CuCl2 were ﬁrst dissolved in ethanol,

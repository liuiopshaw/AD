PDA and CeO2 synergistically functionalized montmorillonite
nanosheets for reinforcing photothermal healing, antibacterial
and anticorrosive coatings

, Lei Huang, Yang-Kai Xiong,

Abstract The introduction of ﬁllers boosts the perfor-
mance of polymer coatings and extends their service life.
However, single component ﬁllers are not sufﬁcient for
intelligent coatings, and compatibility between the polymer
matrix and the ﬁller remains a major challenge. In this
study,
functional
polydopamine
(PDA)
modiﬁed
CeO2/sodium-based
montmorillonite
(Na-MMT)
or
CeO2/calcium-based montmorillonite (Ca-MMT) ﬁllers
were designed and fabricated via facile in-situ method. The
coatings incorporated with MMT-based ﬁllers that were
prepared demonstrated remarkable anti-corrosion capabil-
ities, exceptional antimicrobial resistance, and rapid self-
healing properties. Speciﬁcally, the low-frequency impe-
dance (|Z|0.01 Hz) values of PDA/CeO2/Na-MMT/EP and
PDA/CeO2/Ca-MMT/EP,
after
being
immersed
for
30 days, were sustained at 2.24 9 107 and 1.70 9 107 X
cm2, respectively. Additionally, the bacteriostatic rates of
the ﬁller against E. coli and S. aureus can both reach above
99.9%, respectively, due to the photothermal effect and
synergistic bacteriostatic mechanism of PDA and CeO2.
The scratches healed rapidly within 40 s under near-in-
frared (NIR) irradiation. This work provides valuable
guidance for the utilization of MMT-based sheet ﬁllers for
enhanced corrosion-resistant, antimicrobial, and repairable
coatings.
Keywords Montmorillonite (MMT); Polydopamine
(PDA); Anticorrosion; Antibacterial; Photothermal self-
healing
1 Introduction
The presence of sodium chloride renders seawater a potent
electrolyte, furnishing an ideal conductive medium for the
electrochemical corrosion of metals. Consequently, the
durability of metals within the marine environment is
substantially diminished. To withstand the rigors of such a
harsh marine milieu, a diverse array of anti-corrosion
strategies has been devised to safeguard metallic materials.
These strategies include judicious selection of material

Among them, epoxy-based organic coatings have been
extensively employed for corrosion inhibition due to their
commendable corrosion resistance, outstanding adhesion,
and favorable mechanical properties [11, 12]. However,
during the curing process of epoxy resins, the ring-opening
reaction between epoxy groups and active hydrogen, as
well as the volatilization of solvents within the reaction
system, invariably leads to the formation of micropores and
microcracks within the coating. Furthermore, within the
complex and harsh marine environment, pure epoxy coat-
ings exhibit a high susceptibility to damage caused by
external forces, resulting in a shortened service life
[13, 14]. Notably, the introduction of functional ﬁllers not
only improves the corrosion resistance of organic coatings
but also imparts smarter functions to the coatings, such as
antimicrobial and self-healing.
Two-dimensional (2D) ﬂake materials, such as boron
nitride [15, 16], mica [17], glass scales [18], basalt scales
[19] and montmorillonite (MMT) [20], have been inten-
sively investigated as ﬁllers in organic coatings. MMT
represents a type of layered silicate mineral characterized by
a large speciﬁc surface area and signiﬁcant cation exchange
capacity. The interlayer cations, including Na?, Ca2?, Zn2?,
etc., serve to stabilize the net negative charge of the lami-
nates [21–23]. It is noteworthy that the 2D ﬂake materials
manifest a labyrinthine effect, thereby exerting a signiﬁcant
physical barricading effect against oxygen, water, and
chloride ions in seawater. However, in complex application
scenarios, the improvement of the corrosion resistance of
coatings by relying solely on a single physical barrier effect
is limiting. Consequently, it is necessary to integrate it with
alternative strategies to actualize a synergistic anti-corrosion
effect, thereby securing long-term protection of metallic
substrates. CeO2 manifests a cubic ﬂuorite structure with the
presence of both Ce3? and Ce4? [24, 25]. When the metal
undergoes an electrochemical reaction and loses electrons,
Ce4? can act as an oxidant and be reduced to Ce3? to
consume electrons. Meanwhile, Ce3? could also be con-
verted to Ce4? through an oxidation reaction. Additionally,
the reversible conversion between Ce3? and Ce4? generates
reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as the superoxide anion
radical (O2
-), hydroxyl radical (OH), and hydrogen perox-
ide (H2O2), which are effective in hindering the growth of
bacteria, or even inducing bacterial death [26, 27].
Nevertheless, the incompatibility between inorganic
substances and epoxy resin leads to heterogeneous dis-
persion [28], which hinders the effective improvement of
coating properties. Polydopamine (PDA) contains reactive
functional groups, i.e., phenolic hydroxyl and amino
groups [29] that can chemically react with epoxy, and can
also establish connections via intermolecular interactions,
such as hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces
[30, 31]. Consequently, PDA is commonly employed to
modify inorganic substances to improve their dispersion in
organic
coatings.
Moreover,
PDA
offers
remarkable
advantages as a photothermal agent, with photothermal
conversion efﬁciencies of up to 40% [32]. PDA exhibits
broad-spectrum absorption properties in the ultraviolet to
near-infrared (NIR) range, making it particularly suit-
able for photothermal processes driven by NIR light [33].
In this study, cerium oxide nanoparticles were initially
loaded onto the surface of a two-dimensional sheet of
montmorillonite (Na-MMT and Ca-MMT) via the co-pre-
cipitation
and
sintering
method.
Subsequently,
poly-
dopamine was coated on the surface of cerium oxide-
modiﬁed montmorillonite (CNMMT and CCMMT) to
obtain PDA/CNMMT (PCNMMT) and PDA/CCMMT
(PCCMMT). The synthesized PCNMMT and PCCMMT
were employed to enhance the corrosion resistance and
antimicrobial properties of the epoxy coatings. Moreover,
the effect of MMT with different interlayer cations (Na?
and Ca2?) on the anticorrosion properties was compared.

At room temperature, 0.5 g of dopamine hydrochloride and

and PCCMMT display a redshift, which may be attributed

ally, it can be seen that the characteristic absorption peaks
of PCNMMT and PCCMMT at 1357 and 1583 cm-1
respectively correspond to the stretching vibration of the
C–N bond in the quinone structure of the PDA molecule
and the stretching vibration of the C=C bond in the aro-
matic ring [38]. XPS spectra illustrated in Fig. 1G reveal
that the peaks associated with CeO2, speciﬁcally Ce 3d, Ce

peaks in PCNMMT and PCCMMT decline. Moreover, due
to the complex electronic conﬁguration of the Ce atom,
electrons in its 3d orbital can shift from the ground state to
various excited states. Because of spin–orbit and angular
momentum coupling, the d orbital undergoes splitting [24].
As a result, the Ce 3d peaks can be simulated into ten peaks
(Fig. 1H), namely u0, u, u’, u’’, u’’’, v0, v, v’’, v’’’, and
v’’’’. Among them, u0, u’, v0, and v’ belong to Ce3?, while
u, u’’, u’’’, v, v’’, and v’’’’ belong to Ce4?. As shown in
Table S1, the ratios of Ce3?/ (Ce3? ? Ce4?) of CNMMT
and CCMMT are 35.17% and 33.19% respectively, and the

the samples is higher [39]. Simultaneously, a peak of N 1s
emerges at 399.9 eV (Fig. 1I), which veriﬁes the successful
modiﬁcation of CeO2/MMT by PDA.
3.2 Design and characterization of PCNMMT-EP
and PCCMMT-EP
The schematic illustration of the PCNMMT-EP and
PCCMMT-EP coatings is depicted in Fig. 2A. The layers
PCNMMT and PCCMMT serve as physical barriers to
enhance the corrosion resistance of the coatings. As can be
seen from Fig. 2B–D, the surface of the epoxy coating is
smooth, but a multitude of cracks is observable in its cross-
section. In contrast, the surfaces of the PCNMMT/EP and
PCCMMT/EP are roughened by the incorporation of ﬁllers.
Remarkably, the cross-sectional cracks are substantially
mitigated due to the formation of a favorable interface
layer between the PDA and the epoxy system. The results
of pulled-off adhesion test in Fig. 2E show that the adhe-
sion of EP coating is 3.92 MPa, whereas the NMMT/EP,
CMMT/EP, CNMMT/EP,
and CCMMT/EP
show an
increase of about 30% in adhesion with respect to the pure
EP coatings, which may be attributed to the anchoring of
the coatings by the ﬁller. However, the adhesion of
PCNMMT/EP and PCCMMT/EP coatings increased to
6.31 and 7.00 MPa, respectively, due to the presence of
various oxygen-containing groups in the molecular chain

the metal matrix. In Fig. 2F, NMMT, CMMT, CNMMT,
and CCMMT exhibit distinct absorption in the UV and
violet regions. Nevertheless, PCNMMT and PCCMMT
display relatively signiﬁcant absorption of NIR irradiation,

the PCNMMT/EP and PCCMMT/EP show the optimal
ﬁller content for both samples are 3 wt%, and the |Z|0.01 Hz

Na? in NMMT and Ca2? in CMMT exerts a minimal
impact on the corrosion resistance of the coating coatings,
and further conﬁrming that the corrosion is mainly caused
by chloride anions.
The
corrosion
behavior
of
PCNMMT3/EP
and
PCCMMT3/EP coatings in real seawater was studied
through electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)
tests. In Fig. S10, the Nyquist plots show that both coatings
exhibit complete single capacitive arc characteristics dur-
ing the 0–10 days immersion period, and the radius of the
capacitive arc decreases gradually with the prolongation of
immersion time. The corresponding low-frequency impe-
dance modulus (|Z|0.01 Hz) shows a synchronous attenuation
trend: the |Z|0.01 Hz of the PCNMMT3/EP coating decreases
from the initial 4.04 9 108 to 1.60 9 108 X cm2 after
10 days, while that of the PCCMMT3/EP coating decrea-
ses from 1.53 9 108 to 7.58 9 107 X cm2. Compared with
the 3.5 wt% NaCl solution system, the impedance attenu-
ation laws of the two coatings in real seawater are basically
consistent with those in the simulated solution, and no
double capacitive arcs representing interfacial corrosion
appear, indicating that the corrosive medium does not
penetrate the coatings to reach the metal substrate within
10 days. This is attributed to the dense passivation ﬁlm
promoted by CeO2 nanoparticles, which effectively blocks
the electrolyte penetration path, conﬁrming that the two
coatings have good long-term protective performance in
seawater salinity environments.

3.5 Antibacterial performance
As shown in Fig. 5, the antibacterial rates of NMMT and
CMMT against Gram-negative E. coli were 12.3% and
23.4%, respectively, and against Gram-positive S. aureus
were 27.1% and 28.0%, respectively. The sharp edge of
NMMT can physically damage the bacterial cell membrane
[43, 44]. The inhibitory rates of CeO2-loaded CNMMT and
CCMMT against E. coli were 90.7% and 85.1%, respec-
tively, and against S. aureus were 74.9% and 74.2%,
respectively. The mechanism of the antibacterial effect is
that negatively charged bacterial cells interact electrostat-
ically with the positively charged cerium oxide nanopar-
ticles. Additionally, Ce3? and Ce4? of cerium oxide
undergo reversible oxidation transformation in an aerobic
environment, resulting in the generation of ROS [38],
which can damage the bacterial cell membrane as well as
intracellular components such as DNA and proteins,
thereby effectively inhibiting bacterial growth and repro-
duction [45].

PCCMMT against E. coli attained 94.8% and 90.0%,

4 Conclusion
In summary, multifunctional PCNMMT and PCCMMT
with synergistic corrosion resistance, antimicrobial, and
photothermal effects were obtained by a facile method. The
inhibition rates of PCNMMT and PCCMMT against E. coli
and S. aureus can reach above 99.9% under the pho-
tothermal effect of PDA and ROS mechanism of cerium
oxide. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy results
indicated that after immersed in 3.5 wt% NaCl solution for
30 days, the |Z|0.01 Hz of PCNMMT3/EP and PCCMMT3/
EP were 2.24 9 107 and 1.70 9 107 X cm2, respectively.
Additionally, the scratched coating could be rapidly
repaired within 40 s under NIR illumination. This work
presents a facile method to synthesize multifunctional
nanoﬁllers and coating, which holds the potential to pro-
long the service life of metals in marine environments.

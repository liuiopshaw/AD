a b s t r a c t
Currently, indiscriminate use of traditional antibiotics drives the rapid evolution of multidrug-resistant
pathogenic bacteria. Silver has been regarded as antimicrobial agent since ancient times, while silver
nanostructures can treat bacteria through multiple interaction mechanisms, such as enhanced targetabil-
ity, augmented drug loading efﬁciency and tunable physiochemical property. Generally, nano-silver with
small sizes can eliminate pathogenic bacteria more efﬁciently, while it tends to aggregate, causing insta-
bility. After the formation of alloys with other noble metal elements such as gold, platinum, palladium,
the stability of these nanomaterials can be signiﬁcantly enhanced, with antibacterial activity maintained.
Therefore, in this review, we focus on the roles of silver in Ag-based alloy nanostructure for antibacterial
activity, according to the following mechanisms: the release of Ag+ ions to directly disturb the bacterial
metabolism, the assistance to modulate surface plasmonic resonance adsorption of Ag-alloys to realize
near infrared phototherapy, and the combination with Pt or Pd to the production of catalyze reactive oxy-
gen species under bacterial microenvironment. Based on these understandings, we will summarize some
common antibacterial disciplines for Ag-based nanoalloys, which may guide the further investigation for
efﬁcient antibacterial applications.

coccus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Acinetobacter baumannii,
Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Enterobacter
species, are considered as the most threat for global health [1,2].
This troublesome issue started with the ﬁrst occurrence of MDR
pneumoniae in 1967. From that moment on, the ratio of MDR
pneumoniae cases to total pneumoniae cases was increasing very
rapidly, reaching 85% in 2000 and far more now [3]. For other
antibiotics, similar cases occurred due to their abundant and indif-
ferent usage. More terribly, it was reported that more than 70%
bacteria could resist more than one antibiotic [4]. On the contrast,

antibiotics since 1980 s [5]. Therefore, it is urgent to develop

In recent years, noble metal nanomaterials have been widely
explored and applied clinically because their physicochemical
characteristics
could

antibacterial use. Among them, silver nanostructures with differ-
ent shapes and sizes show great prospect in terms of bacterial
infection [6–9]. Due to the high afﬁnity of Ag to extracellular and
intracellular nitrogen and sulfur-containing biomolecules such as
nucleic acids and proteins, common cell activities such as cell divi-
sion and respiration would be affected, which eventually causes
bacteria death [10,11]. Ag nanostructures exhibit size-dependent
antibacterial efﬁciency. The smaller the Ag nanoparticles (NPs),
the higher the antibacterial efﬁciency. When the size of Ag nano-
materials reaches beyond 40 nm, endocytosis becomes hard and
the amount of bioavailable Ag+ retains bare [12,13]. When the sizes
are same, Ag NPs with a positive charged surface render more
effective antibacterial activity than that by the negative charged
counterparts [14]. Although antibacterial efﬁciency could be
improved by controlling size and surface charges of Ag nanomate-
rials, an unavoidable fact is that they tend to aggregate due to col-
loidal instability [15]. This aggregation makes silver nanomaterials
less entry into bacteria and also decreases the amount of intercel-
lular Ag+ [16,17]. Generally, ultraviolet light drives the aggregation
of Ag NPs. This effect involves the oscillating dipole–dipole interac-
tion due to spectral overlap between ultraviolet band and absorp-
tion band of common silver nanomaterials [18,19]. In addition to
unfavorable aggregation, silver materials express evidently adverse
effect to host than other noble elements [20,21], which is ascribed
to uncontrollable silver ions release that only occurred for silver.
One possible way to defuse these embarrassing issues is to coat
Ag NPs with other noble metal elements, such as Au, Pd and Pt to
form alloy structures [22–24]. As known, the physicochemical
properties of Ag nanoalloys largely determine their antibacterial
efﬁciency. For instance, the dispersion stability of Ag nanoplate is
largely improved after gold-coating, which also renders stronger
antibacterial activity [25]. In the meanwhile, the surface charge
of alloy nanoparticles will shift to be positive as gold coating
increases, which will be beneﬁcial for endocytosis by negatively-
charged surface of bacteria [26]. Another advantage is that the sur-
face plasmonic resonance (SPR) property of silver nanomaterials
could be tailored from visible to near infrared (NIR) Ⅱregion. Under
NIR irradiation these alloys can effectively devastate the bioﬁlm, a
stubborn shield to protect bacteria from antibiotics through pho-
tothermal therapy (PTT). Moreover, the intrinsic catalytic effects
(i.e., oxidase-like, and peroxidase-like activity) are also coupled
with Au, Pd or Pt coated silver alloys [27], which produces a large
amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as O2
– from O2 and
OH from H2O2 to attack MDR bacteria.
In this review, we begin with comparisons of Ag+ releasing efﬁ-
ciency and antibacterial efﬁciency of different Ag-based nanoal-
loys, mainly limited in Ag-Au, Ag-Pd, Ag-Pt and Ag-Ir materials
(scheme). As a key example, Ag-Au nanomaterials are high con-
cerned due to their controllable Ag+ release. Especially, several
adjuvants to help silver etch from Ag-Au alloys are summarized
to assess the antibacterial possibility. In the following section, we
focus on the applications of silver alloys with NIR adsorption and
catalytic activity of ROS production for ﬁghting against MDR bacte-
ria. Based on these understandings, we aim to outline some com-
mon disciplines to enlighten the clinically-wide use of silver-
alloy nanomaterials.
2. A brief survey of antibacterial mechanism of Ag
nanostructures and nanoalloys
Ag nanostructures and nanoalloys have be universally applied
for ﬁghting against bacteria. Upon their stimulation, bacteria will
face many threatens. First, nanoparticles can be attached to the
bacterial surface by multiple coordination bonds including Ag-N,
Ag-S, Au-N, and Au-S bonding with membrane proteins. Nanopar-
ticles with a positively charged surface may have stronger absorba-
bility and are conductive to entry into bacterial cytoplasm. [6,12]
Second, in the cytoplasm, Ag nanostructures and nanoalloys can
be oxidized and released in the form of Ag+. These free Ag+ ions
can further coordinate with intracellular [4Fe-4S] clusters and
induce the release of [Fe]. The elevating Fe content augments the
Fenton’s reaction and produces ROS [28,29]. In addition, the upreg-
ulated ROS can also be generated by endogenic catalysis of Ag, Au
nanoclusters or Ag-Pd, Ag Pt nanoalloys [12,30], which can kill bac-
teria directly. Besides, intracellular components, such as DNA, and
proteins can also coordinate with Ag atoms or Ag+, resulting into a
disorder of cellular metabolism.

protect Ag from oxidation and release.

be extended to other silver-based alloys. For instance, Ag dots array
were respectively deposited on Pt, Pd and Ir thin ﬁlm to form sim-
ilar galvanic cell structures. Ag-Ir system showed the best antibac-
terial performance, followed by Ag-Pd and Ag-Pt [37–39].
In above examples, Ag loses electrons after forming alloys.
However, Ag can also accept electrons from other noble metals,
forming anti-oxidating status at their interface, which may be
called ‘‘electron compensation effect” (Fig. 1C). This effect is incon-
sistent with the sacriﬁcial anode effect as for Ag oxidation and clo-
sely related to average electrons transferred from other metals to
Ag [40,41]. For instance, although both nanoplate and nanocage
have core@shell (Ag@Au) structure, silver at the interface of nano-
plate could accept more electrons offered by gold layer on average.
Thus, the electron compensation effect in Ag-Au nanoplate is
stronger than that of nanocage.
Besides, it should be noted that the distribution of two metal
elements may determine the antibacterial effect, since contradic-
tory results may be obtained if metal components are compared.
For examples, a low cytotoxicity but high antimicrobial activity
was achieved by co-sputtered Ag50Pt50 alloys compared with pure
Ag NPs. In this case, no increasing Ag+ release was observed, indi-
cating the sacriﬁcial anode effect was not enhanced.[42] However,
in another two works, sacriﬁcial anode effect for consecutively
sputtered Ag-Pt nanopatches was proved to impart an increased
antibacterial activity to Ag NPs [43,44]. These opposite outcomes
can be explained by the different distribution styles(Fig. 1B) [45].
The consecutively sputtered Ag-Pt alloys has a clear interface, ren-
dering enhanced sacriﬁcial anode effect. Whilst, in co-sputtered
Ag-Pt structure, electrons from Pt element can be divided to Ag
atoms evenly, resulting into increase of electron compensation
effect and decrease of sacriﬁcial anode effect. In conclusion, a
well-bedded Ag-Pt structure might entail more potential for accel-
erated Ag+ release and antibacterial effect.
Overall, the Ag+ release is modulated by the combination of
electron compensation effect and sacriﬁced anode effect. As for
in vivo applications such as antitumor researches where Ag+
release should be ameliorated in normal tissues, electron compen-
sation effect by nanoalloys should be strengthened. This conclu-
sion may be available for other nano-scale alloy materials [47]. It
was summarized that Ag+ release and antibacterial efﬁciency is
in a well line with the order: Pd-Ag > Pt-Ag > Au-Ag > pure Ag sys-
tems (Fig. 1D). The theoretical evaluation showed that this differ-
ence on earth results from the upshift amount of d band centers,
where Pd-Ag alloys show the largest shift toward the Fermi level.
Under this situation, the Ag core is prone to be oxidized.
In short, heterometal atoms cannot enhance antibacterial prop-
erties of Ag-based nanoalloys only in all cases. In the well-bedded
alloy structures, heterometal element such as Au, Pd, Pt and Ir can
enhance Ag+ release, which can be well explained by sacriﬁcial
anode effect and upshift d-band center theory (Fig. 1). Whist, in
evenly distributed alloy structures, the electron compensation
effect should be taken into serious consideration during design of
anti-infectious alloy structures.
4. Etching agents for Ag+ release from Ag-Au alloys
Although Ir-Ag alloys shows the best antibacterial perfor-
mances prior to Pd-Ag, Pt-Ag and Au-Ag alloys, the high production
cost of Ir, Pd and Pt largely limits their popular use [48,49]. Com-
pared with these specially-rare elements, Au might be the most
potential candidate [50]. As discussed above, a thin and porous
Au-coating could contribute to Ag+ release, whilst a thick or intact
Au-layer might protect Ag from this process. Although the
enhanced release of Ag+ by Au layer is not same as that by Ir, Pd
and Pt layer, it is worthy to seek out valuable means to trigger
Ag+ release from Ag-Au alloys in a controllable way in order to
enhance the antibacterial effect as well as to decrease side-effect.
The galvanic replacement (GR) is a classic method applied to
synthesize Ag-Pt, Ag-Pd, Ag-Au nanoalloys [22,23,55]. During this
process, oxidized Ag species tend to be released rapidly. Moreover,
to modulate the dynamic diffusion of Ag and deposition of Au to
form smooth core–shell nanostructure, it requires a proper tem-
perature and pH environment [56]. Besides, metal salts (HAuCl4,
Na2PdCl4 and Na2PtCl4) have strong oxidization capacity, which
is quite easy to induce side effects and unproper for clinical use
in vivo. Herein, taking GSNCs as an example, we summarize some
mild etching methods and compare their advantages and disad-
vantages for anti-infection applications in vivo (Fig. 2).
The ﬁrst approach is to involve a common oxidant, such as Fe
(NO3)3, to selectively peel off Ag-core (Fig. 2A-B) [51]. Compared
with that etched by HAuCl4, oxidization by Fe3+ in a mild environ-
ment is hopeful for biomedical applications. Moreover, this mild
etching method could preserve main framework of GSNCs even
in the presence of excess Fe3+. Second, Ag inside GSNCs is selec-
tively etched by synergistic effect of oxygen and water-soluble thi-
ols
including
methoxy-terminated
poly
(ethylene
glycol)-SH
(mPEG-SH) and 2-mercaptoethanol (Fig. 2 E-F) [52]. In this combi-
nation, thiolates act as a coordinating bridge between Ag and O2 in
solution as oxidant. A similar mechanism was performed by using
NH4OH to etch Au/Ag nanowires (Fig. 2C-D) [52]. Recently, our
group found that glutathione can trigger the release of ultra-
small silver particles from GSNCs aggregates (Fig. 2I-J) [54]. The
aggregation of GSNCs induced by intermolecular hydrogen bond-
ing from GSH cannot affect Ag+ release, which is converse to the
traditional-common view that aggregation hindered silver release
[17]. Furthermore, by comparing the etching ability of different
thiols, –NH2 and -SH groups might contribute to silver etching
from GSNCs while –COOH might hinder this process [57]. The cor-
responding products contain ultrasmall Ag NPs with an excellent
antibacterial efﬁciency. Last, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), an impor-
tant molecule involved in many biological processes and enzy-
matic reactions, also promotes Ag-etching from GSNCs [53].
Excess H2O2 can preserve the porous structures comprised of Au
frameworks (Fig. 2 G-H), which can be beneﬁcial for catalysis
[58,59].

Gram-negative bacteria including E. coli 013, Pseudomonas aerugi-
nosa CCM 3955 and E. coli CCM 3954 can develop resistance to Ag
NPs after repeated exposures. This resistance was stemmed from
the production of ﬂagellum protein, which triggered the aggrega-
tion of NPs [69]. All these results alarm us to seek for new strate-
gies to deal with the possible resistance by Ag NPs as broad-
spectrum antibiotics.
The combination of Ag based nanomaterials with accompanied
chemical or physical strategies may strengthen their antibacterial
efﬁciency. Metal nanoclusters (i.e., Ag, Au and AuAg nanoclusters)
with core size less than 2 nm are emerging as hopeful candidates
for combat MDR species. First, the sub-nano size imparts metal
nanoclusters with quick diffusion and easy endocytosis by bacteria,
and
oxidase/peroxidase-mimetic
properties
to
produce
ROS
[70,71]. Besides, most nanoclusters have strong and stable lumi-
nescence even in vivo, which can track their interaction with bac-
teria to study antimicrobial mechanisms. Recently, daptomycin
(Dap)-conjugated silver nanoclusters were reported with out-
standing bacterial killing efﬁciency [72]. In this combination, Dap
can scratch bacterial membrane so that Ag NCs can favorably enter
cytoplasm and elevate ROS level. This ‘‘nanometallo-antibiotics”
has been discussed in details [6,62,73,74].
Another potential choice is to combine photothermal irradia-
tion to destroy structure of bacteria due to its advantages including
short treatment time, negligible bacterial resistance, and broad-
spectrum function [75–77]. This requires the effective NIR light
adsorption by metallic nanomaterials. Although the SPR peak of
Ag and Au NPs with size of  40 nm locates at  420 nm
and  550 nm, respectively, [78] this adsorption range can be
easily tuned by depositing gold onto different silver nanostruc-
tures. In this respect, the silver core in the alloy is partially sacri-
ﬁced, while the SPR peak may shift to NIR region [79]. Generally,
the physicochemical and optical properties of Ag-Au, Ag-Pt and
Ag-Pd alloys could be modulated accurately to entail some physical
ways such as PTT [80–82].
Taking GSNCs as example, the SPR absorbance is tuned to NIR
region for GSNCs with a larger size or a deeply etched silver core.
To achieve PTT, almost half part of Ag-core would be etched by
Au (III) during the formation of Au framework. The hollow space
inside cages can be used to load drugs [83,84]. In addition, the orig-
inal Ag-core can be replaced by drug and thermo-responsive phase
transition material to realize controllable drug-release [85]. It
seems to be very conductive to apply antibiotics in this mode to
achieve synergistic antibacterial activity
with PTT
[77].
For
instance, highly hollow GSNCs can be used as an effective PTT
agent to eliminate the bioﬁlm of bacteria under 808 nm irradiation.
A layer of mesoporous SiO2 was coated to improve the biocompat-
ibility and to facilitate sustained Ag+ release, allowing broad-
spectrum antibacterial ability (Fig. 3A) [86]. In another research,
bacterial-pretreated macrophage membranes were coated on the
GSNCs, showing personalized targeting ability to a certain infection
(Fig. 3B) [87]. To enhance antibacterial performances, GSNCs with
808 nm PTT effect was also coupled with daptomycin and Spa-
antibody targeting staphylococcal protein A on the surface of MRSA
[88]. This species-speciﬁc targeting and antimicrobial peptide cou-
pled with NIR therapy was validated to be effective against resis-
tant bioﬁlm infections (Fig. 3C). Compared with the previous two
examples, the design of Dap anchored on the surface of GSNCs
may suffer from low drug-delivering efﬁciency during internal cir-
culation of the body. Although these strategies incorporate both
physical (NIR irradiation) and biochemical (antibacterial peptide)
means, the residual Ag inside nanocages--a native excellent killer,
was ignored. In fact, PTT is of high importance for scratching bio-
ﬁlm by MDR pathogenic bacteria but not efﬁcient in all cases.
Therefore, controllable silver release also should be simultaneously
considered [89–91]. In our recent work, silver components was uti-
lized to cure an infected wound in the presence of cysteamine [57].
Aside from rapid release of silver components, cysteamine could
cause the aggregation of GSNCs, accompanied with SPR adsorption
from visible (530 nm) to NIR-Ⅱ(1064 nm) region, thus allowing
effective PTT. Based on synergistic relationship of PTT and Ag+
release, a broad-spectrum antibacterial strategy was proposed to
treat MDR species effectively.
6. Porous Ag-Pt and Ag-Pd alloys with high antibacterial activity
Another common anti-infection strategy is to drive pathogenic
bacteria
to
unbalanced
oxidation
reduction
reaction
(ORR)
[42,92]. Pt or Pd nanomaterials have been considered as promising
antimicrobials due to that they can catalyze in situ production of
ROS to disturb metabolism of bacteria [93–99]. Generally, the cat-
alytic efﬁciency is positively in line with ratio of surface to volume
of Pt or Pd nanoalloys, [100] which can be synthesized at the sac-
riﬁce of Ag template [101,102].
A hot application is using Pt or Pd-based nanomaterials to accel-
erate the conversion of H2O2 into hydroxyl radicals (OH) that is
poisonous to pathogenic bacteria [102–108]. This strategy can
reduce the necessary concentration of H2O2 for anti-infection to a
level lower than that may cause harm to healthy tissue [105]. For
instance, Ag components were etched from Pt-Ag NPs with differ-
ent Pt/Ag ratios by nitric acid [42]. The as-obtained porous Pt50Ag50
structures endued the enzyme-mimic activities related to ORR and
antibacterial efﬁciency in the presence of H2O2 in acidic environ-
ment (Fig. 4A). Noteworthy, although Pt50Ag50 owns easy etched
effect, the alloys with other Pt/Ag ratios are less efﬁcient because
of stubborn Pt shield in Pt75Ag25, as well as insufﬁcient Pt in Pt25-
Ag75. For Ag-Pd alloy, Xiong and Yang et al. synthesized core@shell
alloy nanocages with different Pd contents. AgPd0.38 alloy was
selected as coating additive of catheter, enabling inhibition of bio-
ﬁlm formation (Fig. 4B) [109]. The AgPd0.38 nanocages utilized
intracellular H2O2 and O2 to generate surface-bounded ROS, with
the toxic effect limited within 20–200 nm. Interestingly, ROS pro-
duced on surface of nanocages exhibit bare toxicity to host mam-
mal cells, but might cause effective damage to pathogenic
bacteria. This selectable toxicity was originated from a fact that
mammalian cells can engulf extracellular AgPd0.38 while bacteria
could not.
Note that different alloy distribution styles were present in the
above two examples. In Pt-Ag alloys, Pt and Ag atoms are dis-
tributed staggered, having no clear boundary. Thus, porous struc-
tures are formed after etching Ag cores, which elevate the ratio
of surface to volume. However, Pd-Ag nanocages are produced
through GR reaction, forming a clear core–shell structure. As dis-
cussed in part 1, Ag core in latter structure is easy to be oxidized
and released, leaving a thin and porous Pd wall. In this respect,
Ag is regarded as a template for producing porous structures and
this role can be replaced by other elements [56,110–114]. As an
example, Pt-Pd core-frame nanodendrites were prepared in two
steps, namely, selective growth of Pt atoms and chemical etch of
Pd atoms by FeBr3 [92]. This nanodentrite structure provides a par-
ticularly active facet and large surface area, exhibiting excellent
bactericidal efﬁciency by peroxidase-like activity to decompose
H2O2 to OH.
7. Conclusion and outlook
The mismatch between evolution of MDR pathogenic bacteria
and new emerging antibiotics alarms us to seek for alternative
antibacterial strategies. Although silver nanomaterials have been
well studied and applied clinically, they still face numerous issues,
especially the emerging anti-silver pathogenic species. This is

Coordination Chemistry Reviews 449 (2021) 214218

mainly caused by the instability of nano silver materials that weak-
ens release and accumulation of Ag+ at infection sites, making Ag+
amount less that minimum bactericidal concentration.
By coating a more inert metal shell to form alloys, the stability
could be well improved while this coating allows controllable Ag+
release. Section 2 introduces some common antibacterial mecha-
nisms of Ag nanoparticles. In Section 3 and 4, we focus on the
Ag+ release behaviors after coating different noble elements. Based
on availability of Ag-Au alloys, a cascade of etching agents was
assessed and thiols might be the most potential candidate. Sec-
tion 5 involves the tunable SPR adsorption by Ag-alloys after deep
etch or controllable aggregation, which permit the introduction of
NIR irradiation for treatment of bioﬁlm by MDR bacteria. In Sec-
tion 6, porous Pt-Ag and Pd-Ag nano alloys are summarized
because they may accelerate the conversion of H2O2/O2 to ROS,
which offer an alternative antibacterial strategy.
Although Ag-alloy nanomaterials exhibit predominant advan-
tages and may act as potential antibiotics to treat MDR, there are
still remaining works to be progressed especially considering its
further clinical applications, especially for in vivo applications:
(1) Stability: it is not clear how to assure the in vivo stability of
Ag based nanoalloys in tissues and blood, as well as the mainte-
nance of their integrity after delivery to infection sites; (2) Toxic-
ity: it is difﬁcult to map the whole metabolic pathways of Ag-
alloy nanomaterials and to decrease the potential health threat
to different host cells in vivo; (3) Targetability: almost all antibi-
otics lack active-targetability, which might be improved by screen-
ing speciﬁc antigens and corresponding antibodies for modiﬁcation
of Ag nanoalloys. (4) Controllability: more attentions are expected
to put on the basic interactions of microenvironment with stable
Ag nanoalloys to build up some trigger systems for Ag release only
at infection sites, largely depending on the smart coordination
chemistry designs. Among these issues, the last three aspects still
lack a comprehensive understanding. So further efforts should be
made to launch new improvements for toxicity and targetability.
Only in this way, a safe, smart, and sustained-therapeutic Ag
nanoalloys can come out for in vivo treatment.

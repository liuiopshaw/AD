A B S T R A C T
The rise of multidrug-resistant bacterial infections presents a critical global health threat, 
significantly challenging clinical treatment. Copper and copper-based nanomaterials are recog­
nized as effective antimicrobial agents. The discovery of the copper-dependent cell death 
pathway, known as cuproptosis, enhances our understanding of their antimicrobial effects. In 
bacteria without mitochondria, similar protein toxicity and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle dis­
ruptions make this effect more accurately termed cuproptosis-like death. Advances in nano­
technology have improved the forms and properties of copper-based nanomaterials, offering 
substantial potential for antibacterial applications through this mechanism. This review begins by 
exploring the dual role of copper in microbiology and examining the mechanisms of cuproptosis- 
like death, with a focus on key detection methods. We further discuss the design strategies for 
developing copper-based nanomaterials, summarizing research progress and applications of 
representative materials such as copper sulfides, copper oxides, metal–organic frameworks, 
nanoclusters, metal-polyphenol networks in the antibacterial field. Additionally, we summarize 
representative cuproptosis-like death-inducing copper-based nanomaterial systems, including 
photo-responsive systems, ultrasound-activated systems, oxidative stress–amplifying systems, 
biofunctionalized systems, multi-metal synergistic systems, gas-releasing systems. Finally, we 
propose future directions for understanding the mechanisms of cuproptosis-like death and opti­
mizing copper-based nanomaterials, aiming to contribute to the development of new methods for 
treating drug-resistant bacterial infections.

disrupt cellular homeostasis [1–4]. However, in 2022, Tsvetkov et al. identified a novel form of copper-dependent programmed cell 
death, termed cuproptosis, which operates through a mechanism distinct from oxidative stress–induced cell death [5]. Cuproptosis is

Copper-based materials, particularly copper-based nanomaterials, have attracted considerable attention in the antimicrobial field

therapy [28–30]. By tuning morphology, valence state, and surface functionalization, various structural types of copper-based 
nanomaterials can achieve controlled redox reactions and stimulus-responsive behaviors within specific infection microenviron­
ments [31–33]. Notably, these systems can selectively elevate copper levels at infection sites to induce bacterial death and can be

lesions.

2. Copper: a Janus-faced element in microbiology
Copper (Cu) is a metal element with unique redox properties, playing a crucial role in cellular metabolism and homeostasis, 
significantly impacting cell function (Fig. 2) [38,39]. In bacteria, copper serves a dual role as both essential and toxic: at low con­
centrations, it supports physiological functions, while at high concentrations, it triggers antimicrobial effects [40–42]. As an essential 
trace element, copper participates in bacterial metabolism and growth regulation under physiological conditions [4,43]. However, 
excess copper can inhibit bacterial activity through oxidative stress and metal interference, causing multi-target damage [3,44]. 
Whether in elemental form or as part of complex compounds, copper's effective antimicrobial properties against various pathogens 
highlight its critical role in infection control [45]. The Janus-faced nature of copper thus reveals intricate connections between metal 
homeostasis, environmental adaptation, and pathogenicity, offering insights into its exploitation for therapeutic interventions and 
biotechnological applications. This section will discuss copper-mediated physiological functions in bacteria and copper-related 
antimicrobial mechanisms, providing a theoretical foundation for developing antimicrobial materials that leverage copper-induced 
effects.
2.1. Copper-mediated physiological functions in bacteria
Copper is an essential trace element for bacterial survival and proliferation, with highly complex and systemic biological functions. 
It can dynamically transition between Cu+ and Cu2+ states through redox reactions and holds the highest position in the Irving- 
Williams series for metal binding stability, granting it strong affinity for biological ligands, particularly those with sulfur or nitro­
gen coordination atoms. This characteristic makes copper a crucial cofactor for various enzymes, including Cu/Zn superoxide dis­
mutase 1 (SOD1), Cytochrome c oxidase (COX), Multicopper oxidases (MCOs), Particulate Methane Monooxygenase (pMMO), Copper- 
Nitrite Reductase (Cu-NiR), Ammonia Monooxygenase (AMO), and Ceruloplasmin. These enzymes play indispensable roles in 
oxidative phosphorylation, superoxide dismutation, metal homeostasis, and electron transport chains [46–48]. Additionally, copper

copper-dependent enzymes like those in the respiratory chain, effectively evading host nutritional immunity [53]. In response to 
copper excess, bacteria initiate layered detoxification mechanisms. Escherichia coli (E.coli) oxidizes highly toxic Cu+ to less toxic Cu2+
via the multicopper oxidase CueO and uses the CusCFBA efflux pump to transport Cu+-glutathione complexes outside the cell, pre­
venting Cu+-induced Fenton reactions and oxidative damage [54]. In summary, copper serves as a central regulatory axis for bacterial 
physiological homeostasis and pathogenicity through its roles in enzyme cofactor function, protein structure stabilization, virulence 
gene network response, and environmental adaptability.
2.2. Copper-related antimicrobial mechanisms
Recent studies have progressively elucidated the diverse molecular mechanisms and complex influencing factors underlying 
copper-related toxicity. The relative importance of these mechanisms in overall copper toxicity appears to vary depending on the 
physical form of copper.
Membrane depolarization has been identified as the primary mechanism of copper ion toxicity in bacteria [55]. Bacterial cell 
membranes normally maintain a transmembrane potential of 100–200 mV during active metabolism. Both membrane surfaces contain 
highly electronegative polymers (e.g., lipopolysaccharides and peptidoglycans) that adsorb metal cations through electrostatic in­
teractions. Copper ion binding to these negatively charged sites reduces the transmembrane potential, causing membrane depolari­
zation and structural integrity loss, ultimately leading to membrane leakage or rupture. Notably, while copper binding to 
lipopolysaccharides, peptidoglycans, carboxyl groups, and sulfhydryl groups occurs in both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bac­
teria, membrane depolarization develops more rapidly and prominently in Gram-negative organisms like E.coli under copper stress 
[56].
The substantial generation of ROS is recognized as a hallmark of the microbicidal effects induced by copper nanoparticles (Cu NPs) 
[4]. Excessive and highly reactive ROS trigger bacterial oxidative stress, subsequently mediating damage to macromolecules such as 
membrane lipids, proteins, and DNA. The elevation of intracellular ROS levels by copper primarily stems from two mechanisms: (1) its 
exceptional catalytic efficiency in driving Fenton chemistry reactions, which synergistically promotes the release of other Fenton- 
active metals (e.g., iron), leading to massive hydroxyl radical (⋅OH) production; and (2) the potential generation of secondary ROS 
through intermediate sulfur radical chemistry during thiol-mediated reduction of Cu2+ [43]. In addition, the direct nanoscale contact 
between Cu NPs and bacterial surfaces forms the physical basis for their high antibacterial efficacy. The high specific surface area of Cu 
NPs significantly enhances their interaction with bacterial cell membranes, facilitating sustained local release of copper ions and 
physically disrupting the integrity of the lipid bilayer. This results in irreversible damage to the membrane's permeability barrier, 
which synergistically amplifies ROS-mediated chemical damage.
Although the theoretical understanding of copper-mediated antibacterial mechanisms is well-developed, the practical application 
of copper-based nanomaterials often exhibits highly dynamic and synergistic effects. Given that copper targets bacteria, and bacterial 
structures and core metabolic pathways are highly conserved, systematically summarizing the universal structural and metabolic 
antibacterial mechanisms can help elucidate the common principles of copper-based nanomaterials. This understanding can guide 
their precise application in the medical field. The antimicrobial mechanisms of copper-based nanomaterials primarily include dis­
rupting the cell wall and membrane, damaging intracellular components, destroying biofilms, and interfering with bacterial 
metabolism.
2.2.1. Disrupting the cell wall and membrane
Disruption of the bacterial cell wall and membrane represents the primary mechanism underlying the antibacterial activity of 
copper-based materials. Cu2+ ions can specifically bind to N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) in the peptidoglycan layer, enabling selective 
adsorption and accumulation on the bacterial surface [1]. In Gram-positive bacteria, Cu2+ interacts with the positively charged tei­
choic acids (TAs), disrupting surface protein anchoring sites and periplasmic spatial organization [2]. In Gram-negative bacteria, Cu2+
engages in electrostatic and redox interactions with the core region of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and lipid A, leading to outer mem­
brane pitting and loss of permeability [57]. Moreover, copper nanoparticles and their released Cu2+ ions can induce lipid peroxidation, 
altering the fluidity and asymmetry of the phospholipid bilayer. This results in membrane disruption, leakage of intracellular contents, 
and ion gradient collapse, ultimately leading to bacterial death [58].

Once internalized, CuNPs catalyze Fenton-like reactions and promote the release of other metal ions (e.g., Fe), generating reactive 
hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH) that cause widespread oxidative damage, including protein denaturation, enzyme inactivation, and DNA 
strand breakage [3]. Even in the absence of exogenous reductants, CuNPs can produce singlet oxygen (1O2), directly oxidizing and 
degrading bacterial DNA [4]. The released Cu2+ ions can also bind dose-dependently to sulfur- and phosphorus-rich regions of DNA, 
disrupting base pairing and double-helix stability, ultimately leading to genetic fragmentation and irreversible bacterial inactivation 
[59]. In addition, Cu2+ interacts with thiol, histidine, and cysteine residues in proteins, inhibiting their biological functions; for 
example, in Haemophilus influenzae, Cu2+ binds to key residues of the virulence factor HvfA, causing peptide degradation and 
attenuating pathogenicity [60].
2.2.3. Interfering with bacterial metabolism
Copper-based materials can further interfere with core bacterial metabolism and genetic information flow through multiple syn­
ergistic mechanisms that impair both energy production and antioxidant systems. Cu2+ competitively replaces essential metal co­
factors in enzymes, leading to their inactivation. In Mycobacterium species, Cu2+ substitutes Fe2+ in IscA/SufA proteins, disrupting 
[4Fe–4S] clusters, inactivating aconitase, and halting the TCA cycle [61]. Similarly, in Staphylococcus aureus, Cu2+ inhibits the for­
mation of [2Fe–2S] clusters, compromising succinate dehydrogenase activity and interrupting the electron transport chain [62]. Under 
copper overload, Cu2+ aberrantly binds to glutathione, ascorbate, and thiol-containing proteins, causing redox imbalance. For 
instance, Cu2+ binds to thioredoxin (Trx) in Bacillus subtilis, depleting key antioxidants and inhibiting the ribonucleotide reductase 
(RNR) cycle, leading to a shortage of DNA precursors and replication arrest [63]. Moreover, copper stress activates oxidative, metal, 
and heat shock pathways (SoxR/OxyR, Cue/Cus, DnaK/GroEL), accelerating ATP and NADPH consumption and disrupting metabolic 
homeostasis [64].
2.2.4. Destroying biofilms
Copper-based materials effectively inhibit and eradicate bacterial biofilms through multi-target and multi-stage synergistic 
mechanisms. Cu2+ reduces cell surface hydrophobicity (CSH), weakens initial bacterial adhesion, and suppresses the synthesis of key 
extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) such as polysaccharides, proteins, and nucleic acids, thereby blocking biofilm matrix for­
mation [65]. Additionally, copper ions modulate bacterial metabolism and signaling pathways, downregulating the transcription and 
translation of biofilm-associated genes and diminishing bacterial adaptability to hostile environments [66]. Owing to their superior 
penetrability, nanoscale copper materials can infiltrate mature biofilms and interact electrostatically or coordinatively with negatively 
charged EPS components, enabling an “adsorption–migration” delivery mode. The sustained release of Cu2+ within biofilms promotes 
ROS accumulation, leading to biofilm disintegration and bacterial death within the protective matrix [67].
3. Cuproptosis-like death: Cu-induced bacterial death
The introduction of the concept of cuproptosis in 2022 established a novel copper-induced cytotoxic mechanism in eukaryotic cells 
[5]. This copper-dependent, oxidative stress-independent, and mitochondria-induced cell death has shown promising results in cancer 
therapy, garnering significant attention from researchers [6,7]. The potential application of cuproptosis-centered nanotherapeutic 
strategies is continually being explored. In 2023, Mei et al. reported that treatment with copper-based nanomaterials in bacteria led to 
a high enrichment of differentially expressed genes in the TCA cycle, decreased activity of respiratory chain components, and the 
emergence of lipid peroxidation products [8]. This discovery differs from the previously known antimicrobial mechanism where 
copper generates ROS through the Fenton reaction, and it provides crucial support for a cuproptosis-like stress response in bacteria. 
Due to structural and metabolic differences between bacteria and eukaryotic cells, copper-dependent cell death in bacteria is not 
entirely analogous to cuproptosis, leading to the introduction of the “cuproptosis-like death” concept. Understanding the molecular 
mechanisms of copper-induced bacterial death can offer new perspectives for developing antimicrobial agents, creating exciting 
opportunities for biomedical applications and clinical translation.
3.1. Copper metabolism in bacteria
Copper is an essential trace element for bacterial physiology and metabolism, playing a vital role in various cellular processes. The 
redox conversion properties of Cu2+ and Cu+ enable catalytic functions for copper-dependent enzymes. While copper is an effective 
enzymatic cofactor, its redox activity can pose risks to the bacterial microenvironment. Therefore, precise regulation of copper 
metabolism is crucial for maintaining copper homeostasis in bacteria. These complex copper acquisition and transport systems involve 
diverse molecular mechanisms, including ATP-driven translocators, chaperone-mediated trafficking pathways, and transcriptional 
regulators [64,68–70]. These systems display lineage-specific adaptations to selectively manage Cu+/Cu2+ species, ensuring cellular 
copper homeostasis.
In regulating copper homeostasis, the forms of copper that reach chemical equilibrium and can be easily complexed by ligands are 
Cu2+ and Cu+ [38]. Cu2+ primarily exists in oxidizing environments, such as the periplasm of Gram-negative bacteria, while Cu+ is 
mainly found in anaerobic and reducing environments, such as the cytoplasm [39]. Once the more biologically inert Cu2+ is absorbed, 
it is reduced to Cu+ by intracellular reductants for copper storage in the cytoplasm and subsequent rerouting to the periplasm [45]. To 
mitigate the toxicity associated with excessive intracellular Cu+, bacteria commonly employ copper buffering strategies, including 
transmembrane copper exporters, copper chaperones, and intracellular metallothioneins [71]. The copper-sensitive operon regulator 
(CsoR) serves as a Cu+-responsive regulator of copper homeostasis in S. aureus (Fig. 3(a)) [72,73]. Upon binding copper ions through

3.2. Characteristics of cuproptosis-like death
The relationship between metal overload and cell death has long been a focus of scientific research. Ferroptosis, a cell death 
pathway induced by iron, was first reported as in 2012 [77]. In contrast, although copper overload in cells has been observed to cause 
oxidative stress and cytotoxicity, the precise mechanism of copper-induced cell death was not elucidated in eukaryotic cells until 2022 
[5]. The core characteristic of cuproptosis is the toxic stress on cellular proteins, marked by the aggregation of acylated mitochondrial 
proteins and the loss of Fe-S cluster proteins in the TCA cycle, rather than excessive ROS accumulation and oxidative stress mediated by 
the Fenton reaction [78–80]. This unique pathway highlights the potential significance of intracellular copper ion accumulation, 
which interacts with TCA cycle components, in the dysfunction of mitochondrial energy metabolism and the imbalance of copper 
homeostasis.
As the characteristics of cuproptosis are increasingly understood, the core molecular regulatory pathways and key biomarker 
events have been gradually elucidated. The initiation of cuproptosis relies on an abnormal increase in intracellular Cu2+ concentration 
[5]. Cu2+ is catalytically reduced to the highly toxic Cu+ by ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) and subsequently released into the mitochondria. It is 
currently believed that Cu+ contributes to the protein toxicity stress and cell death characteristic of cuproptosis through two mech­
anisms. First, Cu+ binds to the lipoylated dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) and dihydrolipoamide succinyltransferase 
(DLST), causing their oligomerization [5]. As key subunits of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex and the α-ketoglutarate dehy­
drogenase complex, the oligomerization of DLAT and DLST disrupts the activity of respiratory enzyme complexes, inhibiting the 
conversion of pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate to acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA, effectively blocking the TCA cycle [5]. Secondly, Cu+
directly targets and disrupts the Fe-S cluster and its biosynthesis system [5]. Cu+ coordinates with solvent-exposed sulfur atoms, 
leading to iron atom displacement and the consequent disruption of Fe-S cluster protein function [81]. Furthermore, Cu+ inhibits the 
ISC and SUF systems through multiple mechanisms, severely impeding the synthesis and repair of Fe-S clusters [82–84]. Fe-S cluster 
proteins are conserved across species and serve as electron transfer mediators in respiratory complexes I, II, and III, facilitating the 
transfer of electrons from NADH and FADH2 to ubiquinone and ultimately to cytochrome c and molecular oxygen [85,86]. The 
disruption of Fe-S cluster proteins hinders electron transfer and proton pumping, undermining the proton motive force necessary for 
ATP synthesis and resulting in mitochondrial respiratory chain failure [5]. The malfunction of Fe-S clusters during electron transfer 
leads to an inability to maintain redox balance, causing electron leakage and excessive ROS generation [87]. Within this cuproptosis 
pathway, FDX1 has been identified as a key regulatory factor and potential biomarker [5,88]. FDX1 is a small iron-sulfur protein that is 
a vital component of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, playing a dual role in cuproptosis. It acts as an upstream regulatory 
factor of lipoylation, promoting the lipoylation modification of mitochondrial proteins by activating lipoate transferases (LIPT1, LIS) 
[89]. During cuproptosis, FDX1 reduces Cu2+ to Cu+, initiating lipoylated protein aggregation and the loss of Fe-S cluster proteins.
The primary characteristics of cuproptosis-like death in bacteria are similar to those of eukaryotic cuproptosis, defined by the 
dysfunction of lipoylated key proteins in energy metabolism and the disruption of the TCA cycle (Fig. 3(d)). As previously noted, 
aberrant aggregation of Cu+-mediated lipoylated proteins and degradation of Fe-S clusters are critical markers of cuproptosis. The 
lipoylation process and Fe-S cluster synthesis pathways are highly conserved in both eukaryotes and prokaryotes, suggesting a sig­
nificant potential for cuproptosis-like stress responses in bacteria, despite their lack of mitochondria. This provides a theoretical 
framework for studying cuproptosis-like death pathways [8]. In bacteria, lipoylated DLAT and DLST are essential components of the 
dehydrogenase complexes required for the normal functioning of the TCA cycle and serve as key targets of Cu+ in cuproptosis-like 
death. Bacterial cytosolic ferredoxins, involved in the electron transfer chain of cytochrome P450 enzymes, are closely related to 
the degree of copper metabolism disorder in bacteria and are thought to play an important role similar to FDX1 in cuproptosis-like 
death [90]. The specific molecular mechanisms underlying cuproptosis-like death may be summarized as follows: Cu2+ is reduced 
to highly toxic Cu+ via ferredoxins, which subsequently targets the lipoyl moieties of lipoylated proteins, including the E2 subunits of 
pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (DLAT homologs) and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex (DLST homologs). This interaction 
induces oligomerization and inactivation of the PDH complex, thereby blocking the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA and dis­
rupting the TCA cycle. Additionally, Cu+ directly oxidizes the cysteine thiol groups of LIAS, leading to its functional impairment and 
consequent collapse of the iron-sulfur cluster assembly systems (ISC/SUF). This cascade results in the inactivation of Fe-S-dependent 
enzymes, including ETC complexes I, II, and III, ultimately causing ETC dysfunction. However, as single-celled organisms, bacteria 
emphasize rapid responses to environmental changes, lacking the complex oxidative stress regulatory networks of eukaryotes, which 
results in specific differences in bacterial cuproptosis-like death. Less than 50% of bacteria contain glutathione (GSH), making it 
generally impossible for bacteria to form a GPX4-GSH antioxidant axis akin to eukaryotes, and instead rely on simpler yet swift- 
responsive antioxidant systems such as OxyR and PerR to eliminate lipid peroxides and ROS [91,92]. In environments with high 
copper stress, bacterial antioxidant enzymes like superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) can become excessively activated 
and rapidly deplete antioxidants such as bacillithiol and mycothiol. The ROS generated by Cu2+ Fenton-like reactions cannot be 
effectively cleared, subsequently attacking polyunsaturated fatty acids in bacterial cell membranes, leading to lipid molecular 
oxidative damage and membrane integrity disruption, which creates a positive feedback loop that amplifies cuproptosis-like death 
[93]. Thus, lipid peroxidation is a distinct feature of bacterial cuproptosis-like death. The similarities and differences between 
cuproptosis-like death and cuproptosis arise from the fundamental cellular structure, conserved metabolic pathways, and varying 
environmental adaptation capabilities of eukaryotic and prokaryotic organisms. These factors reflect unique adaptive strategies 
involved in energy metabolism and provide important insights into understanding the specific mechanisms of copper-mediated cell 
death.

3.3. Comparison with other programmed cell death pathways
Programmed cell death (PCD) in bacteria is an adaptive strategy that enables bacterial populations to survive under stressful 
environmental conditions. In this process, a subset of cells actively initiates a death program, releasing intracellular nutrients and 
freeing ecological space, thereby enhancing the overall survival of the population and the persistence of biofilms. In recent years, 
studies on antimicrobial materials have revealed that artificially inducing bacterial PCD can serve as an effective antibacterial strategy, 
enabling targeted bacterial elimination by activating intrinsic death pathways. In addition to cuproptosis-like death, other actively 
studied forms of bacterial PCD include ferroptosis-like death [94–96] and apoptosis-like death [97,98].
Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of programmed cell death characterized by iron overload–induced bursts of ROS, which lead 
to irreversible lipid peroxidation of cellular membranes [99]. Under conditions of iron excess, bacteria exhibit similar phenotypes, 
including collapse of antioxidant defense systems and abnormal accumulation of lipid peroxides. However, because bacteria lack key 
regulatory molecules involved in eukaryotic ferroptosis (such as ACSL4 and GPX4) and their membranes contain relatively low levels 
of long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), this process is defined as ferroptosis-like death[100]. This mechanism has been 
widely exploited in antimicrobial applications involving various nanomaterials [94–96]. Compared with cuproptosis-like death, both 
processes are metal ion–dependent oxidative death pathways, yet they target distinct metabolic processes. Ferroptosis-like death 
primarily disrupts lipid metabolism, executing cell death through membrane lipid peroxidation, whereas cuproptosis-like death targets 
the TCA cycle, in which copper ions specifically bind to lipoylated proteins of the respiratory chain, triggering toxic protein aggre­
gation. The complementarity of these targets suggests potential synergistic effects. Indeed, studies have shown that copper ions can 
bind to GPX4 and induce its oligomerization, followed by degradation via the TAX1BP1-mediated autophagy pathway, thereby 
promoting ferroptosis-like death [101]. Consequently, dual-metal ion strategies impose combined metabolic stress through simulta­
neous TCA cycle disruption and amplified lipid peroxidation, demonstrating antibacterial efficacy that surpasses single-mechanism 
approaches.
Apoptosis is a genetically regulated form of programmed cell death characterized by cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation, DNA 
fragmentation, caspase cascade activation, and the formation of apoptotic bodies. In 2014, Erental et al. identified a programmed cell 
death pathway in E. coli mediated by the RecA–LexA signaling axis. This process is accompanied by membrane depolarization and DNA 
fragmentation—two hallmark events of apoptosis [102]. Consequently, it was termed apoptosis-like death, and it has since demon­
strated unique antibacterial effects in studies of antimicrobial materials [97,98]. The molecular mechanism of apoptosis-like death is 
primarily initiated by severe DNA damage, which activates the RecA–LexA pathway. Subsequent induction of the edin gene disrupts 
respiratory chain function and triggers the Fenton reaction, leading to excessive production of ROS. Ultimately, cell death is executed 
through YbeY-mediated rRNA degradation and membrane depolarization [103]. Compared with cuproptosis-like death, the central 
trigger of apoptosis-like death is DNA damage rather than metal ion overload, although both pathways involve dysfunction of the 
respiratory chain. Notably, the distinct execution mechanisms of these two forms of programmed cell death suggest the potential for 
synergistic interactions through bidirectional amplification of oxidative stress signaling. This concept provides a theoretical basis for 
designing combinatorial antibacterial strategies targeting drug-resistant bacteria.
3.4. Characterization of cuproptosis-like death
Accurate and sensitive methods for detecting cuproptosis-like death are essential for verifying whether copper-based nanomaterials 
exhibit targeted behavior in biological systems. These methods form the scientific basis for accurately correlating material-biological 
effects, which is crucial for guiding material design and performance optimization. Following the introduction of the “cuproptosis-like 
death” concept, strategies for detecting nanomaterial-induced bacterial cuproptosis-like death have been reported [8]. Researchers 
observed a high enrichment index of downregulated genes in the TCA cycle pathway, decreased activity of respiratory chain complexes 
I and II, and the accumulation of lipid peroxides and their metabolite malondialdehyde in bacterial biofilms treated with these ma­
terials. These phenomena reflect the core characteristics of cuproptosis-like death: TCA cycle inhibition and lipid peroxide accumu­
lation. As our understanding of the molecular mechanisms of cuproptosis-like death deepens and nanotechnology rapidly advances, 
the interactions between copper-based nanomaterials and bacteria across genetic, protein, morphological, and metabolic levels are 
increasingly being used to detect and evaluate cuproptosis-like death. A highly specific, multi-level characterization system for 
cuproptosis-like death is gradually being established.
Transcriptomics and metabolomics, as core technologies in multi-omics research, have been systematically applied to elucidate the 
antibacterial mechanisms of nanomaterials, particularly in detecting Cu+-mediated bacterial programmed death. Transcriptomic data 
reveal global gene expression changes induced by antibacterial materials, providing evidence at the genetic level for copper-based 
nanomaterials mediating bacterial cuproptosis-like death [104]. Differential transcriptomic analysis shows that genes induced by 
copper-based nanomaterials in response to copper toxicity are primarily concentrated in copper transport, quorum sensing (QS), and 
energy metabolism [13,105]. For instance, in MRSA treated with mCuS@lm nanocages, genes related to copper transport, such as 
copA, copB, and copZ, are upregulated, while genes in the QS system, like the psmA and hlg families, and TCA cycle genes, such as acnA 
and ctaA, are downregulated [13]. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis and Gene 
Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis identify that differentially expressed genes induced by copper-based nanomaterials are signifi­
cantly enriched in specific metabolic pathways, revealing the core biological mechanisms of bacterial copper toxicity responses. Key 
enriched KEGG metabolic pathways include the TCA cycle (map00020), oxidative phosphorylation (hsa00190), Staphylococcus aureus 
infection (ko05150), phosphotransferase system (ko02060), and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (map00010) [12,106–108]. The 
disruption of these energy metabolic pathways aligns with the oxidative stress-induced death phenotype in bacteria, suggesting the

occurrence of cuproptosis-like death. GO enrichment terms include stress response to metal ions (GO:0097501), stress response to 
copper ions (GO:1990169), and response to copper ions (GO:0046688), highlighting biological processes related to copper ion stress 
[37,109]. Metabolomics, by identifying differential metabolites and pinpointing the targets of antibacterial materials, directly dem­

significantly enriched in the TCA cycle metabolic pathway, indicating the potential occurrence of cuproptosis-like death [8].
Quantitative changes in the activity of bacterial respiratory chain complexes and key TCA cycle markers are crucial for assessing 
the extent of metabolic impact and determining the cuproptosis-like phenomenon. The inhibition of the TCA cycle is a core charac­
teristic of this copper-dependent bacterial programmed death. As key components of bacterial energy metabolism, the activity of 
respiratory chain complexes directly reflects the functional state of the bacterial respiratory chain and provides strong evidence for 
omics enrichment analysis pointing to the TCA cycle. Therefore, the activity of bacterial respiratory chain complexes I, II, III, and IV is 
extensively studied to support cuproptosis-like evidence [8,13,37,108,111]. Additionally, assessing TCA cycle protein levels and gene 
expression using Western blot and RT-qPCR, alongside transcriptomic sequencing data, offers a multidimensional validation, 
providing a systematic evidence chain for cuproptosis-like research. In S. aureus treated with copper-based nanomaterials, the 
expression of DLAT, FDX1, and LIAS proteins reportedly decreases in a concentration-dependent manner, marking them as molecular 
indicators of cuproptosis-like signal transduction [111,112]. Moreover, genes related to the TCA cycle (sucC, sucD, sdhA, dhaK, nagA, 
dltA, gfpK, gpsB, mtnN, sasF, pdhA), copper transport (CopZ and CopA), oxidative stress (sdhB, queE, queC, lytM), oxidative phos­
phorylation (pyk, pckA, acnA, and CS), and glycolysis (gapA1, eno, and pfkA) are important molecular targets [101,112]. Additionally, 
the accumulation of upstream TCA cycle metabolite pyruvate serves as an indicator of TCA cycle inhibition [109]. The glyoxylate 
cycle, a metabolic bypass of the TCA cycle, is also affected in cuproptosis-like induced metabolic collapse. Therefore, glyoxylate and 
isocitrate lyase (ICL) activity, as important intermediate products and key enzymes of the glyoxylate cycle, are used to evaluate 
cuproptosis-like phenomena [113].
Assessment of bacterial oxidative stress, particularly lipid peroxidation levels and intracellular copper ion indicators, is crucial for 
detecting cuproptosis-like phenomena. The GSH/GSSG ratio serves as a functional response indicator to oxidative stress, reflecting the 
actual load on the antioxidant system and bacterial oxidative damage. Ratiometric fluorescent sensors such as BODIPY 581/591 C11, 
Dihydroethidium, and DCFH-DA, which depend on super-resolution microscopy, provide visualization and quantification of oxidative 
stress at the subcellular level. These methods are essential for studying cuproptosis-like death [8,13,36,111]. The BODIPY 581/591 
C11 probe specifically detects lipid peroxidation, while Dihydroethidium and DCFH-DA are commonly used for ROS detection. 
Compared to ROS, lipid peroxides (LPO) are more reliable and specific indicators of cuproptosis-like processes. The accumulation of 
malonic dialdehyde (MDA), an LPO decomposition product, is considered a key signal of cuproptosis-like phenomena, typically 
detected using an MDA assay kit [8,13,105,109]. Bacterial membrane rupture and cytoplasmic leakage are morphological manifes­
tations of lipid peroxidation damage and cuproptosis-like stress. Protein and ATP leakage, genomic DNA degradation on agarose 
electrophoretic maps, the γ-H2AX method, and the ONPG method are crucial for quantifying bacterial membrane integrity and 
cytoplasmic permeability [35,37,105]. A significant increase in intracellular copper ion concentration is a fundamental aspect of the 
cuproptosis-like phenomenon. This is typically analyzed in the supernatant of lysed bacteria using inductively coupled plasma optical 
emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) [111,112]. Copper chelators such as bathocuproine disulfate disodium (BCS), ammonium tetra­
thiomolybdate (ATTM), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) inhibit the cuproptosis-like death process by reducing intra­
cellular free copper ion concentration [114]. Researchers use these inhibitors to reverse the antibacterial effects of copper-based 
nanomaterials, providing experimental evidence for the existence of cuproptosis-like death [13,37,111].
It is important to emphasize that clarifying the mechanism of “cuproptosis-like death” induced by copper-based nanomaterials 
requires a rigorous distinction between specific biomarkers and general indicators of cytotoxicity. According to current evidence, 
suppression of the TCA cycle and accumulation of LPO are considered core hallmarks of cuproptosis-like death. In contrast, widespread 
ROS generation or membrane damage may result from multiple forms of toxicity and therefore lack mechanistic specificity. 
Furthermore, interspecies variability among bacteria may limit the universality of certain biomarkers; for example, differences in TCA 
enzyme expression across bacterial strains could influence the interpretation of results[115]. Because the signaling pathways asso­
ciated with cuproptosis-like death partially overlap with those triggered by other metal ions, reliance on a single indicator is insuf­
ficient for definitive identification. Consequently, establishing a combinatorial validation framework that integrates multiple specific 
markers will be essential for enhancing mechanistic rigor in future investigations.
4. Strategy for developing copper-based antimicrobial nanomaterials
Over the past decade, significant progress has been made in the synthesis of copper-based intelligent nanomaterials with 
controllable morphology and good biocompatibility. At the nanoscale, copper not only kills bacteria directly through ion release but 
also mimics the activity of natural enzymes such as peroxidase (POD) and oxidase (OXD), thereby enhancing antibacterial effects by 
generating ROS [116]. In addition, copper-based nanomaterials can alleviate local hypoxia, suppress inflammation, and promote the 
proliferation and migration of fibroblasts and endothelial cells. These effects accelerate collagen deposition and angiogenesis, pro­
ducing synergistic antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and wound-healing outcomes [117]. Currently, the design of copper-based 
antimicrobial materials has evolved from single-function antibacterial agents to multifunctional intelligent platforms that integrate 
anti-inflammatory, regenerative, and stimulus-responsive properties. Such systems enable precise spatiotemporal control through

4.1. Copper oxides
Copper oxides, primarily represented by CuO and Cu2O, are significant copper-based inorganic nanomaterials known for their 
excellent antibacterial properties and enzyme-like catalytic activity. These characteristics have garnered considerable attention in the 
field of medical materials. As binary metal oxides, the size, morphology, crystal plane exposure, defect distribution, doping, and 
heterostructure of nanoscale CuO and Cu2O can be precisely controlled [211]. Notably, CuO (bandgap approximately 1.2–1.7 eV) and 
Cu2O (bandgap approximately 2.0–2.2 eV) are narrow-bandgap p-type semiconductors. By forming functional heterojunctions with n- 
type semiconductors, their enzyme-like catalytic efficiency can be significantly enhanced [212,213]. Recently, multifunctional het­
erojunction materials constructed via in situ synthesis strategies have advanced antibacterial therapies. The focus is on optimizing 
carrier dynamics and active site design, effectively addressing key challenges such as low photosensitizer efficiency and insufficient 
nanozyme activity in traditional antibacterial treatments. This approach opens new pathways for effective antibacterial therapy in 
complex infection environments.
Wang et al. developed a copper oxide-based heterojunction, Bi2WO6@Cu2O-GOx (BWCG bio-HJ), using in situ synthesis and multi- 
step cascade assembly strategies for comprehensive treatment of chronic diabetic wound infections (Fig. 4) [119]. Cu2O nanoparticles 
grown in situ on Bi2WO6 nanosheets form a Z-scheme heterojunction that significantly enhances carrier separation efficiency, pro­
moting the efficient formation of high-level ROS (1O2 and ⋅OH) under ultrasound stimulation. Glucose oxidase (GOx), grafted on the 
outer layer, consumes excess glucose in high-sugar environments, providing abundant H2O2 for Cu+-mediated Fenton-like reactions, 
which disrupt bacterial structure and metabolism through strong oxidation, exhibiting antibacterial effects. The BWCG bio-HJ's 
combination of sonodynamic, chemodynamic, and enzyme-catalytic cascade reactions addresses pathogen elimination, blood glucose 
regulation, and angiogenesis stimulation, offering an innovative treatment strategy for stubborn diabetic wound infections.
Zhang et al. reported a piezoelectric-enhanced nanozyme catalytic activity in a p-n heterojunction, CuxO@BMO, providing new 
insights for treating biofilm-related infections (Fig. 4) [121]. This p-n heterojunction suppresses the piezoelectric screening effect, 
boosting the piezoelectric potential of the n-type piezoelectric semiconductor and enhancing electron transfer efficiency. Under ul­
trasound stimulation, CuxO nanoparticles facilitate hole reactions with GSH, while Bi2MoO6 nanosheets (BMO) allow more electron 
carriers to react with H2O2, demonstrating POD-like activity of BMO and GSH oxidase (GSHOx)-like activity of CuxO with high 
selectivity. In hypoxic infectious microenvironments, H2O2 and GSH are catalyzed to promote ROS generation, inhibiting bacterial 
ATP synthesis for effective antibacterial and antibiofilm action. This heterojunction addresses issues of low acoustic efficiency in 
traditional sonodynamic therapy and inherent low catalytic activity of nanozymes, providing a reference for treating infectious 
diseases.
Many copper oxide-based nanomaterials have been reported to achieve photodynamic-chemodynamic cascade reactions through 
precise control of interfacial charge separation, which is significant for efficient and safe antimicrobial therapy. To achieve high near- 
infrared (NIR) light absorption and photothermal conversion capabilities, mesoporous polydopamine (MPDA) was used as a frame­
work and photothermal agent. CuO2 nanodots were confined within the MPDA pores using a coordination-driven in situ growth 
strategy, creating CuO2@MPDA. The shielding effect of the MPDA pores effectively addressed the issues of CuO2 aggregation and 
instability, enhancing the material's dispersion and stability. The incorporation of CuO2 broadened the light absorption range of MPDA 
and improved its photothermal conversion efficiency. The localized temperature increase accelerated the decomposition of CuO2 and 
the Fenton-like reaction rate, generating more OH⋅, thereby enhancing PTT effects on CDT [120]. Researchers have constructed a CuO/ 
ZnCdS heterojunction by in situ growth of ZnCdS nanoparticles on a CuO substrate. This heterojunction can induce ROS to degrade 
environmental pollutants like tetracycline under photocatalysis and exhibits excellent antifungal properties [123]. Additionally, a 
ZnS–CuO/PVA/Chitosan heterojunction was developed using defect-laden ZnS and CuO via Type II band alignment for charge sep­
aration, greatly enhancing the photocatalytic efficiency of ⋅O2−and OH⋅ generation, showing potential for tetracycline degradation 
and antibacterial applications in wastewater [118]. To achieve superior catalytic and photothermal conversion performance, re­
searchers precisely decorated the Cu2O surface with Au and Pt precursors, creating a multimetallic heterojunction Cu2O/AuPt3. This 
design synergistically enhances antibacterial effects through photothermal effects and multi-enzyme mimicry, providing insights for 
the development of long-lasting antibacterial agents [122].

The development of multifunctional materials based on copper sulfides and optimized synergistic strategies has significantly 
enhanced antibacterial effects through multimodal photothermal therapy, drawing attention for clinical applications. Chen and his 
team created an extracellular polymeric substances (EPS)-targeted photothermal nanoplatform, CuS@PPDA, for treating MRSA- 
induced biofilm infections (Fig. 5) [125]. Using a phage display library-based biopanning approach, they identified a seven-peptide 
ligand, KG7, that specifically targets the key functional protein PSMα1 in Staphylococcus aureus biofilm formation. KG7 was pre­
cisely conjugated to polydopamine (PDA)-functionalized CuS nanoparticles via Schiff-base reactions, providing dual molecular 
mechanisms: KG7 specifically binds to PSMα1's fibrous sequence, inhibiting amyloid fiber assembly and blocking initial biofilm for­
mation, while enabling targeted enrichment in the biofilm matrix. Under NIR laser irradiation, CuS@PPDA achieves efficient pho­
tothermal conversion (48.28% at 980 nm), physically disrupting the biofilm core structure. Released Cu2+ ions during photothermal 
treatment modulate macrophage polarization to M2, promoting tissue regeneration. This “molecular targeting-photothermal ablation” 
synergistic strategy offers a promising innovative treatment for refractory MRSA infections. Kong et al. employed microbial engi­
neering strategies to design an environmentally responsive photothermal platform, LC@CuPen, leveraging the pathological features of 
the biofilm microenvironment (Fig. 5) [126]. The Janus-structured PEG-modified CuS nanoparticles, CuPen, retain the crystal 
structure of CuS and adhere electrostatically to the probiotic carrier Lactobacillus casei (L. casei) surface. Under NIR irradiation, the 
CuS nanocore exhibits excellent photothermal conversion, producing mild thermal effects (42–45◦C) that selectively damage the 
probiotic carrier and disrupt the biofilm's three-dimensional structure. Damaged L. casei responds to the BME by releasing bacte­
riocins, further dismantling bacterial biofilms and inducing macrophage polarization to M1, enhancing the host immune response. In 
BME's acidic conditions and high H2O2 levels, CuPen efficiently catalyzes H2O2 decomposition, generating highly toxic •OH, dis­
rupting bacterial metabolism and reducing bacterial resistance to thermal stress. This probiotic-engineered nanoplatform skillfully 
utilizes photothermal regulation and infection microenvironment characteristics for precise treatment, while activating the host 
immune system, offering new directions for treating biofilm-related infections.
Recent studies have reported copper sulfide nanomaterials with significant applications in photothermal antibacterial treatments, 
demonstrating efficient photothermal conversion capabilities. A PVA-SF/CuS nanofiber membrane constructed using bio-template 
electrospinning technology showed enhanced photothermal conversion efficiency with increasing SF/CuS concentration gradient, 
reaching temperatures of 54.5◦C in vitro and 51.5◦C in vivo under 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W/cm2, 2 min) (Fig. 6) [127]. The CuS- 
PEI/OH-Ti composite coating, developed through multistage surface engineering, achieved high antibacterial efficiency with a 34% 
photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE) at low power (0.28 W/cm2, 8 min) (Fig. 6) [128]. Various CuS nanoparticle-based functional 
hydrogels exhibit exceptional photothermal performance: PulMA/CuS@PC achieved a PCE of 52.48% [129]; PEGSDU/CuS2 nano­
composite hydrogel increased in temperature to 64◦C within 10 minutes under NIR irradiation [32]; DFO/CuS-ECMgel demonstrated 
temperature ranges of 30–60◦C under different laser power densities and CuS NP concentrations [130]; PVA/CS/CNF-CuS/CaO2/LA 
hydrogel reached 59.3◦C under 1.4 W NIR irradiation [138]; PCCuT hydrogel increased to 54.3◦C within 2 minutes and to 56.9◦C 
within 3 minutes under 0.8 W/cm2 NIR irradiation [149]. These materials maintain stable temperature response characteristics 
through multiple photothermal cycles, confirming that CuS nanostructures not only enable precise control of photothermal perfor­
mance but also possess the reliability needed for clinical applications.
Copper sulfide nanocrystals exhibit not only excellent photothermal properties but also p-type semiconductor characteristics, 
adjustable bandgaps, and interface electron transfer capabilities, laying the foundation for their nanocatalytic functions [217,218]. 
Through structural engineering, CuS-based materials can enhance peroxidase-like activity to efficiently catalyze ROS generation, 
inducing bacterial oxidative stress damage, while their photothermal effects can disrupt bacterial biofilms and cell structures. Wang 
et al. successfully constructed Ti3C2 MXenes/CuS nano-heterojunctions (TC) using precise interface engineering strategies [131]. This 
structure shows optimized electronic band characteristics, with the optical bandgap significantly reduced to 0.9 eV, enhancing 
photocatalytic and peroxidase-like activities, effectively catalyzing high-activity ROS generation under NIR irradiation. In infection 
microenvironments, Cu+/Cu2+ ions released from the TC heterojunction trigger lipid peroxidation cascades, inducing bacterial fer­
roptosis. To achieve precise delivery and enhanced mechanical stability, TC was selectively integrated into the tip region of photo- 
crosslinked methacrylated gelatin (GelMA) microneedles, forming a dual-layer GTCM microneedle (GelMA-Ti3C2/CuS-Micro­
needles). This design retains the excellent biocompatibility and wound exudate absorption capacity of the GelMA base, while ensuring 
high-density distribution of the TC heterojunction at the microneedle tips through interface engineering. Combined with mild pho­
tothermal therapy (MPTT) strategies, this system activates antibacterial and healing functions under controlled temperature condi­
tions (approximately 45◦C). Sun's team employed a dual-metal nanozyme synergy strategy to construct flower-like ZnO-CuS 
heterojunctions for treating implant-related infections [132]. Quantum chemical calculations revealed the atomic-level mechanism of 
the dual-metal nanozyme, confirming the specific functional division between Cu and Zn sites in the ZnO-CuS heterojunction: ZnO 
provides catalase (CAT) active sites, while CuS provides peroxidase (POD) and glutathione oxidase (GSH-Px) active sites, achieving 
multiple enzyme activity synergy. For precise delivery and controlled release, ZnO-CuS nanoflowers were further loaded into Pluronic 
F127 temperature-sensitive block copolymer hydrogels, forming ZnO-CuS/F127 Hydrogels. At infection sites, acidic conditions pro­
mote the release of Cu2+/Zn2+ ions and POD/GSH-Px synergistic catalysis, generating cascading ROS effects; in healthy tissue, neutral 
conditions activate CAT activity, effectively decomposing excess H2O2 and significantly reducing cytotoxicity. This microenvironment- 
responsive system design utilizes the acidic characteristics of the biofilm microenvironment and the neutral characteristics of healthy 
tissue to achieve pH-smart regulation of nanozyme activity. This research provides precise material guidance for designing more 
efficient nanozymes and offers innovative solutions with a solid materials science foundation for addressing clinically challenging 
biofilm infections.

4.3. Copper nanoclusters
Metal nanoclusters are stable aggregates composed of a few to several hundred metal atoms, typically under 10 nm in size [219]. 
They exist in a transitional state between atomic and bulk matter. Their unique quantum confinement and surface effects endow them 
with discrete energy levels, robust luminescence emission, and efficient HOMO-LUMO transitions, distinguishing them from bulk 
materials [220,221]. Due to their ultra-small size and unique optical and enzyme-like catalytic properties, they exhibit finely tuned 
antibacterial efficacy. Metal nanoclusters used in antibacterial applications include Au nanoclusters (AuNCs), Ag nanoclusters 
(AgNCs), and Cu nanoclusters (CuNCs) [222–224]. The inherent broad-spectrum antibacterial activity of copper makes CuNCs 
particularly effective. Advances in synthesis and characterization techniques have greatly expanded the understanding of structure- 
performance relationships, significantly broadening the applications of CuNCs in antibacterial therapy.
Through molecular-scale design and precise electronic structure control, researchers have developed various CuNCs to significantly 
enhance the antibacterial efficacy of chemodynamic therapy (CDT). Luo et al. employed a p-d orbital hybridization strategy to 
construct silica-copper nanosheets (Si@Cu NSs) for treating orthopedic implant-related infections (Fig. 7) [146]. In this study, CuNCs 
with an average size of approximately 4.83 nm were uniformly loaded onto silicene nanosheet substrates. The hybridization effect 
between the silicon p orbitals and copper d orbitals optimized the electronic structure, significantly increasing the density of active 
sites at the copper atomic catalytic centers and greatly enhancing ROS generation efficiency. As a two-dimensional carrier, silicene 
nanosheets effectively prevent CuNCs aggregation, improve copper catalyst utilization, and provide near-infrared photothermal 
properties, generating local thermal effects under irradiation to directly kill bacteria and synergistically enhance CDT. This innovative 
strategy of orbital hybridization for regulating electron transfer offers new insights into overcoming the inefficiency of traditional 
chemodynamic therapies in physiological environments, while demonstrating the potential of CuNCs in treating orthopedic implant- 
related infections. Meanwhile, Jin et al. reported pH-responsive, multi-enzyme Cu-Fe3O4 nanoclusters for treating drug-resistant in­
fections and promoting wound healing [147]. By constructing copper-iron bimetallic catalytic sites, the study significantly enhanced 
interface electron transfer efficiency, breaking the linear proportional limitations of single metal centers, expanding the working pH 
range, and greatly improving CDT catalytic efficiency. Notably, Cu-Fe3O4 nanoclusters exhibit intelligent environmental respon­
siveness: they demonstrate peroxidase-like (POD-like) activity under acidic conditions in infection microenvironments, effectively 
killing MRSA and E. coli. In neutral or mildly alkaline environments of healthy tissue, they switch to superoxide dismutase-like (SOD- 
like) and catalase-like (CAT-like) antioxidant activities, protecting healthy cells from oxidative damage. Cu-Fe3O4 nanoclusters ach­
ieve both targeted antibacterial action and wound healing promotion, providing a promising solution for developing next-generation 
anti-infection treatment strategies.
In addition to catalytic site regulation, surface modification and size control techniques have been employed to optimize the 
antibacterial efficacy of CuNCs. Modifying the ligands on CuNC surfaces can alter their chemical properties, enhancing their role in 
antibacterial treatments. Mittal et al. used a one-pot green synthesis method to produce bromelain-capped copper nanoclusters (Bro- 
CuNCs) by reducing CuSO4 [148]. Bromelain forms high-affinity metal-ligand complexes with copper through the free cysteine residue 
Cys-25 in its structure, significantly enhancing nanocluster stability. Chen's team employed a ligand-directed self-assembly strategy to 
precisely synthesize Ag6Cu2 nanoclusters by controlling the silver-to-copper ratio (6:2), significantly improving antibacterial and anti- 
inflammatory properties [150]. The ligand H3decz (3,6-Di-tert-butyl-1,8-diethynyl-9H-carbazole) provides steric hindrance, pre­
venting excessive aggregation and maintaining an ultra-small size of approximately 2 nm. This surface modification also enhances the 
nanoclusters' ability to penetrate bacterial membranes. Researchers have achieved monodisperse Cu30 nanoclusters with a diameter of 
about 1.12 nm using a NaOH-mediated NaBH4 reduction method by optimizing pH and solvent polarity [155]. These small Cu30 
nanoclusters efficiently penetrate bacteria, exhibiting broad-spectrum activity against antibiotic-resistant bacteria and fungi. Wang 
et al. prepared iron-based nanocopper cluster bimetallic material (Fe-nCu) by replacing copper nanoparticles on Fe surfaces, forming 
irregular aggregate structures [152]. The physical support of the iron substrate and the aggregation characteristics of copper ensure 
structural stability, maintaining high efficiency in complex aqueous environments. The spike-like aggregation of copper nanoclusters 
on the surface enhances bacterial penetration and internalization, disrupting bacterial DNA and removing antibiotic resistance genes.
CuNCs are ideal for high-efficiency photocatalytic antibacterial materials due to their excellent optical absorption and photo- 
induced charge separation capabilities. Their photoluminescence (PL) mechanism primarily arises from ligand-to-metal charge 
transfer (LMCT), S-Cu-S structure relaxation, and electron transitions in the metal core. Enhancing the electron-donating ability of 
ligands, strengthening ligand-metal core interactions, and suppressing non-radiative transitions can effectively improve the PL in­
tensity of Cu NCs. Researchers have successfully constructed ultra-small copper nanoclusters with aggregation-induced emission (AIE) 
characteristics (Cu-cys-CS NCs) using a dual regulation strategy involving pH and temperature to precisely control the aggregation 
state of cysteine (cys) and chitosan (CS) ligand layers [151]. Strong electrostatic interactions between cys and CS form a stable

excitation. Although fluorescence intensity decreases after MB loading, imaging capability is preserved, providing innovative solutions 
for bacterial infection and cancer treatment.
4.4. Metal-organic frameworks
Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are a class of organic-inorganic hybrid nanoscale crystalline materials formed through coor­
dination between metal or metal clusters and organic ligands [225,226]. The defined orientation between the building units and 
organic ligands in MOFs creates numerous nanoscale channels and a highly ordered network structure. MOFs exhibit unique physi­
cochemical properties from both organic and inorganic components, along with synergistic characteristics beyond simple component 
mixtures. Their designable topology, high surface area channels, and diverse functional binding sites offer significant potential in 
biomedical fields such as drug delivery and imaging [227]. Copper-based metal-organic frameworks (Cu-MOFs), utilizing Cu+/Cu2+ as 
coordination centers with organic ligands like trimesic acid and porphyrin, have shown remarkable applications in the antimicrobial 
domain due to their unique electronic structure, excellent catalytic activity, and inherent bioactivity of copper ions. The high-density 
dissociable Cu+/Cu2+ sites form active copper centers, which are fundamental to their antimicrobial efficacy. Innovations in synthesis 
conditions and ligand selection have led to a variety of Cu-MOFs, each exhibiting distinct and beneficial properties.
HKUST-1, also known as Cu-BTC or MOF-199, is a prominent copper-based metal-organic framework composed of copper nodes 
and 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid (H3BTC) ligands. Its exceptional three-dimensional porous structure, open metal sites, Lewis 
acidity, and benzene rings establish its functional basis in the antimicrobial field. Lai's team developed a multilayer platform, 
AP@CS@Lip@HKUST-1, encapsulating HKUST-1 nanoparticles within lipid layers, further wrapped in CS, and ultimately loaded into 
an ascorbyl palmitate (AP) hydrogel [158]. The anionic hydrogel intelligently targets inflamed areas and precisely releases the payload 
at infection sites. This antibiotic-free multifunctional platform effectively eradicates Helicobacter pylori while minimizing adverse 
effects on gut microbiota and host health, offering a novel approach to addressing antibiotic resistance. Taheri-Ledari et al. utilized 
Cu2+-coordinated SiO2 to connect magnetic Fe3O4 cores, forming HKUST-1 networks in situ. They loaded vancomycin into the pores 
and re-encapsulated with guanidinated β-cyclodextrin, creating the [VAN@Fe3O4/HKUST-1]BCD-Gn antimicrobial delivery system 
[163]. This nanocarrier significantly enhances bacterial adhesion and improves targeted antibiotic delivery efficiency through surface 
functionalization. Zhang's team integrated CS-modified HKUST-1 onto screen-printed carbon electrodes (SPE), developing a wireless 
wearable electrochemical device [160]. The electrochemical signal of the ideal marker PYO for Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections is 
amplified by HKUST-1, enabling specific detection at a limit of 93.5 pM. Lv et al. employed PEG hydrophilic surface modification and 
epitaxial crystallization induction strategies to add MOF nucleation sites on PCL electrospun nanofibers, successfully creating HKUST- 
1@PEG-b-PCL composites. These fibers, originally promoting healing, now also exhibit excellent antimicrobial properties [159]. 
Fang's team crosslinked Cu2+ on few-layer Ti3C2Tx surfaces to grow HKUST-1 in situ, developing HKUST-1@Ti3C2Tx composites [161]. 
This material effectively adheres to bacteria due to its high surface area and demonstrates outstanding photothermal antimicrobial 
capabilities under NIR irradiation. Liu et al. achieved dopamine (DA) self-assembly polymerization on HKUST-1 cores, constructing the 
multifunctional platform Cu-BTC@PDA, which exhibits sustained antimicrobial properties through cascade catalysis in tumor mi­
croenvironments [162]. Sacourbaravi's team modified HKUST-1 with gallic acid (GA) and incorporated it into chitosan-optimized 
hydrogels, developing Cu-BTC-GA@PVA-CS nanocomposite hydrogels, providing a new avenue for safe and effective antimicrobial 
dressings [164].

state under 660 nm red light, which reduces Cu2+ via single electron transfer. The MOF structure serves as a carrier for the photo­
sensitizer and a copper ion source, while its BTC−ligand acts as an electron donor to enhance catalytic efficiency. This integration of 
functions allows MB@PMOF to precisely control space and time, initiating copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) 
reactions under light exposure, synthesizing bisamidine-type antimicrobial drugs in situ at infection sites, offering a novel strategy for 
resistant bacterial infections. Su's team proposed a phased NO release strategy based on photothermal response [157]. They mixed NO-

HKUST-1 is surface-modified to form secondary amines, constructing NONOate structures for NO loading. Under NIR irradiation, heat 
generated by HKUST-1 triggers NONOate decomposition, releasing high-concentration NO for antimicrobial action, followed by slow 
release of low-concentration NO to promote tissue repair and anti-inflammatory regulation. The porous structure of PLLA fibers

Cu-TCPP is composed of Tetrakis(4-carboxyphenyl)porphyrin coordinated with Cu2+, featuring a two-dimensional nanosheet 
structure that forms porphyrin-based MOFs with large surface areas and excellent light-harvesting networks. The conjugated system of

porphyrin ring vacancies with Ga3+/Fe3+, they selectively reduced Fe3+ in the environment, prompting bacteria to absorb large 
amounts of Ga3+. This iron competition mechanism disrupts bacterial iron-sulfur cluster metabolism, TCA cycle, and ATP metabolism. 
The photothermal effect of Cu-TCPP enhances iron deprivation and synergizes with PDT for efficient biofilm clearance and antimi­
crobial performance. Ren's team used electrospinning technology to deposit PAN (polyacrylonitrile) nanofiber networks carrying Cu-

hydrophilic difference between the inner PP layer and outer PAN layer enables unidirectional fluid transport, reducing wound exudate 
accumulation and promoting healing. Cu-TCPP exhibits excellent synergistic PTT and PDT antimicrobial performance under dual-

enhanced antimicrobial effects. Additionally, various Cu-TCPP-based nanocomposites have been widely reported, including Cu-TCPP

4.5. Metal-polyphenol networks
Metal-Phenolic Networks (MPNs) are supramolecular amorphous organic-inorganic hybrid systems formed through dynamic co­
ordination self-assembly between multivalent metal ions and polyphenolic ligands. The reversible coordination mechanism between 
phenolic hydroxyl groups and metal ions endows these networks with structural tunability and multifunctionality. MPNs can be 
synthesized at room temperature in aqueous environments, avoiding the denaturation of bioactive components caused by high 
temperatures or organic solvents, making them particularly suitable for biomedical applications [228,229]. The strong coordination 
between polyphenolic molecules and copper ions confines Cu2+ within the phenolic networks, effectively preserving its antibacterial 
properties while significantly reducing its cytotoxicity in free form. This coordination strategy forms a three-dimensional crosslinked 
network through dynamic covalent and non-covalent interactions, enabling controlled release of Cu2+ [230,231]. Introducing specific 
functional components imparts new physicochemical properties to Cu-based MPN composites, allowing precise regulation of material 
performance. Due to their synergistic effects and multifunctionality, Cu-based MPN composites exhibit significant advantages in the 
field of antibacterial infection treatment. This section discusses the construction of nano-antibacterial materials based on Cu2+ and 
polyphenolic coordination crosslinking, as well as Cu-based MPN composites incorporating various functional components, including 
polymers, biomacromolecules, nanoparticles, and biological entities.
Cu2+ and polyphenol coordination crosslinking in MPN structural materials tightly integrate antimicrobial metal Cu2+ with 
bioactive polyphenols at the nanoscale, providing antibacterial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory functions from a single nano­
structure. The spontaneous assembly of Cu2+ with chlorogenic acid (CA), combined with the surfactant Pluronic F-68 (F68) to address 
nanoparticle aggregation, results in the synthesis of Cu-CA nanozymes (Cu-CA NZs) (Fig. 10) [193]. The MPN structure offers broad- 
spectrum antibacterial properties, with the coordination environment of Cu2+ and polyphenolic ligands mimicking the active centers 
of natural metalloenzymes like SOD and CAT, granting adjustable enzyme-mimetic activity that efficiently catalyzes the removal of 
ROS, achieving anti-inflammatory and immune modulation effects. Its simple, green synthesis, excellent biocompatibility, and multi- 
target intervention capability present a promising nanotherapeutic strategy for complex inflammatory infections such as sepsis. 
Molecular engineering has been employed to reconstruct the crosslinking topology of MPNs, enhancing their stability for sustained 
release. Li et al. utilized covalently polymerized poly(tannic acid) (PTA) as a rigid framework, constructing a “covalent-coordination 
dual network” through Cu2+ coordination with catechol groups, followed by hydrothermal treatment to enhance crosslinking density. 
This resulted in Cu-PTA nanoparticles that remain stable in physiological conditions and release active components responsively in 
infectious microenvironments, overcoming the traditional MPN burst release challenge and achieving prolonged antibacterial and 
anti-inflammatory synergy [194]. Additionally, oxidative reconstruction of polyphenol ligands can expand MPN functionality. Reports 
indicate that partial oxidation of tannic acid (TA) to generate a quinone structure (TAQ) retains coordination ability while introducing 
a quinone-conjugated structure. This enables self-assembly with Cu2+, forming two-dimensional nanosheets with enzyme-like catalytic 
activity and photothermal conversion capability [195]. Various MPN composites, such as Cu-EGCG formed by Cu2+ and epi­
gallocatechin gallate (EGCG) [196], DES + Gel/CuTA sprayable hydrogel modified from Cu2+-TA MPN [197], GCP injectable hydrogel 
using Cu2+-protocatechuic aldehyde (PA) MPN as a functional unit [198], Cu@TA/Zn@TA/ε-PL-SilMA dual-network hydrogel con­
structed from Cu2+ and Zn2+ with TA [203], PC-Cu@K6-PEG formed with proanthocyanidins and Cu2+ [202], and OD-CM/GA-CuIIUV 
hydrogel based on Cu2+-GA MPN composites [201], demonstrate promising antibacterial efficacy and application potential.
Polymers, due to their lightweight nature, ease of processing, thermal and chemical stability, and abundant surface functional 
groups, provide an ideal substrate for constructing Metal-Phenolic Networks (MPNs). They not only stabilize and anchor the MPN 
structure but also offer diverse design flexibility for macroscopic forms. With the advantages of low cost, easy functionalization, and 
scalability, copper-based MPN composites can be efficiently prepared and adapted for applications such as wound dressings and 
antimicrobial coatings, demonstrating excellent clinical translation potential. A one-step coating method was used to assemble Cu2+
and EGCG on the surface of PVDF nanofibers, creating CuEG/PVDF nanofibers with a piezoelectric dual-network structure (Fig. 10) 
[183]. The Cu2+-EGCG MPN enhanced the hydrophilicity and piezoelectric properties of PVDF, promoting cell adhesion, proliferation, 
and migration while integrating the bioactivity of Cu2+ and EGCG. Driven by cellular traction forces, these fibers form a dynamic force- 
electric feedback loop, enabling adaptive electrical stimulation for tissue regeneration and effective antibacterial and anti- 
inflammatory action in treating infected diabetic wounds. On the surface of cationic polystyrene-divinylbenzene (PS-DVB) micro­
spheres, a Cu2+-TA MPN was self-assembled to create a multifunctional hemoperfusion adsorbent, P-PEI Cu/TAn, specifically for sepsis 
blood purification [186]. The PEI grafting provides high-density positive charges for efficient electrostatic capture of negatively 
charged endotoxins (LPS) and cell-free DNA (cfDNA), enhancing both antibacterial properties and blood compatibility. The MPN 
coating offers antioxidant, antibacterial, and auxiliary adsorption functions without compromising the substrate structure, optimizing 
Cu/TA ratios to maximize adsorption capacity and bioactivity. Using biocompatible and degradable polycaprolactone nanofibers (PCL- 
fiber) as the base scaffold, a dynamic Cu2+-PDA MPN coating was constructed on its surface, successfully creating Cu-P-PCL nanofiber 
dressings [185]. These dressings intelligently sense dynamic changes in the wound microenvironment (pH decrease and ROS increase), 
releasing Cu2+ as needed to address early infections or activating the antioxidant function of PDA to promote later-stage repair,

4.6. Other subtypes
Copper selenides and copper tellurides, as significant copper chalcogenides, exhibit unique photoelectric and thermoelectric 
properties, demonstrating substantial potential in energy conversion and electronic devices. These materials can be regulated to 
display a variety of stoichiometric ratios (CuTe, Cu2Te, CuSe, Cu2Se) and non-stoichiometric variations (Cu2-xTe, Cu2-xSe), with their 
crystal structures showing notable differences based on the copper-chalcogenide element ratio [234,235]. Due to the prevalent 
presence of copper vacancies, both materials exhibit typical p-type semiconductor characteristics. Copper tellurides have low thermal 
conductivity and excellent thermoelectric conversion efficiency, making them outstanding in fields such as photothermal conversion, 
thermoelectric generation, gas sensing, and spintronics [236,237]. Meanwhile, copper selenide nanocrystals display size-dependent 
tunable bandgap properties, with bandgap characteristics well-matched to the solar spectrum, offering significant advantages in 
photovoltaic applications [238,239]. Advances in nanotechnology have revealed the immense potential for scalable applications of 
solution-synthesized copper selenide and copper telluride nanocrystals. Recent studies have further expanded the application 
boundaries of these materials, highlighting their emerging antimicrobial efficacy and therapeutic potential in combating infections. 
Zhou et al. synthesized nano-grape clusters of CuTe with rough surface structures via a one-step precipitation method at room tem­
perature [205]. CuTe, with an average diameter of 263.21 nm, can tightly adhere to bacteria and disrupt their cellular structure, 
efficiently killing them through multiple mechanisms such as ROS release and GSH depletion, offering new strategies for treating 
resistant bacteria and biofilm infections. Wang's team successfully constructed CuFeSe2-PVP nano-blades using a PVP-assisted aqueous 
precipitation method [204]. This material exhibits a sheet-like structure assembled from single particles, with lattice spacings of 0.195 
nm ((220) plane) and 0.32 nm ((112) plane). Electron microscopy shows that the nano-blades can directly insert and cut fungal 
membrane structures, leading to leakage of cellular contents. Additionally, CuFeSe2-PVP displays n-type semiconductor character­
istics (bandgap 1.4797 eV, valence band position −4.72 eV), catalyzing ROS production and creating a dual mechanism of physical 
cutting and oxidative stress, significantly inhibiting planktonic fungi and biofilms. These studies not only broaden the application 
range of copper chalcogenides but also provide important insights into designing novel multifunctional antibacterial nanomaterials.
Polyoxometalates (POMs) are unique nanoscale metal-oxide anions that exhibit diverse structural characteristics and variable 
physicochemical properties. They possess adjustable sizes, well-defined structures, diverse topologies, and excellent photoelectric and 
magnetic properties [240]. Due to their rich surface oxygen structures and substitution chemistry, POMs can engage in covalent or 
non-covalent interactions to insert active sites and enable chemical modifications, effectively integrating various materials for 
multifunctional design [241,242]. Copper ions, with their variable valence states (Cu+/Cu2+), are used to dope POMs, enhancing 
electron transfer capabilities and significantly boosting antibacterial activity. Moreover, strong electronic coupling between copper 
and the POM framework stabilizes copper ion valence states, reduces toxicity, and improves biocompatibility. This feature has made 
copper-doped POMs a focal point in antibacterial research. Zhao et al. designed acid/H2S dual-responsive molybdenum-based POM 
nanoclusters (Cu-POM NCs), which are monodispersed (7–10 nm) at pH 7.2 and self-assemble into approximately 220 nm aggregates 
in acidic environments (pH 5.5), enabling site-specific accumulation [206]. At infection sites, Cu-POM NCs catalyze CuAAC reactions 
to activate prodrugs into antibacterial molecules, while H2S-triggered NIR-II photothermal effects (conversion efficiency 49.2%) and 
H2S consumption synergistically enhance antibacterial efficacy. Xing et al. embedded copper-doped POMs into ZnO@PDA core-shell 
structures using ultrasound assistance, creating a ternary heterostructure. XRD analysis confirmed the integration of the Strandberg- 
type POM structure with characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ  = 7.97◦, 9.54◦, 11.91◦, and 19.44◦[207]. The multivalent nature of Cu- 
POMs (Cu2+/Cu+, Mo6+/Mo5+) significantly improved catalytic cycle stability and photocatalytic activity. Similarly, Xing's team 
integrated Cu-POMs into a CS-modified GO carrier, forming a GO/CS/Cu-POM ternary composite. Cu-POMs formed one-dimensional 
chain structures through copper coordination cations, uniformly distributed on the carrier surface, enhancing oxidative stress in­
duction [208]. Wang et al. constructed a dual-crosslinked PCSG hydrogel using hydrogen bonding between POM and gelatin and ionic 
crosslinking between sodium alginate and Ca2+. The Keggin-type POM provided an acidic environment to enhance mechanical 
strength, while its mixed valence states (W6+/W5+) facilitated efficient electron transfer [209]. Zhu et al. encapsulated Keggin-type 
Cu-POM into HKUST-1 MOF pores and coated it with CTAB, creating CuPOM-MOF@S. This structure stabilized POMs within MOF 
channels and enhanced bacterial targeting, with W/Mo-O clusters and Cu2+ synergistically catalyzing high local ROS generation 
[210]. These structurally engineered Cu-POM-based materials achieve site-specific responses and synergistic antibacterial mecha­
nisms, providing powerful tools to address challenges in antibiotic resistance.

the p orbitals of the coordinating atoms results in a unique atomically dispersed structure, where each Cu atom can function as an

5. Copper-based nanomaterials for multimodal induction of cuproptosis-like death
The concept of cuproptosis-like death has introduced a novel theoretical framework and design paradigm for the antibacterial 
application of copper-based nanomaterials. Traditionally, the antibacterial effect of copper ions was primarily attributed to their 
participation in Fenton-like reactions that generate ROS, leading to nonspecific oxidative damage to cellular components. However, 
the discovery of cuproptosis-like death reveals a more targeted metabolic mechanism, in which copper ions disrupt central carbon 
metabolism, particularly the TCA cycle, by inducing aberrant aggregation and inactivation of lipoylated proteins such as DLAT and 
DLST. This disruption causes systemic collapse of the bacterial energy metabolism network, triggering a programmed, controllable, 
and highly efficient mode of cell death. Because this mechanism exerts particularly strong effects on metabolically active cells, copper- 
based nanomaterials exhibit distinctive advantages in inhibiting biofilm formation and eradicating drug-resistant bacterial commu­
nities. Through structural coupling with components that enhance TCA cycle stress or synergistically interfere with key metabolic 
enzymes, these materials enable precise targeting of metabolic vulnerabilities, thereby substantially improving antibacterial selec­
tivity and efficacy.
Recent studies on copper-based nanomaterials designed to induce cuproptosis-like death have further demonstrated their 
remarkable potential for precision antibacterial therapy. With sophisticated structural engineering and functional modulation, these 
materials achieve excellent targeting and controlled-release performance [13,108,111]. Meanwhile, cuproptosis-like death synergizes 
with other bactericidal pathways, effectively overcoming bacterial resistance to single antibacterial mechanisms and significantly 
enhancing bactericidal efficiency and biofilm eradication capacity [37,90]. This section summarizes representative cuproptosis-like 
death-inducing copper-based nanomaterial systems, focusing on how precise regulation of copper ion release kinetics and local 
metabolic interference can optimize antibacterial performance and overcome bacterial resistance (Table 2) (Fig. 13). Collectively, 
these advances provide a solid theoretical basis for the rational construction and functional integration of copper-based materials, 
establishing a practical foundation for developing controllable, traceable, and clinically translatable antibacterial strategies.
5.1. Photo-responsive systems
Photo-responsive Cu-based materials possess unique photothermal and photo-oxidative conversion properties, along with tunable 
ion-release behavior, which endow them with significant advantages in antibacterial applications and infection microenvironment 
regulation [257,258]. Incorporating PTT and PDT into copper-based systems enables externally controllable antibacterial activation 
while markedly amplifying the biochemical reactivity of copper ions, thereby promoting cuproptosis-like death. This light-triggered 
synergistic mechanism effectively disrupts biofilm barriers and bacterial resistance defenses, while mitigating the cytotoxicity asso­
ciated with high copper dosages—offering an efficient and safe strategy for treating complex and drug-resistant infections.
PTT induces local hyperthermia through photothermal conversion, directly disrupting bacterial membranes, increasing perme­
ability, and accelerating copper ion dissolution and diffusion, which ultimately leads to copper overload and cuproptosis-like death. To 
address the acidic and H2O2-rich characteristics of the biofilm microenvironment, Mei et al. developed copper-doped polyoxometalate 
clusters (Cu-POMs) capable of self-assembling under acidic conditions (Fig. 14) [34]. Cu-POMs exist as uniformly dispersed nano­
particles at pH 7.4 but assemble into larger clusters under pH 6.0–5.0, enhancing biofilm penetration and retention within the BME. 
Additionally, reversible valence transitions between Cu+/Cu2+ and Mo6+/Mo5+ enable catalytic decomposition of endogenous H2O2 
into •OH radicals, achieving CDT. The CDT process also restores macrophage functionality in the BME, enhancing chemotaxis, 
phagocytosis, and pro-inflammatory activity. Upon NIR irradiation, Cu-POMs achieve a photothermal conversion efficiency of 
43.22%, rapidly increasing local temperature to 45 ◦C and inducing mild PTT. The photothermal effect enhances bacterial metabolism 
and copper transporter activity, facilitating Cu-POM uptake and intracellular copper accumulation, thereby triggering cuproptosis-like 
death. Similarly, Xue et al. fabricated Cu-doped hydroxyapatite nanorods (Cu-doped HA) on PEEK surfaces as functional coatings [90]. 
Cu doping-induced lattice distortion improved NIR absorption, enhancing bacterial membrane permeability and copper ion influx 
under mild photothermal stimulation. Excess intracellular copper consequently induced cuproptosis-like death. Moreover, Yu et al. 
developed copper–tannic acid (Cu–TA) nanozymes exhibiting high photothermal stability under NIR irradiation [108]. The Cu2+–TA 
coordination structure conferred peroxidase-like activity, catalyzing H2O2 decomposition into •OH radicals to potentiate cuproptosis- 
like death. In another example, Yang et al. electrostatically coordinated copper ions within a Zn–porphyrin framework to form ve­
sicular Cu–PPS structures that undergo photothermal-triggered disassembly and copper release, inducing cuproptosis-like death [251]. 
Gao et al. further reported a metal–organic framework-type Cu–ellagic acid structure (Cu–EA) that generates localized photothermal 
effects under NIR irradiation, accelerating Cu2+ release and intensifying cuproptosis-like antibacterial responses [252].
PDT relies on photosensitizer-mediated ROS generation under light irradiation; however, its efficacy is often hindered in hypoxic 
and metabolically suppressed infection sites, limiting its ability to eradicate deep-seated or biofilm-associated bacteria [259]. The 
integration of copper-based materials into photoresponsive systems addresses this limitation by enabling photoinduced Cu2+/Cu+
cycling, thereby enhancing local redox reactions and triggering cuproptosis-like death. This dual mechanism extends ROS-mediated 
oxidative stress into a deeper and more sustained bactericidal effect. For example, Yang et al. synthesized a Cu7S4@rGO bio- 
heterojunction by in situ growth of Cu7S4 on reduced graphene oxide (rGO) (Fig. 15) [35]. The narrow bandgap of Cu7S4 allows

5.2. Ultrasound-activated systems
Ultrasound, as a noninvasive and controllable external stimulus, holds great promise for nanomaterial-assisted therapies 
[260,261]. Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) exerts potent bactericidal effects primarily through the accumulation of ROS. In the context of 
copper-based materials, the microbubble formation and cavitation effects induced by ultrasound enhance material permeability and 
drug delivery efficiency, thereby promoting efficient copper ion release and enabling precise regulation of the local microenvironment. 
Ultrasound stimulation optimizes copper ion release and redox reactions, which not only improves bacterial clearance but also 
effectively induces cuproptosis-like death, overcoming challenges associated with antibiotic resistance and biofilm reconstruction. 
These findings underscore the synergistic potential of ultrasound and copper-based materials in antibacterial therapy.
The microbubble and cavitation effects triggered by ultrasound markedly enhance the active release of copper-based materials, 
facilitating precise copper ion–mediated bacterial death. This mechanism offers a novel strategy for achieving efficient and control­
lable bacterial eradication, highlighting the unique advantages and far-reaching potential of ultrasound in copper-based antibacterial 
systems. Li et al. developed Fe–CuTA nanoparticles by crosslinking Fe3O4 nanoparticles with tannic acid (TA) and Cu2+ ions to form a 
CuTA coordination layer, which subsequently self-assembled into approximately 17 μm ultrasound-responsive Fe–CuTA microbubbles 
(MB-CuTA) (Fig. 16) [9]. Upon ultrasound irradiation, these microbubbles underwent inertial cavitation, generating high-energy 
microstreams and mechanical disruption that damaged biofilm structures and triggered the rapid release of large quantities of cop­
per ions. The locally accumulated copper ions disrupted bacterial metabolic homeostasis and induced cuproptosis-like death. 
Meanwhile, sonoporation facilitated the uptake of Fe–CuTA by macrophages, enhancing intracellular copper accumulation and 
thereby activating immune cell reprogramming to rebuild the anti-infective immune microenvironment. This acoustically controlled 
system enables spatiotemporally precise copper ion release, directly inducing bacterial cuproptosis-like death while strengthening host 
immune defenses through immune cell modulation.
The deep tissue penetration of SDT enables localized targeting of infection microenvironments, achieving rapid and effective 
bacterial eradication. By integrating SDT with copper-induced cuproptosis-like death, it is possible to further overcome the resistance 
of drug-resistant bacterial infections. Recent studies have explored multiple optimization strategies, providing new insights into the 
comprehensive management of antibiotic-resistant infections. Li et al. developed a sonoresponsive coating (CuTOT) through a step­
wise self-assembly process on tannic acid (TA)–modified titanium substrates [10]. This multilayered system uniformly incorporated 
copper-tetrakis(4-carboxyphenyl)porphyrin (Cu-TCPP), tinidazole (TNZ), and bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), proposing a 
non-antibiotic, multifunctional strategy for antibacterial therapy and bone regeneration in implant-associated infections (IAIs). Upon 
ultrasound irradiation, Cu-TCPP catalyzed the conversion of oxygen into cytotoxic singlet oxygen ({\,}^1O2), while the consequent 
oxygen depletion created a hypoxic environment that activated TNZ’s antibacterial activity. Moreover, the redox modulation of poly 
(tannic acid) (pTA) stabilized Cu(I) ions in aqueous solution and circumvented bacterial defenses against cuproptosis-like death, 
enabling precise copper delivery and controllable induction of bacterial death. This synergistic mechanism markedly enhanced bac­
terial clearance. Additionally, by exploiting the osteogenic and anti-inflammatory effects of OMVs derived from Lactobacillus animalis, 
the system promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby enhancing osteogenesis, mitigating inflammation, 
and supporting tissue repair and regeneration. Huang et al. further developed CpBT nanoreactors exhibiting both ultrasound-activated 
piezoelectric and chemodynamic properties (Fig. 17) [36]. The CpBT nanoreactors consisted of barium titanate (mBT), pDA, and 
copper components. The mBT served as a piezoelectric material capable of generating piezoelectric effects under ultrasonic stimu­
lation, while pDA bound to copper ions through π–π interactions, facilitating the ultrasound-induced transfer of electrons and hot 
carriers from the mBT surface to the copper ions. This process not only enhanced piezoelectric sonodynamic ROS generation but also 
modulated the Cu2+/Cu+ redox cycling, triggering Cu+-catalyzed Fenton-like reactions that produced hydroxyl radicals. These radicals 
disrupted bacterial membrane integrity and caused DNA damage, leading to a cuproptosis-like bacterial death. When incorporated 
onto the surface of 3D-printed polyetheretherketone (PEEK) bone implants, the CpBT nanoreactors exhibited excellent antibacterial 
performance under medical ultrasound and simultaneously promoted angiogenesis and osteogenesis in vivo, demonstrating 
outstanding potential for clinical translation.
To enhance the sonodynamic activity of copper-based systems, many studies have incorporated biocompatible semiconductor 
sonosensitizers to construct heterojunction structures. This approach improves electron–hole separation efficiency, increases ROS 
generation under ultrasonic excitation, and promotes ROS accumulation, thereby strengthening the antibacterial effects induced by 
cuproptosis-like death. Recently, Yue et al. reported a copper-based material (Cu2O-Sr/Cur) with a shell-in-shell architecture, in which

shell contains strontium (Sr), which enhances osseointegration by promoting bone formation and inhibiting osteoclast activity. The 
outer shell consists of Cu2O combined with curcumin (Cur) to form a Cu2O/Cur heterojunction, where curcumin serves as the

5.3. Oxidative stress–amplifying systems
The intrinsic antioxidant defense systems of bacteria can effectively eliminate ROS and alleviate oxidative stress–induced damage 
[262]. These antioxidant mechanisms, to some extent, mitigate the bactericidal effects of copper-induced metabolic disruption, 
allowing certain bacteria to maintain metabolic homeostasis under oxidative stress conditions [263]. In particular, within complex 
infectious microenvironments such as biofilms, the upregulation of bacterial antioxidant systems further diminishes the efficacy of 
copper-based antimicrobial strategies [66]. To overcome this limitation, oxidative stress amplification strategies have emerged as a 
promising approach to enhance cuproptosis-like bacterial death. By promoting sustained ROS generation or inhibiting bacterial 
antioxidant activity, these strategies can significantly exacerbate intracellular oxidative imbalance, thereby strengthening copper- 
induced metabolic interference and improving antibacterial efficacy.
Nanozymes, owing to their ability to mimic the catalytic functions of natural enzymes, have been widely applied to induce intense 
oxidative stress responses. Featuring high stability, low cost, and excellent design flexibility, nanozymes can catalyze the conversion of 
endogenous H2O2 into ROS such as •OH and O2•−within bacterial infection microenvironments, thereby disrupting bacterial anti­
oxidant defense systems. In copper-based antibacterial systems, nanozymes not only mediate CDT but also promote the reduction and 
redox cycling of Cu2+, leading to Cu+ overload and amplifying cuproptosis-like death. Wang et al. developed a morphology- 
controllable multifunctional CuCo2O4 nanozyme for the treatment of biofilm-infected wounds (Fig. 19) [12]. The CuCo2O4 nano­
zyme exhibits a three-dimensional flower-like morphology, in which stacked nanosheets significantly increase the specific surface area 
and exposure of active sites. The coexistence of Cu2+ and Co2+/Co3+ within the spinel structure facilitates electron transfer between 
multivalent Cu and Co ions, providing additional reaction sites, enhancing oxygen activation, and improving catalytic efficiency. As a 
result, the nanozyme exhibits peroxidase (POD-like), oxidase (OXD-like), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px-like) activities, 
collectively enhancing ROS generation and depleting intracellular GSH, thereby intensifying oxidative stress. Under high oxidative 
pressure, CuCo2O4 disrupts bacterial membrane integrity and, through Cu2+ overload, inhibits the TCA cycle and respiratory chain 
complex activity, leading to energy metabolism collapse, lipid peroxidation, and translational dysfunction—ultimately triggering 
bacterial cuproptosis-like death. Similarly, Hu et al. fabricated CuGA–VAN nanoneedles by loading vancomycin (VAN) onto a cop­
per–gallic acid (CuGA) complex (Fig. 20) [113]. The abundant carboxyl groups exposed on the nanoneedle surface enable selective 
adsorption onto amino groups on the MRSA cell wall. VAN blocks peptidoglycan synthesis and disrupts the cell wall, thereby facili­
tating copper ion entry. The CuGA–VAN nanoneedles exhibit two key nanozyme activities: OXD-like activity catalyzes ROS generation, 
broadening the antibacterial spectrum, while GSH-Px-like activity depletes bacterial GSH reserves, weakening their antioxidant de­
fenses. Together, these processes trigger intracellular oxidative stress bursts, creating favorable conditions for Cu2+-induced 
cuproptosis-like death.
Introducing glutathione (GSH) depleting agents represents another effective strategy to enhance cuproptosis-like death. As a major 
nonenzymatic antioxidant, GSH efficiently scavenges ROS and regulates intracellular copper homeostasis, thereby conferring resis­
tance to cuproptosis-like death. In bacteria, the GSH-based antioxidant system plays a vital role in maintaining intracellular redox 
balance and protecting against metal ion toxicity. When GSH is further depleted, this redox equilibrium rapidly collapses. Hu et al. 
developed an inorganic nanoplatform, Cu2O–BSO nanoparticles (Cu2O-BSO NPs), designed for targeted delivery and amplification of 
oxidative stress through GSH depletion [109]. The nanoparticles were surface-modified with pDA and polyethylene glycol (PEG) to 
enhance mucosal penetration and adhesion to lung tissue, enabling precise localization to infected sites. Buthionine sulfoximine (BSO) 
inhibits bacterial GSH synthesis, disrupts antioxidant defenses, and reduces Cu2+ chelation, thereby promoting copper ion

5.4. Biofunctionalized systems
In copper-based antimicrobial systems, integrating biological components—such as biomimetic membranes, enzymes, or other 
functional biomolecules—has emerged as a powerful strategy for material functionalization [264–266]. These bioactive elements 
impart precise targeting capabilities, enabling controlled delivery of copper ions or catalytic species to bacterial sites. Consequently, 
cuproptosis-like death is spatially confined within bacterial cells, enhancing antibacterial efficacy while minimizing nonspecific 
cytotoxicity toward host tissues. Beyond targeted delivery, biological components actively modulate redox homeostasis and local 
metabolic remodeling, thereby alleviating copper overload–induced oxidative stress and tissue damage. This dual-regulation strategy, 
which simultaneously achieves bacteria-specific killing and host protection, defines a promising direction for the rational design of 
next-generation copper-based antimicrobial materials that induce cuproptosis-like death.
The incorporation of biomimetic membranes endows copper-based nanomaterials with precise targeting and superior biocom­
patibility. By coating artificial nanomaterials with plasma membranes extracted from natural cells, a biomimetic interface retaining 
key surface proteins, receptors, and glycolipids is established. This interface confers homologous targeting, pathogen recognition, and 
immune evasion capabilities, enabling precise accumulation at infection sites while enhancing circulation stability. Biomimetic 
membrane technology has emerged as a vital bridge between synthetic materials and biological systems, with broad applications in 
targeted delivery, antibacterial therapy, immunoregulation, and inflammation control. Zhang et al. developed macrophage mem­
brane–coated copper sulfide nanocages (mCuS@lm) integrating copper sulfide, mitoquinone (MitoQ), luteolin, and biomimetic 
membranes for the targeted treatment of multidrug-resistant bacterial pneumonia (Fig. 21) [13]. The immuno-trained alveolar 
macrophage membrane, enriched in pattern recognition receptors such as TLR2, enhanced infection-site targeting, while TNFR1 
expression acted as a cytokine decoy to mitigate inflammatory storms and promote immune evasion. Luteolin disrupted bacterial 
quorum sensing, suppressing biofilm formation and virulence, thereby amplifying copper-induced cuproptosis-like death. MitoQ 
protected host cells by suppressing mitochondrial lipid peroxidation, and CuS enabled NIR-II–responsive Cu2+ release for controlled 
activation of cuproptosis-like death. To achieve bone marrow targeting, Li et al. coated copper–iron–Elesclomol nanoparticles with 
bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC) membranes to form CFE@CM nanocells [106]. The biomimetic BMSC membrane 
facilitated homologous accumulation at osteomyelitis lesions, improving penetration, retention, and biocompatibility while reducing 
nonspecific immune responses. In acidic microenvironments, the CFE core released Cu+ and Fe2+ ions to catalyze Fenton-like ROS 
generation. Together with Elesclomol-mediated Cu2+ transport into bacteria, this process triggered cuproptosis-like death and bac­
terial metabolic collapse.

In addition to directly targeting bacteria, recent studies have enhanced bacterial clearance by inducing cuproptosis-like death in

moted efficient bacterial clearance.
5.5. Multi-metal synergistic systems
The synergistic interaction between copper and multivalent metal ions can markedly enhance catalytic cycle efficiency [267,268]. 
Numerous studies have established multimetal catalytic systems in which redox coupling enables continuous ROS generation, 
effectively elevating oxidative stress levels and amplifying copper-induced metabolic disruption. Moreover, to achieve controlled and 
stimuli-responsive release, researchers have tuned the coordination environment between copper and other metal ions, allowing 
adaptive ion release at infection sites in response to local microenvironmental cues. Under this cooperative mechanism, cuproptosis- 
like death is further intensified.
Researchers designed Cu-enriched hollow Prussian blue nanoparticles (Cu-HMPBs) based on the coordination framework char­
acteristics of Prussian Blue (Fig. 23) [111]. Cu-HMPBs are formed through the coordination of Cu2+–N≡C–Fe3+ bonds and can con­
trollably release Cu2+ ions in the acidic microenvironment of infections. The Fe3+–C≡N bond is more stable, enabling Cu release 
accompanied by Fe capture, thus allowing ion exchange under mildly acidic conditions. The resulting bacterial iron deprivation 
triggers iron starvation, leading to the collapse of energy metabolism and the failure of antioxidant defense systems. This process 
effectively amplifies Cu-induced oxidative stress and promotes cuproptosis-like bacterial cell death.
Wang et al. developed a Ga/Cu-based MOF by coordinating Ga3+ and Cu2+ ions with organic ligands and subsequently loading 
vancomycin to obtain Van@Ga/Cu-MOF (Fig. 24) [101]. The MOF framework provides a high specific surface area and porous ar­
chitecture, serving as a reservoir for the sustained release of Ga3+ and Cu2+ ions. Ga3+ mimics Fe3+ and is mistakenly taken up by 
bacteria, disrupting iron homeostasis and inducing ferroptosis—a non-apoptotic form of programmed cell death characterized by iron- 
dependent lipid peroxidation. Intracellular Fe2+ participates in the Fenton reaction to generate abundant ⋅OH, leading to lipid 
membrane peroxidation, structural collapse, GPX4 inactivation, GSH depletion, and overall oxidative imbalance. Meanwhile, Cu2+
ions interact with and decompose RSNO species, inducing excessive protein S-nitrosylation and Cu overload–mediated cuproptosis- 
like death. Notably, both ferroptosis and cuproptosis-like death share core mechanisms involving metal ion–induced metabolic 
disruption and oxidative imbalance. Their synergistic action enhances antibacterial efficacy by amplifying ROS generation and 
metabolic damage. Thus, the Ga/Cu bimetallic MOF effectively disrupts metal ion homeostasis, simultaneously regulating bacterial 
metabolism and inducing programmed cell death.
5.6. Gas-releasing systems
Gas therapy has attracted considerable attention owing to its unique physicochemical properties and tunable biological effects. 
Gaseous molecules such as NO, CO, H2, and O3 used in gas therapy exhibit broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, low local toxicity, and 
strong synergistic effects with various therapeutic modalities [269,270]. Beyond their intrinsic antimicrobial activity, these gases 
possess exceptional permeability and diffusivity, allowing them to rapidly penetrate physical barriers such as biofilms and reach deep 
infection sites that are often inaccessible to conventional drugs [271]. This property provides a new auxiliary pathway to enhance 
copper-based materials in inducing cuproptosis-like death.
Zhu and colleagues developed L-Arg–modified copper oxide nanozymes (L-CuxO NPs) (Fig. 25) [105]. As a natural nitric oxide (NO) 
precursor, L-Arg can continuously release NO under oxidative conditions while its guanidino group enhances the surface coordination 
and stability of copper oxide. This system exhibits peroxidase (POD)-like activity, catalyzing the generation of •OH in the presence of 
H2O2. Through the Cu2+/Cu+ redox cycle, the nanozyme not only produces ROS but also induces intracellular copper overload. In this 
system, gas therapy synergizes with cuproptosis-like death primarily because NO-mediated membrane permeabilization facilitates 
Cu2+ influx, thereby promoting copper-dependent bacterial lethality. Functional modification with L-Arg enables controlled gas 
release; owing to their strong permeability, gas molecules can penetrate biofilm barriers, allowing the cuproptosis-like mechanism to 
act effectively in deep infection regions.
Moreover, gaseous signaling molecules can modulate the metabolic microenvironment of both bacteria and host cells, redefining 
bacterial energy acquisition pathways and increasing susceptibility to copper-induced metabolic stress—thus indirectly amplifying the 
metal stress response. Luo et al. further developed a multifunctional biomaterial platform, namely MnO2-loaded copper metal–organic 
frameworks (MCMs) (Fig. 26) [112]. The Cu-MOF core releases Cu2+/Cu+ ions to trigger cuproptosis-like death, while MnO2 coating 
on the surface catalytically decomposes H2O2 to generate O2, thereby alleviating biofilm hypoxia. The restored oxygen environment 
forces bacteria to shift from glycolysis to TCA cycle–driven aerobic respiration, thereby activating the key cuproptosis-related target 
protein DLAT and enhancing bacterial susceptibility to copper-induced death. Oxygen generation not only promotes copper-mediated 
bacterial killing but also activates dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages, stimulating chemotaxis and phagocytic activity. Here, the

Overall, the combination of gas therapy with cuproptosis-like death exhibits considerable promise for antibacterial applications.

extended periods. Achieving precise temporal synchronization and spatial colocalization between gas release and copper ion influx 
poses substantial material design challenges. Moreover, the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria may hinder the penetration of 
both Cu2+ and NO, resulting in variable therapeutic efficacy across bacterial species [272]. These issues require further systematic 
investigation. Nevertheless, synergistic strategies integrating gas therapy with metal metabolism interference represent an emerging 
and promising direction for the development of next-generation antibacterial materials.
6. Cu-based nanomaterials for biosafety and toxicity in antibacterial applications
Although Cu-based nanomaterials exhibit potent antibacterial activity, their clinical translation remains constrained by significant 
biosafety concerns. Current studies primarily emphasize their targeted antibacterial efficacy, yet the risk of nonspecific interactions 
with host cells and the resulting cytotoxicity cannot be overlooked. For instance, a previous study reported that F-Cu series films 
fabricated from cellulose/polycaprolactone matrices loaded with CuNPs demonstrated pronounced broad-spectrum antibacterial 
activity at a concentration of 120 µg/mL. Nevertheless, at this concentration, the viability of human embryonic lung fibroblasts (Wi38) 
decreased to 55%, indicating a substantial cytotoxic risk at therapeutically effective antibacterial doses [273]. Moreover, the treatment 
of bacterial infections involves a complex pathological microenvironment that often requires coordination between local adminis­
tration and systemic circulation. This underscores that the biosafety evaluation of Cu-based nanomaterials for antibacterial applica­
tions should not be limited to in vitro cytotoxicity assays. Instead, comprehensive assessments are needed, encompassing their in vivo 
biodistribution, tissue-specific accumulation, pharmacokinetics, and the potential for chronic toxicity under prolonged administration. 
Expanding biosafety evaluation from a “local–short-term” framework to a “systemic–long-term” perspective is therefore essential for 
the rational design of copper-based nanoantibacterial agents that combine robust antimicrobial efficacy with acceptable safety pro­
files, ultimately facilitating their clinical translation.
To address the safety challenges associated with Cu-based nanomaterials, rational material design and surface modification have 
emerged as pivotal strategies for regulating copper ion release, optimizing synergistic antibacterial activity, and improving biocom­
patibility. The construction of intelligent carrier systems enables controlled and sustained Cu2+ release, thereby mitigating dose- 
dependent toxicity. Representative design approaches include composite hydrogels [32,130,138], electrospun nanofibrous scaffolds 
[127,157], core–shell–like architectures [161,162], and microneedle-based platforms [131,187]. The structural characteristics and 
functional mechanisms of these systems are discussed in detail in Section 4 of this work. Surface functionalization with natural 
polyphenols represents another effective strategy. Such modifications reduce the undesirable reactivity of nanoparticles while 
enhancing their physicochemical stability. For example, leveraging the adhesive interactions between the phenolic hydroxyl groups of 
tannic acid and bacterial surface proteins and polysaccharides, PCT NPs can selectively adhere to bacteria and release Cu2+ in situ at 
infection sites. Notably, these nanoparticles exhibit potent bactericidal activity at a Cu2+-equivalent concentration as low as 4 µg/mL 
[254]. Furthermore, NIR photoresponsiveness and infection microenvironment–responsive mechanisms enable on-demand drug 
release while minimizing systemic toxicity through lesion-targeted activation. For instance, Cu-doped HA nanorods significantly 
enhanced the antibacterial efficacy of a low Cu2+ concentration (5 ppm) under precise 808 nm NIR irradiation, thereby reducing 
collateral damage to healthy tissues [90]. Similarly, the oxidized quinone structure of CuTAQ enhances its POD-like catalytic activity; 
in the presence of 250 μM H2O2 within the infectious microenvironment, CuTAQ achieved a 100% antibacterial rate at a concentration 
of 4 µg/mL [195].
For Cu-based nanomaterials that induce bacteria-specific cuproptosis-like death, the fundamental biosafety strategy is to exploit 
the differential regulation of copper homeostasis between bacteria and mammalian cells. Due to the absence of membrane-bound 
organelles and limited copper-buffering capacity, bacteria are highly vulnerable to copper overload. In contrast, mammalian cells 
possess sophisticated copper-chelating systems and regulatory pathways that maintain intracellular copper balance. Building on this 
distinction, recent studies have attempted to selectively disrupt bacterial metabolic centers while safeguarding host mitochondrial 
function, thereby reconciling potent antibacterial activity with improved biosafety. For instance, mCuS@lm nanocages were co-loaded 
with the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant Mitoquinone. While copper ions exerted bactericidal effects, Mitoquinone reduced lipid 
peroxidation in host cells at a concentration of 20 µg/mL mCuS@lm, thereby preserving mitochondrial integrity [13]. Similarly, 
PTCu@GOx incorporates GOx to deplete intracellular glucose and activate the AMPK signaling pathway in host cells. This activation 
suppresses copper-induced oligomerization of DLAT at the molecular level, maintaining mitochondrial structural integrity and pre­
venting cuproptosis in host cells [255]. Collectively, these approaches illustrate the feasibility of enhancing the therapeutic selectivity 
and safety of copper-based antibacterial nanomaterials through metabolic and mitochondrial protection strategies.
The current biosafety evaluation framework for copper-based nanomaterials is largely endpoint-oriented. In vitro assessments 
primarily rely on metabolic activity–based viability assays, such as CCK-8 and MTT, as well as binary live/dead discrimination 
methods, including Calcein-AM/PI staining and flow cytometry. In vivo studies typically focus on serum biochemical parameters and 
histopathological examination of major organs using H&E staining to identify overt tissue damage [10,125,183,184,232,274]. 
However, for copper-based nanomaterials designed to induce cuproptosis-like bacterial death, such endpoint-driven evaluations may 
be insufficient. Although these systems are often engineered with enhanced microbial targeting and reduced dosages, the released

7. Conclusion and future perspectives
Copper possesses a dual nature for bacteria, functioning as both an essential micronutrient and a potential toxin. At physiological 
levels, copper ions participate in various enzymatic reactions and energy-regulating processes that are vital for cellular metabolism. 
However, excessive copper can induce bacterial damage and death through multiple mechanisms, including oxidative stress, metal ion 
imbalance, and protein misfolding. Leveraging this “double-edged sword” effect, copper-based nanomaterials have emerged as a 
promising class of non-antibiotic antibacterial agents owing to their controllable copper release and versatile physicochemical 
properties. In recent years, their design philosophy has evolved from simple antibacterial functionality to multifunctional intelligent 
platforms integrating anti-inflammatory, regenerative, immunomodulatory, and stimuli-responsive capabilities. Benefiting from their 
photothermal, catalytic, and nanozyme-like properties, these materials enable spatiotemporally controlled therapeutic interventions 
to meet the dynamic treatment demands of complex infectious microenvironments.
The introduction of the concept of cuproptosis-like death has provided a novel biological perspective for copper-based antibacterial 
research, expanding this field beyond traditional materials chemistry toward bacterial metabolic homeostasis and programmed cell 
death signaling. Similar to eukaryotic cuproptosis, cuproptosis-like death in prokaryotes involves copper-induced aggregation of 
lipoylated proteins, inactivation of Fe–S cluster proteins, and disruption of the TCA cycle. However, in the absence of mitochondria, 
bacteria rely on the plasma membrane–associated electron transport chain to sustain energy metabolism. Copper interacts with lip­
oylated components and membrane electron carriers to trigger a unique form of programmed bacterial death, offering a theoretical 
basis for precise antibacterial targeting. Based on this mechanism, several innovative copper-based systems have recently been 
developed, in which each component performs distinct and indispensable functions and is assembled in an ordered or integrated 
manner rather than by simple mixing. These systems enable targeted copper ion release and multimodal synergistic antibacterial 
effects. Collectively, these studies not only reveal a new paradigm of metal-induced antibacterial mechanisms but also highlight the 
profound potential of cuproptosis-like copper-based nanomaterials in combating drug-resistant infections and modulating immune 
microenvironments.
Although the concept of cuproptosis-like death provides a novel paradigm for antibacterial therapy, several key challenges remain 
before achieving safe, efficient, and clinically translatable applications in complex infectious microenvironments. Future research may 
advance this field in three major directions: 
1) Mechanistic elucidation and biological validation. The signaling networks underlying cuproptosis-like death in bacteria remain 
poorly characterized. Integrated multi-omics approaches combining transcriptomics, metabolomics, and proteomics should be 
applied to identify copper-induced target proteins and associated metabolic disturbances. Comparative analyses of bacterial and 
host responses under copper stress will facilitate the optimization of pathogen selectivity, whereas modulation of lipoylation levels 
or the introduction of mitochondrial protective factors may help balance antibacterial efficacy with host compatibility.
2) Precise control of copper release and environmental responsiveness. In complex infection sites, the stability, selectivity, and 
controllable copper release of nanomaterials are crucial. Overly rapid release may cause tissue toxicity, whereas insufficient release 
limits efficacy. Smart systems responsive to pH, redox state, or enzyme activity, coupled with surface functionalization using 
antibodies, glycans, or bacterial membrane–mimicking layers, can achieve targeted delivery and dynamic therapeutic control.
3) Intelligent design and sustainable manufacturing. Advances in artificial intelligence and machine learning can accelerate the 
discovery of cuproptosis-like materials by correlating structural features with antibacterial performance and biocompatibility. 
Integrating these tools with green synthesis strategies will enable scalable, low-cost, and sustainable fabrication, ultimately pro­
moting the clinical translation of cuproptosis-like antibacterial strategies.
In summary, the introduction of the cuproptosis-like death concept not only expands our understanding of metal ion–induced cell 
death mechanisms but also provides new conceptual impetus for the antibacterial applications of copper-based nanomaterials. 
Continued research on copper-based nanomaterials that induce cuproptosis-like death is expected to deepen our insight into 
host–pathogen metabolic interactions and offer a solid scientific foundation for developing precise therapeutic strategies against 
multidrug-resistant infections.

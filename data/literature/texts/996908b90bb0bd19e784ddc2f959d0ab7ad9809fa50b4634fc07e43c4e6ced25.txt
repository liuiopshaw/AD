Qihang Ding+, Bo Wang+, Zixuan Zhan+, Paramesh Jangili+, Junyang Chen, Rakesh Mengji,
Hyeonji Rha, Yang Li,* Jian Tian,* and Jong Seung Kim*
Abstract: Chemodynamic therapy (CDT), leveraging Fenton reactions to generate hydroxyl radicals (•OH) from
intracellular hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), offers a promising cancer treatment strategy due to its high speciﬁcity and
low systemic toxicity. However, the targeted delivery of •OH-producing prodrugs using covalent organic frameworks
(COFs) remains a signiﬁcant challenge. Here, we report a mitochondria-targeted COF-based nano prodrug, COF-31@P,
designed for enhanced CDT efﬁcacy. COF-31@P is composed of a Fenton-like copper complex and the hydrogen peroxide
enzyme inhibitor 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT), linked via disulﬁde bonds that are selectively cleaved by tumor-speciﬁc
glutathione (GSH). This cleavage triggers the release of active components, resulting in robust •OH generation and
effective eradication of cancer cells. The platform demonstrates precise mitochondrial targeting, high therapeutic efﬁciency,
and excellent biocompatibility in vivo. By combining organelle targeting and multi-synergistic •OH generation production,
our COF-31@P represents a signiﬁcant advancement in CDT and holds strong potential for clinical translation.
Introduction
Cancer is a leading cause of global morbidity and mortal-
ity in both developing and developed nations.[1–3] Cancer
therapeutic strategies, including chemotherapy, surgery, and
radiotherapy, offer limited efﬁcacy in improving patient
survival and quality of life. These limitations stem from
chemotherapy resistance, surgical trauma, and the adverse
side effects associated with radiotherapy.[4–6] In response to
these challenges, Chemodynamic therapy (CDT) has emerged
as a minimally invasive and highly selective therapeutic
approach. CDT leverages Fenton or Fenton-like reactions to
convert endogenous hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) into hydroxyl
radical (•OH), the most cytotoxic reactive oxygen species
(ROS).[7–9] This innovative therapy offers several advantages
over conventional treatments, including tumor-speciﬁc activa-
tion, minimal invasiveness, and reduced systemic toxicity.[4,10]
To further enhance the efﬁcacy of CDT, recent research has
focused on optimizing Fenton catalysts, tailoring therapies
to the tumor microenvironment (TME), and regulating
H2O2 levels by inhibiting catalase activity.[11] Despite these
advancements, the clinical translation of CDT is still limited
by the lack of a drug delivery system that can simultaneously
induce Fenton reactions, respond to TME cues, and promote
localized •OH generation.[12–14] Moreover, the short lifespan
and diffusion range of •OH radicals necessitate strategies that
ensure their interaction with biological macromolecules to
maximize oxidative damage and therapeutic efﬁciency.[15–17]
Targeted therapy systems capable of transporting therapeutic
agents to speciﬁc cells or organelles represent a promising
solution. By reducing the distance between •OH radical
generation sites and critical biomolecules, these systems
enhance the oxidative damage, increase therapeutic efﬁciency,
and promote cell death. This study explores the development

Covalent organic frameworks (COFs) are a class of porous
material composed of shape-deﬁned organic monomers inter-
connected by dynamic covalent bonds.[22,23] These materials
are highly customizable and feature uniform nanosized
pores,[24] making them promising nanocarriers for inte-
grating diverse functional components. Nanoscale COFs
have been extensively studied for their therapeutic appli-
cations in phototherapy, drug delivery, immunotherapy, and
radiotherapy.[25,26] As organic carriers, COFs offer a selective
platform for incorporating therapeutic agents,[27,28] ensur-
ing targeted delivery and desired therapeutic outcomes
while minimizing the physiological toxicity often associated
with metal-rich inorganic materials.[29–33] These dual advan-
tages position COFs as a versatile platform for advancing
both preclinical and translational clinical investigations on
COFs.[34,35,13,36–38] However, despite their potential, the use
of organelle-targeting COF-based nanomaterials for CDT
remains unexplored.[39–41] This study aims to address this gap
by developing COF-based nanocarriers capable of organelle-
speciﬁc targeting, with a focus on enhancing the efﬁcacy and
precision of CDT.
In this study, we therefore synthesized a nano prodrug
COF-31@P based on COFs that targets mitochondria,
incorporating a copper complex for Fenton-like reaction
and a catalase inhibitor 3-AT, which are linked by disulﬁde
bonds. The over-expression of glutathione (GSH) in tumor
mitochondria facilitates the cleavage of these disulﬁde
bonds, simultaneously releasing the copper complex and
3-AT. The copper complex catalyzes the decomposition
of abundant H2O2 into toxic ROS, speciﬁcally •OH, while
3-AT inhibits catalase activity to sustain the H2O2 levels,
thereby amplifying the Fenton-like reaction. Additionally,
GSH consumption through disulﬁde bond cleavage further
reduces its capacity to scavenge ROS, enhancing the overall
therapeutic effect (Figure 1). Through cellular imaging
and cytotoxicity assays, we demonstrated that COF-31@P
effectively targets mitochondria, releases its therapeutic
payload in response to GSH, generates a substantial amount
of •OH radicals, leading to efﬁcient tumor cell eradication
via CDT. Furthermore, COF-31@P exhibits excellent tumor
enrichment and biosafety in vivo, making it a promising
candidate for CDT-based tumor therapy. To the best of our
knowledge, this is the ﬁrst report of a mitochondria-targeting
COF-based nanomaterial that integrates GSH consumption
and catalase inhibition to enhance the efﬁcacy of CDT,
marking a signiﬁcant advancement in the ﬁeld.
Results and Discussion
To conﬁrm the cleavage of disulﬁde bonds and the subsequent
release of the copper complex prodrugs, we designed a
ﬂuorescent prodrug, C2 conjugated with a rhodamine dye.
The synthesis of C2 involved a multistep process, including
nucleophilic substitution and click reactions initiated from di-
(2-picolyl)amine. Detailed synthetic procedures are provided
in Figure 2a and Supporting Information (Scheme S1). We
were able to identify and characterize the ﬂuorescent prodrug
C2, incorporating a copper complex and a disulﬁde bond,
through the use of 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy in conjunc-
tion with high-resolution mass spectrometry (Figures S1–S11).
As shown in Figure 2a, the disulﬁde bond in C2 was efﬁciently
cleaved in the presence of GSH, resulting in the release of the
copper complex and the formation of rhodamine derivative
as a reaction intermediate. This intermediate subsequently
underwent electrophilic aromatic substitution by attacking an
electron-deﬁcient carbon atom, resulting in the production of
rhodamine dye. These reactions facilitated the GSH-induced
release of the copper complex. To further investigate the
interaction between C2 and GSH, we conducted a titration
experiment using absorption spectroscopy, during which GSH
was incrementally added to C2. Addition of GSH resulted in
a notable shift in the absorption peak from 300 to 350 nm
(Figure S15). Correspondingly, there was an increase in ﬂu-
orescence intensity at 500 nm (Figures 2b,c), which allowed us
to plot it as a function of GSH concentration with respective
detection limits set at 3 mM (Figure 2d). These results also
conﬁrmed that the disulﬁde bond-linked copper complex pro-
drug can be effectively cleaved in the presence of GSH, lead-
ing to the release of the active copper complex. The observed
spectral changes and ﬂuorescence enhancement provide
strong evidence for the GSH-responsive behavior of C2, vali-
dating its potential for targeted drug release in environments
with high GSH concentrations, such as tumor mitochondria.
After validating the ﬂuorescent disulﬁde bond-linked
copper complex prodrug, we extended this GSH-responsive
molecular platform to synthesize a prodrug C1 derived from
the catalase inhibitor 3-AT and characterized its structure
(Figures S12–S14). As illustrated in Figure 2e, the disulﬁde
bond in C1 was efﬁciently cleaved in the presence of GSH,
leading to the release of the copper complex and the catalase
inhibitor 3-AT. To further explore the interaction between
prodrug C1 and GSH, we conducted high-performance liquid
chromatography (HPLC) analysis on the reaction of C1 with
GSH. The HPLC analysis revealed that prodrug C1 exhibited
a single peak at 10.2 min (Figure 2f). After the addition of
GSH for 1 h, the original peak of C1 diminished, and a new
peak appeared at 8 min. Upon extending the incubation time
to 4 h, the original C1 peak completely disappeared, while the
new peak at 8 min intensiﬁed, coinciding with the retention
time of 3-AT (8 min). These ﬁndings indicated that prodrug
C1 releases copper complexes and catalase inhibitor 3-AT
upon interaction with GSH. The observed changes in HPLC
proﬁles provide clear evidence of the controlled and efﬁcient
release of active therapeutic components under conditions of
high GSH concentration, such as those found in tumor envi-
ronments. This GSH-triggered release mechanism highlights
the potential of prodrug C1 as a targeted therapeutic agent
for enhancing chemodynamic therapy through simultaneous
copper complex activation and catalase inhibition.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) mapping
(Figure 3f) conﬁrmed the homogeneous distribution of C,
N, O, S, and Cu throughout COF-31@P. The presence of
Cu validates the successful encapsulation of the copper-
containing prodrug, while S signals the integration of sulfur-
containing functional groups from the prodrug. The EDX
spectrum of COF-31@P showed prominent Cu peaks at
∼1.0 keV (Cu Lα), ∼8.0 keV (Cu Kα), and ∼8.9 keV (Cu
Kβ), conﬁrming the successful loading of the prodrug, as
Cu was absent in pristine COF-31 (Figure 3g). Additionally,
the distinct sulfur (S Kα) peak at ∼2.3 keV served as
further evidence of prodrug incorporation, reﬂecting the
presence of sulfur-containing functional groups speciﬁc to
the drug. The co-localization of the strong Cu and S signals
together validated the effective integration of the prodrug
into the COF-31 structure. As shown in Figure 3h,i, the high-
resolution X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra
of the Cu 2p region conﬁrmed the successful incorporation
of the copper-based prodrug into the COF-31 framework.
Distinct peaks at ∼932.5 eV (Cu 2p3/2) and ∼952.3 eV
(Cu 2p1/2) indicated the presence of both Cu+ and Cu2+
oxidation states, with an additional peak at ∼934.8 eV and
characteristic shake-up satellite features, further conﬁrming
partial Cu2+ coordination. The survey spectrum also revealed
sulfur (S 2p) peaks, supporting the incorporation of sulfur-
containing prodrug moieties, and reinforcing the structural
and chemical integration within COF-31. The coexistence
of Cu+/Cu2+ suggested that the prodrug retained its redox
activity post-loading, ensuring its stability and potential
therapeutic efﬁcacy. Collectively, these results demonstrated
that COF-31@P retained its structural integrity and exhibited
uniform prodrug loading, establishing a strong foundation for
its potential therapeutic applications. The in vitro prodrug
loading capacity of COF-31 was evaluated using ﬂuorescence
analysis (Figure 3j). The results indicated that the prodrug
loading efﬁciency of COF-31 was approximately 15% w/w.
Atomic absorption spectroscopy was used to measure the
Cu concentration in the supernatant after 6 h of preloading
with 3 mg of COF-31. The loading capacity after 6 h was
calculated as 0.665 mg, with an encapsulation efﬁciency of
11.1% (Figure S17).
The release behavior of the prodrug was assessed by mea-
suring copper ion concentrations using inductively coupled
plasma (ICP). COF-31@P was exposed to different GSH
concentrations, and copper ion levels were recorded after
varying reaction times. As shown in Figure S18, prolonged
GSH exposure and higher GSH concentrations gradually
increased copper ion levels, indicating that the prodrug
releases its active components in response to GSH. To
evaluate copper ion release following prodrug activation,
we conducted ICP-MS analysis under conditions of elevated
H2O2, acidic pH, and GSH presence. Cu release was most
pronounced under acidic pH conditions, with a higher
release observed under GSH conditions compared to that
under H2O2 conditions (Figure S19). We evaluated the nano
prodrug COF-31@P in H2O to assess its ability to generate
•OH. Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) can be oxidized by •OH,
and the resulting oxidation product can be quantiﬁed by
measuring its absorbance at a speciﬁc wavelength. This allows
for the analysis of •OH content within a sample. TMB was
used to detect the ability of COF-31@P at different concen-
trations to react with H2O2 to generate •OH. The absorbance
results showed that the generation of •OH increased with the
increase of COF-31@P concentration (Figure S20).
To investigate the cellular internalization of COF-31 and
COF-31@P, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was
performed on CT26 cells. The ﬂuorescence intensity of COF-
31@P was higher than that of free COF-31, peaking at
4 h, indicating enhanced cellular uptake and accumulation
(Figure 4a). Speciﬁcally, after 4 h of incubation, the ﬂuo-
rescence intensity of COF-31@P was 1.37 times higher than
that of free COF-31 (Figure 4b). This enhanced uptake may
be attributed to the lack of regulatory effects after drug
release in the free COF, whereas the drug-loaded COF, upon
releasing the prodrug, may exert cytotoxic effects on tumor
tissue, leading to the formation of additional pores. These
structural changes could further enhance the subsequent
penetration and accumulation of COF-31@P, establishing a
positive feedback loop. Moreover, COF-31@P was incubated
with CT26 cells at a concentration of 100 µg mL−1 for 4
h. The Cu content was then analyzed by ICP spectroscopy,
showing a total uptake efﬁciency of 28.01% (Figure S21). To
investigate the cellular uptake pathways of COF-31@P, we
employed chlorpromazine as a clathrin inhibitor and Dyna-
sore as a caveolin inhibitor. Confocal imaging demonstrated
that the ﬂuorescence intensity in the chlorpromazine-treated
group was markedly reduced compared to other groups,
indicating a signiﬁcant decrease in COF-31@P uptake. These
results suggest that COF-31@P primarily enters cells through
clathrin-mediated endocytosis (Figure S22). To investigate
the intracellular distribution of COF-31@P in cancer cells,
co-staining experiments were conducted using CT26 cells
and commercially available organelle dyes such as Mito-

exhibited a speciﬁc accumulation within the mitochondria

biocompatibility and biosafety in both in vitro and in vivo
models, along with favorable pharmacokinetic properties;
these attributes strongly support its potential application in
CDT and precision cancer treatment.

delivery platform for catalase inhibitors and copper com-

particle size of approximately 180 nm, uniform morphology,
a dense core structure, and ordered macroporous channels
exhibiting two-dimensional layered structural characteristics.
In vitro experiments demonstrated that the disulﬁde bonds
within the prodrug can be efﬁciently cleaved by GSH, releas-
ing copper complexes with Fenton-like reactivity and catalase
inhibitors to suppress H2O2 consumption. Cellular experi-
ments further conﬁrmed that COF-31@P exhibits exceptional
stability, outstanding biocompatibility, and precise mitochon-
drial targeting. Upon mitochondrial targeting, COF-31@P
effectively releases the prodrug. The GSH-triggered release
of the prodrug enables the copper complex to react with H2O2
via a Fenton-like reaction, thereby enhancing therapeutic
efﬁcacy. Additionally, the catalase inhibitor (3-AT) sustains
elevated H2O2 levels, further promoting ROS (•OH) gener-
ation and increasing oxidative stress, ultimately threatening
tumor cell survival. In vivo validation of COF-31@P in CT26
tumor-bearing mice demonstrated its tumor accumulation via
the EPR effect, as well as its therapeutic efﬁcacy through
effective CDT. Moreover, COFs demonstrate signiﬁcant
advantages over metal-organic frameworks, polymer micelles,
and inorganic nanoenzymes in the context of CDT, owing to
their tunable pore structures, high speciﬁc surface areas, excel-
lent biocompatibility, and favorable electronic conductivity
(Table S1). COF-31@P incorporates mitochondrial targeting
and enables the synergistic generation of multiple hydroxyl
radicals. This strategy effectively addresses the key limitations
of conventional CDT, including low catalytic efﬁciency, TME
barriers, and the insufﬁciency of single-mode treatment.
These ﬁndings highlight the potential of COF-based delivery
systems to improve CDT efﬁcacy and advance precision
cancer treatment.

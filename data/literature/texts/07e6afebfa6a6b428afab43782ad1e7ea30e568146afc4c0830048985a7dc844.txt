Ultrasmall silver nanoclusters (Ag NCs) are one of the emerging and highly efﬁcient antibacterial agents,
owing to the unique features of sub-2 nm particle size and the high abundance of the active Ag+ species.
However, practical applications of Ag NCs in biological environment are often hampered by silver oxi-
dization, which results in particle aggregation and loss of antibacterial activity. In this study, for the ﬁrst
time, we develop a facile method to synthesize highly dispersed Ag NCs decorated mesoporous silica
nanoparticles (Ag NC-MSNs) capable of long-term and efﬁcient release of Ag+ ions. This novel Ag NC-
MSNs nanocomposite was demonstrated as an effective antibacterial agent against both Gram-positive
and Gram-negative pathogenic bacteria. Compared with the counterparts Ag NCs and silver nanoparticles
decorated mesoporous silica nanoparticles (Ag NP-MSNs), Ag NC-MSNs exhibit 17-fold and 27-fold
enhancement in antibacterial potency, respectively. The homogeneous distribution of ultrasmall Ag
NCs in the mesoporous architecture of supporting MSNs matrix is crucial for the controlled release of
Ag+ ions, leading to the superior broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity. Moreover, the cytotoxicity assay
indicated that the effective antibacterial concentration of Ag NC-MSNs shows minimum toxicity on mam-
malian cells. This new Ag nanocomposite developed in this work is promising for practical applications
against various microbial infections.

turies attributed to its strong antiseptic effect [1,2]. However, the
direct use of Ag+ ions and their complexes as antimicrobial agents

mammalian cells associated with the silver-mediated oxidative
stress [3,4]. The innovation in nanotechnology allows the synthesis
of smaller scale silver nanomaterials in higher precision. The resul-
tant silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) maintain a sufﬁcient reservoir of
Ag+ ions for bactericidal effect with reduced toxicity on human
cells through controlled release of Ag+ ions [5,6].
Recently, it become technically feasible to further enhance the
antimicrobial activity through reducing the size of Ag NPs to a
value comparable to the Fermi wavelength of electrons (1 nm),
producing the next generation Ag-based antimicrobial agents,
ultrasmall Ag nanoclusters (NCs) [7]. The higher surface area to
volume ratio of Ag NCs enables the acceleration of Ag+ ions release
[8]. With comparable size as subcellular organelles, Ag NCs are
capable to internalize the cellular membrane and directly interact
with the subcellular organelles of pathogenic microorganism
[9,10]. In addition, Ag NCs possess higher local concentration of
Ag+ ions assembled on the surface [11,12]. Overall, these appealing
physicochemical features of Ag NCs would potentially boost their
bactericidal performance [13].
Nevertheless, Ag NCs are reactive and unstable in physiological
conditions. Practical applications of Ag NCs are often hampered by
oxidization [14], which resulted in aggregation and loss of antibac-
terial activity. To solve this problem, Ag NCs could be designed to
be encapsulated inside or doped into other drug carriers to achieve
long-term antimicrobial efﬁciency. Among which, the Ag NCs-SiO2
composite has intrigued considerable attention owing to the excel-
lent biocompatibility of silica nanomaterials. Forming core-shell or
surface dispersed structures are two major designs of Ag NCs-SiO2
antibacterial agents [15]. Although the core-shell Ag NCs-SiO2
facilitates a steady release of silver ions from Ag NCs core, the
antibacterial ability of the nanocomposites is unsatisfactory due
to its inefﬁcient Ag+ ion release across the SiO2 shell. On the con-
trary, directly depositing of Ag NCs on the surface of silica nanopar-
ticles enables a high Ag+ ion release rate, but the Ag NCs are easily
consumed by weak binding or chemical erosion in the biological
media [16], resulting in a rapid decrease of antibacterial activity.
Therefore, for practical antibacterial application, it is imperative
to design a new structure of Ag NCs-SiO2 capable of efﬁcient
release of Ag+ ions for long-term.
Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) are popular carriers for
controlled release of various therapeutic agents attributed to their
large surface area of the ordered pore structure and good biocom-
patibility [17–19]. Herein, MSNs were adopted as the support for
anchoring Ag NCs. Ag NCs decorated mesoporous silica nanoparti-
cles (Ag NC-MSNs) were prepared by a one-pot synthesis. The
homogeneous dispersion of ultrasmall Ag NCs in the whole frame-
work of MSNs effectively prevented the aggregation of Ag NCs and
promoted the continuous release of Ag+ ions. By the merits of Ag
NC-MSNs structure, Ag NCs resituated on the surface ensures a rea-
sonable Ag+ ion release rate thus a high antibacterial activity, while
the Ag NCs embedded at the pores of MSNs serves as a reservoir of
the Ag+, endowing the antibacterial activity sustainable. Further-
more, the mesoporous structure would facilitate the adsorption
and interaction of bacteria with the Ag NC-MSNs.

2.2. Synthesis of silver nanoclusters (Ag NCs)
An aqueous solution of NaBH4 (112 mM) was freshly prepared
by dissolving 43 mg of NaBH4 in 2 mL of 1 M NaOH solution, fol-
lowed by the addition of 8 mL of ultrapure water. The addition of
NaOH improves the stability of borohydride ions against hydroly-
sis. The aqueous solutions of AgNO3 (0.5 mL, 20 mM) and GSH
(1 mL, 20 mM) were mixed in water (18.3 mL) under vigorous stir-
ring to form thiolate–AgI complexes, followed by the addition of an
aqueous solution of NaBH4 (200 lL, 112 mM). The mixture was
then stirred at room temperature for 5 h until the formation of a
brown Ag NC solution. The Ag NCs were collected without further
puriﬁcation and stored at 4 C for further characterization.
2.3. Synthesis of silver nanocluster decorated mesoporous silica
nanoparticles (Ag NC-MSNs)
Ag NC-MSNs were synthesized according to a modiﬁed Stöber
method. CTAB (73.3 mg), KH2PO4 (228.7 mg), NaOH (38.7 mg),
and an aqueous solution of the as-synthesized Ag NC solution
(20 mL, 0.5 mM, on the basis of Ag atoms) were added in a
100 mL round bottom ﬂask under stirring at 40 C. The mixture
was stirred for 15 min until homogeneous. TEOS (356 lL) was
added dropwise, and the reaction mixture was stirred at 40 C for
12 h. The crude product was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for
15 min. The precipitated Ag NC-MSNs were washed with excess
amount of water, and dried under vacuum at 60 C for 4 h. To
remove the surfactants from the pores, the precipitated Ag NC-
MSNs were dispersed in NH4NO3 ethanol solution (32 mg NH4NO3
in 12 mL 95% ethanol) and heated at 60 C for 60 min with vigorous
stirring. The ion-exchange process was repeated three times, fol-
lowed by thoroughly rinse with ethanol and ultrapure water. The
products were then dried under vacuum at 60 C for 4 h and stored
at room temperature for further use.

2.5. Bacterial inhibition assay on LB agar plates
The Ag NC-MSNs (1000 lg/mL) were mixed with molten LB
agar. Serial dilution (1/102, 100 lL) of the cultured bacteria from
a single colony (OD600 = 0.6) were plated onto solidiﬁed Ag NC-
MSN agar plates and incubated at 37 C for 24 h. The LB agar plates
prepared with MSNs, Ag NCs, or ultrapure water, were used as the
control groups. Each condition was prepared in triplicate.
2.6. Bacterial inhibition assay in culture
1.8 mL of the bacterial culture (OD600 = 0.06, corresponding to
1  106–2.5  106 CFU/mL) was added in a sterilized round bottom
tube, followed by the addition of 0.2 mL Ag NC-MSN suspensions in
different concentrations (200, 1000, 5000, 10,000 lg/mL) or the
control groups (500 lM Ag NCs or ultrapure water). The bacterial
suspension was incubated at 37 C with continuous agitation at
150 rpm. Bacterial concentration of the culture was determined
every 2 h using a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800) to mea-
sure OD600. Each condition was prepared in triplicate, and all
experiments were repeated three times.
Plate count method was used to estimate bacterial population.
In brief, after 12 h incubation in the above medium, serial dilutions
from the bacterial suspension (1, 102, 104, 106, 108) were pre-
pared and 100 mL aliquot was plated on LB agar. The bacterial colo-
nies formed after 24 h incubation at 37 C were then counted and
were expressed as the colony forming unit per mL (CFU/mL). Each
condition was prepared in duplicate.
2.7. Characterization of bacteria after treatment with Ag NC-MSNs
The bacterial morphology after treatment with Ag NC-MSNs
was examined by scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-
6700F, JOEL, Japan). In brief, the Ag NC-MSNs treated bacteria were
ﬁxed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.01 M PBS for 6 h at 4 C, and
then washed with 0.01 M PBS. 0.1% OsO4 in 0.01 M PBS was added
to the samples for 2 h incubation at room temperature as post-
ﬁxation, and subsequently removed by rinse with 0.01 M PBS.
The samples were dehydrated in an ascending ethanol gradient
(25%, 50%, 70%, 85%, 95%, and 100%) for every 10 min. The dehy-
drated samples were incubated in t-butanol for 20 min, followed
by lyophilization on a polished silicon substrate using a freeze-
drier (Scientz-12N, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology, China). The
freeze-dried samples were then examined by SEM, operated at
an accelerating voltage of 8 kV.
2.8. Analysis of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS)
Brieﬂy, 10 mM DCFH-DA and 1 mg/mL Hoechst 33342 were
added to the bacterial solution for 15 min incubation at 37 C,
200 rpm. Afterwards, the bacterial cells were isolated by centrifu-
gation at 10,000 rpm for 2 min, followed by washing with PBS solu-
tion for three times. The cell pellet was resuspended in an equal
volume of PBS solution. The concentration of DCF was measured
by Tecan Spark multimode microplate reader at excitation/emis-
sion wavelength of 488/525 nm, as the indication of cellular ROS
level. The ROS level was normalized to the total cell number, which
was determined from the ﬂuorescence intensity of Hoechst 33342
at excitation/emission wavelength of 350/461 nm. The relative ROS
level was calculated by normalizing the ROS/cell of the treated
groups with the ROS/cell of the untreated group. All experiments
were repeated three times.
2.9. Evaluation of cytotoxicity
The in vitro cytotoxicity was evaluated by cell proliferation kit
(MTS). The HeLa cells were purchased from American Type Culture
Collection (ATCC, USA). HeLa cells were seeded into a 96-well plate
at a concentration of 1  104 cells/well in 100 lL DMEM culture
medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 lg /mL penicillin, and
100 lg/mL streptomycin. After 24-hour culture at the standard
condition (37 C, 5% CO2), the culture media was replaced with
new media containing 20, 100, and 1000 lg/mL Ag NC-MSNs,
50 lM Ag NCs, 50 lg/mL AgNO3, and 1000 lg/mL MSNs to incu-
bate for 12 – 48 h. HeLa cells without any treatment were used
as the negative control. At the selected time point, the media
was replaced with MTS solution for 4 h incubation at 37 C. The
absorbance at 490 nm was measured by Tecan Spark multimode
microplate reader. Each condition was prepared in ﬁve replicates
and the experiment was repeated three times. The results were
expressed as the mean percentage of cell viability relative to the
negative control. Additionally, the interference of Ag NCs to the
MTS assay measurement was evaluated (Table S3). The absorbance
at 490 nm of the Ag NC-MSN solution was as low as the blank con-
trol (Abs490 nm < 0.06).
Cytotoxicity of Ag NC-MSNs was also evaluated by LIVE/DEAD
viability/cytotoxicity kit following the manufacturer protocol.
Brieﬂy, HeLa cells were incubated for 12 h in a 96-well plate. Stain-
ing was performed by adding 150 lL staining solution contained
calcein acetoxymethyl ester (2.0 lM) and ethidium homodimer
(4.0 lM). Samples were imaged by an Olympus IX73 ﬂuorescence
microscope (Olympus, Japan) under 100  magniﬁcation, with
excitation and emission wavelengths of 494/517 nm for calcein
acetoxymethyl ester and 528/617 nm for ethidium homodimer.
All images were analyzed by cellSens data analysis software
(Olympus, Japan).
2.10. In vitro degradation and Ag release assay
Ag NC-MSNs were suspended in LB (pH = 7.0) or ultrapure
water (18.2 MX cm) at a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL and were
incubated at 37 C for 7 days without agitation. The suspension
was centrifuged and the supernatant was carefully collected daily.
An aliquot of the supernatant was ﬁltered through 0.22 mm PTFE
ﬁlter and the Ag and Si content were analyzed by inductively cou-
pled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7700 ICP-MS,
USA). The precipitated particles were resuspended in fresh LB or
ultrapure water for continuous incubation. This sampling process
was repeated for 7 days and each sample was prepared in
triplicate.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Characterization of Ag NCs
As the pore size of MSN is commonly below 10 nm, the desired
ultrasmall Ag NP to be incorporated into MSN should be smaller
than 2 nm [20,21], which are typically termed nanoclusters (NCs)
[15]. The well-developed glutathione (GSH)-protected Ag NCs were
used in this study [16]. GSH is a naturally occurring tripeptide with
a thiol group in the cysteine residue, which is capable to serve as a
protecting ligand in the synthesis of Ag NCs [22]. The GSH–Ag+–R
NCs were synthesized by a cyclic NaBH4 reduction–decomposition
method [23]. Brieﬂy, AgNO3 and GSH were mixed together to form
GSH–Ag+ complexes, followed by NaBH4 reduction–decomposition
as well as a size-focusing process for 12 h at room temperature.
The size of the as-synthesized GSH protected Ag NCs was deter-
mined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis to be

3.2. Characterization of Ag NC-MSNs
Ag NC-MSNs were prepared by an in situ one-pot synthesis
(Scheme 1) [25]. The as-synthesized Ag NCs were ﬁrst mixed with
the surfactant CTAB as a template to form CTAB–Ag NCs micelles.
Afterwards, TEOS was added and these micelles began to aggregate
into larger nanospheres. In order to eliminate the cytotoxic CTAB,
the crude product was extracted by ammonium nitrate ethanol
solution and washed thoroughly to yield the puriﬁed Ag NC-
MSNs [26].
The morphology of Ag NC-MSNs was revealed through TEM
analysis (Fig. 2a and 2b). The synthesized Ag NC-MSNs were spher-
ical particles with 61.1 ± 7.9 nm in diameter (Fig. 2a inset), and the
diameter of the supported Ag NCs was 2.0 ± 0.7 nm (Fig. S2). The
dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis showed that the Ag NC-
MSN size distribution was centered at 76.8 ± 4.7 nm (Fig. S5). The
diameter of hydrated particles was 20% larger than the diameter
determined from TEM analysis. The energy dispersive X-ray
(EDX) elemental mapping (Fig. 2c–e) of Ag NC-MSNs conﬁrmed
the presence of Ag and demonstrated the homogeneous Ag distri-
bution in the composite. Inductively coupled plasma mass spec-
trometry (ICP-MS) analysis provided quantitative evidence that
the Ag content in Ag NC-MSNs is about 0.5 wt%.
The porous structures of Ag NC-MSNs were further conﬁrmed
by nitrogen adsorption and desorption isotherms (Fig. 3), which
can be classiﬁed as type IV isotherms with type H4 hysteresis loop
according to the IUPAC nomenclature. It represents a characteristic
of mixed micro- and meso-porous structure. Barrett-Joynerr-
Halenda (BJH) method was used to determine the pore size from
the adsorption branch of the isotherm. The corresponding pore size
distribution curve is centered at about 2.6 nm for both MSNs and
Ag NC-MSNs, (Fig. 3 inset) which corroborates with the TEM
results. The pore formation was owed to the CTAB template
removal. The Brumauer-Emmet-Teller (BET) speciﬁc surface area
of the Ag NC-MSNs was 697 m2/g, and the respective single point
adsorption total pore volume was 0.55 cm3/g, which was estimated
from the single-point absorption at P/P0 = 0.99. Both speciﬁc sur-
face area and pore volume of Ag NC-MSNs are slightly higher than
those of MSNs (632 m2/g, 0.43 cm3/g, Fig. 3), which suggested that
the incorporation of Ag NCs promoted the formation of both silica
mesopores and Ag NC micropores in the interior of the nanocom-
posite. Remarkably, the enhanced porosity may facilitate the acces-
sibility of the interior Ag NCs in the nanocomposite and the
efﬁcient release of both Ag+ ions and Ag NCs.
3.3. Antibacterial activity
We evaluated the antibacterial efﬁcacy of this novel Ag NC-
MSNs in two most common pathogens responsible for the wound
bed infection and bioﬁlm formation, Gram-positive Staphyloccocus
aureus (S. aureus), as well as the Gram-negative Pseudomonas
aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa). To directly compare the bacterial inhibi-
tion effect, the pathogens were cultured on a Luria–Bertani (LB)

lent to 5 lg/mL Ag). As shown in Fig. 4, there is a drastic improve-
ment in the bacterial inhibition of Ag NC-MSNs LB agar plate,
particularly with complete inhibition of P. aeruginosa. LB agar
plates, and LB agar supplemented with MSNs (1000 lg/mL) or Ag
NCs (50 lM, equivalent to 5 lg/mL Ag) were used as controls.
Although the MSNs agar plates and Ag NCs agar plates contained
the equivalent amount of silica and Ag, respectively, they exhibited

3.4. Antibacterial mechanisms of Ag NC-MSNs
Considering that the Ag-free MSNs show no inhibitory effect on
bacterial growth (Fig. 4 and Fig. S6), the antibacterial activity of Ag
NC-MSNs is closely related to Ag species and is primarily driven by
the reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. DCFH-DA was
applied to test the cellular ROS concentration, which could be con-
verted to DCFH by the cellular esterases. In the presence of ROS,
DCFH would be further oxidized into ﬂuorescent DCF (20,70-dichlor
oﬂuorescein). The level of the cellular ROS in the S. aureus and P.
aeruginosa population after exposure to Ag NC-MSNs was mea-
sured. As shown in Fig. 6, it was found that Ag NC-MSNs, from
20 lg/mL to 1000 lg/mL, induced 1.1–7.5-fold increase in the cel-
lular ROS level in both two bacterial populations. The elevated
level of ROS induced by Ag NC-MSNs could contribute to the excel-
lent antimicrobial activity shown in Fig. 5. Moreover, the formation
of Ag NC-MSNs and bacteria aggregation was visually observed for
both S. aureus (Fig. 7a inset) and P. aeruginosa (Fig. 7b inset). Thus,

Tian et al. [18], we measured the zeta potentials of Ag NC-MSNs
and bacteria (Fig. S4). The results show that all the surfaces were
negatively
charged,
ruling
out
the
electrostatic
interaction
mechanism. Since the mesoporous structure may support the efﬁcient
release of Ag+ ions, the Ag+ ions release proﬁle from Ag NC-MSNs
was investigated (Fig. S7). Notably, Ag NC-MSNs exhibited a con-
tinuously cumulative release of Ag+ ions (solid curves) for up to
15 days and the daily release of Ag+ ions (dashed curves) remained
on a high level until day 7. A faster silver release rate was observed
at a higher concentration of Ag NC-MSNs (0.2 mg/mL, black curve)
compared to a lower concentration of Ag NC-MSNs (0.1 mg/mL,
blue curve), with a 1.7-fold increase in the cumulative release
amount of Ag+ ions.
Based on these results, the improved antimicrobial effect of Ag
NC-MSNs could be attributed to the following unique features: (1)
the ultrasmall size and high surface area to volume ratio of Ag NCs

3.5. Cytotoxicity evaluation
Although silica materials are proven to be biocompatible and
widely used as biomedical materials [33], silver salts or nanoparti-
cles were reported to damage the cellular mitochondria and DNA
through promoting ROS formation, leading to cell death [34].
Herein, the in vitro cytotoxicity of the Ag NC-MSNs was evaluated
on HeLa cells using a MTS assay. The degree of cytotoxicity is
related to the Ag NC-MSNs concentration and the treatment time.
As showed in Fig. 9a, the effective antibacterial concentration of Ag
NC-MSNs (100 lg/mL) showed no obvious cytotoxicity within
12 h, which is similar to that of MSNs. However, the elevated con-
centration of Ag NC-MSNs to 1000 lg/mL (corresponding to 5 lg/
mL Ag) reduce the cell viability to 50%. The prolonged incubation
also reduced the cell viability to 60% for 24 h and <40% for 48 h
(100 lg/mL Ag NC-MSNs). It appeared that 20 lg/mL Ag NC-
MSNs did not induce cytotoxicity within 48 h. In comparison, Ag
NCs and AgNO3 containing the equivalent amount of silver showed
similar or higher cytotoxicity. The viability of cells treated with Ag
NCs and AgNO3 was decreased to 75% and 16% within 12 h, respec-
tively. After 48 h incubation, the cell viability further dropped to
32% and 15%, respectively. These results suggested the reduced
cytotoxicity of Ag NC-MSNs compared to commercial Ag NCs or
AgNO3.
LIVE/DEAD viability assay was also used to evaluate the cyto-
toxicity on HeLa cells. In this assay, the cell-impermeant ethdium
homodimer-1 selectively stained the non-viable cells with dam-
aged cell membrane (red color), and the cell-permeable calcein
acetomethoxy reacted with cellular esterases to generate green ﬂu-
orescence in live cells. Fig. 8b shows the co-existence of live and
dead HeLa cells when treated with Ag NC-MSNs (1000 lg/mL,
equivalent to 5 lg/mL Ag). In Fig. 8c–8e and 8 g, HeLa cells
remained mostly alive after treated with Ag NC-MSNs (100
lg/mL, equivalent to 500 ng/mL Ag), MSNs (1000 lg/mL), and Ag
NCs (50 lM, equivalent to 5 lg/mL Ag), similar to the control
supplemented with no drugs. In contrast, no HeLa cells survived
after treated with AgNO3 (8 lg/mL), as shown in Fig. 8f.
3.6. In vitro degradation assay
An in vitro degradation assay was performed to investigate the
stability of Ag NC-MSNs in the biological media [35,36]. The as-
prepared Ag NC-MSN particles were incubated in LB (pH = 7.0) or
ultrapure water (18.2 MX cm) for up to 7 days. The soluble silica
degradents in the supernatant is the indicator of the nanocompos-
ite degradation, which was quantiﬁed by ICP-MS. As showed in the
cumulative degradation proﬁle, the nanocomposite undergoes
graduate degradation in both media. The total degradation is more
pronounced in LB (Fig. 9a, blue curve), reaching the maximum
amount of 29Si wt% at day 7, compared to the 11Si wt% degrada-
tion in ultrapure water at day 7. Consistently, the Ag+ ions release
in LB (Fig. 9b, blue curve) was 1.85-fold higher than that in ultra-
pure water (Fig. 9b, black curve). It indicated that the stability of
Ag NC-MSNs and the release of Ag+ ions were affected by the bio-
logical media.
4. Conclusions
In conclusion, we describe for the ﬁrst time the synthesis of
sub-2 nm
silver
nanoclusters
decorated
mesoporous
silica
nanoparticles (Ag NC-MSNs) and demonstrate their exceptional
antimicrobial activity against both Gram-positive (S. aureus) and
Gram-negative (P. aeruginosa) pathogenic bacteria. Remarkably,
the effective antimicrobial silver concentration in Ag NC-MSNs is
reduced to the range of nano-gram per milli-liter, which is more
than one order of magnitude lower than their larger counterpart
of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) (>2 nm) decorated MSNs. The
improved antimicrobial activity is largely attributed to (1) ultra-
small Ag NCs are well-dispersed in the framework of supporting
matrix MSNs, which protect Ag NCs from aggregation and modu-
late the continuous Ag+ ions release from the MSN vessels; (2)
the mesoporous structure of this nanocomposite generates high
afﬁnity of Ag NC-MSNs to the bacterial surfaces that substantially
promotes Ag-bacterial interaction; (3) the enhanced ROS produc-
tion by Ag+ ions and Ag NCs induces destructive oxidative stress
to the bacterial cells. In addition, this novel nanocomposite shows
acceptable
biocompatibility
on
mammalian
cells
and
was
biodegradable in biological media. This study demonstrated the
importance of proper protective carrier in stabilizing silver nan-
oclusters and improving the antimicrobial activity. The hybrid sil-
ver nanocomposite reported here will be a promising candidate as
antimicrobial coating and therapeutic.

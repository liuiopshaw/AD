A B S T R A C T
The poor mechanics and functionality of natural-polymer hydrogels from gellan gum (GG) prohibit their prac­
tical application, despite the intrinsic thermo-reversible gelation nature, structural and quality consistency, 
biocompatibility, biodegradability and sustainability of microbial fermentation-produced GG. Herein, a dual- 
reinforcing strategy, i.e., ionic-crosslinking along with rigid nano-particulate reinforcing, was realized to 
modify pristine GG hydrogels for the first time during the in-situ formation of zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 
(ZIF-8). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, rheology and X-ray 
diffraction confirm the synchronous formation of ZIF-8 and ionic bonds between Zn2+ and –COO−of GG mol­
ecules. The mechanics of the final hydrogels are far superior to GG hydrogel, with the maximum compressive and 
tensile strength attaining 1.17 MPa and 592.2 kPa, ~4 times and ~ 66 times respectively of the corresponding 
GG hydrogel. Especially, the obtained hydrogels display selective antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus 
aureus as a modeling Gram positive strain. Whereas, the hydrogels retain good biocompatibility despite the 
introduction of ZIF-8 and Zn2+ crosslinks, with the cell viability (NIH3T3 cells as the model) >79 % after 48 h' 
cultivation in either the hydrogels or gel extracts. The excellent properties suggest them tremendous application 
prospects as biomedical materials, instrumental in averting drug-resistant problems.
1. Introduction
Hydrogels are wet and soft but macroscopically nonflowing mate­
rials, bearing a crosslinked three-dimension network skeleton of hy­
drophilic polymers imbibing copious water inside the network. With the 
water-rich nature and three-dimensional network structure, hydrogels 
show much similarity to biological tissues. They have been widely 
applied in many fields like drug delivery (B. Liu & Chen, 2024), tissue 
engineering (Hu, Zeng, Jiang, Kong, & Zhu, 2024), wound dressing (Y. 
Liang, He, & Guo, 2021), contact lenses (Saptaji, Iza, Widianingrum, 
Mulia, & Setiawan, 2021), and are extending to many new realms like 
flexible sensing, energy storage and production (Fu et al., 2024; Shi 
et al., 2024). There are two essential types of polymer hydrogels ac­
cording to the substrates employed in gel manufacture, i.e., synthetic- 
polymer hydrogels and natural-polymer hydrogels (Bashir et al., 2020).
Synthetic-polymer hydrogels, such as polyacrylamide, poly(acrylic 
acid), poly(2-hydroxylethyl methacrylate), poly(vinyl alcohol) and poly 
(ethylene glycol) are manufactured through either crosslinking poly­
merization of hydrophilic monomers or direct cross-linking of hydro­
philic polymers. They are facile to achieve high mechanics and 
functionalities through delicate selection of the monomers or polymers, 
elaborate engineering and tuning of the network structure and meticu­
lous controlling of the crosslinking methods, thus received rapid 
development in the past 20 years (Magno, Meinhardt, & Werner, 2020). 
Nonetheless, the hydrophilic monomers/polymers for synthetic- 
polymer hydrogels are originated from nonrenewable fossil resources 
like petroleum. And most synthetic-polymer hydrogels are non- 
biocompatible and difficult to biodegrade after end use, deviating 
from the green and sustainable development opinion. In contrast, 
hydrogels based on natural polymers derived from plants, animals or

development, natural-polymer hydrogels are more advantageous over 
synthetic-polymer hydrogels, and are receiving much more attention 
than ever before (Gul et al., 2022). Natural polymers such as cellulose, 
gelatin, chitosan, sodium alginate, hyaluronic acid, gellan gum and so 
on (Bhaladhare & Das, 2022; Luo et al., 2023; Lv et al., 2023; Mushtaq 
et al., 2022; Tan et al., 2023) are commonly-employed matrixes of 
hydrogels. Among natural polymers, extracellular polysaccharides pro­
duced via microbial fermentation are superior to those originated from 
animals and plants, because of their controllable production processes, 
short production cycles, and consistent product quality (Giavasis, Har­
vey, & McNeil, 2000), wherein gellan gum (GG) is a favorable candidate 
for hydrogels' substrate owing to the inherent thermo-reversible gelation 
behavior.
GG is a linear anionic polysaccharide produced through the Sphin­
gomonas elodea fermentation, with tetra-saccharide repeating units 
composed of β-(1 → 3)-D-glucose, β-(1 → 4)-D-glucuronic acid, β-(1 → 
4)-D-glucose and α-(1 → 4)-L-rhamnose (Gomes, Batista-Silva, Sousa, & 
Passarinha, 2023). In its native form, there is one glycerate per repeating 
unit and one acetyl per every two repeat units, appearing on the β-(1 → 
3)-D-glucose residues at the O(6) and O(2) positions respectively (Zia 
et al., 2018). Native GG is also known as high-acyl GG, the deester­
ification of which, i.e., removal of acyl groups by means of hot alkali 
treatment, results in low-acyl GG. Aqueous dispersions of high/low-acyl 
GG undergo thermo-reversible sol-gel transformation, wherein heating 
and cooling of the GG dispersion contributes to the formation of fibrils as 
neighboring molecules form double helices, which aggregate into a 
three-dimensional structure, forming a gel. Even though the acyl groups 
do not interfere with helix formation, the glycerate groups intervene 
with the helix aggregation. Consequently, native GG yields a soft and 
elastic hydrogel whereas deacylated GG yields a hard and brittle one. GG 
has been approved as the gelation and thickening agents in food and 
pharmaceuticals. However, the wide application of GG hydrogels is 
restricted severely due to the poor mechanics and insufficiency of 
functionality, despite the inherent gelation behavior, greenness and 
sustainability. Endeavors have been made to improve the performance 
of GG hydrogels via tactics like compositing with other components and 
chemical modification, to extend their application in tissue engineering 
(Wu, Xiao, Wu, & Xu, 2024), wound dressing (Ng et al., 2021), water 
treatment (Njuguna & Schoenherr, 2024), flexible electronics (P. Li 
et al., 2024; C. Wang, Jiang, Ji, Xing, & Ma, 2024) and so on. For 
instance, Kim et al. (Kim et al., 2019) prepared GG/hyaluronic acid 
hydrogels with effective regeneration promotion of chondrocytes, which 
show great application potential in cartilage tissue engineering. Zheng 
et al. (Zheng et al., 2018) reported injectable hydrogels with antibac­
terial property and cell adhesion exploiting GG, gelatin and tannic acid, 
potential as wound dressings. Liang et al. (L. Liang et al., 2024) prepared 
GG/chitosan/Ca2+ multi-network hydrogels with good mechanics (1 
MPa of compression strength) and potential application prospect in 
water treatment (the uranium adsorption capacity reaches 180 mg/g). 
Nevertheless, there are still great challenges in developing functional GG 
hydrogels with high mechanics till now.
Recently, metal organic frameworks (MOFs), an emerging type of 
nanocrystalline materials with porous structures, are receiving much 
attention in hydrogels (H. Liu, Peng, Xin, & Zhang, 2019; Y. Yang et al., 
2021). MOFs are self-assembled through metal ions (Zn2+, Cu2+, Ag+, 
Fe3+, Co2+, Ni2+, etc.) or metal clusters with organic multidentate li­
gands (like carboxyl, hydroxyl and nitrogen-containing ligands). MOFs 
possess splendid characteristics such as high porosity, tunable porous 
structure, large specific surface area, controllable physical and chemical 
properties, easily functionalized surfaces and so forth (Kirchon, Feng, 
Drake, Joseph, & Zhou, 2018). Owing to the excellent features, MOFs 
show intense potential in gas storage, separation and adsorption (Assen, 
Adil, & Belmabkhout, 2024; Tehrani & Chehrazi, 2024), drug delivery 
(Lawson, Walton, & Chan, 2021), catalysis (Ding, Li, Chen, & Liao, 
2024), energy production and storage (Chettiannan, Dhandapani, Aru­
mugam, Rajendran, & Selvaraj, 2024; Gupta & Puri, 2024), bacterial 
killing (Guo et al., 2024; L. Yang et al., 2024) and some other fields (L. 
Wang et al., 2022). Notwithstanding the obvious advantages, the pow­
der form of MOFs limits their widespread application, necessitating the 
developing of MOF-containing composite materials. The coupling of 
MOFs with hydrogels can combine the excellent functional character­
istics of both MOFs and hydrogels. Additionally, the interaction between 
central metals (or metal clusters) with the hydrophilic functional groups 
of hydrogel matrix can conduce to improving the mechanical perfor­
mance of the hydrogels (Duan et al., 2020; H. Liu et al., 2019). Thus, 
MOF-hydrogel composite materials become a rising star (Lim et al., 
2023; W. Sun et al., 2023).
Zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8), the most prestigious 
representative of zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs, a prominent 
subset of MOFs), has garnered significant attention, owing to the 
intriguing structural characteristics of both zeolites and MOFs. ZIF-8 is 
formed through coordination self-assembling of Zn2+ and 2-methylimi­
dazole under different conditions. The potential application of ZIF-8 has 
been widely explored, wherein biomedical application is a major 
concern (Abdelhamid, 2021). ZIF-8 has been validated an ideal carrier 
for drug delivery owing to the tunable structure, low or non-toxicity, 
excellent biocompatibility, high chemical and thermal stability, and 
pH-responsiveness (Xie et al., 2022). Moreover, it provides advanta­
geous antibacterial capability over many other MOFs, and exhibits great 
potential in other bio-applications like bone tissue engineering and 
bioimaging (D. Wang et al., 2024).
Given the functionality and bio-benignity of ZIF-8, it is probable to 
exploit ZIF-8 to improve the mechanical performance of GG hydrogels as 
well as endow them functionality. Based on the feasibility of synthe­
sizing ZIF-8 at room temperature in aqueous solution (Pan, Liu, Zeng, 
Zhao, & Lai, 2011), we contemplated to introduce ZIF-8 into GG 
hydrogels in situ rather than physical blending, whereby ZIF-8 particles 
are prone to aggregate owing to the poor dispersibility in water. It is 
conceivable that ionic bonding of Zn2+ with the carboxylate ions of GG 
molecules occurs simultaneously upon the in-situ formation of ZIF-8, 
which will promote the mechanical performance further. The forma­
tion of ZIF-8 as well as ionic bonding of Zn2+ with the carboxylate ions of 
GG molecules was confirmed by FTIR, XPS, rheology and XRD. As 
anticipated, the mechanical properties of the final hydrogels outdistance 
the pristine GG hydrogel vastly. More strikingly, incorporation of ZIF-8 
has little influence on the biocompatibility of the final hydrogels, and 
the final hydrogels exhibit selective antibacterial activity.
2. Experimental
2.1. Materials
Gellan gum (high-acyl, food grade, with number average molecular 
weight of 1–2*106 Da and degree of acylation of 0.375, Xinjiang Fufeng 
Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), 2-methylimidazole (2-MiM) and zinc acetate 
(Zn(Ac)2) (analytical reagent, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technol­
ogy Co., Ltd.), and deionized water were used for the preparation of 
hydrogels without further treatment. Luria-Bertani (LB) broth bouillon, 
agar powder, Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC25922) and Staphylococcus 
aureus (S. aureus, ATCC29213) were employed for antibacterial experi­
ments. PBS (MA0015, pH 7.4, cell culture grade, Dalian Meilunbio Co. 
Ltd., China), NIH3T3 cells and culture medium specific for NIH3T3 cell 
(CM-0171, Wuhan Procell Science and Technology Co. Ltd., China), 
CCK-8 (MA0218, Dalian Bergolin Biological Science and Technology 
Co., Ltd., China) were used for biocompatible evaluation.
2.2. Preparation of hydrogels
~20 g of GG aqueous dispersion was concocted with 2-MiM added. 
The dispersion was stirred at 90 ◦C till a homogeneous solution. Then the

employed was determined based on the optimized m2-MiM and n(2-Ml: 
Zn2+) (40 wt% and 5 respectively). The formulations for hydrogel

2.3. Measurement and characterization
2.3.1. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)
The hydrogel samples were freeze-dried in an FD-1A-50 vacuum 
freeze drier (manufactured by Beijing Boyikang Lab Instrument Co., 
Ltd.). Infrared spectrums of the dried sheet samples were recorded on a 
Fourier infrared spectrometer (Nicolet iS 50, Thermo Scientific Co., U.S. 
A). The wavenumber was scanned from 400 to 4000 cm−1 for all 
samples.
2.3.2. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy(XPS)
Elemental analysis of the freeze-dried samples was performed on an 
X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (Nexsa, Thermo Scientific Co., U.S.), 
with Al-Kα rays as the radiation source. The full spectra as well as the 
fine spectra of different elements were recorded, with a scanning step 
size of 0.1 eV for the fine spectrums.
2.3.3. X-ray diffraction (XRD)
XRD analysis of the freeze-dried samples was carried out on an X-ray 
diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker, Germany) under 40 kV voltage and 
40 mA current. The scanning angle (2θ) was ranged from 5◦to 60◦.
2.3.4. Rheological tests
The rheological behavior of different hydrogels was explored by a 
rotational rheometer (MCR302, Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK), equip­
ped with a parallel plate geometry (50 mm diameter, 1 mm gap). The 
cylindrical hydrogels were cut into slices of 1 mm thickness. Frequency 
sweeps were conducted at 25 ◦C under a shear stress of 1 Pa and a strain 
of 1 % from 1 to 100 rad⋅s−1 angular frequency. Temperature sweeps 
were performed from 25 to 90 ◦C at a constant angular frequency of 1 
rad⋅s−1 and a heating rate of 1 ◦C⋅min−1.
2.3.5. Mechanical testing
The mechanical properties were evaluated on a universal electronic 
testing machine (HRJ WDW-5T, Jinan Henruijin Testing Machine Co., 
Ltd., China), equipped with a 5000 N and a 100 N cell for compressive 
and tensile tests respectively. The compression testing was conducted on 
cylindrical specimens of ~10 mm heigh and ~13 mm in diameter. The 
cross-head was moved at 5 mm⋅min−1 during testing. The tensile testing 
was carried out on rectangular specimens with the length of ~100 mm, 
width of ~10 mm and thickness of ~2 mm. The crosshead was moved at 
20 mm⋅min−1. The stress-strain curves were plotted according to the 
literature (X. Ma et al., 2016). For each sample, the testing was repeated 
for 5 times, and the results were expressed as the averages ± standard 
deviations. The compressive/tensile Young's modulus and fracture en­
ergy were calculated accordingly (X. Ma et al., 2016).
2.3.6. Scanning electron microscope (SEM)
The microstructure of the hydrogels was observed by an SEM 
(TM3000, Hitachi High Technology Co., Ltd.). Before observation, the 
lyophilized samples were cut into thin slices and treated with gold 
spraying.
2.3.7. Antibacterial property
Antibacterial activity was investigated referring to previous report 
(H. Du, Ji, Xing, Ma, & Xia, 2023). Single colonies of S. aureus and E. coli 
were incubated respectively in Luria-Bertani (LB) liquid medium over­
night (15 h) in a thermostatic oscillator (37 ◦C, 200 rpm), diluted with 
sterile PBS solution to form a bacterial suspension with a concentration 
of 1 × 106 CFU/mL. 100 μL of bacterium suspension was extracted and 
equably coated on an LB agar plate using a sterile glass coater. Cylin­
drical samples with the same diameter and thickness were sterilized by 
ultraviolet light and then placed on the LB agar plates stained with 
bacteria. Afterwards, the LB agar plates were incubated at 37 ◦C for 24 h. 
Finally, the diameter of the inhibition zone around each sample was 
measured. The result was expressed as the average of triplicates with 
standard deviation.
2.3.8. In-vitro cytocompatibility tests
Mouse fibroblast NIH3T3 cells were employed to assess the 
biocompatibility of the hydrogels via CCK-8 test. The gel sample was cut 
into thin slices with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 2 mm, 
subjected to ultraviolet radiation (UV) for sterilization (1 h for each side 
of the slice). Then, the gel slice was rinsed with 0.1 M PBS solution, 
placed in a 12-well plate (one slice per well). To each well, 1 mL of 
culture medium specific for NIH3T3 was added. After 24 h' immersion at 
37 ◦C in a humidified atmosphere of 5 % CO2 (Thermo CO2 incubator 
311, U.S.A), the extractant in each well was collected respectively and 
diluted with specific NIH3T3 culture medium to 25 %, giving the 
leaching solution. NIH3T3 cells were seeded onto a 96-well culture disk 
(2 × 105 cells per well), with 6 repeated wells for each group. The 
leaching solution was added after cell's adhesion to the well wall. The 
morphology and growth feature of the cells were observed after 24/48 h 
of cultivation with an inverted biological microscope (Motic AE2000, 
China). Afterwards, the culture medium in each cell was sucked out and 
the cells were washed with sterile PBS solution. Then, 110 μL of a mixed 
solution containing 10 % CCK-8 and culture medium was added, which 
were cultured in 5 % CO2/95 % O2 incubator at 37 ◦C for 1 h. A 
microplate reader (VL0L00D0, Thermo Scientific Co., U.S.) was 
employed to measure the absorbance at 450 nm. As a control, parallel 
experiment was conducted in the meantime by culturing the NIH3T3 
cells with no addition of any leaching solution. A complete specific 
NIH3T3 culture medium was employed as the blank. The cell viability 
was calculated according to Eq. (1), where As is the absorbance of the 
sample tested, Ac is the absorbance of the control, and Ab is the absor­
bance of the blank. 
Cell viability (%) = (As −Ab)/(Ac −Ab) × 100%

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Formation mechanism of GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogels
Scheme 1 outlines the formation mechanism of GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 
hydrogels. On heating the aqueous dispersion of GG powder, confor­
mational transformation of GG molecules from an ordered and non- 
aggregated double helix to a random coil occurred as a result of H- 
bond breakage. Upon cooling, random GG coils self-assembled to double 
helices through hydrogen-bonding and van der Waals force, which 
associated and connected with each other into a 3D network structure, 
forming a gel (denoted as GG©2-MiM with 2-MiM encapsulated). Sub­
sequently, during the immersion of GG©2-MiM hydrogel in Zn(Ac)2 
aqueous solution, Zn2+ ions permeated in the network as a result of 
osmotic pressure and formed linkages between –COO−
on GG side 
chains through ionic bonding. The formation of ionic bonds between 
Zn2+ and –COO−
promoted the association and aggregation of GG 
double helices because of shielding of the electrostatic repulsion be­
tween –COO−ions, leading to increment in the cross-linking density of 
the network, literally Zn2+-induced ionic-crosslinking arose. In the 
meantime, some Zn2+ ions permeated coordinated with the encapsu­
lated 2-MiM and assembled to ZIF-8 nanoparticles (Y. Wang et al., 
2019). The formation of ionic crosslinking and ZIF-8 nanoparticles was 
confirmed by IR, XPS, rheological analysis and XRD.
3.2. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis
FTIR was employed to investigate the components of the hydrogels 
and their chemical structure. Fig. 1 displays the FTIR spectra of ZIF-8 
powder (laboratory-made, Experimental section and Fig. S1 in Sup­
porting information), GG hydrogel, GG-Zn2+
hydrogel and GG- 
Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogels (at different m2-MiM) with the same CGG of 2 wt% 
(see Table S1). In the spectrum of ZIF-8, the bands at 3136 and 2926 
cm−1 are attributed to the aromatic and aliphatic C–H stretching vi­
bration of the imidazole, respectively. Specifically, the band at 1585 
cm−1 is ascribed to the stretching vibration of CN bonds. The bands at 
1458 and 1382 cm−1 are ascribable to the characteristic absorption 
bands of –CH3 (Y. Wang et al., 2019). Other bands at 1350–1500 cm−1 
are associated with the stretching vibration of the imidazole ring, 
whereas the bands in the region of 900–1350 cm−1 are assigned to the 
in-plane bending of the imidazole ring, those in the region of 500–800 
cm−1 are ascribed to the out-of-plane bending of the ring. There is a 
strong absorption peak at 421 cm−1, attributed to the stretching vibra­
tion of Zn-N.
In the spectra of all hydrogels, a wide band around 3320 cm−1 ap­
pears, which corresponds to the stretching vibration of O–H groups. 
The bands at around 2917 cm−1 and 2850 cm−1 are caused by –CH2 
groups. The band at 1723 cm−1 is assigned to the stretching vibration of 
C––O in high-acyl GG, and the band at around 1606 cm−1 is caused by 
the asymmetric stretching vibration of COO−. The strong bands at 
around 1023 cm−1 in GG and GG-Zn2+ hydrogels correspond to the 
stretching vibration of C–OH, which blue shifts to 1027 cm−1 and 1029 
cm−1 in GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 respectively, caused by the chelation of C–OH 
with Zn2+. Compared with GG and GG-Zn2+ hydrogels, the character­
istic absorption bands of ZIF-8 at around 1422 cm−1, 1311 cm−1, 993 
cm−1, 759 cm−1 and 421 cm−1 appear in GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogels, and 
the intensity of which increases with the increase of m2-MiM, confirming 
the formation of ZIF-8.
3.3. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis
Fig. 2 presents the full XPS spectrums of GG hydrogel, GG-Zn2+
hydrogel, and GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogels obtained under different m2- 
MiM. In all samples, peaks of C1s and O1s appear at ~285.95 and 
~532.80 eV. Different from GG hydrogel, a new multiple-peak at around 
1022.96 and 1046.32 eV appears in the spectrum of GG-Zn2+ hydrogel, 
attributed to Zn2p1/2 and Zn2p3/2 respectively, resulted from the 
permeated Zn2+. Similar to GG-Zn2+ hydrogel, the multiple-peak of 
Zn2+ appears in GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogels. There is an apparent peak at 
~401.64 eV in GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogels which is ascribable to N1s. 
The N1s peak along with the peaks of Zn2p3/2 and Zn2p1/2 confirms 
further the formation of ZIF-8. The weak N1s peaks in both GG and GG- 
Zn2+ hydrogels should be attributed to the residual microorganisms in 
the raw GG. Additionally, the total atomic percentages of Zn and N in­
crease with increment in m2-MiM, whereas the atomic ratio of N to Zn is 
lower than the stoichiometric ratio (4:1) of ZIF-8 (Table S2), suggesting 
the presence of element Zn in other form aside from forming ZIF-8 
particles with 2-MiM.
The fine spectrums of carbon, oxygen and zinc elements are dis­
played in Fig. 2b–d. The C1s spectrum (Fig. 2b) can be deconvoluted into

288.11/288.17/288.48 eV, corresponding to C–C/C–H, C–O/C–N, and 
O–C=O bonds respectively. The C–O/C–N peaks of GG-Zn2+ hydrogel 
and GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogels shift towards high binding energy in 
comparison with the GG hydrogel, suggesting chelation with Zn2+. The

attributable to the ionic binding between the diffused Zn2+ ions and

Fig. 4b depicts the changing trends of G′(G″) with temperature. On 
heating from 25 ◦C to 90 ◦C, G′ and G″ of GG hydrogel decline slightly

section point appears at 81.3 ◦C between the G′ and G″ curves. Below 
81.3 ◦C, G′ is always larger than G″, denoting the elastic gel state. G″ 
exceeds G′ from 81.3 ◦C on, signifying the gel-sol transition at around 
81.3 ◦C from a solid-like to a liquid-like state (Fig. 4d). The gel-to-sol 
transition is attributable to the dissociation of the helix aggregates to 
individual double helix and the unwinding of the double helices to 
random coils with raising temperature, resulted from the breakage of H- 
bonding between GG molecules.
Unlike the GG hydrogel, neither GG-Zn2+ hydrogel nor GG- 
Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogel underwent gel-sol transition when heated in the 
same temperature range, suggesting stronger interactions inside the two 
gels in addition to H-bonding, which made the two hydrogels maintain 
the gel state even at high temperatures (Morris, Nishinari, & Rinaudo, 
2012). The additional stronger interaction is legitimately attributed to 
the ionic bonds between Zn2+ and -COO−of GG molecules in GG-Zn2+
hydrogel. For GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogel, besides the ionic bonds be­
tween Zn2+ and -COO−, there are H-bonding between the imidazole 
rings of ZIF-8 and GG molecules as well as coordination interaction 
between Zn2+ of ZIF-8 and polar groups of GG molecules, leading to the 
much higher values of both G′ and G″ than GG-Zn2+ hydrogel. Therefore, 
not only the viscoelasticity but also the thermal stability of the GG-Zn2+
hydrogel and GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogel is promoted.
3.6. Mechanical performance
To optimize the mechanics, the influence of the preparation condi­
tions on the mechanical performance of the final hydrogels were 
explored by single-factor experiments, including the molar ratio of 2- 
MiM to Zn(Ac)2 (n(2-MiM:Zn2+)), the mass percentage of 2-MiM to GG 
(m2-MiM), and the GG content (CGG).

With constant CGG and m2-MiM, increase in n(2-MiM:Zn2+) means 
reduction of the concentration of the Zn(Ac)2 aqueous solution 
employed. Therefore, lower n(2-MiM:Zn2+) (4:1) means higher concen­

infiltration of Zn2+ ions. The more Zn2+ ions permeated in, the higher 
was the cross-linking degree of the network elevated and the more were 
ZIF-8 particles formed, which would contribute to fragile but relatively 
stiff network. With the increase of n(2-MiM:Zn2+), Zn2+-induced ionic 
crosslinking relented and ZIF-8 particles formed reduced, contributing 
to elastic but relatively soft network. Therefore, the compressive frac­
ture strain and the tensile breakage elongation increase with the in­
crease of n(2-MiM:Zn2+). Whereas, both the maximum compressive 
strength and tensile strength are achieved at n(2-MiM:Zn2+) of 5:1. So does 
the toughness.
3.6.2. Influence of m2-MiM on the mechanical performance
Figs. 6 and S3 demonstrate the effect of m2-MiM on the mechanical 
performance of the final hydrogels prepared at 5:1 of n(2-MiM:Zn2+) and 2 
wt% of CGG. Figs. 6a, b and S3a indicate the compressive strength, 
Young's modulus and fracture energy increase gradually with the in­
crease of m2-MiM, attaining the maximums of 0.82 MPa, 139 kPa and 
9.42 kJ/m3 at 40 % of m2-MiM, increased by 19.5, 63 and 18 respectively 
compared with the corresponding pristine GG hydrogel (0.04 MPa, 2.15 
kPa and 0.49 kJ/m3 only). The compressive fracture strain tends to 
descend slightly, reducing to 63 % at 40 % of m2-MiM. Figs. 6c, d and S3b 
indicate that with the increase of m2-MiM, the tensile strength, Young's 
modulus and fracture energy gradually increase, reaching the maxi­
mums of 533.31 kPa, 115.13 kPa and 38.52 kJ/m3, increased signifi­
cantly in comparison with the pristine GG hydrogel (merely 3.13 kPa, 
3.05 kPa and 0.15 kJ/m3). In contrast, the tensile elongation at break 
increases first and then decreases, with the maximum of ~250 % at 10 
% m2-MiM.
At fixed n(2-MiM:Zn2+) and CGG, the concentration of Zn(Ac)2 aqueous 
solution increases with increasing m2-MiM, hence Zn2+ ions permeated 
into the gel network increase. From one aspect, the more Zn2+ ions 
permeated in, the higher was the crosslinking density of the network 
enhanced as a result of the ionically bonding of Zn2+ with –COO−along 
with association and aggregation of GG double helixes. From another 
aspect, the amount of ZIF-8 formed would increase with raising m2-MiM, 
and the rising rigid ZIF-8 particles have an enhancing effect on the 
hydrogel rigidity either. Consequently, with the increase of m2-MiM, the 
compression strength, Young's modulus, and fracture energy as well as 
the tensile strength, Young's modulus and fracture energy of the GG- 
Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogel increased. However, the densification of the 
network would prohibit the mobility and extension of GG chains, 
causing the reduction of the deformation (strain), hence the compressive 
strain decreases with increase in m2-MiM. It is also plausible that the 
largest elongation at break is achieved at 10 % of m2-MiM. At low or no 
m2-MiM, the final hydrogel is weak and easy to be fractured with low or 
no Zn2+ ionic crosslinking, while at too high m2-MiM, the high cross­
linking density of the network constricts the extension of the GG 
molecules.

3.6.3. Influence of CGG on the mechanical performance
Figs. 7 and S4 demonstrate the effect of CGG on the mechanical 
performance of the final hydrogels prepared at fixed n(2-MiM:Zn2+) of 5 
and m2-MiM of 40 wt%. It can be seen from Figs. 7a, b and S4a that the 
increase in CGG promotes the compressive performance of the final 
hydrogels. In particular, the compressive strength, Young's modulus and 
fracture energy increase gradually with the rise of CGG. The compressive 
strength, Young's modulus and fracture energy achieve the maximums of 
1.17 MPa, 267 kPa and 16.99 kJ/m3 respectively at 3.5 wt% CGG, 
increased by 400 %, 2615 % and 550 % compared with the corre­
sponding pristine GG hydrogel (G3.5, Fig. S5 in Supporting informa­
tion). The results are reasonable since the degree of the double helix 
aggregation and chain entanglement increases as CGG rises, resulting in a 
more compact network structure, leading to the stronger network. 
Therefore, the compressive strength, fracture strain, Young's modulus, 
and fracture energy of GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogels gradually increase 
with CGG.
As shown in Figs. 7c, d and S4b, the changing trends of the tensile 
strength and Young's modulus versus CGG are similar to the compressive 
strength and Young's modulus, which is understandable. The elongation 
at break tends to increase first and decrease afterwards with raising CGG. 
This is plausible since the network is too weak and fragile at low CGG 
which is prone to fracture on stretching. Whereas at high CGG, the 
compact network depresses the extension of the chains and hence 
reducing the breakage elongation.
However, the solution is very viscous from 3.5 wt% CGG on, and lots 
of solution was left in the container when transferred to the mold, so 3.0 
wt% CGG is more favorable for the preparation of the GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 
hydrogel, with a slightly weaker compressive and tensile performance. 
The compressive strength, Young's modulus and fracture energy attain 
at 1.04 MPa, 214 kPa and 13.58 kJ/m3, ca. 7 times, 35 times and 9.5 
times those of G3 (Fig. S5) respectively. The tensile strength, breakage 
elongation, Young's modulus and fracture energy reach 588.5 kPa, 
172.53 %, 180.46 kPa and 46.25 kJ/m3, ca. 81 times, 1.6 times, 29 times 
and 106 times those of G3 (Fig. S5) respectively.
As far as we know, the mechanics of the GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogels 
developed here surpasses those of almost all of the GG-based hydrogels 
previously reported (Table S3 in Supporting information).

3.8. Antibacterial property
The antibacterial activity of the hydrogels against E. coli (as the 
modeling strain of gram-negative bacteria) and S. aureus (as the 
modeling strain of gram-positive bacteria) was investigated. The results 
are presented in Fig. 9a. Evidently, pristine GG hydrogel shew no 
bacterium-inhibitory effect against both E. coli and S. aureus in 24 h. GG- 
Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogels displayed no inhibitory effect against E. coli 
similar to GG hydrogel. However, they shew antibacterial activity 
against S. aureus, indicating selective antibacterial activity. The di­
ameters of the inhibition zone of different hydrogels against S. aureus are 
displayed in Fig. 9b, which tend to increase slightly with m2-MiM.
The antibacterial activity should be presumably associated with the 
release of Zn2+ and 2-MiM. As displayed in Figs. 9c, d and S7, both Zn2+
and 2-MiM are released in PBS over time, with the cumulative release 
higher in the acidic microenvironment (pH 5.6) than in the neutral 
microenvironment (pH 7.4). The release of Zn2+ ions and 2- MiM is 
resulted from the ion exchange of Zn2+-crosslinked gellan gum with PBS 
along with the degradation of ZIF-8 (M. Sun et al., 2022). Zn2+ has been

Additionally, 2-methylimidazole is a commonly used antibacterial

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case exploiting MOF 
materials to reinforce and functionalize GG hydrogels. Purely natural 
GG was employed as the hydrogel matrix herein, rather than subjected 
to chemical modification or hybridized with synthetic polymers (Han 
et al., 2020; Sacourbaravi, Ansari-Asl, Hoveizi, & Darabpour, 2024; Y. 
Wang et al., 2024). The incorporation of MOF particles is achieved 
through in-situ generation in aqueous environment at ambient temper­
ature, circumventing the employment of volatile organic solvents and 
avoiding agglomeration of MOF particles (Gwon et al., 2023). The 
preparation protocol for the hydrogels involves no polymerization re­
actions and chemical crosslinkers (Gwon, Han, Lee, Kim, & Lee, 2020), 
hence simple and environmental friendliness. Most notably, the me­
chanics of the GG-Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogels developed here surpasses those 
of most GG-based hydrogels previously reported for biomedical appli­
cation (Table S3 in Supporting information). Although the antimicrobial 
activity of the developed hydrogels is not too strong as compared with 
some previously reported polymer-MOF composites potential for 
biomedical applications, they are the few with no extra antibacterial 
agents added. Whereas in most antibacterial polymer-MOF composites 
reported, MOFs act primarily as carriers for those playing the antibac­
terial role (J. Li et al., 2023; Tian et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2024; Xiong 
et al., 2023; Zhang, Wang, Xiang, Jiang, & Zhao, 2023). The excellent 
mechanics, selective antibacterial activity against gram-positive bacte­
ria and good biocompatibility suggest them great prospects in biomed­
ical fields, e.g., as wound dressings, which is conducive to solve the 
drug-resistant problem brought especially by methicillin-resistant 
Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), a major cause of morbidity and mortal­
ity to hospitalized patients since the late 1970s.
4. Conclusion
Nano-reinforcement effect and ionic crosslinking was integrated in 
the modification of GG hydrogels, resulting in GG hydrogels encapsu­
lated with in-situ generated ZIF-8 and ionically-crosslinked by Zn2+ for 
the first time. The obtained hydrogels demonstrate far superior me­
chanical properties to pristine GG hydrogels. The maximum compressive 
and tensile strength reach 1.17 MPa and 592.2 kPa respectively. FTIR, 
XRD, XPS as well as rheological results confirm the successful formation 
of ZIF-8 particles inside GG hydrogels and the simultaneous ionic 
bonding of Zn2+ with –COO−
of GG molecules. The prepared GG- 
Zn2+©ZIF-8 hydrogels demonstrate good biocompatibility and selective 
antibacterial activity against S. aureus as the modeling Gram positive 
bacteria. The excellent mechanics, biocompatibility and antibacterial 
activity make them potential candidates for green and sustainable 
functional materials in biomedical fields, like wound dressings, which 
are instrumental in averting drug-resistant problems.

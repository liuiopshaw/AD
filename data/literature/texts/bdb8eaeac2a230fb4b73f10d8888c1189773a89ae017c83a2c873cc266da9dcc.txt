S-adenosylmethionine/homocysteine cycle alterations modify DNA
methylation status with consequent deregulation of PS1 and
BACE and beta-amyloid production
Andrea Fuso, Laura Seminara, Rosaria A. Cavallaro, Fabrizio D’Anselmi, and Sigfrido Scarpa*
Laboratorio di Ricerca, Dipartimento di Chirurgia bP. ValdoniQ, Universita` di Roma bLa SapienzaQ, 00161 Rome, Italy
Received 20 May 2004; accepted 14 September 2004
Few diseases are characterized by high homocysteine (HCY) and low
folate and vitamin B12 blood levels. Alzheimer disease (AD) is among
these. It has already been shown that DNA methylation is involved in
amyloid precursor protein (APP) processing and B-amyloid (AB)
production through the regulation of Presenilin1 (PS1) expression
and that exogenous S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) can silence the gene
reducing AB production. Here we demonstrate that BACE (b-
secretase), as well as PS1, is regulated by methylation and that the
reduction of folate and vitamin B12 in culture medium can cause a
reduction of SAM levels with consequent increase in presenilin1 and
BACE levels and with increase in AB production. The simultaneous
administration of SAM to the deficient medium can restore the normal
gene expression, thus reducing the AB levels. The use of deprived
medium was intended to mimic a mild nutritional deficit involved in the
onset of AD.

Introduction
Elevated HCYplasma level is a serious risk factor for the onset of
AD (Seshadri et al., 2002), as well as for cardiovascular pathologies.
Moreover, SAM levels decrease in AD patients and, generally, with
aging (Beyer et al., 2003; Bottiglieri and Hyland, 1994; Mizrahi et
al., 2003; Morris, 2003; Morrison et al., 1996). These data lead us to
hypothesize that alterations in SAM/HCY cycle (producing HCY
accumulation) are responsible for decreased SAM levels and, in
turn, for reduced DNA methylation (Medina et al., 2001; Miner et
al., 1997). HCY accumulation causes the accumulation of S-
adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) because of the reversibility of the
reaction converting SAH to HCY and adenosine (Ado); the
equilibrium dynamics favors SAH synthesis. The reaction proceeds
in the hydrolytic direction only if HCY and Ado are efficiently
removed (Fig. 1). SAH is a strong DNA methyltransferases
inhibitor, which reinforces DNA hypomethylation (Chiang et al.,
1996; Clarke et al., 1998; Lu, 2000; Weir and Scott, 1999).
The great relevance of Ah production in AD is largely
documented and accepted (Chaney et al., 2003; De Strooper and
Konig, 1999; Selkoe, 2000) as well as the importance of reducing
this protein in the therapy (Dodart et al., 2002; Haass, 2002). On the
other hand, it is also well understood that a complete elimination is
not possible because of the physiological activity of Ah and APP
(Atwood et al., 2003; Kontush et al., 2001; Munch and Robinson,
2002). Particular attention has been dedicated to the regulation of g-
and h-secretases, in order to reduce the amyloidogenic cutting of
APP (Ohno et al., 2004; Selkoe, 2001). APP could be processed by
g- (PS1 and PS2) and a-secretases (ADAM10 and TACE)
producing non-amyloidogenic peptides, or by g- and h-secretases
(BACE) producing the Ah fragments (Evin et al., 2003; Marlow et
al., 2003; Nunan and Small, 2000). The balance between the
different secretase activities could be very important in the
maintenance of the physiologic Ah levels (De Strooper, 2000; De
Strooper et al., 1998; Haass and De Strooper, 1999; Li et al., 2000;
Plant et al., 2003; Wolfe et al., 1999). Secretase activities also could
not be completely eliminated since they are implicated in several
cellular functions (like Notch1 processing) besides Ah processing
(Cai et al., 2001; Hardy and Selkoe, 2002; Shen et al., 1997; Sinha
et al., 1999; Wong et al., 1997).
In order to verify the correlation between the SAM/HCY
cycle, DNA methylation and AD, we prepared a medium
deprived of folate and vitamin B12 to study DNA methylation,
gene expression and protein synthesis on SK-N-SH and SK-N-BE
neuroblastoma cells. Folate and vitamin B12 are essential for the
transformation of HCY to SAM; the failure of this step leads to
decreased SAM synthesis, accumulation of HCY and methyl-
transferases inhibition. As previously shown, exogenous SAM
can inhibit the demethylation of a gene promoter and modulate

Results
Cell lines
Two different neuroblastoma cell lines, SK-N-SH and SK-N-
BE, were used to validate the experiments. Since the two lines gave
similar results in all the experiments performed, we decided to
show only the results relative to SK-N-BE cell line. SK-N-BE cells
showed a better differentiated neuronal morphology when grown
in differentiation medium (DM), but the differentiation seemed to
be independent from the other studied parameters; medium
deprivation and SAM administration did not influence cell growth
and differentiation in DM (data not shown).
Medium deprivation does not induce apoptosis
As measured by automatic instrumentation analysis, the
residual folate and vitamin B12 concentrations were 60 and 2.3
pg/ml, respectively, in deprived DM (DDM); these vitamins
amounts derived only from the fetal calf serum (FCS) added to
the medium. The normal concentrations of folate and vitamin B12
in serum-free F14 medium (SF) are 1.32 and 1.36 Ag/ml,
respectively.
Previous papers suggested that folate deprivation from the
culture medium, in association with the administration of
methionine, could induce cell death by apoptosis (Kruman et al.,
2002). Our experiments were performed in the presence of serum
that generally reduced the induction of apoptosis; nevertheless, we
decided to test this possibility in order to exclude that any change
in gene expression and protein synthesis could depend on the
induction of apoptosis. As shown in Fig. 2A, the level of apoptosis
evaluated by the ELISA test remained constant when the cells were
grown either in DM or in DDM, with or without 100 AM SAM. We
found that, also in serum-free SF (Fig. 2B), the deprivation of
folate and vitamin B12 did not lead to any induction of apoptosis;
on the contrary, the lack of the two vitamins significantly reduced
cell death. Retinoic acid and SAM induced, in SF medium, a high
increase of apoptosis (Fig. 2B), but this effect was just lightly
evident (i.e., not significant) in the presence of fetal calf serum in
the medium (Fig. 2A).
TUNEL analysis confirmed the results of the ELISA test and
evidenced general low levels of apoptosis in all the experimental
conditions.

Protein synthesis is correlated to methylation and gene expression
Western blot analysis of the factors involved in AD substan-

of the differences, probably due to post-transcriptional regulation
(i.e., proteins and RNA methylation).
Figs. 7A–D show a typical Western blotting for APP, PS1,
PS2, BACE, respectively; panels E–H show the densitometric
analysis from different experiments. Histograms are expressed
as percentage F SE, with the highest value assumed as 100%,
and represent the average O.D. normalized versus 14-3-3h
protein O.D. (not shown). The 110-kDa APP isoform (Figs.
7A,E) increased in DM and was more abundant after 144 h in
all the experimental conditions; nevertheless, there was no
significant differences between DM-, DDM-, and SAM-treated
cells (except for a decrease in DDM after 144 h). PS1 protein
(panels B and F) was synthesized at similar levels in GM and
DM, but was markedly reduced by SAM (Scarpa et al., 2003);
as observed for the gene expression, medium deprivation could
induce an increase in PS1 synthesis that could be reverted by
the presence of SAM. PS2 (panels C and G) synthesis seems
to be independent from the experimental conditions, with a
lower level in GM. Even BACE (panels D and H) was
overproduced in DM and SAM could repress its increase;
folate and vitamin B12 deprivation was sufficient to induce a
marked increase in protein synthesis that, also in this case, was
reverted by SAM.
Fig. 8 shows the Western blot analysis for ADAM10 (A, C) and
TACE (B, D), as described in Fig. 7. The protein synthesis
followed the gene expression: a very high overproduction in GM
and substantially no differences between the DM, DDM, and
SAM.
The ELISA test shown in Fig. 8E clearly demonstrates that
deprivation of folate and vitamin B12 could induce the increase of
Ah 1–40 production. The presence of SAM in the medium could,
evidently, reduce the amyloidogenesis.
Discussion
Folate, vitamin B12, and SAM frequent reduction in the elderly
(Miner et al., 1997) were the inspiring factors for the development
of this work. The fundamental hypothesis was that nutritional
deficits could lead to HCY/SAM metabolism alteration (hyper-
homocysteinemia) with the consequent decrease of SAM levels.
Methyl donor decrease, in turn, could induce demethylation
(particularly of DNA) responsible for the activation and over-
expression of genes involved in AD pathology (Richardson, 2003).
The general correlation between DNA methylation and gene
silencing is well known (Bergman and Mostoslavsky, 1998; Razin,
1998); previous experiments carried out in our laboratory
demonstrated how exogenous SAM is able to reduce in vitro
PS1 expression, PS1 protein synthesis, and Ah 1–40 production,
and how these results are directly related to the methylation of a
CCGG site in PS1 promoter (Scarpa et al., 2003). With this work,
we set up an experimental model to study in vitro the metabolic
HCY alterations and analyze the genes mostly involved in APP
processing. Complete and deprived medium as well as FCS were
preliminarily analyzed (with automatic analyzer IMX, Abbott) in
order to verify that folate and vitamin B12 content was really low.
We think that such a partial vitamin deprivation from the medium,
instead of a total depletion, is more representative for a nutritional
deficit.
Two other conditions had to be clarified to support the
suitability of our model. The first was the supposed apoptotic
induction due to folate deprivation: We found that in the medium
with 1% FCS, the apoptotic ratio was very low and similar in all
the experimental conditions; the results by Kruman et al. (2002),
apparently in contrast with ours, were obtained under extreme
conditions, i.e., in a medium also deficient in methionine and under
apoptosis induction by Ah, on primary neuronal cultures. The
second was the real ability of SAM to cross the cell membrane
(Chiang et al., 1996). We performed HPLC analysis for SAM
intracellular levels in all the experimental conditions, verifying that
the pharmaceutical preparation(s) we used can easily enter into the
cells. The HPLC analysis also allowed us to demonstrate that the
intracellular SAM levels of cells grown in deprived medium were
lower respect to the complete medium; this finding confirmed that
HCY metabolism alteration due to folate and vitamin B12
deprivation really caused the reduction of SAM levels in the cells.
A further development of this analysis will request setting up a
different chromatographic method to determine also SAH and
HCY levels (i.e., capillary electrophoresis) in order to clarify the
relationship between the different metabolites of the cycle;
experiments are in progress in this respect. On the basis of these
data, we can stress that the obtained results were only due to the
different levels of SAM, folate, and vitamin B12 on the regulation
of DNA methylation and gene expression.

The increasing attention toward BACE as preferential target for
AD therapy has convinced us to further study its regulation. We
found that BACE gene expression is modulated by the presence of
SAM. We demonstrated that PS1 and BACE are induced by folate
and vitamin B12 deprivation and that SAM can restore the normal
expression pattern. Protein production analysis confirmed that PS1
and BACE are modulated by the alteration of SAM/HCY cycle
(among the studied genes). The other genes involved in APP
processing and APP itself seemed to be independent on medium
deprivation and on methylation. Thus, the two factors primarily
involved in Ah production (g- and h-secretases) result correlated
to alterations of SAM/HCY cycle. The different regulation of the
two presenilins could find an explanation in the theory, not yet
proved, that PS2 represent a sort of redundant or accessory g-
secretase activity (Herreman et al., 1999).
The observation that the two a-secretases (ADAM10 and
TACE) are independent from methylation proves that the
regulatory effect is gene-specific; this is very important for a
possible clinical monitoring and pharmacological intervention
based on SAM/HCY cycle. In fact, if the induced or reduced
levels of h- and g-secretases were related to a similar regulation of
the a-secretases, we could have an unchanged ratio in APP
processing. ADAM10 seems eventually to be slightly up-regulated
by SAM; we hypothesized that the presence of SAM could act at
protein methylation levels. Although we demonstrated that Notch1
expression is not affected by SAM/HCY cycle alterations, it
remains to be clarified if the regulation of secretases activity could
influence Notch1 processing. However, we think that the PS1 and
BACE partial inhibition could not be critical in this respect.
The methylation analysis clearly demonstrated the correlation
between SAM/HCY cycle and DNA methylation as well as the
involvement of DNA methylation in PS1 and BACE regulation,
providing a possible molecular mechanism responsible for the
overproduction of Ah. Unfortunately, the analysis of the 5V-
flanking regions of BACE, PS2, ADAM10, and TACE is, instead,
complicated by the presence of CpG islands that do not allow the
application of the HpaII/PCR technique; the methylation analysis
of BACE promoter should be in the future investigated by bisulfite
treatment and genomic sequencing, whereas the analysis of the
other three genes seems to be less interesting as the gene
expression results independent from SAM/HCY cycle.
One of the most relevant aspects of this work is the partial
SAM inhibition of gene expression and protein synthesis. As it
has been pointed out several times, although the g- and h-
secretases could represent the main target for the AD therapy, it is
fundamental to avoid their complete silencing, since the basal
activity is necessary for the correct cellular metabolism (Plant et
al., 2003). The Ah accumulation in the senile plaques is known

The mechanism we outlined in this paper is in agreement with the
necessity to recover the physiological Ah production. Moreover,
the analysis of the SAM/SAH ratio and the levels of HCY, folate,
B vitamins, could become a potent tool in the early diagnosis of
sporadic AD.
Experimental methods
Cell cultures
SK-N-SH and SK-N-BE human neuroblastoma cell lines were
cultured in F14 medium with 8% fetal calf serum (growth medium,
GM) or with 1% fetal calf serum plus 10 AM retinoic acid
(differentiation medium, DM); deprived medium was prepared
subtracting folate and vitamin B12 from the preparation of F14
medium and used with 1% fetal calf serum plus 10 AM retinoic
acid (DDM); SAM was added to DM and DDM according to the
experimental design. Cultures were re-fed every second day; the
times indicated are referred to medium shift (or change) as day 0.
Experiments were repeated at least three times, giving similar
results. The residual levels of folate and vitamin B12 in the serum
were measured by IMX automatic analyzer (Abbott) following the
instruction supplied with the kit.
Apoptosis assays
Growth rate was calculated counting the cells by electronic
Coulter (Z2, Coulter). Apoptotic rate was calculated either by
ELISA test (Cell Death Detection ELISA Plus Kit, Roche
Diagnostics, Germany), following the indication of the producer,
and by TUNEL method, after cytospin centrifugation and fixing of
the cells with 4% paraformaldehyde.
HPLC assays
Cell lysis, macromolecules precipitation, and HPLC measure-
ments on the supernatants were carried out as previously described
(Fuso et al., 2001).

Nucleic acid analysis
RNA and DNA extractions were performed by standard
procedures (Lucarelli et al., 2001). The semiquantitative gene
expression analysis by RT-PCR were performed on 1 Ag of total
RNA, using 50 pmol of oligo-d(T)16, and 50 units of M-MuLV
reverse transcriptase (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA) at 428C for 1 h,
as indicated by the manufacturer. PCR assays were carried out as
previously described (Fuso et al., 2001); h-actin was used as a
standard for the subsequent densitometric analysis. For each gene,
we performed several amplification reactions at different number
of cycles (20–40), choosing the reactions in the linear logarithmic
phase for the semiquantitative analysis (Lucarelli et al., 2001).
DNA methylation assays were performed by multiplex HpaII/
PCR on PS1 promoter as previously described (Fuso et al., 2001;
Lucarelli et al., 2001; Scarpa et al., 2003). Briefly, genomic DNA
was treated with the restriction endonuclease HpaII, which has
one recognition site internal to the 5V-flanking region and is
methylation sensitive (it fails to cut if the CCGG recognition
sequence is methylated at any C). Intron 2, which possesses no
HpaII or EcoRI recognition sites, was used as internal standard.
Two micrograms of genomic DNA were digested overnight at
378C with 5 units of enzyme, and then with 3 units more for
additional 6 h, in a final volume of 40 Al of appropriate buffer
(Roche Diagnostics). PCR reactions were carried out as described
above for the semiquantitative analysis. GenBank accession
numbers; primers name, sequence, and position; expected
products; and annealing temperatures are shown in Table 1.
Gel electrophoresis and analysis of PCR products
PCR products were examined by electrophoresis in 1.2%
agarose gel. Each gel was acquired and analyzed on a
computerized densitometer (Fluor-S MultiImager, BIO-RAD,
Hercules, CA). Fragments specificity was assessed by restriction
analysis.
Western blot analysis
Cells were lysed with 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl,
0.2% Nonidet P-40, 1% CHAPS, 2 mM EDTA, PMSF (200 AM),
leupeptin (1 AM), pepstatin A (1 AM), calpain inhibitor I (5 AM),
all from Sigma (St. Louis, MO). Twenty micrograms of each
protein extract were run on 6% (APP) or 12% (PS1, PS2, BACE,
ADAM10, TACE) SDS-PAGE, then blotted onto nitrocellulose
(BIO-RAD). Primary antibodies and band sizes are resumed in
Table 2; secondary antibodies were from ECL Western blotting
reagents kit (Amersham, UK).

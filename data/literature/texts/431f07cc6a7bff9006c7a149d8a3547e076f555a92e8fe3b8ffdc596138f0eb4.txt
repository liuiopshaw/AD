An eﬃcient treatment of bioﬁlm-induced
periodontitis using Pt nanocluster catalysis
Tao Wu,†a Jiahui Sun,†a Jie Lei,b Qihang Fan,a Xiuyu Tang,a Guixin Zhu,a Qi Yan,a
Xiaobo Feng*b and Bin Shi
*a
Periodontitis is a common chronic inﬂammatory disease associated with bioﬁlm formation, gingival reces-
sion, and supporting bone loss that can lead to the formation of periodontal pockets and, ultimately,
tooth loss. Clinical treatment for periodontitis through scaling and antibiotics still faces the problems of
unavoidable bleeding, injury to periodontal tissue, drug resistance, and insuﬃcient treatment. Herein we
prepared an injectable anti-periodontitis ointment with catalytic activity that consists of Pt nanocluster
(PtNC) modiﬁed g-C3N4 (CN), and PEG400/PEG4000, which eﬃciently treated bioﬁlm-infected periodonti-
tis. PtNCs (<2 nm) with ultralow content (0.07%) were formed on the surface of CN using mild ultraviolet
(UV) irradiation. Due to the strong O2 adsorption and activation ability of CN-PtNCs and their mutual elec-
tron transfer, they show both oxidase-like and peroxidase-like activities and produce reactive oxygen
species (ROS) in the dark. CN-PtNCs showed strong bioﬁlm elimination ability towards Staphylococcus
aureus (S. aureus) and Escherichia coli (E. coli). Furthermore, beneﬁting from the good biocompatibility of
CN-PtNCs and the injectable property of the PEG400/PEG4000 ointment, the CN-PtNC ointment with
high bioavailability successfully treated periodontitis in rats, alleviating inﬂammation and reducing bone
loss, and showed better performance than periocline. Therefore, this catalytic system is promising for an
eﬃcient, non-invasive, and antibiotic-free treatment of periodontitis.
Introduction
Periodontitis is an oral chronic disease aﬀecting more than
90% of the worldwide population.1 Periodontitis is considered
the main reason for tooth loss and has many stubborn symp-
toms, including bleeding, swelling, pain, or tooth loosening.2
Plaque biofilm is thought to be the primary cause of periodon-
titis through bacterial toxins and related host immune reac-
tion. As for biofilms, the administration of antibiotics cannot
eﬃciently eradicate them due to the protection of extracellular
polymeric substances.3 Therefore, the standard clinical treat-
ment for periodontitis is through mechanical scaling4 or root
planning combined with antibiotics. Antibiotics such as perio-
cline ointment, which contains minocycline hydrochloride
(Mino), can be used locally after the disruption of the biofilm
by scaling. However, these treatments still face some problems.
During mechanical scaling, patients will suﬀer from un-
avoidable bleeding and injury to alveolar bone and gums.
Further, scaling usually cannot completely remove a deep
plaque biofilm. Because drugs injected subgingivally are easily
lost, frequent use of antibiotics is required because of their low
antibacterial activity. Moreover, the frequent administration of
antibiotics will result in drug resistance and side eﬀects.5 To
address these problems, antibiotics loaded in nanoparticles
such as polymers, silica, and lipid polymer vesicles have shown
the potential to combat biofilms through low-dose antibiotic
therapy.6 However, it is desirable to develop antibiotic-free,
eﬃcient, and non-toxic strategies to treat periodontitis. Metal
nanoparticles,7
peptides,8
metal–organic
frameworks,9
and
photothermal10 and photodynamic therapy have been used to
combat biofilms and treat periodontitis.11 Among these, nano-
particles of metals such as Ag, Au, Pt, and Cu possess anti-
biofilm abilities.6,12 But a high concentration of heavy or noble
metals has the problem of potential tissue cytotoxicity.13
Nanoparticles with enzyme-like properties have attracted
wide research interest in the fields of cancer and infection
treatment.14–20 Nanoparticles with oxidase-like (OXD) or per-
oxidase-like (POD) abilities can catalyse the related substrate to
generate ROS, thus killing pathogens.21–23 Nanoparticles such
as Fe3O4, Pt, Pd, Au, CeO2, CuO, etc. have exhibited good OXD-

In this study, we prepared an injectable anti-biofilm oint-
ment consisting of PtNCs, g-C3N4 (CN), and PEG400/PEG4000.
PtNCs (<2 nm) with ultralow content (0.07%) were formed on
the surface of CN using mild ultraviolet (UV) irradiation.
CN-PtNCs exhibited both OXD-like and POD-like properties,
meaning that they could produce ROS in the dark due to their
strong O2 adsorption and activation abilities. The CN-PtNCs
showed strong anti-biofilm action towards S. aureus and
E. coli. With the good biocompatibility of CN-PtNCs and the
injectable property of PEG400/PEG4000 ointment, CN-PtNC oint-
ment showed great treatment eﬃciency for periodontitis in
rats, performing even better than the standard clinical drug
periocline. This work presents a new strategy for the eﬃcient
treatment of periodontitis using g-C3N4 based Pt nanocluster
catalysis.
Results and discussion
Characterization of CN-PtNCs
To prepare the CN-PtNCs, a mild UV reduction (0.71 mW
cm−2, 6 h) of chloroplatinic acid was performed on the surface
of CN. The morphology of the CN-PtNCs was observed by
transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Fig. 1a shows that
some nanoparticles existed on the surface of the two-dimen-
sional nanosheets. Due to their low contrast, aberration-cor-
rected
high-angle
annular
dark-field
scanning
TEM
(HAADF-STEM) was applied to observe whether PtNCs were
formed. As Fig. 1b shows, many bright NCs with a mean size
of 1.6 nm can be seen on the surface of the nanosheets. The
lattice of the (111) facets with a d-spacing of 0.23 nm was
attributed to the PtNCs (Fig. 1c). Energy-dispersive X-ray spec-
troscopy elemental mapping exhibited uniformly dispersed
elements C, N, and Pt throughout the nanosheets, demonstrat-
ing the successful deposition of PtNCs on the surface of CN,
which might result from the mild UV reduction of chloroplati-
nic acid and the confinement eﬀect of CN (Fig. 1d).
Fig. 1e presents the X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) patterns of CN
and CN-PtNCs. The (100) and (002) peaks at 12.9° and 27.6°
originate from the in-plane packing of the tri-s-triazine ring
structure and the layer graphitic structure stacking, respect-
ively. After the introduction of PtNCs, no obvious change of
diﬀraction peaks could be detected, suggesting that the
content of PtNCs in the CN-PtNCs was low and did not influ-
ence the structure of CN.28 From the X-ray photoelectron spec-
troscopy (XPS) spectra (Fig. 1f), elements C and N were
detected in CN and CN-PtNCs. Compared with CN, a weak Pt
4f signal at 72.8 eV was detected in the CN-PtNCs, and its
content was calculated to be 0.07%. It is known that Pt is an
expensive precious metal, and high concentrations of Pt
(>15 mg kg−1) lead to acute hepato-renal injury.29 In contrast,
the content of Pt we used in the animal experiments was extre-
mely low (<3 µg kg−1), which reduces the usage and toxicity of
Pt. As the N 1s spectra show (Fig. 1g), the C–CvN, N–(C)3, and
C–N–H signals of both CN and CN-PtNCs were assigned to the
sp2-hybridized aromatic nitrogen, tertiary nitrogen, and free
amino groups, respectively. The N binding energy of the
CN-PtNCs shifted to a higher position compared with that of
CN due to the interaction between lone pair electrons of N in
CN and 3d unoccupied orbitals of Pt.30 Fig. 1h shows the
chemical state of Pt in the CN-PtNCs. The peaks of the Pt 4f
spectra can be deconvoluted into Pt0 4f7/2 (72.3 eV), Pt0 4f5/2
(75.2 eV), Pt2+ 4f7/2 (73.2 eV), and Pt2+ 4f5/2 (76.7 eV). The ratio
of metallic Pt to Pt2+ was calculated to be 1.45 : 1.
Catalytic activity assays
To investigate the OXD activities of the samples, 3,3,5,5-tetra-
methylbenzidine (TMB) was used to monitor the catalytic reac-
tion. TMB can be oxidized into ox-TMB in the presence of O2
and OXD, and ox-TMB has a characteristic absorbance at
652 nm with a blue colour. The PBS (pH = 7.4) solutions of the
samples were mixed with TMB for 30 min at 37 °C to detect
the following. The absorbance spectra of the samples (Fig. 2a)
show that the CN + TMB groups had no obvious absorption
peak at 652 nm, indicating that CN did not have OXD-like pro-
perties. After the modification of the PtNCs, the CN-PtNCs dis-
played a significantly enhanced absorption peak at 652 nm
due to the production of ox-TMB, which proves the OXD-like
activity of CN-PtNCs. The distinct colour change of the TMB +
CN-PtNC group was also observed. In addition, once the

activity of the CN-PtNCs.
Theoretical evaluation of OXD-like activity
The OXD-like activity of CN and CN-PtNCs was investigated
through density functional theory (DFT) calculations. As
reported, metal nanoparticles, such as Pd, Pt, Ag, and Au, with
OXD-like activity can catalyse the dissociation of O2 into O
adatoms (O*) to oxidize organic substrates. Therefore, we cal-
culated the adsorption, activation, and dissociation processes
of O2 on the surface of CN and CN-PtNCs. As shown in Fig. 2g,
CN and the CN-PtNCs had adsorption energy levels of −0.82
eV and −3.10 eV for O2, respectively, indicating that the PtNCs
significantly improved the adsorption capacity of CN. The acti-
vation energies (Ea) of the surface-bound O2 dissociation for
CN and CN-PtNCs were also calculated, with values of 3.23 eV
and 0.26 eV, respectively. The related O–O distances of O2
binding on CN and CN-PtNCs were 1.23 Å and 1.42 Å, respect-
ively. The O–O distance in CN-PtNCs was longer than that of
CN and free O2 (1.23 Å), suggesting that the CN-PtNCs could
weaken the O–O bond but not CN because electrons were
transferred from the PtNCs to the antibonding π* orbital (i.e.,
LUMO) of O2. Next, the Ea barriers of CN and CN-PtNCs were
calculated to be 3.23 eV and 0.26 eV, respectively. The surface-
bound O2 dissociation into O* was associated with Ea, which
can reflect the magnitude of OXD-like activity. Generally, lower
Ea indicates stronger OXD-like activity.25 Based on these find-
ings, we concluded that the dissociative adsorption of the O2
molecule on the CN-PtNCs is more favourable than that on
CN, indicating the stronger OXD-like activity of the former.
These results were in line with the OXD-like activity detection
using TMB.
Antibacterial ability and mechanism of CN-PtNCs
After considering the OXD activity of CN-PtNCs, we next
studied their antibacterial ability towards E. coli (Gram-posi-
tive) and S. aureus (Gram-negative) biofilms. The CN, Mino,
and CN-PtNC samples were incubated with a biofilm for 12 h.
From the spread plate results (Fig. 3a and b), CN showed no
antibacterial ability towards either E. coli or S. aureus biofilm
because it did not exhibit OXD-like activity or generate ROS.
The Mino (520 ppm) and CN-PtNC (400 ppm) samples showed
antibacterial eﬃciencies of 91.3% and 97.9% to E. coli biofilm,
respectively. For the S. aureus biofilm, Mino and CN-PtNCs
showed antibacterial eﬃciency of 82.3% and 92.9%, respect-
ively.

To study the antibacterial mechanism, we evaluated the
ATP content, o-nitrophenyl-β-D-galactoside (ONPG) hydrolysis,
GSH consumption, and intracellular ROS production. Since
the bacterial metabolism process relies on ATP, the reduction
of ATP indicated a decline in metabolism.34 As Fig. 4a shows,
the luminescence signal of ATP measured by an ATP kit
showed that the ATP content of bacteria treated by CN-PtNCs
was significantly reduced compared with that in the control
and CN groups, which was due to the decreased ATP synthase
enzyme activity in the presence of ROS.35 ONPG was used to
detect the membrane permeability of the bacteria. Fig. 4b
reveals that CN also enhanced the membrane permeability
after 12 h of treatment, which might be because the sharp
edges of the CN nanosheets stimulated the cell membrane of
the bacteria.36 With the addition of OXD-like activity, the
CN-PtNCs further increased the permeability due to the oxi-
dation of membrane proteins. As an antioxidant in bacteria,
glutathione (GSH) can neutralize ROS to maintain the intra-
cellular redox state. As indicated in Fig. 4c, the CN-PtNCs
showed a strong GSH deletion ability compared with CN,
which can oxidize GSH into colourless glutathione disulphide
(GSSG). As a result, the highest accumulation of intracellular
ROS was also found in the CN-PtNC group, suggesting that the
ROS-dependent oxidative stress was increased due to its strong
OXD-like activity (Fig. 4d and e). The above antibacterial
process is illustrated in Fig. 4f. The cytotoxicity of CN-PtNCs to
gingival fibroblasts was evaluated through the CCK-8 assay. As
Fig. 4g shows, after 12 h or 24 h of coculture, although the
cytotoxicity of CN-PtNCs increased slightly with the concen-
tration, cell viability of gingival fibroblasts remained at a
normal level. A haemolysis test demonstrated that neither CN
nor CN-PtNCs caused haemolysis, having relative haemolysis
(%) below the national standard of 5% (Fig. 4h).

DFT calculations of OXD-like activity
The DFT calculations for the OXD-like activity of CN and
CN-PtNCs were performed using the Vienna ab initio simu-
lation package. The electron–ion interactions were described
by the projector augmented wave method. The exchange-func-
tional was treated by the generalized gradient approximation
of the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional. The energy cutoﬀ
was set as 450 eV, and the force of each atom was less than
0.03 eV Å−1. The calculation model was built with a 2 × 2
supercell of C3N4. A vacuum was placed along the z direction
to prevent the interaction of periodic structures. Brillouin zone
integration was performed using 3 × 3 × 1 k-point sampling. A
convergence energy threshold with 10−4 eV was used in the
self-consistent
calculations.
The
climbing-image
nudged
elastic band method was used to calculate the transition state.
In vitro anti-biofilm assay
S. aureus (ATCC 25923) and E. coli (ATCC 8099) were chosen to
investigate the antibiofilm ability of the samples. First, 200 µL
of a 106 CFU mL−1 bacterial solution (S. aureus or E. coli) was
cultured in a 96-well plate for 24 h, and the culture medium
was replaced every 12 h. Each kind of biofilm was treated with
PBS, CN (400 μg mL−1), Mino (520 μg mL−1), and CN-PtNCs
(400 μg mL−1) in a 96-well plate for 12 h, respectively. Then, the
antibacterial performance was evaluated through the spread
plate method. The antibacterial eﬃciency of the samples was
calculated using the following formula: antibacterial eﬃciency
(%) = (ACtrl −Aexperiment)/ACtrl × 100%. The biofilm treated by the
samples was also stained by crystal violet (200 µL, 0.1% wt/vol)
for 15 min. The biofilm was then washed with PBS until the
supernatant was colourless. After drying, 5% ethanol was added
into the biofilm to dissolve crystal violet dye for the biofilm,
and the corresponding ethanol solution of crystal violet was
measured at an absorption peak of 590 nm. The biofilm treated
by the samples was also stained by PI and SYTO9 (LIVE/DEAD
BacLight bacterial viability kit, Invitrogen) for 30 min and
washed with PBS. The live/dead staining of the biofilm was
observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (IFM,
Olympus, IX73). The morphology of the biofilm was further
observed by SEM. The biofilm was treated by PBS before and
treated with glutaraldehyde solution (2.5%) for 2 h. After that,
graded ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%, v/v) dehydra-
tion was performed for 15 min at each concentration.
ATP activity and ONPG hydrolysis assay
S. aureus (106 CFU mL−1) was treated with PBS, CN (400 μg
mL−1), and CN-PtNCs (400 μg mL−1) in a 96-well plate for 12 h.
The supernatant was collected to detect ATP activity using an
enhanced ATP assay kit (Beyotime).
The change of membrane permeability was evaluated by
ONPG hydrolysis. S. aureus (106 CFU mL−1) was treated with
isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, 10 μg mL−1) for
12 h at 37 °C before use. After that, PBS, CN (400 μg mL−1),
and CN-PtNCs (400 μg mL−1) were added for 1 h. Then, the
ONPG (3.8 μg mL−1) and 7% DMSO were added into the bac-
terial solution. The absorption peak of the solution at 420 nm
was recorded.
Ellman’s assay
The PBS, CN (400 μg mL−1), CN-PtNCs (400 μg mL−1), and
H2O2 (1 mM, positive control) samples were added into 150 µL
of GSH (0.8 mM) and mixed for 12 h. The GSH solution was
then collected and mixed with 450 µL of Tris-HCl (50 mM, pH
8.0) and 100 µL of 5,5′-dithio-bis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (10 mM)
for 30 min. The solution was further blended and placed in a
rocking bed for 30 min to fully react. The optical density of the
solution at 410 nm was recorded.
Intrabacterial ROS detection
A 200 µL PBS solution of DCFH-DA was incubated with
S. aureus solution (1 mL, 106 CFU mL−1) at 37 °C for 30 min.

In vitro cytocompatibility evaluation
The cell viability of gingival fibroblasts treated by samples was
evaluated using the CCK-8 method. Gingival fibroblasts were
seeded into 48-well plates (1.5 × 104 per well). After 12 h, the

10% CCK-8 solution was added to the 48-well plates and incu-

Haemolysis assay
Haemolysis assay of the samples was performed using fresh
blood from the rats. The red blood cells were obtained by centri-
fuging fresh blood (3500 rpm at 4 °C, 10 min) and washing
them with a phosphate buﬀer solution three times. Then, the
samples, including H2O (positive control), CN (400 μg mL−1),
CN-PtNCs (400 μg mL−1), and PBS (negative control), were
mixed with 1 mL of 10% red blood cell dispersion and placed at
37 °C for 4 h. Then, the samples were centrifuged and photo-
graphed. The corresponding supernatants of the samples were
collected, and their absorbance was recorded at 570 nm. The
haemolytic rates (%) were calculated using the following
formula: haemolytic rate (%) = (Asample −APBS)/(Awater −APBS).
Animal experiment
Eight-week-old male SD rats were used in this study, and
animal surgical procedures were conducted according to the
guidelines for animal care and use as set forth by the
Committee of Wuhan University, People’s Republic of China,
and approved by the Ethics Committee at the School of
Dentistry. Rats were randomly allocated to the four groups: (1)
sham, (2) periodontitis, (3) Mino, and (4) CN-PtNCs. A rat peri-
odontitis model was created using the silk ligature method.
We placed 4–0 silk sutures subgingival around the cervical
region in the first molar tooth on the left and right mandible
for three days, and the rats were given high-sugar drinking
water (100 g L−1). After suture ligation for three days, the
diﬀerent samples (40 μL per site) were given. After seven days,
the animals were sacrificed to remove the maxillae for further
study.
Probing depth assessment
Before animal sacrifice, a UNC-15 periodontal probe was used
to record probing depths at the mesial-buccal, mesial-palatal,
middle-buccal,
middle-palatal,
distal-buccal,
distal-palatal
points. Then, the mean value was calculated to compare the
probing depths.
Visualization of plaque biofilms
Gingiva was removed carefully to expose the subgingival area,
followed by a 2% fuchsin solution (Sigma, USA) staining for
2 min. After washing with PBS two times, the staining areas of
the teeth cervix were photographed using a stereomicroscope
(Zeiss Stemi SV11 Apo, Germany).
Micro-CT assessment of bone resorption
All samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and
scanned by micro-CT (CT50, Scanco Medical, Basersdorf,
Switzerland). Scanning was performed at 70 kV and 114 μA
with a thickness of 15 μm per slice to evaluate bone resorption.
The vertical distance between the cemento-enamel junction
(CEJ) and alveolar bone crest was measured to analyse the
bone loss of the first maxillary molars.
Histological analysis
All samples were decalcified with 10% ethylene diamine tetra-
acetic acid (EDTA) for 8 weeks and routinely dehydrated,
embedded, and cut into 4 μm sections in the mesial-to-distal
direction. Haematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining, TRAP staining,
and immunohistochemical staining for TNF-α were conducted.
Conclusions
In summary, we developed a CN-PtNC ointment with OXD and
POD activity and eﬃcient anti-biofilm property for the treat-
ment of biofilm-induced periodontitis. PtNCs fixed on the
surface of CN significantly improved the O2 adsorption and
reduced the activation energy barrier of O2, which improved
the dissociation of surface-bound O2 into O* due to the elec-
trons transferred from PtNCs to the antibonding π* orbital of
O2. The strong oxidation ability of CN-PtNCs eﬃciently dis-
rupted the S. aureus and E. coli biofilm. Furthermore, PEG400/
PEG4000 ointment endowed CN-PtNCs with injectability, which
can
prolong
their oral residence time
and
elevate
bio-
availability. In vivo, the CN-PtNC ointment successfully treated
periodontitis in rats, showing better performance than perio-
cline at alleviating inflammation and reducing bone loss. This
work provides a new strategy for an eﬃcient, non-invasive,
antibiotic-free
treatment
of
periodontitis
and
stimulates
further investigation for the development of anti-periodontitis
ointment using Pt nanocluster catalysis.

MINI-REVIEW
Probiotics as potential therapeutic options for Alzheimer’s disease
Hong‑Fang Ji1,2   · Liang Shen1,2
Received: 2 July 2021 / Revised: 15 September 2021 / Accepted: 18 September 2021 
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2021
Abstract 
The steadily increasing prevalence of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) worldwide and the lack of effective therapeutic agent attract 
novel therapeutic approach in recent years. In view of the close relationships between gut microbiota and AD, probiotics 
have been suggested as potential therapeutic options for AD in recent years. The present review discussed the research 
progresses concerning the effects of probiotics administration to combat AD. A total of 35 studies, including 26 animal 
model studies and 9 human studies, were included herein. Among the 26 animal model studies, 24 used mice model, and 2 
used Caenorhabditis elegans and Drosophila melanogaster AD models, respectively. As for probiotics, a total of 13 studies 
employed single-strain probiotic, and the rest studies used multi-strain probiotics (ranged from 2 to 9 probiotic strains), 4 used 
probiotic-fermented milk or probiotic-fermented soybean, 2 studies used engineered probiotic strain, and 4 studies focused 
on the combined effect of probiotics with AD drug memantine, selenium, or exercise. Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus 
species were the most frequently used probiotics in the included studies. Overall, currently available studies showed that 
probiotic administration conferred neuroprotective benefits and could attenuate cognitive deficits and modulate gut micro-
biota dysbiosis, which may be related to oxidative and inflammatory pathways. Several perspectives on future studies on this 
topic are proposed. Thus, probiotics seem to be an attractive approach to combat AD, which deserves to be further studied 
by well-designed large-scale clinical studies.
Key points 
•We discussed the recent progresses concerning the effects of probiotics administration to combat AD.
•A total of 35 associated studies consisted of 26 animal model studies and 9 human studies were included.
•Most studies found that probiotic administration conferred neuroprotective benefits and could attenuate cognitive deficits.
Keywords  Alzheimer’s disease · Gut microbiota · Probiotics
Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common form of dementia 
among the elderly. AD has steadily increasing prevalence 
worldwide, while developing drugs to combat this disorder 
encounters great challenge in the past decades [Shah, 2016; 
Alzheimer’s Association, 2019; Citron, 2010]. As no disease 
modifying treatment currently exists for AD, there is an urgent 
need to develop novel type of therapeutic approach for this disorder. 
In recent years, a large number of studies indicated that gut 
microbiota was closely related to AD development by a series 
of animal model and human studies [Shen et al. 2017; Zhang 
et al. 2017; Itzhaki et al. 2016; Harach et al. 2017; Zhuang et al. 
2018; Kim et al., 2020; Haran et al., 2019; Vogt et al. 2017; 
Ghaisas et al. 2016]. Many studies indicated the associations 
between the gut microbiota alterations with behavioral and 
histological manifestations related to AD. Thus, it has been 
proposed that microbiota-targeted intervention may represent 
a promising therapeutic strategy for AD [Shabbir et al. 2021; 
Shen and Ji 2019; Sun et al. 2020a,b, Pluta et al. 2020]. In the 
past few years, the therapeutic potential of probiotics for AD

Overview of the included studies
Through searching the PubMed database with the keywords 
“Alzheimer’s disease” and “probiotics” or “probiotic,” a total 
of 35 eligible research studies were included. Among the 35 
studies, 1 was published in 2016, 4 in 2017, 5 in 2018, 7 in 
2019, 15 in 2020, and 2 in 2021, respectively, suggesting 
the increasing interest on the potential of probiotics to treat 
AD. The 35 studies consisted of 26 animal model studies 
and 9 human studies. Among the animal model studies, 24 
used mice model, and only 2 used Caenorhabditis elegans 
and Drosophila melanogaster AD models, respectively. As 
for the probiotics, the species used varied largely. Among 
the 35 studies, a total of 13 studies employed single-strain 
probiotic (including 2 strains of engineered probiotics), and 
the rest ones employed multi-strain probiotic (containing 2 
to 9 probiotic strains) or probiotic-fermented milk or probi-
otic-fermented soybean. There were 4 studies focused on the 
combined effects of probiotics on AD with drug memantine, 
selenium, or exercise, respectively.
Animal model studies
A total of 26 animal model studies concerning the effects 
of probiotic administration on AD were included, among 
which, 24 used mice model, and 2 used Caenorhabditis 
elegans and Drosophila melanogaster AD models, respec-
tively (Table 1).
Single‑strain probiotic studies
There were 10 animal studies which used single-strain 
probiotic. Nimgampalle et  al. evaluated the anti-AD 
potential of Lactobacillus plantarum, and they found that in 
comparison with the control group, L. plantarum MTCC1325 
administration to D-Galactose-induced AD model mice could 
ameliorate cognition deficits and also restore histopathological 
characters [Nimgampalle and Kuna 2017]. Interestingly, as L. 
plantarum MTCC1325 strain can produce the neurotransmitter 
acetylcholine, administration of this probiotic could restore 
the acetylcholine level in the cortex and hippocampus of AD 
model mice [Nimgampalle and Kuna 2017]. Kobayashi et al. 
found that Bifidobacterium breve strain A1 administration 
to Aβ-induced AD model mice could prevent the cognitive 
dysfunction induced by Aβ and also suppress the inflammation 
and immune-reactive gene expressions in hippocampus of 
AD model mice [Kobayashi et al. 2017]. Lee et al. found that 
Bifidobacterium longum NK46 administration could alleviate 
cognitive decline in 5xFAD-transgenic mice through regulating 
gut microbiota lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-mediated nuclear 
factor-kappa B (NF-κB) activation. Probiotic administration 
exhibited modulative effect on gut microbiota composition 
and could suppress the accumulation of a series of parameters 
associated with AD, including Aβ and Aβ accumulation, β/γ-
secretases, and caspase-3 expression, while could increase the 
expression of BDNF in the hippocampus of 5xFAD-Tg mice 
[Lee et al. 2019]. Sun et al. found that probiotic Clostridium 
butyricum administration could significantly alleviate cognitive 
deficits and attenuate microglia-mediated neuroinflammation 
and Aβ deposition, which acted via the regulation of the 
probiotic on gut microbiota and the level of the important 
metabolite butyrate [Sun et al. 2020a,b]. Go et al. found 
that Agathobaculum butyriciproducens administration could 
ameliorate the cognitive impairment and decrease Aβ plaque 
burden and neuroinflammation through attenuating microglial 
activation [Go et al. 2021]. Ou et al. found that Akkermansia 
muciniphila administration exhibited benefits in improving 
glucose tolerance and intestine barrier dysfunction, relieving 
hyperlipidemia, ameliorating the cognitive impairment, and 
also reducing the Aβ plaque burden in the APP/PS1 mice brain 
[Ou et al. 2020].
Two studies employed the Caenorhabditis elegans and 
Drosophila melanogaster AD model to evaluate the effect 
of probiotics on AD, respectively. Employing Caenorhab-
ditis elegans AD model, Cogliati et al. found that probiotic 
Bacillus subtilis NCIB3610 exhibited protective effect on 
age-related neurodegeneration and behavioral deficits and 
could also restore the lifespan level [Cogliati et al. 2020]. 
Employing Drosophila melanogaster AD model, Tan et al. 
indicated that Lactobacillus plantarum DR7 administration 
could alleviate neurodegeneration in the eye and possessed 
modulative effect on the gut microbiota, especially increased 
the abundance of Wolbachia, which was functionally pre-
dicted to be positively associated with neurodegenerative 
disorders like AD [Tan et al. 2020].
Besides, 2 studies have investigated the effect of single 
strain of engineered probiotic on AD. Cecarini et al. evaluated 
the effect of Lactobacillus lactis MG1363 strain harboring 
the pExu plasmid containing a eukaryotic expression cassette 
designed to express the human SQSTM1/p62. They found 
that the administration of this probiotic exhibited benefits on 
memory function improvement, could modulate neuronal 
proteolysis, and attenuate the Aβ levels and inflammatory and 
oxidative processes in 3xTg-AD model mice [Cecarini et al. 
2020]. Fang et al. found that the administration of engineered

Multi‑strain probiotics studies
Athari Nik Azm et al. investigated the effect of probiotics 
containing 4 species of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium 
on insulin resistance biomarkers. They found that probiotic 
administration could decrease the insulin level and homeo-
stasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) 
index, while no significant difference was observed for tri-
glyceride levels between the probiotics group and AD model 
mice group [Athari Nik Azm et al. 2017]. Bonfili et al. 
investigated the effects of a probiotic formulation made of 
9 live bacterial strains on oxidative damages in brain of AD 
transgenic mice, and they found that probiotic administra-
tion could markedly reduce brain oxidative stress through 
activating sirtuin-1-dependent mechanisms [Bonfili et al. 
2018]. Athari Nik Azm et al. found that comparing with 
the Aβ-induced AD model mice group, administration of 
probiotics containing 4 Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium 
species could significantly improve the memory deficit 
and also inhibit the AD-related pathological mechanisms 
[Athari Nik Azm et al. 2018]. Mohammadi et al. indicated 
that probiotic administration of a combination of Lactoba-
cillus helveticus R0052 and Bifidobacterium longum R0175 
could attenuate the detrimental effect of lipopolysaccharide 
on memory by regulating BDNF expression [Mohammadi 
et al. 2019]. Rezaeiasl et al. found that administration of pro-
biotics containing 3 bacterial species might possess effects 
on learning capacity and long-term potentiation, but not 
memory possibly through increased release of presynaptic 
neurotransmitter [Rezaeiasl et al. 2019]. Kaur et al. found 
that probiotics containing a total of 8 different lactic acid 
producing bacteria exerted effects on serum and brain levels 
of lactate and acetate, and the hippocampal expression of the 
neuronal activity marker, c-Fos, also increased [Kaur et al. 
2020a]. Another study by the same group indicated that pro-
biotic administration could significantly decrease intestinal 
inflammation and gut permeability, while no obvious effects 
on Aβ, cytokine, or gliosis levels in the brain were observed 
[Kaur et al. 2020b]. Wang et al. found that 22 weeks of pro-
biotic administration exhibited benefits in improving spatial 
memory impairment, possessed modulation effect on the gut 
microbiota, and could reverse the gut microbiota dysbiosis 
[Wang et al. 2020a]. Yang et al. found that the administration 
of multi-strain probiotics exhibited modulation effect on the 
gut microbiota dysbiosis and could improve the cognitive 
dysfunction in aged mice possibly through inhibiting TLR4- 
and RIG-I-mediated NF-κB signaling pathway [Yang et al. 
2020]. Mehrabadi et al. found that probiotic administration 
exhibited benefits on improving the memory impairment and 
attenuating the oxidative stress and inflammation [Mehrab-
adi and Sadr, 2020]. The study by Bonfili et al. explored the 
effect of probiotics on glucose metabolism in AD model 
mice, and they found that probiotics administration could 
ameliorate impaired glucose metabolism, improve glucose 
uptake through restoring the levels of glucose transporters 
GLUT3 and GLUT1, and reduce phosphorylated tau aggre-
gation in mice brain [Bonfili et al. 2020].
Probiotic‑fermented milk or soybean studies
Two studies have assessed the effects of probiotic-fermented 
milk or probiotic-fermented soybean on AD. The study 
by Musa et al. indicated that Lactobacilli-fermented cow 
milk administration to lipopolysaccharide-induced AD 
model mice could attenuate the memory deficit and improve 
several related antioxidant parameters in mice brain and 
decrease pro-inflammatory parameters in serum [Musa et al. 
2017]. Lee et al. found that Lactobacillus plantarum C29 or 
Lactobacillus plantarum C29-fermented soybean adminis-
tration could alleviate the cognitive decline and affect some 
pathological factors of AD, including inhibiting Aβ plaques 
and β- and γ-secretases, and caspase-3 expressions, increas-
ing BDNF expression, and inhibiting NF-κB activation in 
5xFAD-transgenic mice [Lee et al. 2018].
Probiotics combined with other treatment studies
Three studies have focused on the combined effects of pro-
biotics with selenium, memantine, or exercise, respectively, 
on AD. Abraham et al. indicated that the combination of 
exercise training and probiotic administration could decrease 
AD progression characterized by behavior and histological 
parameters in APP/PS1 transgenic AD mice [Abraham et al. 
2019]. Wang et al. found that the combination of probiotic 
Lactobacillus plantarum with memantine could augment 
the beneficial effects of the later, which included ameliorat-
ing cognitive impairments, attenuating Αβ levels, protect-
ing neuronal integrity and plasticity in the mice hippocam-
pus, and also lowering the plasma trimethylamine N-oxide 
(TMAO) levels [Wang et al. 2020b]. The study by Téglás 
et al. assessed the effect of exercise in combination with pro-
biotics on the liver of APP/PS1 transgenic AD mice, which 
indicated that exercise and probiotics exerted modulative 
effects on the gut microbiota, while could not strongly affect 
the signaling pathways parameters in the mice liver [Téglás 
et al. 2020].

Human studies
A total of 9 human studies, which were published from 
year of 2016 to 2021, were included. The probiotic sup-
plementation period ranged from 4 to 24 weeks. The total 
number of subjects recruited in these clinical studies was 
439, and the number in each study ranged from 13 to 117 
(Table 2).
A randomized, double-blind and controlled trial by 
Akbari et al. investigated the therapeutic potential of pro-
biotic milk on cognitive function and metabolic param-
eters in AD patients. It was indicated that probiotic admin-
istration could improve the cognitive function and affect 
a series of metabolic parameters like plasma malondi-
aldehyde, serum high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and 
triglycerides, homeostasis model of assessment-estimated 
insulin resistance, beta cell function, and quantitative 
insulin sensitivity check index [Akbari et al. 2016]. An 
intervention study by Leblhuber et al. mainly explored the 
beneficial effect of multi-strain probiotic supplementation 
on gut inflammation and immune activation. It was found 
that probiotic supplementation modulated gut microbiota 
composition and tryptophan metabolism in serum. The 
observed correlation between Kyn/Trp and neopterin con-
centrations might reflect the activation of macrophages 
and/or dendritic cells [Leblhuber et al. 2018]. A study by 
Kobayashi et al. investigated the effect of Bifidobacte-
rium breve A1 supplementation on cognitive function of 
elderly subjects with memory complaints. After 12 weeks 
of supplementation, no significant intergroup difference 
regarding changes in scores from the baseline scores was 
found between the probiotic and placebo groups, while a 
significant difference concerning the subscale “immediate 
memory” was observed by a stratified analysis. No signifi-
cant differences regarding blood parameters between the 
probiotic and placebo groups were observed [Kobayashi 
et al. 2019a,b,c]. A double-blind clinical trial by Agahi 
et al. found that for patients with severe AD, probiotic 
supplementation did not affect pronouncedly the cogni-
tive test score, the biochemical parameters like inflam-
matory or anti-inflammatory, the oxidants, or antioxidant 
parameters [Agahi et al. 2018]. A randomized, double-
blind, controlled trial conducted by Tamtaji et al. evalu-
ated the effect of co-supplementation of probiotics and 
selenium to AD patients and found that in comparison 
with only selenium and placebo, co-supplementation of 
probiotics and selenium led to improvement in cognitive 
function and some metabolic profiles, including hs-CRP, 
TAC, GSH, insulin metabolism markers, triglycerides, 
and VLDL-, LDL-, and total-/HDL cholesterol [Tamtaji 
et al. 2019]. An open-label, single-arm study conducted 
by Kobayashi et al. indicated that Bifidobacterium breve 
A1 supplementation to older adults with mild cognitive 
impairment (MCI) for 24 weeks could improve cognitive 
function [Kobayashi et al. 2019a, b, c]. Kobayashi et al. 
also conducted a randomized, double-blind, placebo-con-
trolled trial, which indicated that Bifidobacterium breve 
A1 supplementation conferred benefits on maintaining the 
cognitive function, especially the immediate memory out-
comes and Mini-Mental State Examination total scores, of 
elderly with memory complaints [Kobayashi et al. 2019a, 
b, c]. A further randomized, double-blind, placebo-con-
trolled trial by Xiao et al. investigated the effect of Bifido-
bacterium breve A1 supplementation to restore cognitive 
functions in a physically healthy, suspected MCI older 
adult population. It was found that probiotic supplemen-
tation could improve memory functions of older adults 
with MCI as assessed by several sets of neuropsychologi-
cal tests scores [Xiao et al. 2020]. Ton et al. investigated 
the effects of probiotic-fermented milk supplementation 
in AD patients, and the results indicated that fermented 
milk with kefir grain supplementation could improve cog-
nitive deficits and also exert beneficial effects on systemic 
inflammation, oxidative stress, and blood cell damage [Ton 
et al. 2020]. A recent randomized, double-blind, placebo-
controlled, multicenter trial by Kim et al. showed that 
probiotic supplementation for 12 weeks was beneficial to 
improve mental flexibility test and stress score in com-
munity dwelling older adults, and probiotics could also 
significantly increased serum brain-derived neurotrophic 
factors [Kim et al. 2021].
Conclusions and perspectives
To summarize, we discussed the effect of probiotic adminis-
tration on AD on the basis of the currently available animal 
model and human studies. Despite the outcomes from most 
studies support the beneficial effects of probiotic supple-
mentation for AD in many aspects like cognitive deficit and 
related histological parameters, the following aspects need 
to be further studied.
First, despite in recent years significant progresses have 
been made to elucidate how gut microbiota promoted the 
onset of AD [Rothhammer et al. 2018; Seo and Holtzman, 
2020; Dinan and Cryan 2017; MahmoudianDehkordi et al. 
2019], more efforts are still strongly encouraged on this 
topic, which will provide a prerequisite for more rational 
use of probiotics to combat this disorder. Second, as gut 
microbiota and probiotics could generate a large number of 
biomolecules, and the pathogenesis of AD is rather compli-
cated, despite several pathways like oxidative and inflamma-
tory factors have been proposed to be involved in the effects 
of probiotics for AD, other pathways should exist and remain 
to be elucidated. Third, the number of subjects recruited in

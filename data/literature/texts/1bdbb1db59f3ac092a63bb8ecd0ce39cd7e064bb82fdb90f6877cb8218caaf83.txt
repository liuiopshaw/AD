Biocompatible Polycationic Silver Nanocluster-Impregnated PLGA
Nanocomposites with Potent Antimicrobial Activity
Evelyn Osehontue Uroro,[a] Richard Bright,[a, d] Panthihage Ruvini L. Dabare,[a, d]
Debkumar Bera,[b, c] Jing Yang Quek,[a] Nirmal Goswami,*[b, c] and Krasimir Vasilev*[a, d]
Abstract: Ultrasmall cationic silver nanoparticles (AgNPs)
have recently emerged as highly potent antimicrobial agents
for the treatment of multidrug-resistant bacteria and their
biofilms. However, the clinical application of these cationic
AgNPs is hampered by their poor stability and high reactivity
in solution, leading to uncontrolled release of toxic silver
ions. An ideal platform featuring broad-spectrum antibacte-
rial activity and high biocompatibility that prevents over-
exposure to silver ions, is therefore highly desirable. Herein,
we explored a biocompatible and biodegradable polymer,
poly(lactic-co-glycolic) acid (PLGA) as an effective carrier for
the
recently
discovered
polycationic
silver
nanoclusters
(pAgNCs). These pAgNCs impregnated PLGA nanocomposites
(pAgNCs@PLGA) were developed by water-in-oil-in-water
(W1/O/W2) emulsion method and characterized by various
analytical techniques. Our experimental results reveal that
pAgNCs@PLGA had spherical morphology with an average
diameter of ~188 nm and consists of multiple ultrasmall (~
2 nm) pAgNCs at the polymeric core. The minimum inhibitory
concentration of pAgNCs for Staphylococcus aureus and
Pseudomonas aeruginosa were found to be 6.9 μg/mL. After
impregnation within PLGA, the antimicrobial efficacy of our
pAgNCs against Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas
aeruginosa remained consistent, while the nanocomposites
were biocompatible at the minimum inhibitory concentration
(MIC) against both bacteria. The pAgNCs@PLGA nanocompo-
site developed in this work may present a path forward to
bring these highly potent pAgNCs into medical practice.
Introduction
With the emergence of antimicrobial resistance, the fast-
growing field of nanotechnology has started to offer alter-
natives to traditional antibiotics.[1–4] Nanomaterials have poten-
tial to mitigate the drawbacks of traditional antibiotics due to
their small size, shape, high surface area, and unique phys-
icochemical properties.[5–7] The special properties of nanomate-
rials allow them to fight drug-resistant microbes by a multi-
faceted mechanism thereby preventing the development of
bacterial resistance.[8–10]
Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) as antibacterial agents have
particularly attracted a lot of research interest in recent
times.[11–14] When metallic silver or AgNPs are exposed to
oxygen, silver atoms at the surface oxidise and can be dissolved
in an aqueous environment to form silver ions, which are
biochemically active and can interfere with bacterial cell wall
permeability and cellular metabolism.[15] Further contribution to
the bactericidal effect of silver ions is their affinity to binding
sulfur-containing proteins, which leads to loss of respiratory
functions in bacteria[16–17] and cause oxidative stress.[18–19] Silver
also induces DNA damage, and in this way interferes with
bacterial replication.[20–21]
Silver nanoparticles can be synthesized by either physical or
chemical methods.[22–25] The chemical reduction method in-
volves the use of silver salts (precursors), a reducing agent, a
stabilizing/capping agent, and a solvent. Often the capping and
reducing agents could be the same.[26] The capping agents are
used to prevent aggregation of the synthesized nanoparticles
(NPs) by providing steric or electrostatic repulsion. Without
these, the nanoparticles would coalesce with each other due to
attractive Van-der Waals forces.[27–29] An example of such
capping agent that has been used in the synthesis of various
AgNPs is chitosan.[30] Chitosan is biocompatible and antibacte-
rial in nature on its own. It contains positively charged groups
that can bind to the negatively charged bacterial cell wall
causing disruption.[31–32] Recently, we have developed ultrasmall,
polycationic AgNPs with a particle size of less than 3 nm using
chitosan as both a reducing agent and a capping ligand.[33]
These very small NPs are typically referred as nanoclusters (NCs)
due to their distinct physicochemical properties (referred as
pAgNCs henceforth). These pAgNCs have highly potent anti-
bacterial properties due to their ultrasmall size, cationic surface
charge, and the presence of high percentage of silver ions on
their surface.

While the high antibacterial efficacy of many cationic
molecules
and
nano-antibiotics
are
now
well-established,
cytotoxicity against mammalian cells hampers their application
in clinical studies. To tackle this major challenge, biocompatible
and biodegradable polymers have been used as potential
delivery platforms.[2,34] NPs can be incorporated into biodegrad-
able polymers leading to a controlled release profile assisted by
degradation of the polymeric matrix.[35–36] Poly(lactic-co-glycolic)
acid (PLGA) has been used as a polymer in conjunction with
nanomaterials for antibacterial application due to its unique
properties, e.g. excellent biocompatibility and safety profile,
low level of immunogenicity and toxicity.[37–38] PLGA can be
used
to
encapsulate
both
hydrophobic
and
hydrophilic
drugs[39–40] and nanoparticles.[41] Since AgNPs do not appear to
affect the degradation mechanism of the polymer instead, their
release can be controlled by the polymer degradation rate
which in turn helps to minimize their side effects.[42]
Considering
the
superior
antibacterial
performance
of
pAgNCs against a wide variety of bacteria and their biofilms,[33]
we have used them as model cationic silver-based antimicrobial
for the development of a suitable delivery platform based on
PLGA (pAgNCs@PLGA). We hypothesize that impregnation of
these pAgNCs within the core of PLGA NPs, would produce
pAgNCs@PLGA nanocomposites which are biocompatible with
improved
antimicrobial
performance.
We
have
developed
pAgNCs@PLGA nanocomposites (~188 nm) and studied their
biocompatibility, antibacterial properties, and silver ion release
profiles.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)
TGA is an investigative technique used to determine a material‘s
thermal stability and its fraction of volatile components. The
analyses carried out for chitosan, pAgNCs, PLGA NPs, and
pAgNCs@PLGA are shown in Figure 3B. For chitosan, three
weight loss stages occur: the first stage weight loss of about 9%
occurs when the temperature increases from 20°C to 107°C,
which can be attributed to loss of absorbed water[45] as
polysaccharides are easily hydrated due to strong affinity to
water. The second stage is in the temperature range of 250°C~
400°C and is associated with a weight loss of about 57%. This
stage can be attributed to the degradation of polysaccharide as
well as the depolymerization and decomposition of acetylated
and deacetylated units.[46] The final stage between 400°C and
700°C can be associated with the complete decomposition of
chitosan. This decomposition corresponds to the thermal
degradation of the polysaccharide and vaporization.
Four weight loss stages were observed in the TGA thermo-
gram of pAgNCs. The first stage occurs at around 160°C and
ends at about 180°C, displaying a weight loss of about 17%.
Thereafter, the second weight loss of about 27% occurs when
the temperature increases from 210°C to 270°C. The third
weight loss stage starts as the temperature increases from
293°C to 416°C. The weight loss at this stage was 42%, which
corresponds to the original chitosan content.[47] The final weight
loss stage of about 55% occurs as the temperature increases
from around 416°C to 700°C.

Release Study
Finally, we wanted to know the rate of silver release from the
pAgNCs@PLGA. The release study was carried out in phosphate-
buffered saline for up to 24 hours. The release kinetics was
determined by measuring the mass percentage of silver ion
released at specific intervals over the time course of the study.
Figure 4
shows
the
release
profile
of
silver
ions
from
pAgNCs@PLGA. The data indicates that an initial fast release
occurs in the first 3 h when about 50% of silver is released. This
is followed by slow release leading to approximately 70% of
silver being released after 24 h. The initial fast release of Ag
ions may be due to the diffusion of silver ions absorbed at the
surface or just beneath the surface of PLGA.[48] The slow-release
which follows the initial outburst occurs when the polymer is
hydrolyzed into its soluble products.[48–49]
Antibacterial Studies

with Gram-negative P. aeruginosa and Gram-positive S. aureus,
both being important clinically relevant pathogens. The pAgNCs
alone exhibited excellent antibacterial activity against these

This high antibacterial potency was confirmed in this study.

Conclusion
In summary, we have synthesized chitosan-capped ultrasmall
pAgNCs and subsequently impregnated them into PLGA nano-
spheres using the double phase emulsification method. The
obtained pAgNCs@PLGA nanocomposites showed an encapsu-
lation efficiency, reaching ~12%, and had controlled release
properties. The antibacterial tests against bacteria known to
cause serious infections, i.e., S. aureus and P. aeruginosa
revealed the high efficiency of pAgNCs in their encapsulated
form. Our experimental data revealed that pAgNCs@PLGA
nanocomposites not only showed high bacterial killing effi-
ciency like pAgNCs but also offer controlled leaching of silver
ions due to the gradual degradation of PLGA NPs. The
pAgNCs@PLGA nanocomposites also had good biocompatibil-
ity. The results presented here highlight a promising delivery
system that can be potentially used in antibacterial applica-
tions.
Experimental section
Materials
Silver Nitrate (AgNO3) with 99.99% purity, sodium hydroxide
(NaOH), chitosan (medium molecular weight), poly (lactic-co-
glycolic acid) 50:50 (PLGA) resomer RG 502 (Mw=7,000–17000),
polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) (Mw=9,000–10,000, 80% hydrolyzed) and
dichloromethane (DCM) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, (MI,
USA). Phosphate buffer saline (PBS) tablets were purchased from
Thermo Fisher Scientific, (MA, USA). Acetic acid was purchased from
Chem-Supply Australia (Adelaide, Australia). Ultrapure water with
18.2 mΩ
(Millipore)
was
used
for
all
experiments
otherwise
mentioned.
Synthesis of polycationic silver nanoclusters (pAgNCs)
The synthesis of the pAgNCs was done following our previously
published protocol with a slight modification.[33] First, 5 mg/mL
chitosan solution was prepared in 1% acetic acid (v/v). Next, 10 mL
of the 5 mg/mL chitosan solution was added to 39 mL of Milli Q
water under continuous stirring (500 rpm) at room temperature.
Subsequently, 1 mL of 100 mM AgNO3 was added into the aqueous
dispersion under continuous stirring. After 3 min, 1 M NaOH was
added dropwise to adjust the pH to 6.5 and the solution was left to
stir at 500 rpm for 24 h at room temperature. The change from
cloudy colorless solution to clear brown color confirmed the
formation of pAgNCs. Finally, aqueous solution of pAgNCs was
purified through dialysis (MWCO 3.5 kDa) against Milli Q water and
then, lyophilized using a ModulyoD freeze dryer (Thermo Fisher
Scientific, MA, USA) for 72 h and stored as powder for further use.
Synthesis of PLGA NPs and pAgNCs@PLGA nanocomposites:
The water-in-oil-in-water (W1/O/W2) method was used to prepare
pAgNCs@PLGA nanocomposites (Scheme S1). In a typical synthesis,
200 mg of Poly Lactic-co-Glycolic Acid (PLGA) was first dissolved in
30 mL DCM to form the oil phase. The W1 phase was then prepared
by sonicating 105 mg of the freeze-dried pAgNCs in 15 mL of Milli-
Q water. Next, the W1 phase was sonicated with the oil phase to
form the W1/O phase under ice condition for 1 min. Thereafter, the
W1/O phase was added dropwise to 60 mL of 2.0% PVA solution
under
mild
stirring
(500 rpm)
condition.
The
emulsion
was
sonicated for 1 min to form the W1/O/W2 phase. Subsequently, the
emulsion was diluted with 300 mL of 0.3% PVA solution and then
kept overnight for solvent evaporation and particle hardening.[54]
The resulting dispersion was centrifuged at 23,200 g using a
Thermo Scientific Sorvall RC 6+ centrifuge for 15 min and washed
thrice with Milli-Q water to remove excess PVA and free pAgNCs.
Finally, the as-synthesized nanocomposites were lyophilized using a
ModulyoD Freeze Dryer for 72 h. Blank PLGA nanoparticles with no
pAgNCs (PLGA NPs) in the water (W1) phase was prepared using a
similar method where 200 mg of PLGA was first dissolved in 30 mL
DCM to form the oil phase. Next, the W1 phase (milli-Q water
without freeze-dried pAgNCs) was sonicated with the oil phase to
form the W1/O phase under ice condition for 1 min. Thereafter, the
W1/O phase was added dropwise to 60 mL of 2.0% PVA solution
under
mild
stirring
(500 rpm)
condition.
The

An Agilent 8900 Triple Quadrupole ICP-MS with octupole reaction

In Vitro Release Study
To conduct the release study, 2 mg of lyophilized powder was
dispersed in 3 mL of phosphate saline (PBS) buffer with a pH of 7.4
in a dialysis tube (Pur-A-Lyzer Dialysis membrane, MWCO 12–
14 kDa, Sigma-Aldrich, MI, USA). The dialysis tube was immersed in
a closed tube containing 50 mL of PBS with a pH of 7.4 and was
placed on a shaker at 100 rpm at 37°C. At 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 24 h,
a 1 mL aliquot was removed and replaced with fresh buffer to keep
the volume consistent. The samples were then digested overnight
with 1 mL of nitric acid, and 7 mL of milli-Q water was added to
make 10 mL. The 10 mL sample was further centrifuged at 3760 g
for 15 min and the supernatant analyzed using Agilent Technolo-
gies 8800 ICP-MS Triple Quad (Agilent Technologies, Stockport, UK).
The cumulative percentage amount of silver ion release was
obtained by dividing the cumulative amount of silver release at
each sampling interval by the initial amount of sample and
multiplied by 100 (Equation 3).[55] The experiment was performed in
triplicate.

Antibacterial Properties of pAgNCs, pAgNCs@PLGA and PLGA
The antibacterial properties of pAgNCs, pAgNCs@PLGA and PLGA
NPs were tested against Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S.
aureus) ATCC 25923 and Gram-negative Pseudomonas aeruginosa
(P. aeruginosa) ATCC 15692 which are two problematic opportun-
istic pathogens.
MIC and MBC Determination on pAgNCs and pAgNCs@PLGA
To evaluate the MIC, the standard broth dilution method was used
to

of
pAgNCs
and

pAgNCs@PLGA in tryptone soy broth (TSB; ThermoFisher Scientific,
MA, USA), with Ag concentrations restarting from 54.7 μg/mL or
8.3 μg/mL, respectively with adjusted bacterial concentration of 2×

controls were incubated at 24 h at 37°C. The optical density was

at

using
a

(BioTek, Synergy HT, VT, USA). The MIC endpoint was determined as
the lowest concentration of nanoparticles with no absorbance
reading. Aliquots of 50 μL from the wells showing no growth were
seeded on TSB agar plate and incubated for 24 h at 37°C. Plates
with no bacterial growth from the lowest concentration of Ag was
determined
to

the

with

Agar Well Diffusion Assay
Well
diffusion
assay
was
performed
on
both
pAgNCs
and

PLGA NP was used as control. The pAgNCs were prepared by

were aseptically made using a modified 1000 μL pipette tip onto
the agar plates, and aliquots of 100 μL of each test compounds
were inoculated into the wells. After 24 h incubation at 37°C, the
zone of inhibition diameter was measured using a meter rule.

Statistical Analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism ver-
sion 8.3.0 (San Diego California, USA, www.graphpad.com). Statis-
tical significance was analyzed by means of one-way ANOVA
followed by post hoc analysis using a Tukey’s multiple comparisons
test. Particle size distribution was done using ImageJ version 1.52a
(NIH, USA, http://imagej.nih.gov/ij). All experiments were performed
in triplicate. A p-value less than 0.05 was deemed to be statistically
significant.

Marrying luminescent Au nanoclusters to TiO2 for
visible-light-driven antibacterial application†
Haiguang Zhu,
‡a Naiwei Liu,‡a Ziping Wang,b Qiang Xue,a Qing Wang,a
Xiaomeng Wang,a Yong Liu,
a Zhengmao Yina and Xun Yuan
*a
Long-lasting yet visible-light-driven bacterial inhibition is highly desired for environmental protection and
public health maintenance. However, conventional semiconductors such as titanium dioxide (TiO2) are
impotent for such antibacterial application due to their low utilization rate for visible light. Herein we report
the design of a long-lasting yet visible-light-driven antibacterial agent based on marrying luminescent Au
nanoclusters (Au NCs for short) to TiO2 (TiO2-NH2@Au NCs). The as-obtained TiO2-NH2@Au NC antibacter-
ial agent not only possesses superior utilization for visible light due to the participation of Au NCs as a good
photosensitizer, but also has excellent separation eﬃcacy of photogenerated carriers, thereby eﬃciently
enhancing the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) for killing bacteria. Consequently, the TiO2-
NH2@Au NCs display excellent antibacterial activity with good durability against both Gram-positive and
Gram-negative bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus (99.37%) and Escherichia coli (99.92%) under visible-
light irradiation (λ ≥400 nm). This study is interesting because it provides a paradigm change in the design of
long-lasting yet visible-light-driven NC-based antibacterial agents for diversiﬁed bactericidal applications.

Therefore, tremendous eﬀorts have been devoted to the devel-
opment of high-eﬃciency antibacterial agents such as antibac-
terial peptides and inorganic metallic-based bactericides for
decades.4–8 Recently, ultrasmall metal nanoclusters (NCs) have
emerged as a promising class of broad-spectrum antibacterial
agents.9–13 For example, Ag NCs have been demonstrated to be
eﬃcient in fighting bacterial infections owing to their ultra-
small

wide-spectrum

low

and

Very

bacterial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative
bacteria.17 However, similar to antibiotics, the bactericidal
actions of such NC-based antibacterial agents are all based on
their own sacrifice while killing bacteria via metal ion release
or cell adhesion/internalization.12,18 This bactericidal mode
makes them impotent in long-lasting bacterial inhibition as
they can be quickly consumed by bacteria.11,16,19 In fact, the
diversity of bactericidal applications (e.g., bacterial inhibition
of outdoor facilities) highly demands the design of long-acting
antibacterial agents with diﬀerent bactericidal behavior from
previous metal NC-based antibacterial agents, which may
further extend their application territory.16,20–23

metal NC-based hybrid materials have been designed for
synergistic antibacterial application,19,20,47 studies on visible-

Herein we report our design on a visible-light-driven anti-
bacterial agent based on marrying highly luminescent Au NCs
to TiO2 nanoparticles. The key design is the usage of 3-amino-
propyl-triethoxysilane (APTES) as a linker to chemically graft
glutathione (GSH)-protected Au NCs on the TiO2 surface via a
1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochlo-ride

(NHS)
crosslinking
strategy
(Scheme 1). Furthermore, the marriage of highly luminescent
Au NCs and TiO2 nanoparticles ensures the high visible-light
absorption eﬃciency as well as the separation eﬃciency of the
photo-generated charge carriers,35,48 augmenting the pro-
duction of abundant ROS. Consequently, outstanding visible-
light-driven antibacterial activities against both Gram-negative
and Gram-positive bacteria are achieved with excellent dura-
bility. This study is interesting not only because it provides a
novel visible-light-driven yet long-acting NC-based photo-
dynamic antibacterial agent but also because it suﬃces the
specific need of some antibacterial terminal applications.

2.3.
Synthesis of highly luminescent Au NCs with
aggregation induced emission (AIE)
Highly luminescent AIE-featured Au NCs were synthesized
according to a previously reported procedure with a minor
modification.49,50 Firstly, freshly prepared aqueous solutions
of GSH (20 mM, 15 mL) and HAuCl4 (20 mM, 10 mL) were
added into a flask (100 mL) containing 25 mL of ultrapure
water. After that, the reaction mixture was kept stirring for
1.5 h at room temperature to colorless, and then heated at
85 °C for 2 h under stirring conditions. The AIE-featured Au
NCs were collected and purified by centrifuging the mixture
(10 mL Au NCs and 30 mL acetonitrile) at 8000 rpm for 5 min.
The obtained pellets of Au NCs were redissolved in 10 mL of
ultrapure water for further use.
2.4.
Preparation of TiO2-NH2
TiO2 nanoparticles (200 mg) were mixed with isopropanol
(15 mL) and APTES (25 μL), and the mixture was then heated
at 80 °C for 2 h under stirring conditions. After that, the sus-
pension was centrifuged and then washed with ethanol three
times to obtain TiO2-NH2. TiO2-NH2 with diﬀerent NH2
loading amounts were prepared by changing the dosage of
APTES. The obtained samples were named TiO2-NH2-X (note:
X = 1, 2, and 3, corresponding to the dosage of APTES 15, 25,
and 35 μL, respectively).
2.5.
Preparation of visible-light-driven TiO2-NH2@Au NC
antibacterial agents
The solution of AIE-featuring Au NCs (3 mL, 4 mM) was intro-
duced into a 20 mL round-bottomed flask containing 3 mL of
MES (3.9 mg·mL−1), and then was heated to 65 °C under stir-
ring conditions. After that, 0.49 mg of EDC and 0.13 mg of
NHS were separately added into the flask. After stirring for
15 min, 100 mg of TiO2-NH2 was added into the reaction solu-
tion, followed by stirring at 65 °C for 6 h to ensure the linking
between Au NCs and the TiO2-NH2 via the amidation reaction.
Finally, the mixture was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min and
then washed with ultrapure water three times to obtain TiO2-
NH2@Au NCs. By varying the concentration of the introduced
Au NC solutions from 4 mM to 8 mM, 12 mM, 16 mM, and
20 mM, an additional four TiO2-NH2@Au NC samples could
be obtained under the same fabrication conditions. These
samples were named TiO2-NH2@Au NCs-X (note: X = 1, 2, 3, 4
and 5, which correspond to samples with diﬀerent concen-
trations of introduced Au NCs from 4 mM, 8 mM, 12 mM,
16 mM, and 20 mM, respectively).
2.6.
Preparation of the TiO2 + Au NC reference sample
The reference sample, TiO2 + Au NCs, was synthesized by phys-
ically mixing Au NCs (3 mL, 12 mM) with the pristine TiO2
solution (100 mg of TiO2 in 3 mL of water) under stirring con-
ditions at room temperature for 6 h. The Au NCs could interact
with the pristine TiO2 via electrostatic attraction. Afterwards,
the mixture was centrifuged, washed several times to remove
free Au NCs, and finally dried in an oven at 35 °C.
2.7.
Preparation of the Au25(SG)18 reference sample
Non-luminescent Au25(SG)18 NCs were synthesized according
to a previously reported reference. In a typical synthesis of
Au25(SG)18, aqueous solutions of HAuCl4 (20 mM, 250 μL) and
GSH (50 mM, 200 μL) were mixed in water (1.15 mL) and
ethanol (3 mL). An aqueous NaOH solution (1 M, 0.3 mL) was
then introduced into the reaction mixture, followed by the
addition of 0.1 mL of NaBH4 solution (prepared by dissolving
43 mg of NaBH4 powder in 10 mL of 0.2 M NaOH solution).
The Au25(SG)18 were collected after 3 h.51
2.8.
Photodynamic antibacterial test
Gram-negative E. coli and P. aeruginosa, and Gram-positive
S. aureus and B. subtilis were used as bacteria models to evalu-
ate
the
photodynamic
bactericidal
activity
of
the
TiO2-
NH2@Au NC antibacterial agent under visible light irradiation.
All the glassware and medium solutions were sterilized in an
autoclave at 121 °C for 30 min before the antibacterial test. All
tests were performed under sterile conditions. The bacterial
cells were cultured in the LB medium in an orbital shaker at
37 °C for 18 h, and then centrifuged at 8000 rpm to remove
the metabolites, and finally diluted with PBS solution (0.01 M,
pH 7.4) to prepare the bacterial suspension with a cell count of
107 colony forming units per milliliter (CFU mL−1). In a typical
antibacterial test, 12 mg of TiO2-NH2@Au NC antibacterial
agent was added into 5 mL of bacterial suspension, which was
irradiated by a commercial 300 W xenon lamp with an optical
cut-oﬀfilter (λ > 400 nm, 150 mW cm−2). Afterwards, 100 μL of
the bacterial suspension was pipetted and spread on the fresh
agar plate at given irradiation time intervals (0, 15, 30, 45,
60 min), and the plate was then cultured at 37 °C for 24 h.
Finally, the colonies growing on the plate were counted to
confirm the number of viable bacteria after incubation. In
addition, corresponding control tests (e.g., bacteria with or
without antibacterial agents under visible light irradiation or
dark conditions) were performed under the same conditions.
2.9.
Cytotoxicity assessment
The cytotoxicity of the antibacterial agent was evaluated by
cell proliferation kit (MTT) with the HeLa cell as a cell
model. Firstly, the cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and
cultured under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2)
for 24 h. TiO2-NH2@Au NCs (0.6 mM) were put into wells for
illumination and darkness treatments. After the post-seeding
for 24 h, 20 μL of MTT solution was added into the wells
and allowed to continue to culture in the incubator for 4 h.
Afterwards, the MTT solution was discarded, and DMSO was
added to the wells. After stationary culture for 30 min, the
optical density of the supernatants in the 96-well plate was
measured at 570 nm using a Tecan Spark® multimode
microplate reader.

3.
Results and discussion
3.1.
Synthesis and characterization of the TiO2-NH2@Au NC
antibacterial agents
The highly luminescent Au NCs used in this study were pre-
pared through a simple one-pot method. As shown in Fig. 1a,
the as-synthesized Au NCs show yellowish color under visible
light irradiation (item 1 in the inset) and show an obvious
absorption band at 380–450 nm in the UV-visible absorption
spectrum (black curve), which suggests the good visible-light
harvesting capability of Au NCs.35 Interestingly, such Au NCs
also emit intense orange luminescence with an emission peak
at 610 nm under UV light irradiation (red curve, and item 2 in
the inset). Both the optical absorption and the luminescence
feature of the sample clearly suggest the successful synthesis
of ultrasmall Au NCs, excluding the possibility of forming
large-sized non-luminescent Au nanoparticles (>3 nm) with a
typical
surface
plasmon
resonance
(SPR)
absorption
at
∼520 nm. As shown in Fig. 1b, the TEM image confirms that
the as-prepared Au NCs are all below 2 nm in size with an
average size of ∼1.4 nm (inset).49,52
The as-synthesized Au NCs possess a high luminescence
quantum yield (>10% in solid), which may be beneficial to
enhance the photodynamic antibacterial activity due to the
easy formation of photogenerated charge carriers.35 Another
salient feature of the Au NCs is their large Stokes shift
(>200 nm) with the excitation peak at 380 nm, which indicates
that the luminescence of the Au NCs is generally attributed to
the relaxation of ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) or
ligand-to-metal–metal
charge
transfer
(LMMCT).49,53–55
In
addition, our previous reports confirmed that this type of Au
NCs has a typical AIE feature with a long luminescence life-
time up to several microseconds,49,50 which originates from
the unique Au(0)@Au(I)-GSH structure and could further facili-
tate the photodynamic antibacterial performance in terms of
retarding the recombination of photogenerated charge car-
riers. Owing to the unique Au(0)@Au(I)-GSH core–shell struc-
ture as well as the trait of high-temperature synthesis, the
obtained Au NCs have extremely high structural stability and
photostability, which may guarantee excellent durability in
diverse applications.
Prior to crosslinking the GSH-protected Au NCs with TiO2,
the TiO2 nanoparticles were first modified by APTES to intro-
duce abundant –NH2 groups on their surface, which enabled
the grafting of the Au NCs containing carboxyl groups on the
TiO2 surface via a simple amidation reaction for the formation
of the photodynamic antibacterial agent (TiO2-NH2@Au NCs). It
should be mentioned that the amination of TiO2 nanoparticles
by APTES did not obviously alter their size and morphologies
except for the formation of an amorphous thin layer, which was
evidenced by their TEM images before and after surface modifi-
cation (Fig. S1†). After grafting Au NCs, it is observed that ultra-
small Au NCs are uniformly distributed on the surface of the
TiO2-NH2 according to their TEM images with diﬀerent magnifi-
cations (Fig. 2a–c). Furthermore, EDX elemental mapping was
also used to investigate the elemental distribution in the TiO2-
NH2@Au NC sample. As shown in Fig. 2c, elements of Ti, O,
Au, N and S were distributed uniformly across the TiO2-
NH2@Au NCs. In addition, the XPS results (Fig. S2 and ESI
Note I†) and the FTIR spectra (Fig. S3 and ESI Note II†) provide
supportive evidence of the successful grafting of Au NCs on the
TiO2-NH2 via the formation of amide bonds,45 which could be
favorable for enhancing the photodynamic antibacterial activity
and durability of the antibacterial agent in bactericidal appli-
cation. Taken together, we may conclude that the Au NCs were
successfully deposited on the TiO2-NH2 surface.

stability of the TiO2-NH2@Au NC photocatalyst.
3.3.
The antibacterial mechanism of the TiO2-NH2@Au NCs

visible-light-driven antibacterial activity of TiO2-NH2@Au NCs,
the optical absorption properties of those antibacterial agents
were first evaluated because their high light-harvesting capacity
is the prerequisite for high-eﬃcacy photodynamic bacterial
killing. As evidenced by the UV-visible DRS curves (Fig. 4a) and
Kubelka–Munk plots (the inset of Fig. 4a), the pristine TiO2

electron pair in –NH2 groups,56 suggesting that the NH2 groups
on TiO2 can improve the light absorption capability to some
extent. Upon introducing Au NCs on the surface of TiO2 and
TiO2-NH2, both TiO2 + Au NCs and TiO2-NH2@Au NCs show
excellent light-harvesting capacity in the visible light region
because of their narrow bandgaps (3.09 eV for TiO2 + Au NCs,

for photocatalytic killing of bacteria by oxidatively damaging the
nucleic acids and proteins of bacteria.3,58 In this study, the ROS
generation ability of various photodynamic antibacterial agents

and dichlorofluorescein (DCF) dye as an indicator. As shown in

level among all the antibacterial agents, which is consistent with
its photodynamic antibacterial performance under visible light
irradiation. In addition, the morphologies of S. aureus (round)
and E. coli (rod-shaped) changed from plump and smooth to

ment (Fig. S13†). Such severe morphological changes of bacteria
manifested that the cell membrane of bacteria had been
damaged by the photo-induced ROS, which led to the ultimate
inactivation or dysfunction of the cell metabolism.
Based on the above analysis, a plausible photodynamic bac-
tericidal mechanism over the TiO2-NH2@Au NCs was proposed
and is shown in Fig. 5. Specifically, the chemically grafted Au

photosensitizer to harvest visible light to promote the gene-

TiO2 via amide bonds and finally migrate to the surface of
TiO2, reacting with the dissolved oxygen (O2) for the pro-
duction of ROS. In addition, the electron-rich amino groups
on the TiO2-NH2 surface can further enhance their surface
charge density via charge transfer, leading to the generation of
more ROS. Taken together, under visible light irradiation, the

4.
Conclusions
In summary, we have successfully developed a long-acting and
wide-spectrum Au NC-based antibacterial agent for visible-
light-driven bacterial killing by marrying ultrasmall Au NCs to
the amino-functionalized TiO2 via simple amidation. The as-
synthesized TiO2-NH2@Au NCs exhibited enhanced antibacter-
ial activity with ∼3 orders of magnitude higher than the pris-
tine TiO2 and ∼2 orders of magnitude higher than the sample
of TiO2 + Au NCs. It was found that marrying Au NCs to TiO2-
NH2 nanoparticles is beneficial for visible light harvesting and
the separation/transfer of charge carriers from Au NCs to TiO2-
NH2, which facilitated the generation of abundant ROS for bac-
terial killing. In addition, the TiO2-NH2@Au NC antibacterial
agent shows good biocompatibility and recyclability (more
than 4 times), implying the promising potential in long-lasting
photodynamic antibacterial terminal applications. We believe
that the interesting strategy and design in this work would
shed light on the design of other novel photodynamic antibac-
terial agents with desired functions.

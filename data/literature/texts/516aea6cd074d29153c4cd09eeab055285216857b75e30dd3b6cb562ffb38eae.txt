Nanomaterial-based antimicrobial photodynamic therapy (aPDT) is a promising approach for the
eradication of drug-resistant bacterial biofilms. However, the intrinsic cytotoxicity of commonly used
nanomaterials and their limited capacity to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) have remained major
shortcomings for their use in aPDT. In this study, we report a nanocomposite design strategy that
integrates a non-toxic, well-defined antimicrobial gold nanocluster protected with mercaptopropionic
acid (denoted Au18(MPA)14 NC), and a photosensitizer, protoporphyrin (PpIX), within a chitosan polymer
matrix (the nanocomposite is denoted as PpIX–Chito–Au18). The nanocomposite showed B2 times
higher ROS generation capacity upon white light irradiation and thus effectively killed both Gram-
positive and Gram-negative pathogens by damaging their membrane and DNA. Furthermore, our confo-
cal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) data demonstrated that the PpIX–Chito–Au18 nanocomposite
(white light irradiated) has better biofilm penetration and elimination capacity (against both S. aureus and
P. aeruginosa biofilms) than Au18(MPA)14 NCs alone or the nanocomposite (under dark conditions),
revealing the efficacy of the combinatorial therapy through a synergistic killing mechanism. Our work
deepens the understanding of the importance of combinatorial antimicrobial therapy using metal NCs
and offers the opportunity to explore them in various areas where drug-resistant pathogens are
concerned.
Introduction
Drug-resistant pathogens have become a serious threat to
global public health.1–3 The pathogens resistant to commonly
used antibiotics and responsible for the majority of hospital
and community-related infections are Enterococcus faecium,
Staphylococcus
aureus,
Klebsiella
pneumoniae,
Acinetobacter
baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter species
(also generally identified as ‘‘ESKAPE’’).1,4 The resistance could
enhance dramatically when these pathogens form biofilms.
Biofilms are surface-attached communities of bacteria enclosed
in an extracellular matrix of substance that displays a high level
of antibiotic tolerance.5–7 They are responsible for almost 80%
of the hospital-related infections, showing about a 1000-fold
increase in resistance compared to their planktonic counterparts.7–9
Recently, nanomaterials have emerged as an alternative to
antibiotics for the treatment of drug-resistant bacteria.10–13
Some of these nanomaterials could kill pathogens directly
(e.g., silver nanoparticles (NPs), copper NPs, ZnO and TiO2
NPs) by their multifaceted mechanism of action, while others
are used as carriers to improve the therapeutic efficacy of drugs
(e.g., antibiotics). Over the past few years, we have seen rapid
growth in this field, and particularly, significant attention has
been paid on improving the antimicrobial efficacy of these
nanomaterials by tailoring their size, shape, and surface pro-
perties. Indeed, some of them showed broad-spectrum activity
against drug-resistant pathogens and are currently being used
in various products available in the market. However, these
antimicrobial nanomaterials do have inherent drawbacks.
In particular, their cytotoxicity and poor clearance remain
major limitations for their use in the clinic.14–16 In this
context, gold (Au)-based nanomaterials have huge potential
due to their non-toxicity, versatility in surface modification,

shown to treat intestinal pathogens without affecting gut

inside the liver and spleen has hindered their translation from

On the other hand, Au nanoclusters (NCs), ultrasmall NPs of
sizes less than 2 nm, have received immense attention in recent
years. Au NCs exhibit unique physicochemical properties that
are distinct from those of their larger counterparts (i.e.,
Au NPs).22–26 Recently, our group reported the antibacterial
properties of Au NCs with a broad spectrum of activity.27 Unlike
Au NPs that exert antibacterial effects through attached anti-
biotics or antibacterial agents, Au NCs as a whole entity exhibit
antibacterial properties.28–32 Though Au NCs or Au NC-based
nanocomposites developed so far are found to be effective
against planktonic bacteria, their efficiency against established
and matured biofilms is rarely reported.33–35 Therefore, a
considerable amount of effort is needed to develop an efficient
Au NC based platform that can eliminate both the planktonic
and
biofilm
forms
of
Gram-positive
and
Gram-negative
pathogens.
Herein, we employ an antimicrobial photodynamic therapy
(aPDT) approach to eradicate both the planktonic and biofilm
forms of Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive) and Pseudomonas
aeruginosa (Gram-negative). The aPDT approach relies on three
main components: a photosensitizer (PS), light, and oxygen.
In the presence of a suitable wavelength of light and oxygen,
photosensitizers will be activated and subsequently produce
reactive oxygen species (ROS). ROS, including singlet oxygen
(1O2), superoxide anions, and hydroxyl radicals, exhibit a broad
spectrum of activity by destroying numerous molecular targets
(e.g., proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids), thereby making it
unlikely for microbes to develop resistance.36–39 Various photo-
sensitizer molecules were used recently for antibacterial appli-
cation. Among them, porphyrins have a special advantage
in eradicating broad-spectrum pathogens.40–43 In this study,
protoporphyrin (PpIX) was chosen for a dual purpose: (i) to act
as a traceable moiety due to its high luminescence pro-
perties and (ii) to perform aPDT for its high 1O2 yield.44–46
We hypothesize that combining PpIX with antimicrobial Au
NCs could serve as an efficient synergistic therapeutic strategy
in combating drug-resistant pathogens. To prove that, we
used negatively-charged, well-defined mercaptopropionic acid
(MPA)-protected Au NCs (i.e., Au18(MPA)14 NCs) as model anti-
microbial nanomaterials, and conjugated them with PpIX by
using a polymeric linker. Chitosan, a cationic polysaccharide,
was particularly chosen for this purpose for its good biocom-
patibility and rich surface functionality.47–49 Chitosan-based
nanocomposites have also been shown to enhance the efficacy
of the individual components in a nanocomposite.50–53 The as-
designed nanocomposites were studied initially on planktonic
Gram-positive S. aureus and Gram-negative P. aeruginosa strains
and then subsequently on their corresponding biofilm forms.
Our experimental findings demonstrate that the combinatorial
approach (combining the anti-bacterial effects of Au18 NCs and
aPDT) is more effective than the individual approaches. Our
work thus paves the way for the design and synthesis of Au
NC-based composites for their applications in the medical field
and beyond.

Preparation of chitosan stock solution
Chitosan stock solution was prepared by mixing 50 mg of
chitosan powder with 9.6 mL of ultrapure water in the presence
of 400 mL of HCl (1 M) under gentle stirring (500 rpm) for 1 h.
The reaction took place at room temperature to produce a stock
solution with a final chitosan concentration of 5 mg mL1.
Synthesis of Au18(MPA)14 NCs
The synthesis of Au18(MPA)14 NCs is based on a previously
reported protocol.57,58 In a typical synthesis, aqueous solutions
of HAuCl4 (20 mM, 2.5 mL) and MPA (50 mM, 2 mL) were mixed
in water (45.5 mL), leading to the formation of Au(I)–MPA-
complexes. After 2 min, an aqueous NaOH solution (1 M) was
introduced to increase the reaction mixture pH to 11.0. Then
1 bar CO was bubbled for 2 min and the reaction setup was kept
undisturbed for 24 h at room temperature at 500 rpm. Finally,
the resulting aqueous solution of green Au18 (MPA)14 NCs was
obtained and further purified by ultrafiltration using a stirred
ultrafiltration cell (model 8010, Millipore Corporation, USA)
with a semipermeable membrane of 3000 Da (3 kDa) MWCO for
further use.
Conjugation of Au18 (MPA)14 NCs with chitosan (Chito-Au18)
In a typical synthesis, purified Au18(MPA)14 NCs (hereafter
referred to as Au18 NCs) were mixed under stirring with freshly
prepared aqueous solutions of EDC (100 mM, 0.1 mL) and NHS
(100 mM, 0.1 mL) at pH 6.0. After activating the carboxylic
groups of MPA-protected Au18 NCs, they were added dropwise
into the chitosan solution (5 mg mL1, 1 mL) under stirring at
500 rpm at room temperature with a reaction mixture pH of
6.0–6.5. The formed Chito–Au18 nanocomposite collected after
4 h was further purified using a dialysis membrane of 10 kDa
MWCO to remove unconjugated Au18 NCs.
Preparation of the PpIX–Chito–Au18 nanocomposite
PpIX (65 mg), DCC (52 mg), and NHS (29 mg) were first
dissolved in anhydrous DMSO and kept stirring in an inert
atmosphere for 12–16 h in the dark at room temperature. The
activated PpIX molecules were then added to the dissolved
chitosan solution (5 mg mL1, 2 mL) which was co-incubated
with Au18 NCs (1 mM, 1 mL), EDC (100 mM, 0.1 mL) and NHS
(100 mM, 0.1 mL). The reaction mixture pH was carefully
adjusted to 6.0 (not above that to avoid precipitation of the
solution) and kept stirring for 2 h at room temperature. The
formed nanocomposite was then purified through dialysis
using a 10 kDa membrane.
Bacterial cell growth
Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Gram-
negative Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) strains from
glycerol stock were streaked onto an LB agar plate (15 w/v%
agar in LB medium) and incubated at 37 1C for 12 h to form
bacterial colonies. For each experiment, a single isolated colony
of bacteria was picked from the fresh LB agar plate and
cultured in LB medium in a shaker incubator pre-set to
37 1C, 200 rpm for 8–12 h. The cultured cells were then diluted
by observing their optical density at 600 nm (OD600) to 0.1
(B1  108 CFU mL1).
Measurement of extracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS)
generation
All the samples (i.e., Au18 NCs, Chito–Au18, and PpIX–Chito–
Au18) were incubated in LB medium without bacterial cells for
2 h. Meanwhile, DPBF stock solution was prepared by dissol-
ving DPBF in oxygen-saturated dimethylformamide (DMF)
(1 mM). To the incubated samples, DPBF (100 mM) was
added, then the samples were irradiated under white light
(100 mW cm2) for 5 min. The detection of extracellular ROS
was done by observing their absorbance at 410 nm. The same
concentration of DPBF in LB medium alone (without samples)
was used as control.
Determination of the antibacterial eﬃcacy of the formed
nanocomposite (PpIX–Chito–Au18)
First, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values
of the formed nanocomposite (PpIX–Chito–Au18) on both the
bacterial strains of S. aureus and P. aeruginosa were deter-
mined. The nanocomposite at various concentrations was
serially diluted in PBS and added to 200 mL of bacterial
suspension of 1  108 CFU mL1 in a 24 well-plate. The plates
were then incubated at 37 1C for 2 h, and the samples to
be irradiated were taken out for irradiation (white light of
100 mW cm2 for 5 min). The light-treated wells were then
incubated at 37 1C for another 2 h. The samples in the 24 well-
plates that were both dark- and light-treated were then serially
diluted 100-fold in PBS and the diluted samples (100 mL) were
spread onto LB agar plates to count the viable colonies. The
spread agar plates were incubated at 37 1C for 12 h. The next
day, the colony-forming units (CFUs) formed on the agar plates
were imaged and counted. The calculated CFU mL1 values of
both dark- and light-treated samples were plotted and further
used to calculate the survival fraction and percentage of inhibi-
tion [here, the survival fraction (SF) and percentage (%) of
inhibition were calculated using the formula, SF = [CFU mL1
(of treated wells)/CFU mL1 (of the water-treated group)]  100,
and % of inhibition = 100  SF]. From the percentage of
inhibition curve, the concentration of Au NCs or the nano-
composite that killed 50% of the bacterial population was
determined as IC50. Based on the IC50 value of PpIX–Chito–
Au18, the other treatment compounds (Au18 NCs only and
Chito–Au18) were assessed for their anti-bacterial efficacy.

Bacterial cell death analysis
The antibacterial activity of various treatment compounds (Au18
NCs only, Chito–Au18 and PpIX–Chito–Au18) were assessed
further using Live/Dead assay under both dark() and light(+)
conditions. SYTOX green is a cell impermeant dye that enters
bacterial cells with a compromised cell membrane and stains
the nucleic acid, leaving the healthy cells unstained. To deter-
mine the total population of bacterial cells (both live and dead),
Hoechst 33 342 was used. The IC50 concentration of the treat-
ment compounds mentioned above was added onto 200 mL of
bacterial suspension (in MHB) of 1  108 CFU mL1 in a
24 well-plate. After irradiating the light-treated groups, the well
plate was incubated at 37 1C for 2 h. Then, 5 mM SYTOX green
and 1 mg mL1 Hoechst (1 mL) were added to all the wells
including the control wells. After 15 min of incubation at 37 1C,
the treated bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 5000 rpm
for 5 min. The pellet formed was resuspended in PBS and
the washing steps were repeated three times. After the final
washing, the resuspended PBS solution was analyzed using a
microplate reader where the SYTOX-stained cells were deter-
mined at an excitation/emission wavelength of 488/523 nm and
the Hoechst-stained cells were determined at an excitation/
emission wavelength of 350/461 nm. The percentage of SYTOX-
stained bacteria was calculated by dividing the fluorescence
intensity of SYTOX-stained cells by the fluorescence intensity of
Hoechst-stained cells.

Determination of outer membrane permeabilization using NPN
Outer membrane permeabilization of Gram-positive and Gram-
negative bacteria was studied using the polarity sensitive dye
N-phenyl naphthylamine (NPN). The bacterial suspension was
treated with various concentrations of the PpIX–Chito–Au18
nanocomposite and then incubated at 37 1C for 2 h. The
light-treated groups were irradiated using white light for
5 min. And then to all the treated bacterial suspension and
control (water-treated group), NPN dye (10 mM, 50 mL) was
added. After 15 min, the fluorescence intensity was estimated
at an excitation/emission wavelength of 350/420 nm.
Measurement of Au NC and nanocomposite uptake
Bacterial strains of both S. aureus and P. aeruginosa were treated
with Au18 NCs, Chito–Au18, and PpIX–Chito–Au18 for 2 h
at 37 1C. Then, they were washed thrice to remove any
residual samples that were not internalized inside the bacteria.
Following that, the optical density (OD600) of the resuspended
solution was used to calculate the concentration of bacterial
cells in the suspension. The samples were then digested with
aqua regia and analyzed for the Au content taken up by the cells

Determination of DNA fragmentation using terminal
deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL)
assay
A TUNEL test was performed based on the manufacturer’s
protocol. Bacterial cells (S. aureus) were treated with Au18 NCs
and Chito–Au18 and PpIX–Chito–Au18 nanocomposites for
B2 h at 37 1C. The light-treated group (PpIX–Chito–Au18(+))
was irradiated under white light (100 mW cm2) for 5 min.
After treatment, the cells were fixed on slides with 4% para-

prepared by adding 5 units mL1 DNaseI solution to control
groups and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Next,
the TdT reaction was performed by adding TdT reaction buffer
and incubating for 10 min at room temperature, followed by
adding TdT reaction cocktail to incubate for 60 min at 37 1C.
The slides were then washed and a Click-iTs reaction was
performed by adding Click-iTs reaction cocktail to incubate
for 30 min at room temperature protected from light. Finally,
1 mg mL1 Hoechst was added on slides for 15 min followed by
adding ProLongs Gold antifade reagent with DAPI to prevent
bleaching of stained cells with time. The bacterial cells were
then observed under the fluorescence microscope. The green
and blue stained cells were measured under the same area of
all treated samples using ImageJ, and the ratio between the
green and blue cells taken was used to represent the DNA
damage index.

Determination of biofilm mass using crystal violet assay
Bacterial suspensions (100 mL, 1  108 CFU mL1) were added
to each well of a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 1C for 24 h.
The formed biofilm was treated with Au18 NCs only, Chito–
Au18, PpIX–Chito–Au18(), and PpIX–Chito–Au18(+), and then
incubated at 37 1C for 2 h. The light-treated group (PpIX–Chito–
Au18(+)) was irradiated under white light (100 mW cm2) for
5 min. After irradiation, the plates were incubated further for
12 h at 37 1C. Then, the medium was discarded, and each well
was washed with PBS to remove planktonic bacteria. The
adhered biofilm after treatment was fixed using 95% methanol
for 15 min and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min.
After washing with PBS, 33% acetic acid was added to dissolve
the crystal violet stain, and then the absorbance at 590 nm was
measured. The viable biofilm mass was estimated by dividing
the absorbance at 590 nm of the treated group to that of the
control group.
Live/dead staining of biofilms
Briefly, diluted S. aureus and P. aeruginosa suspensions (400 mL)
were added to a m-slide 8 well chamber and cultured at 37 1C for
24 h. After washing the formed biofilm with PBS three times,
the fresh medium containing various treatment compounds
were added. The light-treated group was irradiated under white
light (100 mW cm2) for 5 min. After treatment, the chamber
was incubated at 37 1C for 24 h. Then, the old medium was
discarded, the treated biofilm was washed with PBS, and then
stained using a Live/Dead BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit
(Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) for 15 min at room temperature.
After staining, the wells were washed and viewed under a
confocal laser-scanning microscope (CLSM, Nikon Eclipse
Ti-E confocal microscope (Tokyo, Japan)). The z-stack images
were obtained to understand the depth of eradication. Using a
FIJI 3D viewer plugin, the z-stacked images were reconstructed
and then analyzed section by section to observe the viability of
the cells throughout the biofilms after treatment.
Observation of biofilm eradication under SEM
Bacterial suspensions (1  105 CFU mL1) were added onto
silicone films (1  1 cm2) placed into the wells of a 24-well
plate. The plates were then incubated for 24 h at 37 1C for the
development of biofilms. The formed biofilms were then

treatment compounds. The light-treated groups were irradiated
under white light for 5 min and further incubated for 24 h
at 37 1C. After treatment, the biofilms were washed with PBS
and viewed under SEM. Before SEM observation, the bacterial
biofilms on the silicone films were fixed with glutaraldehyde in

Cell viability assay
NIH 3T3 cells bought from American Type Culture Collection
(ATCC; Manassas, VA) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified
Eagle’s medium (DMEM, 10% FBS, 1% antibiotic) at 37 1C with
5% CO2 conditions. Cells were then treated with diﬀerent
concentrations
of
PpIX–Chito–Au18()
and
PpIX–Chito–

Statistical analysis
The quantitative data were reported as means  standard
deviation (SD). The statistical analysis was conducted in tripli-
cate (n = 3) unless otherwise mentioned. The statistical signi-
ficance of diﬀerences between two groups was determined
using Tukey–Kramer’s test. Results were considered as statisti-
cally significant with p o 0.05. All statistical analyses were
performed using Excel and OriginPro software.
Results and discussion
Synthesis and characterisation of Au18 NCs, Chito–Au18 and
PpIX–Chito–Au18 nanocomposites
In this work, we employed atomically precise mercaptopropio-
nic acid (MPA)-protected Au18 NCs as one of the key compo-
nents of
the designed
nanocomposite for
combinatorial
antibacterial therapy. Atomically precise Au NCs exhibit unique
physicochemical properties diﬀerent from those of their larger
counterparts (Au NPs), in which even few atom diﬀerences
among Au NCs induce huge eﬀects or even contrasting bio-
logical behaviors.54,55 For instance, Zhang et al. observed a
unique size-dependent nanocatalysis at the single Au NC level.
They observed that small Au NCs (i.e., Au15(MPA)13) catalyze
product formation through the competitive Langmuir–Hinshel-
wood catalytic mechanism, whereas the non-competitive Lang-
muir–Hinshelwood catalytic mechanism was observed for
relatively larger Au NCs, such as Au18(MPA)14 and Au25(MPA)18.
The influence of the atom number on the corresponding
properties and applications of atomically precise Au NCs was

TUNEL
(terminal

eukaryotic cells.71 In prokaryotes like bacteria, ROS-induced

fragmented 30-OH end of DNA was enzymatically labeled using
dUTP-fluorescein isothiocyanate (dUTPFITC) through click

green color under CLSM. The extent of DNA fragmentation by
various treatment compounds on S. aureus is shown in Fig. 6.
Here, Hoechst was used to stain the nucleus of all cells, to
determine the total population. The extent of DNA damage was
quantified using the DNA damage index, which was calculated
by dividing the number of TUNEL-stained bacterial cells by that
of Hoechst-stained cells. This quantification helps in determin-
ing the eﬃcacy of the diﬀerent treatments on bacterial DNA
damage. The deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I)-treated group was
used as the positive control, with a DNA damage index of 0.98.
The water-treated group was used as the negative control,
which showed no damage to DNA with a DNA damage index
of 0. In the case of the Au18 NC alone treated group, the DNA
damage index was 0.1. The DNA damage indices of Chito–Au18
and PpIX–Chito–Au18() were 0.30 and 0.32, respectively,

is nearly 8-fold higher than that of the Au18 NC alone treated
group. This may be due to: (1) the higher uptake of PpIX–Chito–
Au18 than Au18 NCs alone and (2) significant intracellular ROS
production of the PpIX-conjugated nanocomposite. Recently,
our group designed traceable Au NCs to study the antimicrobial
mechanisms of functional nanomaterials.73 It was shown that
the luminescent Au NCs would first attach onto the bacterial
membrane, penetrate, and subsequently accumulate inside the
bacteria. The internalized Au NCs would then induce ROS genera-
tion and damage the bacterial membrane, resulting in the leakage
of bacterial contents. Additionally, significant DNA fragmentation
was also observed, which finally killed the bacteria. The reported
findings match with our observation in the current study.

were seen. On the other hand, for PpIX–Chito–Au18(+), the
formation of a red film was seen in both the top view of the
3D biofilm and its middle section (Fig. 8b). These results
highlight the efficacy of the nanocomposites in eradicating
the matured biofilms of both S. aureus and P. aeruginosa by
combinatorial therapy. A potent eradication of P. aeruginosa
biofilms requires special mention, as very few drugs and
strategies were reported for the anti-bacterial treatment of
P. aeruginosa when compared to Escherichia coli (i.e., E. coli,
Gram-negative pathogen).70,77 The difference in the constituents of
the outer membrane of P. aeruginosa and E. coli might be the
reason for the difference in their anti-bacterial response.78–82
The corresponding SEM images also support the results
obtained from the quantitative crystal violet assay and CLSM
images. From Fig. 9, the significant biofilm inhibition of PpIX–
Chito–Au18(+) was seen for both S. aureus and P. aeruginosa
formed biofilms when compared to the control and Au18
NC-treated groups. For understanding the mechanism of bio-
film eradication, the depth of penetration of our designed
nanocomposite could be well observed from Fig. 8a and b.
The presence of chitosan enhanced the depth of penetration of
the PpIX–Chito–Au18 nanocomposite when compared to Au18
NCs alone. Additionally, aPDT showed much higher killing
efficacy which correlated with the antibacterial efficacy on the
planktonic counterparts. The results suggest that Au NCs could
penetrate through the biofilm barrier and exert their anti-
microbial efficacy even on the biofilm-protected bacterial cells,
which offers an advantage over large Au NPs.
Finally, the safety profiles of PpIX–Chito–Au18 with and
without light were evaluated on the mouse embryonic fibro-
blast cell line (NIH 3T3). PpIX–Chito–Au18 was found to be safe
under dark conditions for Au atom concentrations above
100 mM (Fig. S6, ESI†). In the case of light-treated PpIX–
Chito–Au18, slight reduction in cell viability was observed at
higher concentrations. These data suggest that PpIX–Chito–
Au18 would not cause significant side effects and has good
biocompatibility for combinatorial antibacterial therapy.
Conclusion
In summary, a nanocomposite was designed by conjugating
ultra-small Au18 NCs and a photosensitizer (PpIX) with the
chitosan nanocarrier to realize synergistic antimicrobial activity

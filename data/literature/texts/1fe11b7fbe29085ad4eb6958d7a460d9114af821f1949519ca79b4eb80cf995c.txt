Synergistic Antimicrobial Capability of Magnetically 
Oriented Graphene Oxide Conjugated with Gold 
Nanoclusters
Kaiyuan Zheng, Kerui Li, Ting-Hsiang Chang, Jianping Xie,* and Po-Yen Chen*
Bacterial resistance toward antibiotics has been a worldwide threat; one way to 
fight against the resistance is to develop a multimechanism antibacterial agent 
to achieve synergistic performance. Graphene oxide (GO) is an emerging 
antibacterial agent combining multiple mechanisms (physical insertion and 
chemical disruption), and its rich functional groups enable the complexation/
conjugation of nanomaterials to further improve antibacterial performance. 
Herein, a synergistic antimicrobial agent is established through the assembly 
of paramagnetic holmium ions and gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) onto GO 
nanosheets. The assembled nanosheets can be vertically aligned under weak 
and practical magnetic fields (<0.5 T ), providing high-density sharp edges with 
preferential orientation to effectively pierce the bacterial membrane. Also, the 
conjugated AuNCs are efficiently delivered into bacteria to induce high oxida-
tive stress, which strongly disturbs bacterial metabolism, leading to the death 
of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. The antibacterial agent 
uses both physical (via oriented GO) and chemical (via GO and AuNCs) mech-
anisms to achieve synergistic antimicrobial performance with low IC50 values 
of 9.8 µg mL−1 on the basis of GO and 0.39 × 10−6 m on the basis of AuNCs. 
This multicomponent agent with dual antimicrobial mechanisms is expected 
to be a promising multifunctional-antimicrobial agent with high biosafety.

1. Introduction
Bacterial resistance toward antibiotics results in serious infec-
tion and has been one of the increasing worldwide threats. 
Such kind of resistance is accumulated due to the single 
mechanism of conventional antibiotics, which either target 
the specific domains/receptors of bacteria or interrupt the syn-
thetic pathways of bacterial subcellular structures.[1] Therefore, 
several nonspecific and broad-spectrum antimicrobial agents 
have been developed, such as antimicrobial peptides that

orientation. Such magnetic fields require ­specialized equipment 
containing superconducting magnet(s) and only exist within a 
small space only, which cannot be utilized to easily fabricate large-
area nanocoatings or treat large-quantity bacterial media. There-
fore, a more scalable method that uses lower magnetic fields to 
tune the orientation of GO nanosheets will be highly desired.
Besides physical interruption, the GO nanosheets exhibit 
rich functional groups on their surfaces and can generate large 
amount of ROS to chemically damage bacteria.[9a] The GO 
nanosheets can further serve as efficient assembly platforms to 
conjugate various nanomaterials, such as metal NPs, metal oxides, 
and polymers, to achieve improved antimicrobial performance.[12] 
One emerging antimicrobial agent with high biosafety is gold 
nanoclusters (AuNCs), which are the ultrasmall Au NPs with 
core sizes less than 2 nm.[13] They hold discrete electronic states 
as well as characteristic geometric structures, leading to several 
intriguing molecular-like properties, such as well-defined mole-
cular structure, quantized charging, highest occupied molecular 
orbital–lowest unoccupied molecular orbital transition, molecular 
magnetism, molecular chirality, and strong luminescence.[14] 
Very recently, we found the ultrasmall AuNCs can be internal-
ized into bacteria to disturb normal metabolism and induce ROS 
accumulation, leading to the death of bacteria.[15] Importantly, the 
AuNCs do not cause cytotoxic and genotoxic effects toward mam-
malian cells, and can serve as the biosafe antimicrobial agent 
for potential bench-­to-bed clinical applications.[15a,16] The AuNCs 
are expected to be the promising conjugation guests for GO 
nanosheets, and the composites of AuNCs and GO nanosheets 
may provide synergistic antimicrobial performance through 
disrupting the bacterial metabolism physically (via GO) and 
chemically (via AuNCs and GO) simultaneously.[9a,15a] In addition, 
both AuNCs and GO nanosheets have been reported as biosafe 
antibacterial agents, so the composites of AuNCs and GO could 
be the promising candidate for clinical applications.[15a,17]
Until now, there is no report regarding the conjugation of 
AuNCs onto GO nanosheets for antimicrobial purpose, and we 
further introduced paramagnetic metal ions (i.e., holmium ions 
(Ho3+)) to the GO-Au nanosheets in order to provide preferential 
nanosheet orientation and thus to achieve synergistic antimicro-
bial efficacy. We first complexed Ho ions onto GO nanosheets to 
increase their magnetic response in order to allow us to control 
the nanosheet orientation under more practical field intensity. 
The GO nanosheets with Ho ions could be vertically oriented 
under weak magnetic fields (<0.5 T), which were achieved by 
commercial NdFeB magnets. Such kind of preferential ori-
entation increased the density of sharp edges of GO to better 
penetrate bacterial membranes. Afterward, AuNCs were conju-
gated onto the magnetically responsive GO nanosheets, which 
induced high oxidative stress and strongly disturbed bacterial 
metabolism, leading to the death of both Gram-positive and 
Gram-negative bacteria. The multicomponent agent with dual 
antimicrobial mechanisms is expected to be a promising multi-
functional antimicrobial agent with high biosafety.
2. Results and Discussion
In order to control the orientation of GO nanosheets under 
lower magnetic fields without the use of specialized ­equipment, 
paramagnetic metal ions were used to complex with the GO 
nanosheets to enhance the magnetically responsive behav-
iors. We selected holmium ion (Ho3+) as the assembly guest, 
which exhibits four unpaired electrons in 4f shell to be aligned 
under weak magnetic fields[18] and low nanotoxicity (no tox-
icity if under 50 ppm).[19] The Ho ions can strongly coordinate 
with one or multiple carboxyl group(s) on the GO nanosheets 
(n  −RCOO−  + Ho3+  ↔  −(RCOO)nHo(3−n)+, 1 ≤ n  ≤ 3, Keq  ≈ 
500–1000 m−1), resulting in a stable Ho3+-coordinated GO 
complex with remained 2D nanosheet morphology (abbrevi-
ated to Ho-GO).[20] The Ho ions can also be stabilized through 
the complexation with other oxygen-containing groups (e.g., 
hydroxyl and epoxy groups) and aromatic rings.[21] As shown 
in the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results in 
Figure S1a (Supporting Information), Ho-GO exhibited the Ho 
4d peak at 159 eV, indicating the complexation of paramagnetic 
Ho ions on GO nanosheets. The transmission electron micro-
scope (TEM) image with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDX) mapping of Ho-GO (Figure  1a,i and Figure S1b, Sup-
porting Information) showed the Ho3+ complexation was uni-
formly distributed on the GO nanosheet, and no large HoOx 
nanocrystals were observed.
After the complexation of Ho ions, the size distribution 
slightly increased from ≈1.2  µm (GO) to ≈1.6  µm (Ho-GO), 
as shown in the size distribution results estimated from TEM 
images (Figure 1b). The slight agglomerate may be attributed 
to the neutralization of electronegative GO nanosheets by Ho 
ions, reflected by the change of zeta potential from −42  mV 
(GO) to −37.6 mV (Ho-GO). The optimal amount of Ho ions 
was ­determined at 0.1 × 10−3 m on the basis of 1 mg mL−1 of GO 
dispersion by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spec-
trometry (ICP-OES). As long as the concentration of Ho ions was 
less than 0.1 × 10−3 m, no aggregation was noticed (Figure S1c, 
Supporting Information), and further complexation of Ho ions 
resulted in serious aggregation of GO nanosheets (Figure S1d, 
Supporting Information).
Another key component to conjugate with GO nanosheets is 
ultrasmall AuNCs, which hold average core size less than 2 nm 
as shown in Figure 1a,ii. The biligand protected AuNCs were 
synthesized by reducing Au(III) in the presence of two thiolated 
ligands, 6-mercaptohexanoic acid (MHA) and cysteamine 
(Cystm), by sodium borohydride (NaBH4) under alkaline condi-
tion.[22] MHA, a commonly used protecting ligand, was selected 
to generate the water-dispersible AuNCs. Cystm was doped to 
provide primary amine groups to undergo 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimeth-
ylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) reactions to conjugate 
AuNCs onto GO nanosheets (the synthesis is discussed in the 
next paragraph). The molecular formula of the biligand pro-
tected AuNCs was further characterized at the atomic precise 
level by electrospray ionization mass spectra. As shown in the 
analysis in Figure S2a (Supporting Information), the as-synthe-
sized AuNCs are Au25Cystm1-4MHA17-14; that is, one AuNC con-
tains an icosahedral kernel with 13 Au(0) atoms, protected by 
surface motif of 12 Au(I) and 18 thiolated ligands (Figure S2b, 
Supporting Information).[22,23] The 18 ligands are composed of 
Cystm and MHA, with the ratio of Cystm to MHA varying from 
1/17 to 4/14.
Afterward, the AuNCs were conjugated onto Ho-GO 
through the carbodiimide crosslinker chemistry by using EDC 
crosslinkers. The EDC crosslinkers reacted with the primary

the TEM images in Figure S3a (Supporting Information).

The loading of AuNCs in the Ho-GO-Au assemblies was 
adjusted by controlling the amount of AuNCs used in the 
EDC crosslinking (Figure S7, Supporting Information). If the 
concentration of AuNCs used in the EDC reaction was set 
at 44  × 10−6  m, the Ho-GO-Au exhibited the concentration of 
Au25Cystm1-4MHA17-14 NCs at ≈40 × 10−6 m on the basis of 1 mg 
mL−1 of GO dispersion, quantified by ICP-OES. If only half of 
AuNCs were involved (22  × 10−6  m), the AuNC concentration 
in the Ho-GO-Au assemblies decreased to ≈19 × 10−6 m on the 
basis of 1 mg mL−1 of GO dispersion. If twice of AuNCs were 
applied (88 × 10−6 m), the AuNC concentration only increased to 
≈45 × 10−6 m on the basis of 1 mg mL−1 of GO dispersion. All 
of the Ho-GO-Au groups showed the same Ho ions loading at 
≈0.1 × 10−3 m on the basis of 1 mg mL−1 GO dispersion, sug-
gesting again that the EDC crosslinkers only reacted with free 
carboxyl groups. Thus, the optimized Ho-GO-Au nanosheets 
were composed of ≈0.1  × 10−3 m Ho ions and ≈40  × 10−6  m 
AuNCs on the basis of 1  mg mL−1 GO dispersion. A control 
sample without the complexation of Ho ions (i.e., GO-Au) was 
also synthesized, and the AuNC concentration was controlled at 
≈40 × 10−6 m, which was the same as Ho-GO-Au (see Figure S8 
and Note S1 in the Supporting Information for detailed 
characterizations).

To observe the magnetic response of Ho-GO nanosheets in 
a more convenient way, we poured the suspension of Ho-GO 
nanosheets in a petri dish, and the NdFeB magnets were 
placed under the dish (Figure  2c). The paramagnetic Ho-GO 
nanosheets were attracted and gradually accumulated above the 
magnets. The magnetic response of Ho-GO nanosheets was 
recognized after 1 h: the area above the magnet surfaces (espe-
cially the edge of magnets where the highest flux intensity was 
present) became darker from 0 to 24 h. Figure  2d shows the 
field patterns of different shapes and configuration of magnets, 
and the boundaries between four circular or ring magnets are 
clearly shown. Also, since the majority of Ho ions were coordi-
nated with the carboxyl groups on GO, the −(RCOO)nHo(3−n)+ 
complexation remained stable at both neutral and basic condi-
tions. Therefore, the magnetically responsive behaviors main-
tained at both neutral and basic conditions. On the other hand, 
in acidic conditions, protons have higher tendency to proto-
nate the carboxyl groups of GO to replace Ho ions.[18,20a,25] As 
shown in Figure  2e, the improved paramagnetism of Ho-GO 
nanosheets disappeared after the pH value was adjusted to 
lower than ≈1.8.
To observe the orientation of Ho-GO nanosheets along 
the magnetic fields, the Ho-GO dispersion was mixed 
with an acryl monomer (acrylamide), a crosslinker (N,N′-
methylenebisacrylamide), and a photoinitiator (2-hydroxy-
2-methylpropiophenone), and the mixture was then placed in 
a magnetic field with maximal intensity of 400 mT for 24 h. 
The magnetically responsive behaviors of Ho-GO nanosheets 
did not get disrupted after the addition of precursors. After 
the Ho-GO nanosheets were magnetically oriented along the 
magnetic fields, the UV light was illuminated to activate the 
in situ polymerization and preserved the orientation of Ho-GO 
nanosheets in a free-standing hydrogel. We utilized confocal 
microscopy to observe the luminescence of Ho-GO nanosheets 
in the hydrogels (three locations of hydrogels were investi-
gated). The confocal photographs in Figure 2f,i demonstrated, 
after 24 h magnetic alignment, the paramagnetic Ho-GO 
nanosheets followed the direction of magnetic fields and 
became more vertically oriented. In comparison, without the 
presence of magnetic fields, the Ho-GO nanosheets presented 
random orientation as shown in Figure 2f,ii.

by conducting several sets of antimicrobial testing. The 
antibacterial performance was first tested in a Gram-positive 
bacterial model, S. aureus. The antimicrobial agents (including 
GO, Ho-GO, and Ho-GO-Au) were diluted 50 times, and the 
concentration was kept at 20  µg mL−1 on the basis of GO 
nanosheets in the bacterial incubation for all the antibacte-
rial tests. After the dilution, the concentrations of Ho ions 
and AuNCs incubated with bacteria were ≈2  × 10−6  m and 
≈0.8 × 10−6 m, respectively, throughout the rest of the paper.

We also investigated the antimicrobial efficacy of Ho-GO. 
The antibacterial ability of Ho ions was tested by adding 
Ho(NO3)3 into the bacterial incubation (without the complexa-
tion with GO), and the Ho ions at ten-time higher concentra-
tion (20  × 10−6  m) showed negligible antibacterial ability (see 
representative fluorescence images of bacterial treatment in 
Figure S11a of the Supporting Information), which cohered to 
the low toxicity of Ho ions reported in the literature.[19] After 
the complexation of Ho ions with GO nanosheets, the Ho-GO 
demonstrated slight increase in the antimicrobial ability com-
pared to the GO-only group (Figure S11b, Supporting Infor-
mation), which may result from the increased zeta potential 
of Ho-GO (from −42 mV of GO to −37.6 mV of Ho-GO), pro-
viding better contact with bacterial membrane.
With the complexation of paramagnetic Ho ions on GO 
nanosheets, the orientation of Ho-GO can be magnetically 
manipulated, leading to improved antimicrobial performance, 
as displayed in Figure  3c,d (see representative integrated flu-
orescence images of bacterial treatment in Figure S12 of the 
Supporting Information). After mixing Ho-GO with Luria-­
Bertani (LB) medium, we applied the magnetic fields with 
maximal intensity at ≈418 mT to the LB medium by placing the 
magnets below for 24 h. The medium containing magnetically 
oriented Ho-GO was further incubated with bacteria in a hori-
zontally rotated shaker for 2 h. Two Ho-GO groups were pre-
pared: one Ho-GO group was aligned both before and during 
the bacterial incubation, which was denoted as “Ho-GO(+), 
­prealignment”; and the other one was aligned only during 
the incubation, denoted as “Ho-GO(+), postalignment.” As 
exhibited in Figure 3c,d, the “Ho-GO(+), prealignment” group 
showed significant improvement on the antibacterial efficacy 
in terms of the killing efficiency to ≈79% and the ROS level to 
≈5.7-fold, which were higher than the nonaligned group (i.e., 
Ho-GO(-)) (≈36% and ≈4.4-fold) and the postalignment group 
(≈36% and ≈4.3-fold).
The momentum of vertically oriented Ho-GO nanosheets 
increased in the horizontally shaken incubator, and the sharp 
edges of GO nanosheets could serve as high-speed blades with 
preferential orientation (similar to the blades in a juice blender) 
to effectively cut the bacterial membrane. The enhanced inser-
tion capability of GO nanosheets under magnetic prealign-
ment also enabled the accumulation of intracellular ROS to 
cause lipid peroxidation, inactivation of intracellular proteins, 
and dysfunction of mitochondria, finally leading to bacteria 
death.[8a,9a] Without the prealignment prior to bacterial incuba-
tion, Ho-GO would require higher magnetic fields and longer 
time to orientate the nanosheets against the shear stress under 
vigorous shaking. Therefore, the postalignment Ho-GO group 
demonstrated similar antibacterial performance as the nona-
ligned Ho-GO group.
The amount and degree of magnetically oriented Ho-GO 
largely influenced their antimicrobial performance. Prior to 
the bacterial incubation, the Ho-GO samples were placed 
above the NdFeB magnets for two different time lengths, 4 and 
24 h. As shown in Figure 2c, the amount of Ho-GO attracted 
by the magnets for 4 h was less than the one for 24 h. As 
shown in Figure  4a,b (see representative fluorescence images 
of bacterial treatment in Figure S13 of the Supporting Informa-
tion), when the paramagnetic Ho-GO was allowed to align for 
24 h, it showed improved killing efficiency of ≈78% and higher 
ROS level of 5.7-fold than the 4 h group (≈49%, 4.9-fold). As the 
time length of magnetic prealignment increased, more Ho-GO 
nanosheets were attracted and aligned, which provided higher 
density of sharp edges to disrupt the bacterial membrane.
We further investigated the effect of field intensity on the 
antimicrobial performance of Ho-GO. As shown in Figure 4c,d 
(see representative fluorescence images of bacterial treatment 
in Figure S14 of the Supporting Information), we gradu-
ally decreased the field intensity from ≈400, ≈200, to ≈80 mT 
by increasing the distance between bacterial incubation and 
NdFeB magnets from 0, 1.5, to 3 cm, respectively. As the mag-
netic fields decreased, the bacterial killing efficacy decreased 
from ≈79% (≈400 mT) to ≈68% (≈200 mT) and ≈36% (≈80 mT), 
and the ROS generation level also dropped from 5.8-fold (≈400 
mT) to 4.7-fold (≈200 mT) and 4.2-fold (≈80 mT). When Ho-GO 
was under larger magnetic fields, the nanosheets experienced 
stronger torque and became more vertically oriented. In the fol-
lowing antibacterial tests, the magnetic fields and prealignment 
time length were set at ≈400 mT and 24 h, respectively.
The Ho-GO-Au nanosheets under magnetic orientation 
demonstrated synergistic antibacterial performance, as shown 
in Figure 5a,b (see representative fluorescence images of bac-
terial treatment in Figure S15 of the Supporting Information). 
Similar to Ho-GO, Ho-GO-Au also demonstrated improved 
paramagnetism and could be magnetically aligned into ver-
tical orientation under the ≈400-mT magnetic fields (Figure S9, 
Supporting Information). The antibacterial performance of 
magnetically oriented Ho-GO-Au, denoted as Ho-GO-Au(+), 
reached the killing efficacy of ≈99% and the ROS level of ≈9.1-
fold. In comparison, the magnetically aligned Ho-GO (Ho-
GO(+)) exhibited the killing efficacy of ≈78% and the ROS 
generation level of ≈5.9-fold, and GO-Au showed lower killing 
efficacy of ≈36% and ROS level of ≈4.5-fold. In addition, colony-
forming unit (CFU) counts were conducted (Figure S16, Sup-
porting Information), and significant inhibition of bacterial 
growth was achieved by Ho-GO-Au(+) to 0.72 × 106 CFU mL−1 in 
comparison with the water treated group (9.05 × 106 CFU mL−1), 
while the GO-Au and Ho-GO(+) groups were 2.36 × 106 CFU mL−1 
and 1.27 × 106 CFU mL−1, respectively. A synergy of antibacte-
rial performance was observed in the Ho-GO-Au(+) nanosheets: 
once Ho-GO-Au was magnetically oriented, the sharp edges of 
GO nanosheets could efficiently cut the bacterial membrane, 
and the conjugated AuNCs (along with the functional groups 
of GO) were internalized to generate a large amount of ROS. 
To prove that AuNCs were better delivered with the presence 
of magnetically oriented GO nanosheets, the uptake of AuNCs 
was investigated by ICP-OES (Figure S17, Supporting Informa-
tion). The uptake of Au element of Ho-GO-Au(+) group was 
≈1.6-fold and ≈1.15-fold higher than the AuNC-only (the same 
AuNC concentration yet no GO involved) and GO-Au groups, 
respectively.

3. Conclusion
In conclusion, we have established a synergistic antimicrobial 
agent through the conjugation of AuNCs onto magnetically 
responsive Ho-GO nanosheets. The Ho-GO-Au nanosheets 
were able to be vertically oriented under weak magnetic fields 
(≈400 mT), which provided high-density sharp edges with 
preferential orientation to effectively pierce bacterial mem-
branes. Also, the conjugated AuNCs were efficiently delivered 
into GO-cut bacteria and induced high oxidative stress, which 
strongly disturbed bacterial metabolism, leading to the death 
of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Magneti-
cally oriented Ho-GO-Au agent utilizes both physical (via ori-
ented GO) and chemical (via GO and AuNCs) mechanisms to 
achieve a synergistic antimicrobial performance. In addition, 
the components inside this antimicrobial system are all con-
sidered to be biosafe with low IC50 values. Moreover, the Ho3+-
complexation method reduced the required magnetic intensity 
from ≈6 T to lower than 0.5 T, demonstrating a more scalable 
and economic-available approach to manipulate the orientation 
of other antibacterial nanomaterials. Therefore, with practical 
magnetic fields, the Ho-GO-Au agent with dual antimicrobial 
mechanisms could be a promising multifunctional-antimicro-
bial agent with high biosafety.
4. Experimental Section
AuNC Synthesis: The synthesis of AuNCs followed a reported 
method.[22] First, freshly prepared aqueous solutions of HAuCl4  
(20 × 10−3 m, 0.25 mL), MHA (5 × 10−3 m, 1.75 mL), and Cystm (5 × 10−3 m, 
0.25 mL) were mixed in water (2.35 mL) under stirring, leading to the 
formation of Au(I) complexes. Afterward, 1.0 m NaOH solution was 
added to dissolve the mixture and bring the pH to ≈12. Then, freshly 
prepared NaBH4 solution (112  × 10−3 m) was obtained by dissolving 
43  mg NaBH4 in NaOH solution (0.2 m, 10  mL). Finally, 0.1  mL of 
as-prepared NaBH4 solution was added into the aforesaid solution, 
and the AuNCs could be collected after 3 h. After the synthesis, a 
stirred ultrafiltration cell (Model 8010, Millipore Corporation, USA) 
with a semi-permeable membrane of 3000 Da molecular weight cut off 
(MWCO) was used to purify and concentrate the AuNCs.
Ho-GO Nanosheet Complexation: GO solution (0.5 mg mL−1, 5 mL) was 
mixed with Ho(NO3)3 solution (1 × 10−3 m, 0.5 mL) under stirring, and 
the complexation reaction was allowed for 2 d. After the complexation, 
the final product of Ho-GO nanosheets was purified and concentrated by 
stirred ultrafiltration cell with a semi-permeable membrane of 100 kDa 
MWCO. The concentration of Ho ions was tested by ICP-OES, which was 
around 0.1 × 10−3 m on the basis of 1 mg mL−1 of GO dispersion.
Ho-GO-Au Nanosheet Conjugation: In a typical synthesis, Ho-GO 
dispersion (0.5 mg mL−1, 5 mL) and freshly prepared AuNC dispersion 
(10  × 10−3 m on the basis of Au atom, 0.55  mL) were mixed under 
stirring, and the freshlly prepared aqueous solutions of EDC (50 × 10−3 m, 
0.05 mL) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (50 × 10−3 m, 0.05 mL) were added 
afterward. The final product was collected after 4 h, and purified and 
concentrated to 2.5  mL by stirred ultrafiltration cell with a semi-
permeable membrane of 100 kDa (MWCO). The concentration of GO in 
the solution is 1 mg mL−1, and the concentration of AuNCs is 40 × 10−6 m 
(on the basis of AuNCs) on the basis of 1 mg mL−1 of GO dispersion.
Magnetically Directed Orientation: Dispersions of Ho-GO and 
Ho-GO-Au nanosheets (1 mg mL−1 on the basis of GO) were placed on 
rare-earth permanent magnets (i.e., Nd-Fe-B) at field intensity of ≈418 
mT for 24 h. For antimicrobial tests, Ho-GO or Ho-GO-Au dispersions 
(both at 1 mg mL−1 on the basis of GO, 0.1 mL) were added into LB 
medium (5  mL) in a beaker under sterile condition, and the final 
concentration of Ho-GO or Ho-GO-Au was 20 µg mL−1 on the basis of 
GO. Then the samples were sealed and put on the top of magnet for 24 h 
before adding bacteria.

Gold Nanoclusters for Targeting Methicillin-Resistant
Staphylococcus aureus In Vivo
Yangzhouyun Xie, Yong Liu, Junchuan Yang, Yuan Liu, Fupin Hu,* Kui Zhu,* and
Xingyu Jiang*
Abstract: Widespread multidrug resistance caused by the
abuse of antibiotics calls for novel strategies and materials.
Gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) are scarcely explored for combat-
ing multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria in vivo. We herein
synthesized a novel class of AuNCs, namely quaternary
ammonium (QA) capped AuNCs (QA-AuNCs) as potent
antibiotics selectively targeting MDR Gram-positive bacteria,
including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)
and vancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VRE), in vivo. QA-
AuNCs kill bacteria through a combined physicochemical
mechanism, and show excellent therapeutic effects in both
a skin infection model and a bacteremia model induced by
MRSA. In addition, owing to their intense fluorescence, QA-
AuNCs can be used for the discrimination of live/dead bacteria
and bacteria counting, suggesting their potential for clinical
theranostics.
Few treatment options are available when patients become
infected
with
multidrug-resistant
(MDR)
Gram-positive
bacteria, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus
(MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VRE).[1–5]
This worldwide health threat suggests an urgent need for
novel antibiotic agents. Gold nanomaterials are promising
candidates for addressing this demand owing to their tunable
size, facile functionalization, and minimal toxicity.[6,7] Unlike
gold
nanoparticles
(AuNPs),
smaller
gold
nanoclusters
(AuNCs) have recently emerged as innovative nanomedicines
with interesting properties, such as size-dependent fluores-
cence.[8–10] It was recently demonstrated that AuNCs with
diameters below 2 nm exhibit antibacterial activity.[11] How-
ever, studies describing the effect of AuNCs on MDR bacteria
are scarce, particularly for combating MDR bacteria in vivo.
Bio–nano interface engineering can be used to fine-tune
the interactions between nanomaterials and biological sys-
tems.[12] Herein, we synthesized AuNCs functionalized with
quaternary ammonium (QA) salts (QA-AuNCs) for combat-
ing MDR bacteria. QA-AuNCs selectively target Gram-
positive bacteria, showing an antibacterial spectrum that is
complementary to those of their larger counterparts reported
in our previous work.[13] The QA-AuNCs exhibit excellent
therapeutic effects against Gram-positive “superbugs” both
in vitro and in vivo, without any detectable toxicity or drug
resistance. The intensely fluorescent QA-AuNCs interact
with live and dead bacteria differently, which allows for
imaging-guided bacteria discrimination and counting.
As it is known that positively charged ligands can induce
increased membrane permeability and reduced drug efflux in
MDR bacteria,[14,15] we functionalized and optimized AuNCs
with positively charged ligands. Three commonly used
positive
ligands,
namely
QA,[16]
nona-arginine
peptide
(R9),[17] and the transactivator of transcription peptide
(Tat),[18] were utilized (for their chemical structures, see the
Supporting Information, Figure S1). Among them, R9 and
Tat are typical antibacterial peptides. We prepared various
AuNCs capped with different ligands in one-pot processes
with glutathione (GSH) as the reductant, avoiding complex
ligand exchange processes. Owing to the thiol groups on the
ligands and GSH, they can concurrently bind to AuNCs via
Au@S bonds. For all of the ligands, the size of the AuNCs
decreased as the ligand/reductant (L/R) ratio was increased,
and the zeta potential of the AuNCs gradually changed from
negative to positive (Figures S2a,b and S3). There is a slight
blue-shift in both the excitation and emission wavelengths
(Figures S2c and S4).[19] These changes in size, surface charge,
and optical properties indicate the successful synthesis of
ligand-protected AuNCs, showing the strong impact of ligand
functionalization on the chemistry of nanomaterials. These
AuNC properties are completely different from those of
AuNPs.
We screened the functionalized AuNCs as potential
antibiotics by determining their minimal inhibitory concen-
trations (MICs) with a broth dilution method. In particular,
we chose Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus)
and Gram-negative Escherichia coli (E. coli), along with their
MDR counterparts (MRSA and MDR E. coli) as model
bacteria. We identified three candidates with low MIC values

superior antibacterial performance, we used the QA-AuNCs
with an L/R ratio of 0.5:1 in the following experiments.
We compared the activity of the QA-AuNCs towards
S. aureus with those of four commonly used antibiotics,
namely ampicillin, oxacillin, linezolid, and vancomycin. The
time-kill kinetics of the QA-AuNCs were similar to those of
the established antibiotics during both the exponential (Fig-
ure 1a) and stationary growth phase (Figure S7), and the QA-
AuNCs resulted in the same dose-dependent inhibition of the
growth of S. aureus as vancomycin (Figure S8). QA-AuNCs
can eliminate mature biofilms of S. aureus (Figure S9),
whereas
most
known
antibiotics,
including
AuNPs,
cannot.[20] Biofilms can increase antibiotic resistance and
medical-device-associated infections.[21]
Additionally, we studied the long-term response of
S. aureus treated with oxacillin and QA-AuNCs. As expected,
S. aureus rapidly developed resistance to oxacillin at sub-
inhibitory levels within a few days. However, no resistance
towards the QA-AuNCs was observed even after serial
passage for 30 days (Figure 1b). Compared to the four
antibiotics that have an effect on specific strains, the QA-
AuNCs showed a broader antibacterial spectrum against
MDR Gram-positive bacteria, including VRE and MRSA
(Figure 1c). As AuNCs are more effective against a broader
array of MDR infections by clinical isolates than known
antibiotics, including AuNPs, the QA-AuNCs may be bene-
ficial for clinical treatments. The QA-AuNCs showed similar
MICs for clinical MDR isolates and standard strains (Figur-
es S10 and S11). All of these results suggest that QA-AuNCs
are a new class of nano-antibiotics against MDR Gram-
positive bacteria that do not induce drug resistance.
We studied the antibacterial mechanism of the QA-
AuNCs using S. aureus as the model bacteria. The positively
charged surface of the QA-AuNCs facilitates electrostatic
adsorption onto the bacterial cell membrane, enabling
efficient
bonding
and
internalization
(Figure S12).
We
observed the morphological changes of S. aureus treated
with QA-AuNCs by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and
transmission electron microscopy (TEM). S. aureus bacteria
tend to be wrinkled in the presence of 10 mgmL@1 QA-
AuNCs, with nanosized pores at the membrane surface
implying the disruption of membrane integrity (Figure 2a).
When treated with higher concentrations (20 and 40 mgmL@1)
of QA-AuNCs, the bacteria fused together owing to the total
destruction of the membrane (Figure 2a,b). AuNC-induced
membrane injury further leads to increased membrane
permeability (Figure 2c), which was visualized by live/dead
staining with PI and SYTO9 (Figure S13). We measured the
inner membrane potential of AuNC-treated S. aureus by
using the cyanine dye diSC3(5). As expected, we observed
a gradual dissipation of the membrane potential as the QA-
AuNC dose was increased, which was revealed by the
increasing fluorescence of diSC3(5) (Figure 2d). In short,

QA-AuNCs significantly affect the bacterial cell membrane,
including the membrane integrity, permeability, and inner
potential, which contributes to the initiatory mechanism for
their antibacterial activity.
We next investigated the biochemical processes triggered
by QA-AuNCs when they encounter bacteria. Several
bactericides can induce the generation of reactive oxygen
species (ROS) and cause bacterial death.[23] We measured the
ROS levels in AuNC-treated S. aureus and observed a dra-
matic increase in the presence of varying concentrations of
QA-AuNCs (eightfold increase for 10 mgmL@1 QA-AuNCs;
Figure 2e), which is possibly due to the catalytic activity of the
QA-AuNCs.[22] To evaluate the impact of QA-AuNCs on
bacterial metabolism, we measured the intracellular ATP
levels. QA-AuNCs cause a decline in the ATP levels (Fig-
ure S14a), which was attributed to the dissipation of the
membrane potential and the inactivity of F-type ATP
synthetase (Figure S14b).[24,25] Overall, we propose a com-
bined physicochemical mechanism that endows QA-AuNCs
with potent antibiotic activity: a) disruption of the bacterial
cell membrane, b) catalytic generation of ROS, and c) dis-
turbance of intracellular metabolic pathways. Such a multi-
path mechanism also explains the absence of drug resistance
to QA-AuNCs. The comprehensive evaluation of the anti-
microbial actions and mechanisms of QA-AuNCs indicates
the robustness of their antimicrobial activities.
The strong interactions between the QA-AuNCs and the
bacterial cell membrane inspired us to make use of their
intrinsic fluorescence as a “molecular label” for bacteria
counting. QA-AuNCs can be used for the rapid and efficient
determination of the live/dead status of bacteria. After
incubation with live S. aureus for 10 min and washing, we
observed intense red fluorescence, indicating efficient bond-
ing and/or internalization of the QA-AuNCs (Figure S15a).
However, no fluorescence was observed for dead bacteria
under the same conditions, which is likely due to the
dissipated membrane charge, which fails to bind QA-
AuNCs.[26] The QA-AuNCs also enable the rapid quantifica-
tion of the total number of live bacteria by simply applying
a filter to remove the S. aureus–AuNC conjugates (Fig-
ure S15b), which is applicable to microbial assays in real
clinical samples (Figure S15c). Compared to most known
antibiotics (including AuNPs), QA-AuNCs, with both potent
antibacterial activity and imaging-guided detection, may be
more suitable for the development of theranostic antibacte-
rial platforms.
The biocompatibility of the QA-AuNCs was excellent
both in vitro and in vivo. The QA-AuNCs are not toxic to
mammalian HUVEC and A375 cells at concentrations of up
to 160 mgmL@1 (64-fold MIC; Figure S16a), consistent with
the well retained morphology of the cells (Figure S16b).
Furthermore, QA-AuNCs induced no obvious damage to
human erythrocytes at 80 mgmL@1 (32-fold MIC), which is
beneficial for blood contacting applications (e.g., wound
dressings;[27] Figure S16c). By contrast, many types of AuNPs
capped with positive ligands can damage human erythrocytes
or other host tissues.[16] QA-AuNCs are non-toxic to mice at
doses of up to 100 mgkg@1 (Figure S17a), and the physiolog-
ical biomarkers of the liver and kidneys also remained within
the normal range (Figure S17b). The histopathological results
indicated no obvious histological changes in the main organs
(heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) between the QA-
AuNC group and the control group (Figure S17c). Together,
QA-AuNCs exhibit low toxicity to both mammalian cells and
animals, and are thus suitable for further clinical use.
To demonstrate the potential application of QA-AuNCs
to combat MDR infections in vivo, we first applied QA-
AuNCs in a mouse skin infection model. We selected MRSA
T144 (106 CFUs/wound), a kind of MDR bacterium that is
resistant to oxacillin but sensitive to vancomycin,[24] to infect
the wounds, which was followed by single-dose treatment of
QA-AuNCs. A low dose of QA-AuNCs (100 mL, 10 mgmL@1)
is efficacious for bacteria elimination and wound healing, as
observed through the decreased wound sizes and bacterial
counts (Figures 3a and S18). A mouse bacteremia model was
also employed to further examine the therapeutic effect of
QA-AuNCs; MRSA T144 (107 CFUs/mouse) was intraper-
itoneally injected at a 90% lethal dose. QA-AuNCs and
various antibiotics, including oxacillin and vancomycin, were
injected intraperitoneally 1 h post-infection. The mice treated
with saline and oxacillin all died within 48 h (Figures 3b and
S19). However, all mice survived when treated with QA-
AuNC doses of 10 mgkg@1 or higher, which resembles the
effective dose of vancomycin. The bacterial counts in differ-
ent organs decreased significantly with the administration of
QA-AuNCs (Figure 3c). Typical pathologic changes of these
organs were alleviated as well (Figure S20). Additionally, the
QA-AuNCs do not cause an inflammatory response in mice,
suggested by the normal range of inflammation-related
biomarkers (Figure S21). To completely cure the mice,
which generally takes one to two weeks in this model,[28] we

QA-AuNCs possess a reasonable circulation time in mice,
making intravenous administration possible and valuable for
clinical use. The QA-AuNC concentration continuously
decreased after a single dose (20 mgkg@1) of tail vein injection
(Figure S23), corresponding to an elimination half-life of
7.5 : 2.1 h (Table S2), which is probably due to their ultra-
small size.[29] Such a long half-life is comparable to that of
vancomycin and allows for sufficient accumulation of QA-
AuNCs at the infection site to kill the bacteria.[30] We applied
QA-AuNCs in the mouse bacteremia model by tail vein
injection to test the efficacy of circulated QA-AuNCs. One
hour
after
intraperitoneal
infection
of
MRSA
T144
(107 CFUs/mouse), the mice were treated with QA-AuNCs
at single doses ranging from 10 to 40 mgkg@1 by intravenous
administration. MRSAT144 killed all mice treated with saline
and oxacillin. In contrast, throughout the 15 day feeding
period, groups treated with QA-AuNCs exhibit survival rates
greater than 90% at doses of 20 mgkg@1 or higher, which is
similar to the effective dose of vancomycin (Figure 3 f). Our
QA-AuNCs showed a good therapeutic effect in multiple
animal infection models, whereas most existing alternatives
fail to do. Together, the use of QA-AuNCs constitutes a safe
and efficient strategy for the clinical treatment of Gram-
positive MDR infections.
In conclusion, a novel class of AuNCs protected with
rationally selected ligands has been shown to be potent nano-
antibiotics against multidrug-resistant Gram-positive bacteria
in vivo, without inducing drug resistance or showing detect-
able toxicity to mammalian cells/animals. The intrinsic
fluorescence of the QA-AuNCs also enables imaging-guided
bacteria counting, suggesting that they have great potential as
theranostic antibacterial platforms for future clinical appli-
cations. Our strategy expands the versatility of nanomaterial-
based antibiotics, and also provides unique insight into
bacteria–materials interactions.

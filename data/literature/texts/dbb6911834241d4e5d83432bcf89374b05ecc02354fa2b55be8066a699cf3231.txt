A B S T R A C T
Alzheimer’s disease, a progressive neurodegenerative pathology, is characterized by the accumulation of Amy-
loid-β plaques in the brain. Lecanemab (BAN2401), a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody, binds with high
affinity to Amyloid-β protofibrils. It is the first monoclonal antibody for Alzheimer’s disease to receive full FDA
approval. This systematic review, conducted meticulously, examines the current use and safety of Lecanemab in
treating Alzheimer’s disease. We screened literature from databases such as PubMed Central, PubMed (MedLine),
ScienceDirect, Scopus, Web of Science, and Wolters Kluwer for randomized controlled trials testing Lecanemab
for cognitive decline in patients with mild cognitive impairment due to Alzheimer’s disease. Outcomes measured
included CDR-SB, ADCOMS, ADAS-Cog, and Amyloid burden on PET in centiloids. Likewise, reports were
analyzed for adverse events associated with ARIA-A and ARIA-H. Five papers were included in the systematic
review and three in the meta-analysis. The meta-analysis showed that Lecanemab slowed the progression of
cognitive impairment as measured by CDR-SB, ADCOMS, and ADASCog, and significantly reduced Amyloid
burden on PET in centiloids. However, Lecanemab was associated with an increased risk of ARIA-E and ARIA-H.
Lecanemab has demonstrated efficacy in slowing cognitive impairment progression in Alzheimer’s disease as
measured by ADCOMS, ADAS-Cog, and CDR-SB. However, it is associated with an increased risk of ARIA-E and
ARIA-H, particularly in ApoE4 carriers.
1. Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive and devastating neurode-
generative pathology that affects millions of people around the world. It
is estimated that 50 million people suffer from this disease, and by 2060
it is projected that a prevalence of 13.8 billion people affected world-
wide will be reached (“2023 Alzheimer’s disease facts and figures,”
2023). A progressive cognitive deterioration manifests itself, resulting in
a decrease in daily functions, personality alteration, and problematic
behaviors. In advanced stages, Alzheimer’s disease leads to dementia
and impacts vital functions (Atri, 2019; Rao et al., 2023).
As the world’s population ages, the incidence of this disease in-
creases, representing a significant challenge to public health and the
quality of life of patients and their families. Despite decades of intensive
research, there is still no completely effective treatment, and therefore
available therapeutics only provide temporary symptomatic relief.
Conventional therapeutic approaches have focused mainly on the
inhibition of neuronal functions on the enzyme acetylcholinesterase and
N-methyl-D-Aspartate (NMDA) receptors with limited results in terms of
slowing the progression of the disease or reversing its effects (Breijyeh
and Karaman, 2020; Buccellato et al., 2023). Therefore, there is a critical
need to develop treatments that directly address the underlying causes
of AD, such as the accumulation of Aβ plaques (Zhang et al., 2023).
The search for more effective and innovative therapies to treat AD
has become an urgent priority in the field of scientific and medical
research, which has introduced monoclonal antibody therapies in Alz-
heimer’s disease and even where the study of amyloidogenic pathways
has been addressed, mainly from the formation of Aβ, which plays an
important role in the progression of this disease. Thus, the first proposals
for new alternatives were based on bapineuzumab and solanezumab,
monoclonal antibodies proposed for treating Alzheimer’s but failed in
the last phases of experimental development (Lozupone et al., 2024;
Thangwaritorn et al., 2024). On the other hand, donanemab showed
positive results but with evidence of an increase in adverse events

(Mintun et al., 2021). Likewise, one of the molecules that has aroused
considerable interest in recent years is lecanemab, identified as
BAN2401, it is a humanized monoclonal antibody type IGg1 that binds
with high affinity to the protofibrils of the beta-amyloid peptide (Aβ)

Preclinical studies have demonstrated the ability of this monoclonal
antibody to cross the blood-brain barrier and specifically bind to Aβ
plaques, promoting their elimination through natural immune mecha-
nisms. Phase I and II clinical trials have provided promising preliminary
evidence for the safety and efficacy of lecanemab in reducing Aβ plaques
and improving cognitive function in patients with mild to moderate AD.
These findings have generated great interest in continuing clinical
research with lecanemab in larger and longer phase-III trials (van Dyck
et al., 2023).
This systematic review focuses on the current use of lecanemab in
Alzheimer’s disease and the safety record in the studies considered.
2. Material and methods
2.1. Type of study and search strategy
Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and
Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement (Page et al., 2021), a review was
developed by searching information in six databases: PubMed Central
(PMC), PubMed (Medline), ScienceDirect, Scopus, Web of Sciences, and
Wolter. The results were filtered according to restrictions on publication
interval, publication type, and language.
2.2. Eligibility criteria
Original scientific publications with complete results in indexed
journals were selected, regarding the individualized use of lecanemab in
patients diagnosed with Alzheimer’s Disease. Studies carried out in a
population under 18 years of age, neurodegenerative pathologies other
than Alzheimer’s disease, combinations of different treatments or ad-
juvants, and publications without reporting clinical studies were
excluded. On the other hand, publications such as clinical case reports,
brief communications, and systematic reviews or meta-analyses were
excluded. As well as original publications in different languages.
2.3. Study selection and data extraction
The Rayyan web application was used to detect and debug duplicate
files. Followed by the review of the titles and abstracts of potentially
eligible publications by three authors in parallel. The authors down-
loaded and analyzed the full texts jointly until the articles that met the
criteria were selected. The information from the finally chosen publi-
cations was captured through a data repository built using Microsoft
Office Excel (Microsoft Corp.). Independently, 2 authors recorded the
information of each study according to the following aspects: authors
and year of publication, title, objective of the study, type of study, study
population, age, sex, dosage, outcomes, and adverse events.
2.4. Risk of bias of included studies
Three reviewers assessed the risk of bias using the CASP Checklist
tool. They identified the type of study and implemented the corre-
sponding 11-question questionnaire for randomized controlled studies.
They were assigned a point (1) if the question was applicable, 0.5 if it
was not clear, and zero (0) if it was not applicable.
2.5. Statistical analysis
Review Manager (RevMan) software (version 5.3 for Windows)
(Cochrane Collaboration, UK) was used. The weighted mean difference
(WMD) and OR (CI 95 %) were applied to compare continuous and
dichotomous variables. The heterogeneity in studies was measured
through I2 > 50 % and chi-square p < 0.05, considering a significant
heterogeneity (Huedo-Medina et al., 2006; von Hippel, 2015). The
funnel plots were developed on Review Manager version 5.3. (Fig. 1S).
3. Results
The search identified 1056 articles, of which 258 were eliminated
due to duplicity, followed by the exclusion of 787 due to non-
compliance with the inclusion criteria. 11 potentially eligible articles
were again filtered from their full texts, and only 5 were strictly sub-
mitted for validation and definitive inclusion (Fig. 1).

The articles included information about the type of publication,
population size, and age of patients between 70 and 72 years old, which
contributed to evaluating the efficacy and safety results of lecanemab
treatment in patients with Alzheimer’s disease (Honig et al., 2023;
Logovinsky et al., 2016; McDade et al., 2022; Swanson et al., 2021; van
Dyck et al., 2023). Participant characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

3.4. Efficacy of lecanemab in the population with Alzheimer’s disease
Studies have indicated that the application of doses of 10 mg/kg of
lecanemab biweekly could reduce cerebral amyloid with favorable
scores on the ADCOMS, ADAS-Cog14, and CDR-SB scales when con-
trasting drug versus placebo according to Bayesian analysis and fre-
quencies. Likewise, clinical studies have shown that lecanemab can
significantly reduce amyloid plaques measured by positron emission
tomography (PET). Notably, this reduction was correlated with a
slowing of cognitive decline in treated patients.

3.5. Adverse events
Overall, the incidence of adverse events was similar between the two
groups. The most common adverse events in the lecanemab group
(which occurred in more than 10 % of patients) were infusion-related
reactions, the presence of microhemorrhages from hemosiderin de-
posits (ARIA-H), edema (ARIA-E), headache, and parallel presence of
other clinical entities such as COVID-19. Likewise, it has been reported
that ARIA-E was more common in ApoE-ε4 carriers than in ApoE-ε4
homozygous individuals. Finally, it has been reported that around 9
deaths were quantified during the studies, 4 of them possibly related to
the study treatment, some linked to intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH).
In Fig. 4A, Swanson et al., (2021) found a risk ratio (RR) of 12.17
(95 % CI: 2.84–52.24), while Van Dyck et al., 2023 reported a RR of 7.52
(95 % CI: 4.43–12.79). In total, events were 129 for Lecanemab and 17
for placebo, with a pooled RR of 7.97 (95 % CI: 4.85–13.10). Low het-
erogeneity was observed (I² = 0 %). The global effect test indicated high
significance with Z = 8.18 (P < 0.00001). In Fig. 4B, belonging to ARIA
H, Swanson et al., (2021) observed a risk ratio (RR) of 1.27 (95 % CI:
0.56–62.87), while Van Dyck et al.2023 reported a RR of 1.91 (95 % CI:
1.49–2.46). In total, events were 165 for Lecanemab and 93 for placebo,
with a pooled RR of 1.85 (95 % CI: 1.45–2.35). No significant hetero-
geneity was observed (I² = 0 %).
4. Discussion
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease
that impairs cognition and function. Although its pathophysiology is not
very well established, the main theory involves the accumulation of
amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau proteins, producing neuronal dysfunction and
a decrease in synaptic strength, respectively (Abubakar et al., 2022). Aβ
exists in various conformational states, including soluble monomers and
aggregates (oligomers, protofibrils), and insoluble fibrils and plaques. It
has been shown that soluble aggregates are more toxic than insoluble
ones, with protofibrils being proposed as the main toxin of this pathol-
ogy. Therefore, it is believed that reducing these could represent an
effective treatment approach in the early stages of AD and this is how
new therapeutic alternatives emerge (Swanson et al., 2021).

Lecanemab is a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody directed
against soluble and insoluble forms of Aβ, with a high affinity for olig-
omers, protofibrils, and fibrils, with a thousand-fold greater selectivity
for protofibrils than for monomers (Yoon et al., 2024). It has been shown
that lecanemab can reduce pathogenic Aβ, prevent Aβ deposition, and
selectively reduce Aβ protofibrils in the brain and CSF of AD animal
models (Shi et al., 2022). The first clinical study of lecanemab was
published in 2016, this focused on safety and tolerability at multiple
doses while evaluating pharmacokinetic properties. Using non-contrast
brain MRI, we looked for amyloid-related imaging abnormalities
(ARIA) that cause brain edema (ARIA-E) or cerebral microbleeds or
hemosiderin deposits (ARIA-H), however, no cases of ARIA-E were
found during the study and only two patients with asymptomatic
ARIA-H were found. Based on this, a phase 2b proof-of-concept dose-r-
anging efficacy study was initiated (Swanson et al., 2021; Yoon et al.,
2024).

Lecanemab 201 was an 18-month, multicenter, double-masked,
placebo-controlled, 6-arm trial. There were five lecanemab regimens:
5 mg/kg monthly, 10 mg/kg monthly, 2.5 mg/kg biweekly, 5 mg/kg
biweekly, and 10 mg/kg biweekly. The primary analyses were com-
parisons with placebo. All patients received biweekly infusions of a
placebo or the assigned dose of lecanemab in a blinded manner (Berry
et al., 2023). As a result, the 10 mg/kg biweekly and monthly doses were
identified as potentially effective dose 90 % (ED90) at the beginning of
the study. The 10 mg/kg biweekly dose was determined to be the final
ED90 dose (defined as the simplest treatment group that achieves at
least 90 % of the maximum modeled treatment effect) (Swanson et al.,
2021).
Dhadda et al., 2022, described the results of sensitivity analyses
evaluating the consistency of lecanemab efficacy results in study 201 at
the identified dose. The primary efficacy endpoint was a change from
baseline in the Alzheimer’s Disease Composite Score (ADCOMS) at 12
months with Bayesian analyses. Key secondary endpoints were ADCOMS
at 18 months Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) and
Alzheimer’s Disease Rating Scale Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-Cog14) at
18 months (Dhadda et al., 2022). At 12 months, the biweekly dose of
10 mg/kg had a 97.5 % probability of superiority over placebo, and at
18 months, it had a 97.7 % probability of superiority over placebo.
Additionally, sensitivity analyses estimate that monthly and biweekly
doses of 10 mg/kg reduced the rate of clinical disease for ADCOMS,
CDR-SB, and ADAS-Cog14 at 18 months in all statistical sensitivity
models(Dhadda et al., 2022). Hayato et al., 2022, from the analysis of
biomarkers, simulations show that a biweekly dose of 10 mg/kg results
in a more significant and faster decrease in p-Tau and an increase in the
Aβ42 ratio compared to a monthly dose of 10 mg/kg. Likewise, the
plasma proportion of Aβ42 and tau reflects the changes in CSF Aβ42 and
p-Tau, these findings being consistent with a beneficial biological effect.
Similarly, it was shown that after treatment interruption, the reac-
cumulating of brain amyloid to initial levels is slow with a recovery
half-life of 4 years, while the plasma proportion of Aβ42 and tau return
to initial levels quicker than amyloid(Hayato et al., 2022).
Likewise, a phase 3 study was developed to determine the safety and
efficacy of the drug, based on the phase 1 and 2 trials previously
described. It was assessed using CDR-SB, ADAS-cog14, ADCOMS, and
ADCS-MCI-ADL (Alzheimer’s Disease Cooperative Study: Activities of
Daily Living Scale for Mild Cognitive Impairment) where all showed

In the PET amyloid load substudy (a key secondary endpoint), the mean
amyloid level at baseline was 77.92 centiloids, at 18 months, it was

than 40 centiloids is correlated with neuropathology with a high load of

PET reverts to normality in many treated patients (P´erez Martínez,

ARIA is one of the main adverse events associated with this drug. Its
risk is believed to be linked to the severity of Aβ deposits and the extent
of cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) in the already deteriorated
vasculature, the presence of the ApoE4 genotype, and the dose of the
drug administered (DiFrancesco et al., 2015). Patients with the ApoE4
genotype have an increased risk of producing ARIA, as its presence in-
fluences the integrity of the neurovascular unit, deregulates the immune
response, and is strongly linked to the accumulation of Aβ found in ce-
rebral amyloid angiopathy (Foley and Wilcock, 2024).
Studies have shown that ApoE plays a critical role in reducing in-
flammatory signals by binding to the low-density lipoprotein receptor-
related protein 1 (LRP1) on endothelial and smooth muscle cells of the
blood-brain barrier (BBB); this interaction suppresses the Cyclophilin A
(CypA) pathway, a pro-inflammatory and matrix-degrading pathway,
the ApoE E4 isoform has a reduced affinity to LRP1, which impairs its
ability to suppress the CypA pathway, this results in the activation of the
CypA-NF-kB
signaling
cascade
that
finally
induces
Matrix
metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9). MMP9 degrades the extracellular matrix
and tight junctions in the BBB leading to increased permeability, this
leakage allows fluids, toxins, and proteins (e.g. thrombin) to infiltrate
the brain parenchyma (Kirchner et al., 2023). While thrombin is
essential for coagulation its accumulation in the brain has been shown to
increase the risk of intracranial hemorrhage in animal models, this by
activation of protease-activated receptor 1 (PAR-1), this mechanism
could explain the persistent high risk or ARIA-H in APOE4 homozygotes
regardless of whether they are taking amyloid-targeting treatments,
APOE4 has an inherent role in vascular instability that predispose in-
dividuals to microhemorrhages (Cheng et al., 2014).
Similarly, selectivity and affinity for soluble or fibrillar Aβ and
antibody isotype appear to be essential for the risk of developing ARIA-
E; antibodies with preferential binding to fibrillar over soluble Aβ spe-
cies potentially have more probabilities of involving fibrillar Aβ
deposited in CAA. Such linkage to CAA may increase the risk of ARIA-E.
In the case of lecanemab, having a strong and preferential binding to
protofibrils compared to monomeric and fibrillar forms, it may explain
the difference in clinical efficacy and the lower frequency of ARIA-E
compared to other drugs, but it still requires more research (S¨oderberg
et al., 2023).
The study’s main limitations revolve around the availability of
clinical studies to date, which condition the development of more reli-
able hypotheses regarding the efficacy and safety of lecanemab in pa-
tients with mild Alzheimer’s disease. Likewise, the continuation of
studies by other authors is required in terms of a larger number of pa-
tient cohorts and a more complete safety evaluation. However, this
contribution supports that it should be developed in other drugs under
evaluation, such as donanemab, which shows a similar outlook to
lecanemab.
5. Conclusions
It has been established in this review that lecanemab in patients aged
70–72 years with Alzheimer’s disease showed promising results in terms
of efficacy and safety. The trials, with an average quality of 10.1 ± 0.42,
evaluated different dosages, with the 10 mg/kg dose standing out for its
positive effects on cognitive functioning and the reduction of brain
amyloid. Despite the effectiveness observed in the ADCOMS, ADAS-
Cog14, and CDR-SB scales, the treatment also presented significant
adverse events, including ARIA-H, ARIA-E, and other complications
such as microbleeds and edema, being more frequent in ApoE-ε4 car-
riers. The incidence of adverse events was comparable between groups,
although 9 deaths were recorded, 4 of them possibly related to treat-
ment. These findings underscore the need to carefully balance the
cognitive benefits and risks associated with lecanemab treatment.

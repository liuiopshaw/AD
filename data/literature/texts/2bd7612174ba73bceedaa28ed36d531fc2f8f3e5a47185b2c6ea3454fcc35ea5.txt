ABSTRACT: The indoor air quality should be better controlled
and improved to avoid numerous health issues. Even if different
devices are developed for air filtration, the proliferation of
microorganisms under certain conditions must be controlled. For
this purpose, a silver nanocluster/silica composite coating was
deposited via a cosputtering technique onto fiber glass and
polymeric based substrates. The aim of this work is focused on the
evaluation of the antibacterial and antiviral effects of the developed
coating. The preliminary results of the compositional and
morphological tests showed an evenly distributed coating on
filters surfaces. Several antibacterial tests were performed,
confirming strong effect both in qualitative and quantitative
methods, against S. epidermidis and E. coli. To understand if the coating can stop the proliferation of bacteria colonies spread on it,
simulation of everyday usage of filters was performed, nebulizing bacteria solution with high colonies concentration and evaluating
the inhibition of bacteria growth. Additionally, a deep understanding of the virucidal action and mechanism of Ag nanoclusters of the
coating was performed. The effect of the coating both in aqueous medium and in dry methods was evaluated, in comparison with
analysis on ions release. The virucidal performances are assessed against the human coronavirus OC43 strain (HCoV-OC43).
KEYWORDS: silver, composite coating, virucidal, antibacterial, filters, sputtering
1. INTRODUCTION
The poor quality of indoor air can have dire consequences for
the health, comfort, and productivity of people inside
buildings. In general, a decrease in the air quality level is
caused by airborne pollutants, which are mainly constituted by
bacteria, viruses, and fungi, (known as “bioaerosols”), by
nonorganic particles, such as dust and tobacco smoke, as well
as by certain occupants, such as pets, or even by the building
materials and organic waste.1−3 Therefore, air purification
becomes of fundamental importance for indoor environments,
but also considering that people spend about 90% of their time
indoors.4−7
Because such airborne particles may be very small and light,
they can flow easily from one place to another.8 When
bioaerosol particles have a size within the 0.01−100 μm range,
they can be inhaled, enter the respiratory tract, and reach
secondary organs, thereby causing numerous health problems,
such as asthma, allergies, and several infectious diseases.2,9,10
Air filtration technologies are able to improve indoor air
quality by retaining airborne microorganisms and drastically
reducing their amounts, but bacteria, fungi, and spores can
proliferate in heating, ventilation, and air conditioning
(HVAC) systems, especially under certain temperatures,
moisture, and dirt accumulation conditions. In addition, the
growth of microorganisms on the surfaces of HVAC systems
decreases the filtering performances and deteriorates the
system, with a possible release of microorganisms in the
indoor environment.11−13
Therefore, a durable filtration device capable of preventing
the proliferation and spread of microorganisms is needed to
avoid these problems.
In the context of high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA)
filters and HVAC systems, researchers have developed
modification techniques for existing systems to create devices
with dual functionalities, encompassing effective filtration and
virucidal or antibacterial effects. Novel materials have also been
explored for this purpose. Notably, Mallakpour et al.15 and
Madushani et al.16 extensively discussed various options for
potential new air filter devices. Surface functionalization
techniques, such as those involving tannic acid17 or hybrid
materials,18 and the incorporation of antiviral and antibacterial

have been extensively tested on a broad spectrum of

mentally friendly technique,26,27 aligning with a key objective
of the present research.
In more detail, among the various proposed methodologies,
particularly metallic nanoparticles such as gold, silver, and
copper as antiviral/antibacterial agent are widely used for their
well- known antimicrobial effects.29−33 Silver nanoparticles, or
nanoclusters (AgNPs), have in particular shown an antimicro-
bial effect toward a wide spectrum of bacteria and viruses,
including Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli,34,35 HIV,36,37
and the influenza A virus.38,39 AgNPs have recently also shown
an antiviral effect toward coronaviruses, including the SARS-
CoV-2 virus, which caused the Covid-19 pandemic in 2020.
For this reason, AgNPs have been used to produce sprays and
common mouthwash solutions,40 or in individual protective
equipment, such as facial masks.41,42
However, the mechanism behind the conference of
antimicrobial properties to silver nanoparticles is not yet
well-known but is probably the result of a combination of the
reactive nature of AgNPs and the release of silver ions, which
are able to form a bond with the bacterium cell wall, destroy its
membrane, and inhibit cell replication.43
To better understand the interaction between micro-
organisms and AgNPs, the influence of factors such as size,
dimension, and concentration on the microbicidal effect is
widely explored in the literature. It is widely demonstrated that
nanoparticles with dimensions less than 100 nm are commonly
used in antimicrobial and antiviral applications, with the
greatest efficacy observed in smaller particles due to the ratio
between the exposed surface area and volume.44−48 Moreover,
smaller nanoparticles can readily interact with bacteria and
viruses, penetrating the membrane and causing its destruction.
Concentration also plays a pivotal role: as the nanoparticle
quantity increases, the observed effect becomes stronger.49−51
However, an excess of silver nanoparticles could lead to
cytotoxic effects.52,53 During nanoparticle synthesis, it is also
feasible to control their shape. Although nanorods or
nanowires can be obtained, spherical nanoparticles have been
shown to exhibit superior with microorganisms, facilitating the
inhibition of viral replication and proliferation.54,55
AgNPs can be synthesized via chemical, physical, or
biological methods.57 Haggag et al. tried to synthesize silver
nanoparticles through the green method, starting from air-
dried powder of L. coccineus and M. lutea, in order to avoid the
use of chemicals and the production of waste and to obtain
nanoparticles that could be used in biomedical applications to
reduce cytotoxicity or pollution problems.58 AgNPs obtained
by means of a biological method are usually larger in size than
those obtained through an electrochemical process.50
In some instances, chemically synthesized AgNPs are
incorporated into an organic or nonorganic matrix, serving a
dual purpose of preventing the dispersion of nanoparticles in
the surrounding environment and ensuring a gradual and
controlled release of silver. This results in a prolonged lifetime
of the device.43,44 AgNP/chitosan composites have indeed
demonstrated antiviral effects against the H1N1 influenza A
virus.44 The antiviral effect was exclusively attributed to the
presence of silver, while the chitosan matrix proved
instrumental in preventing the dispersion of nanoparticles in
the environment.
Commercial silver nanoparticles have also been dispersed in
a carbon foam matrix to prevent coalescence during synthesis.
However, the bond between AgNPs and a carbon foam matrix
was weak, leading to an easy release of nanoparticles from the
matrix. This resulted in close interaction with the HIV-1 virus
but also increased their toxicity.36 Some systems incorporate
silver nanoparticles for their antibacterial and virucidal effects
within a silica matrix. The use of silica offers advantages,
including preventing agglomeration of nanoparticles. For
instance Acharya et al.59 conducted a study comparing the
effects of Ag Nps and Ag-doped SiO2 NPs against various
bacteria. They found that the presence of a silica shell
surrounding AgNPs enhanced their antibacterial activity by
prolonging the release of Ag ions. Similar effects were observed
using silica as a shell for AgCl nanoparticles, as demonstrated
by Tan et al.60 Due to these benefits, systems composed of
silver/silica nanoparticles have been employed in HVAC
production to limit the proliferation of bacteria and to
inactivate viruses. Various approaches can be used for filter
production, with a common method involving the creation of
silver/silica nanoparticle systems for coating air filters. Air filter
media coated with AgNPs or AgNPs-coated SiO2 spheres have
shown a strong antiviral effect against bacteriophage MS2,
without altering the filtration performances.61,62 Haeng et al.61
used AgNPs-coated nanosilica particles to coat air filters,
providing effective virucidal action against MS2 viruses. To
avoid the use of liquid in the coating process, studies on dry
aerosol coatings have been conducted. Park et al.23 developed a
system for producing aerosolized SiO2−Ag nanoparticles,
which are dried using a sheath air flow, for coating air filters.
This approach maintains the filtration performance while
imparting antiviral properties to the filters.
An alternative method involves incorporating the SiO2−Ag
system into a material, typically a polymeric matrix. This is
exemplified by membranes obtained by Wu et al.,63 who
electrospun PVDF/SiO2 solution with silver nitrate as the
precursor for AgNPs to produce PVDF/SiO2/Ag composite
nanofiber membranes. These membranes exhibit good
mechanical and antibacterial properties, coupled with super-
hydrophobicity and self-cleaning capacity, making them
suitable for HEPA applications. They also attempted to
achieve a multilayer filter material by alternating layers of
PVDF/SiO2/Ag composite nanofiber with PET nonwoven
fabric. The high filtration efficiency of the membranes and
strong antiviral capability make this system promising for the
production of new filtration systems. To understand the role of
silica in the antiviral effect within these systems, Assis et al.64
immobilized silica/Ag particles within a polymeric matrix and
tested the system against bacteria (S. aureus and E. coli) and
the SARS-CoV-2 virus. They concluded that the antibacterial
and antiviral effects of the system are due to the surface plasma
resonance effect, enhanced by the simultaneous presence of Ag
anchored to silica. This makes the SiO2/Ag system potentially
applicable in various devices requiring biocidal action such as
HVAC systems.
In this context, a silver nanocluster/silica composite coating,
deposited by means of cosputtering, has been developed by the
authors.65−68 The presence of a silica matrix allows the gradual
release of silver ions, in a controlled manner, and prevents the
dispersion of nanoclusters in the environment.69 The coating

coatings is discussed hereafter.
2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. Coating Deposition. The coatings are composed of silver
nanoclusters embedded in a silica matrix and deposited, by means of a
patented cosputtering process,39 onto glass fiber and polymeric air
filters, named Glass and Pol in the text, respectively, provided by GV
Filtri Srl, Italy. The sputtering equipment was set up for two targets
for each deposition: a silica one (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.99% purity) and a
silver one (Franco Corradi S.r.l., 99.9% purity). A 200 W power was
applied, in radio frequency mode (RF), to the silica target for both of
the air filters, while different powers were applied to the silver target, 3
and 5 W, applied in direct current mode (DC), to the glass fiber-
based filter (called Glass-Ag3 and Glass-Ag5 in the text), while 3 W
was applied, in DC, to the polymeric filter (called Pol-Ag3 in the text).
All the depositions were performed for 1 h in a pure argon
atmosphere, under a pressure of 5.5 dPa.
2.2. Coating Characterization. Compositional analyses were
conducted by means of energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, EDAX
PV 9900TM), which allowed the atomic percentage of the elements
constituting the coatings to be determined, that is, Si for the silica
matrix and Ag for the silver nanoclusters.
Morphological analyses were performed using a field emission
scanning electron microscope (FESEM, QUANTA INSPECT 200,
Zeiss SUPRA 40), at different magnifications, in order to verify that
the deposition had occurred successfully with a homogeneous
distribution of the silver nanoclusters inside the silica matrix. For
this analysis, the in-lens detector was used with secondary electrons.
2.3. Leaching Test in Water. The amount of silver ions released
by the coated air filters was evaluated by means of a leaching test in
Milli-Q water at room temperature. Samples (1 × 1 cm2) were
immersed in 30 mL of water, with the coated surface face up. The
solutions were analyzed after 3 h, as well as after 1, 3, 7, and 14 days,
by means of a spectrophotometer (Hanna Instruments), and the
concentration of silver ions was determined in ppm. Each measure-
ment was performed in triplicate.
2.4. Antibacterial Tests. The antibacterial properties of the
coated air filters were evaluated by means of three different tests:
inhibition halo test, dilution test, and a bacterial contamination test.
2.4.1. Inhibition Halo Test. The inhibition halo test was performed
according to the NCCLS M2-A9 performance standard,77 and two
nonpathogen bacteria, Staphylococcus epidermidis (Gram-positive,
ATCC14990) and Escherichia coli (Gram-negative, ATCC8739)
were tested. The bacterial solutions (McFarland index 0.5) were
spread on Mueller Hinton agar plates. Coated and uncoated air filters
(1 × 1 cm2) were then placed onto the agar plates and incubated at 35
°C for 24 h. After the incubation, the formation of a halo was
observed around the samples and evaluated.
2.4.2. Dilution Test. The dilution test was used to evaluate the
number of colony-forming units (CFU) of S. epidermidis that had
adhered to the surface samples and proliferated in the broth.78 The
starting solution for this test was a broth containing a standard
number of bacteria, that is, of approximately 5 × 105 CFU/mL. Three
uncoated and three coated air filters (2 × 0.5 cm2) were immersed in
the bacterial broth and incubated at 35 °C for 24 h. After incubation,
the turbidity of each tube was evaluated, and the dilutions were
prepared with physiological solutions. In order to calculate the
number of CFU that had adhered to the surface, the samples were
removed from the broth, immersed in 3 mL of physiological solution
water, and vortexed for 5 min. These solutions were then diluted with
other physiological solutions. Finally, an aliquot of the diluted
solutions was spread onto a blood agar plate and incubated at 35 °C
for 24 h. At this point, it was possible to count the number of CFU on
each plate.
2.4.3. Contamination Test. This test consists of the contamination
of the filters in a control manner through a bacterial bioaerosol in
order to simulate the HVAC system. This is an experimental setup
developed by author and already described in a previous paper.75 The
device is composed of a compressed air system, a bioaerosol generator
(TOPAS ATM 220), and a filter holder. The bacterial aerosol was
generated from a bacterial suspension containing a high concentration
of S. epidermidis colonies (McFarland index 5.0) at an airflow of 3 bar.
Bioaerosol was nebulized on the filter and put inside the filter holder
for 30 min. After the contamination, the filters were placed on Mueller
Hinton plates and incubated at 35 °C for 24 h. Then, bacterial growth
was evaluated around and below the filter surface. Three coated and
one uncoated filters as control (diameter 4 cm) were tested.
2.5. Antiviral Tests. The glass fiber and polymeric filters were
subjected to different antiviral tests in order to evaluate the virucidal
effect of the coating. The tests were performed by comparing coated
and uncoated substrates.
2.5.1. Cell Lines and Viruses. Human lung MRC-5 fibroblast cells
(ATCC CCL-171) were propagated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle
Medium (DMEM; Sigma, St. Louis, MO) supplemented with a 1%
(v/v) penicillin/streptomycin solution (Euroclone, Milan, Italy) and
heat inactivated with 10% (v/v) of fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma).
The human coronavirus OC43 strain (HCoV-OC43) (ATCC VR-
1558) was propagated in MRC-5 cells at 33 °C, in a humidified 5%
CO2 incubator, and titrated in a focus-reduction assay in MRC-5 cells.
After 20 h of incubation at 33 °C, the cells were fixed with cold
acetone−methanol (50:50) and subjected to indirect immunostain-
ing, using an anticoronavirus monoclonal antibody (MAB9013,
Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and a peroxidase-conjugated AffiniPure
F(ab’)2 Fragment Goat Anti-Mouse IgG secondary antibody (H + L)
(MAB9013, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories Inc., West Grove,
PA). The number of immunostained foci was counted, and the titer
was expressed in terms of focus forming units per mL (FFU/mL).
HCoV-OC43 has an extremely high structural homology with SARS-
CoV-2, from both the phylogenetic and molecular points of view, and
it has been used in several viral persistence/inactivation studies as a
model substitute for the highly pathogenic SARS-1, SARS-2, and
MERS coronaviruses.79,80
2.5.2. Virucidal Test in an Aqueous Medium/Screening Assay.
This assay was performed as a first screening to evaluate any antiviral
activity mediated by both a direct interaction of the virus with the
silver nanocluster/silica composite coating deposited on the
substrates and the Ag ions released from the silver nanoclusters
dispersed in the silica matrix of the coating. The experiment was
performed as previously reported.76 Briefly, an aliquot of approx-
imately 106 HCoV-OC43 of viral particles was resuspended in a final
500 μL volume of a cell culture medium (DMEM), supplemented
with 2% of serum. The virus + medium mixture was then incubated
under continuous oscillation together with the samples (cut into1
cm2, coated or uncoated pieces) for 1 h 30 min at room temperature.
The same number of viral particles was incubated under the same
experimental conditions, but without any samples, and used as a
control. Subsequently, the preincubated virus + medium mixture was
collected, and the residual viral infectivity was assessed, by means of
titration on MRC-5 cells, as described above. Viral inactivation was
determined by comparing the titer of the virus inoculated on the
coated filters to that of the viral suspension inoculated on the

2.5.3. Virucidal Test through the Drying Method. This assay was
designed to obtain preliminary results on the mechanism of action of
the silver nanocluster/silica composite coatings. It was performed to
evaluate whether the direct interaction of viral particles with the
coatings could have been the cause of the titer reduction observed in
the screening assay. A fixed virus inoculum (25 μL, ∼106 FFU) was
inoculated onto samples cut into 1 cm2 coated and uncoated pieces.
In parallel, the same viral inocula was placed into a sterile plastic
support. The samples were then incubated at room temperature for
60 min until the 25 μL initially inoculated onto the filters had
completely dried. After the incubation, the samples were transferred
to a sterile 24-well plate; an aliquot of 300 μL of DMEM,
supplemented with 20% FBS, was dispensed in each well, and the
plate was incubated at 150 rpm at room temperature for 15 min.
Subsequently, three sequential 3 s vortexing passages were performed
for each sample. These steps were necessary to recover residual viral
particles from the filters. Virus inactivation of the coating was
evaluated by means of titration on MRC-5 cells as described above.
Inactivation was determined by comparing the titer of the inoculated
virus on the coated filters to the titer of the inoculated suspension on
the uncoated ones. The logarithm of inactivation (R) was calculated
as described above.
2.5.4. Ion Release Antiviral Test. This assay was performed to
evaluate any indirect virucidal action mediated by the Ag+ ions
released by the nanostructured coatings. An aliquot of 500 μL of
DMEM + 2% FBS was preincubated with the 1 cm2, coated, and
uncoated samples for 1 h 30 min at room temperature under
continuous stirring. Subsequently, the preincubated medium contain-
ing any eventual Ag ions released from the coatings (ionic extract)
was inoculated with 106 viral particles of HCoV-OC43. The viral
particles were then incubated with the ionic extract under continuous
oscillation for 1 h 30 min to ensure that the time condition was the
same as that used for the virucidal assay conducted in an aqueous
medium. At the end of the treatment, viral inactivation of the ionic
extract was evaluated by means of titration on MRC5 cells, as
described above. Virus inactivation was determined by comparing the
titer of the virus incubated with the ion extract to the titer of the virus
incubated with the control extract (the medium incubated with
uncoated samples). The logarithm of inactivation (R) was calculated
as described above. To exclude the possibility of the uncoated filter
releasing antiviral substances, an additional control was added to the
assay: an aliquot of 500 μL of DMEM + 2% FBS was treated under
the same time and temperature conditions, but without any sample.
2.5.5. Statistical Analysis. The results were presented as the mean
values of three independent experiments (mean ± SEM). One-way
ANOVA and the Bonferroni test were used to assess the statistical
significance of the differences between the treated and untreated
samples, where appropriate (significance was set at P < 0.05).
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

glass fiber and polymeric air filters, as confirmed by the EDS
analysis results reported in Table 1. Si, which was already
present in the uncoated glass fiber filter, was found to have
increased, due to the deposited silica matrix. Ag was only
detected on the coated substrates and increased from 0.9 to 1.6
at. % as the power applied to the target was increased. The
polymeric filter only contained carbon, oxygen, and nitrogen
before the coating deposition. Si and Ag appeared on the Pol-
Ag3 sample with atomic percentages of about 1 and 0.8,
respectively.

3.2. Release Test in Water. The precise mechanism of the
interaction between silver and bacteria or viruses is not fully
known. However, both nanoparticles and ions play crucial
synergistic roles in their interaction with microorganisms. It is
well-known that silver ions are released into the surrounding
environment from silver nanoparticles, as a function of their
dimension, concentration, and the covering agents that could
coat the nanoparticles.81,82 The silica matrix coating embeds
silver nanoclusters and prevents their dispersion in the
environment:69 this feature is of fundamental importance
because it prevents the occurrence of potential respiratory
issues resulting from the inhalation of silver nanoparticles. In
addition, the porous nature of the sputtered silica matrix also
allows for silver ions to be released from the embedded
nanoclusters.
For this reason, the overall amount of silver ions released
into water at room temperature was investigated: this test
condition is far more severe than the environmental conditions
under which air filters work, but it is useful to understand the
kinetics of released silver ions as a function of time.
Figure 2 reports the release of the silver ions from the silver
nanocluster/silica composite coated glass fiber and polymeric
filters. The quantity of ions detected in the water for the coated
glass fiber filters is about 0.2 and 0.4 ppm for Glass-Ag3 and
Glass-Ag5, respectively. Indeed, the higher the amount of silver
in the coating is, the higher the leached amount. However, the
value is about 0.5 ppm for the Pol-Ag3 filter, even though the
Ag amount is similar to that of Glass-Ag3. The polymeric filter
is probably easily soaked with water and thus releases ions
faster.
3.3. Antibacterial Tests. Figure 3 shows the inhibition
halo results of the silver nanocluster/silica composite coating
deposited on both the (a) glass fiber and (b) polymeric filters
against S. epidermidis and E. coli. A bacteria-free zone of about
3−5 mm is clearly visible around all the coated glass fiber and
polymeric filters.
No significant differences are evident when comparing the
coating with higher silver content to the one with less silver
amount (Ag5 vs Ag3). However, it is noteworthy that the
inhibition zone of Glass-Ag3 appears slightly larger than that
obtained for Glass-Ag5. It is crucial to emphasize that this test
is qualitative rather than quantitative. Furthermore, while the
amount of Ag in Glass-Ag5 is higher than that in Glass-Ag3,
the distribution of nanoclusters on the sample surface and
within the matrix could vary. The inhibition halo did not form
around the uncoated substrates, which means that bacteria
were able to proliferate.
Because the inhibition halo evaluation is a qualitative test, a
further antibacterial test of the CFU count was performed in
order to quantitatively assess the proliferation of bacteria and
the adhesion of S. epidermidis onto samples. Figure 4 reports
the histograms relative to the results of the CFU (a)
proliferated in broth and (b) the CFU that remained adhered
to the sample surface and detected in the vortex solution.
As reported in the histograms in Figure 4a, the silver
nanocluster/silica composite coating reduced the bacterial
proliferation in the broth by 3 and 5 orders of magnitude for
the polymeric and glass fiber filters, respectively, even though
only one side of the substrate had been coated. The vortex
solution (Figure 4b) registered a reduction in the CFU that
had adhered to the coated sample surfaces. This decrement in
number of CFU is more remarkable (of 4 orders of magnitude)
for the glass fiber filters than that obtained for the polymeric

Both the Glass-Ag3 and Glass-Ag5 samples demonstrate a
significant reduction in the number of infectious HCoV-OC43
particles, compared to the uncoated glass fiber filter (Glass), of
around 0.8 log (logarithm of inactivation). No statistically
significant difference was observed in the anti-CoV efficacy
between the two coatings. Nevertheless, the Glass-Ag5 sample
showed a numerically superior anti-CoV efficacy compared to
Glass-Ag3, and it was therefore selected for the continuation of
the study. The Pol-Ag3 sample also showed a significant anti-
CoV activity, whereby the HCoV-OC43 titer was reduced by
1.1 log compared to the uncoated polymeric filter. Altogether,
these results demonstrate that the analyzed composite coatings
are endowed with anti-OC43 properties regardless of the
substrate on which they are deposited.
Two different antiviral tests were performed to preliminarily
investigate the mechanism of action of the coatings when
deposited on glass fiber (Glass-Ag5) and polymeric (Pol-Ag3)
air filters (Figure 7).
The virucidal test, conducted through a drying method, was
designed to evaluate whether the direct interaction of viral
particles with the coating could be the cause of the virucidal
activity observed in the screening assay. Two different
mechanisms were observed as a function of the air filter
material, and numerical results are shown in Table 3. In fact,
the Pol-Ag3 samples (Figure 7a) showed an anti-CoV efficacy
that was comparable to that obtained in the screening assay.
This suggests that the coating is able to directly alter and
inactivate the viral particles, regardless of the ion release. In
this case, the inactivation logarithm was about 1.4. On the
other hand, the antiviral activity of Glass-Ag5 in an aqueous
medium was mediated by the silver ions released from the
coating (inactivation logarithm only 0.3), and the coated glass
fiber filter therefore did not show any direct virucidal activity.
The second antiviral test (Figure 7b) was thus conducted to
evaluate an indirect virucidal action mediated by the released
silver ions. The Glass-Ag5 sample reduced the viral titer by
about 0.7 Log, thus confirming that the virucidal action of the
sample was mediated by the release of silver ions from the
silver nanocluster/silica composite coating. The Pol-Ag3 filter
also showed a residual anti-CoV action mediated by the ion
extract (0.5 log), and the mechanism of action was thus
ascribable more to direct contact, thus verifying the previous
experiment.

4. CONCLUSIONS
The composite coating made of silver nanoclusters that were
embedded well within a silica matrix were successfully
deposited onto glass fiber and polymeric air filters by means
of a cosputtering technique. Morphological and compositional
analyses confirmed the homogeneity of the coating on each
substrate. Silver ion release tests were conducted in water for
14 days, and they showed a gradual release of ions, which is
known to be necessary to obtain a prolonged and continuous
antibacterial effect of the coating. The coating showed
antibacterial properties against Gram-positive and Gram-
negative bacteria strains, that is, S. epidermidis and E. coli. A
test of the inhibition halo and a CFU count confirmed the
strong antibacterial effect of the silver nanocluster/silica
composite coating on both air filters. The antiviral activity of
the composite coating was assessed against the human
coronavirus OC43 strain. The virucidal mechanism of action
of the coating seemed to be different for each substrate. The
coated glass fiber air filter demonstrated an indirect virucidal
effect related to the release of silver ions, whereas the coated
polymeric filter was able to directly alter and inactivate the viral
particles. A direct virucidal action, demonstrated by means of
the drying assay, could be more interesting for the application
of coatings on materials in contact with air. On the other hand,
the ion release-mediated mechanism of action means the
coating would function better in an aqueous medium.

ABSTRACT: Although a homometallic (isotopic metal) exchange reaction has been
reported, the in-depth understanding of the interaction between a metalloid cluster and
the homometal (representing the same metal element as the metalloid cluster) thiolate
is quite limited, especially at the atomic level. Herein, based on Ag44(SR)30 (where SR
represents 4-mercaptobenzoic acid), we report a facile approach for investigating the
metalloid cluster−homometal thiolate interaction at the atomic level, i.e., isotopic
exchange in the Ag metalloid cluster. Since such a reaction takes no account of the
enthalpy change-related heterometal (representing a diﬀerent metal element) exchange,
the intrinsic metalloid cluster−homometal thiolate interaction can be thoroughly
investigated. Through analyzing the ESI-MS (electrospray ionization mass spectrom-
etry) and MS/MS (mass/mass spectrometry) results of the reversible conversion
between
107Ag44(SR)30 and
109Ag44(SR)30, we observed that all Ag atoms are
exchangeable in the Ag44(SR)30 template. In addition, through analyzing the ESI-MS
results
of
the

between
107Ag29(BDT)12(TPP)4
and
109Ag29(BDT)12(TPP)4, we demonstrated that the metal exchange in the Ag29(BDT)12(TPP)4 metalloid cluster should be a shell
→kernel metal transfer process. Our results provide new insights into the metalloid cluster reactivity in the homometal thiolate
environment, which will guide the future preparation of metalloid clusters with customized structures and properties.
1. INTRODUCTION
Scientiﬁc research on metalloid clusters has a long history, and
signiﬁcant eﬀorts have been made on manipulation over the
structures (e.g., metal doping and ligand exchange) and the
properties (e.g., optical and catalytic properties) of nano-
particles.1−16 However, research on metalloid clusters has
generally focused on structure determination and property
investigations, while analysis on interactions between a
metalloid cluster and foreign species just began to receive
attention in the past decade.17−23 Previous studies on such
interactions mainly include the following two aspects: (i) the
metal exchange process between a metalloid cluster (as
precursors) and heterometal (representing a diﬀerent metal
element from the metalloid cluster) thiolate in the environ-
ment21,24−42 and (ii) the intercluster reactions between
diﬀerent types of metalloid clusters in the same environ-
ment.43−46 Speciﬁcally, for the metal exchange process,
heterometal substitution is always determined by the relative
energies of the doping sites and the characteristic of
heterometals because of their diﬀerent electron aﬃnities. In
this context, a fundamental understanding of the interaction
between a metalloid cluster and the homometal thiolate still
remains challenging. Recently, the Pradeep group exploited the
isotopic exchange on metalloid clusters to understand their
dynamics and interactions and found that the reaction between
two metalloid silver clusters made of 107Ag and 109Ag was
similar to that of H2O and D2O forming HDO, demonstrating
the equilibrium statistical distribution between these metalloid
clusters.44 However, the in-depth understanding of the
interaction between a metalloid cluster and the homometal
thiolate is still limited, especially at the atomic level. A
homometallic exchange platform that can be utilized to probe
the interaction between a metalloid cluster and homometal
thiolate and to avoid the relative enthalpy changes of diﬀerent
heterometals is highly desirable.
Herein, based on the Ag44(SR)30 metalloid cluster template,
we have probed the metalloid cluster−homometal thiolate
interaction via reacting the 107Ag44(SR)30 metalloid cluster with
109Ag-SR thiolate or reacting
109Ag44(SR)30 with
107Ag-SR.
Since such reactions take no account of the enthalpy change-
related heterometal exchange, the intrinsic metalloid cluster−
homometal thiolate interaction can be thoroughly investigated.
Interestingly, all metal atoms in the Ag44(SR)30 metalloid
cluster are exchangeable by metal atoms in the Ag(I) thiolate.

2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Chemicals. Isotopically pure Ag powders, 107Ag (97.8%) and
109Ag (99.67%), were purchased from ISOFLEX, California, USA.
Other reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All reagents and
solvents were used directly without further puriﬁcation. 4-
Mercaptobenzoic acid (C7H6O2S, >95%), sodium borohydride
(NaBH4, >98%), Caesium hydroxide (CsOH, 50 wt % solution in
water), 1,3-benzenedimethanethiol (BDT, >98%), triphenylphos-
phine (TPP, >99%), dichloromethane (DCM, HPLC grade,
≥99.9%), ethanol (EtOH, HPLC grade, ≥99.9%), methanol
(MeOH, HPLC grade, ≥99.9%), deionized water (H2O, ordered
from Wahaha Co. LTD.), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, HPLC
grade, ≥99.9%), toluene (Tol, HPLC grade, ≥99.9%), acetic acid
(HOAc, ≥99.8%), and nitric acid (HNO3, 65−68 wt %) were used.
2.2. Synthesis of Isotopically Pure Silver Nitrate (107AgNO3
and 109AgNO3) from Isotopically Pure Metal Foils (107Ag and
109Ag). The preparation of 107AgNO3 and 109AgNO3 was based on
the method reported by the Pradeep group with minor modiﬁca-
tions.44 Especially, about 100 mg of the metal powder of the isotopes
of Ag (107Ag and 109Ag) was separately reacted with about 1.6 mL of
concentrated nitric acid (65%−68%) in a 25 mL reaction round-
bottom ﬂask and heated at 100 °C inside a fume hood. The heating
process was allowed to continue for ∼30 min. Then, 5 mL of water
was added to dilute the solution, and heating was continued. The
addition of water was repeated a few times. Finally, 0.8 mL of a
concentrated solution of AgNO3 was incubated at 135 °C for 2 h and
the colorless crystals of AgNO3 (107AgNO3 and 109AgNO3) were
obtained.
2.3. Synthesis of [107Ag44(SR)30]4−and [109Ag44(SR)30]4−NCs.
Isotopically pure [Ag44(SR)30]4−NCs were prepared according to the
reported method.47,48 107AgNO3/109AgNO3 (0.25 mmol, 43 mg) was
dissolved in 21 mL of H2O, and C7H6O2S (SR, 1.0 mmol, 154 mg)
dissolved in 12 mL of ethanol was added under vigorous stirring
(1000 rpm) to yield 107Ag(I)SR/109Ag(I)SR thiolate. The pH of the
solution was adjusted by adding 400 μL of CsOH solution (50 wt %
in H2O). After that, an aqueous solution of NaBH4 (0.27 mmol/mL,
9 mL) was added dropwise. The solution gradually turned to reddish
black, and the purplish-red solution was obtained after the reaction
was kept under room temperature for 7 h.
The raw [107Ag44(SR)30]4−/[109Ag44(SR)30]4−NCs were separated
from the purplish-red solution by centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 5
min and precipitated with ethanol to remove any soluble impurities.
The precipitate was then dissolved in 7 mL of DMF solution
(containing 30 vol % HOAc). The protonated clusters (as precipitate)
were collected by centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 3 min. The
collected clusters were then re-dissolved in 5 mL of DMF solution
(containing 1 vol % HOAc) followed by centrifugation in the
presence of 15 mL of toluene. Finally, the precipitate was subjected to
rotary evaporation for further use.
2.4. Synthesis of Isotopically Pure [107Ag29(BDT)12(TPP)4]3−
Cluster. The synthesis of [Ag29(BDT)12(TPP)4]3−referred to the
previous work.44,49,50 107AgNO3 (20 mg, 0.12 mmol) was dissolved in
a mixed solution of CH3OH (5 mL) and DCM (10 mL). BDT (13.5
μL, 0.2 mmol) was added to the mixed solution in a dropwise manner
under vigorous stirring (∼1200 rpm). TPP (200 mg, 0.76 mmol) was
added to the solution simultaneously. The reaction was allowed to
proceed for 10 min at room temperature. After that, a freshly prepared
aqueous solution of NaBH4 (10.5 mg, 0.277 mmol, 0.5 mL) was
added. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 8 h under a N2
atmosphere. After reaction, the mixture was centrifuged and the
precipitate was retained. Then, approximately 10 mL of DCM was
added to wash the synthesized products (three times). The puriﬁed
products were left to dry overnight under vacuum.
2.5. Synthesis of 109Ag(I)BDT Thiolate. 109AgNO3 (108.39 mg,
0.633 mmol) was dissolved in CH3OH (10 mL). BDT (40.5 μL, 0.33
mmol) was added under vigorous stirring (∼1200 rpm), and stirring
was continued for 20 min. After that, the resulting product was
washed with CH3OH three times.
2.6. Synthesis of 107Ag(I)SR and 109Ag(I)SR Thiolate. A total
of 10 mL of 107AgNO3/109AgNO3 (0.8 mmol, 140 mg) aqueous
solution was added into 7 mL of ethanol solution of C7H6O2S (SR,
3.2 mmol, 492 mg) followed by the addition of 358 μL of HOAc.
Then, the solution was vigorously stirred (1000 rpm) for 10 min,
yielding a milk-white suspension of 107Ag(I)SR/109Ag(I)SR thiolate.
The milk-white suspension was washed several times with ethanol.
The complexes were obtained by centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for 3
min.
2.7. Reactions between 107Ag(I)SR/109Ag(I)SR Thiolate and
[109Ag44(SR)30]4−/[107Ag44 (SR)30]4−NCs. The reaction of 107Ag(I)-
SR thiolate and [109Ag44(SR)30]4−NCs proceeded in DMF solution.
Especially, 220 mmol of 107Ag(I)SR thiolate was added to 50 mL of
DMF solution of [109Ag44(SR)30]4−with a concentration of 1.0 mM.
The reaction was monitored by ESI-MS, tandem MS, and UV−Vis
absorption spectroscopy. The reaction of 109Ag(I)SR thiolate and
[107Ag44(SR)30]4−NCs was analogous except that the corresponding
isotopes are replaced. It is worth noting that, to prevent the eﬀects of
excessive acetic acid, all samples were further puriﬁed by using the
desalinating column before being tested. Noted that, for accurate
UV−Vis spectroscopy tracking, samples were dissolved in DMF and
diluted in the same solvent to 100 times.

Ag44(SR)30 framework.47 Although the precise alloying process
as well as the corresponding energy barriers remains unclear,
the introduced heteroatoms should occupy the sites with the
lowest energies or the lowest surface electrostatic potential.24
Nevertheless, several other questions remain unanswered:
Could all sites in the cluster framework be doped? If so, what

As for the interactions between the metalloid cluster and the
heterometal atoms in the environment, Bürgi and co-workers
proposed that the metal exchange might occur directly at the
kernel position by analyzing the alloying process from
Au38(PET)24 to AgxAg38‑x(PET)24.33 In addition, Yao et al.
suggested that the Ag−Au metal exchange process started at
the exterior shell of Ag44(SR)30 and obtained the
Ag32Au12(SR)30 nanocluster with mere surface doping.47
Both cases analyzed the reactions between the host nano-
clusters and the environmental heterometal atoms; however, all
of these heterometal exchange processes were determined by
energies of diﬀerent metal atoms, which were not strictly sole-
entropy-driven interactions between a metalloid cluster and the
metal thiolate. Our reported process herein, i.e., homologous
metal exchange via isotopes, avoided the relative energetics of
substitution and thus directly implied the metalloid cluster−
homometal complex interaction.

Then, we analyze the essence of the metal exchange of the
metalloid cluster. The isotopic Ag exchange in this work is
apparently diﬀerent from previously reported heterologous
metal exchange.21,24−42,53−57 The aforementioned results
demonstrate that the isotopic Ag exchange in both
Ag44(SR)30 and Ag29(BDT)12(TPP)4 templates is a dynamic
equilibrium process. In colloidal chemistry, metalloid clusters
are regarded as molecular compounds.2,58,59 In this context,
the reactions between a metalloid cluster and metal thiolate are
prone to occur on the exposed metal atoms in the metalloid
cluster, which is similar to the heterogeneous equilibrium of
ions.24−42 However, such an isotopic metal exchange is still
diﬀerent from the heterogeneous equilibrium of ions in
electrolyte crystals or solutions since metals in a metalloid
cluster are not strictly ions due to their delocalized free valence
electrons.14,60 Although the electrons in the metalloid cluster
are delocalized, the DFT calculations show that the electron
density is diﬀerent, i.e., the metals prefer to follow positive
valence in the shell and zero valence in the kernel.61 In this
context, the isotopic metal exchange is not a standard redox
process, but this process involves electron transfer.
Since it is a spontaneous process, metal exchange should be
an entropy-driven equilibrium process. Here, we propose a
possible path for isotopic metal exchange of silver clusters
(Scheme 1, path I). Interestingly, we also found that the kernel
metal atoms can be exchanged by metal atoms in the shell of
the metalloid cluster, which is not observed in the
heterogeneous equilibrium (Scheme 1, path II).

been mapped out by using isotope-labeled silver thiolate and
Ag44(SR)30 and Ag29(BDT)12(TPP)4 as a platform. Through
analyzing the ESI-MS and MS/MS results of the metal
exchange reaction between isotope-labeled silver clusters and
silver(I) thiolate, several important conclusions have been
obtained: (i) all Ag atoms in the silver nanoclusters could be
substituted by the thiolate Ag atoms and (ii) the metal atoms

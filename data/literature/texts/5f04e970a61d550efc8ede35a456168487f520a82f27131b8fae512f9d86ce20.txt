ABSTRACT
High-touch electronic displays represent a significant source of pathogenic contamination, creating an urgent need for protective
coatings that combine antibacterial efficacy and mechanical robustness. However, developing protective coatings for emerging
flexible displays faces a formidable challenge, as they must simultaneously satisfy a series of often-conflicting property
requirements: potent antibacterial activity, high biocompatibility, excellent transparency, robust mechanical durability, as well
as intrinsic flexibility. Developing a single coating that integrates all the required functionalities therefore remains a highly
anticipated goal. Herein, we resolve this challenge through an “all-in-one” precursor strategy involving the incorporation of the
bifunctional coupling agent, 3-mercaptopropyltrimethoxysilane, into a perhydropolysilazane-Ag+ system. This multicomponent
design enables a synergistic chemical cascade that controls the in situ formation, stabilization, and subsequent covalent
anchoring of silver nanoparticles within a dense SiOx matrix during a mild curing process. The resulting coating demonstrates
a seamless integration of multiple high-performance properties: potent and durable antibacterial activity (>99.99%), excellent
biocompatibility, polymer-like flexibility, glass-like damage tolerance, and high transparency. This work provides a reliable and
scalable approach for fabricating robust, multifunctional protective surfaces, making it highly promising for next-generation
flexible electronics where both durability and hygiene are paramount.

Introduction
Touchscreen displays, ubiquitous in personal devices ranging
from smartphones to public kiosks and central to the rise of flex-
ible electronics, have become integral to modern life. However,
their high-frequency touch nature also renders these surfaces
potent vectors for pathogenic contamination [1, 2]. Studies have
shown that harmful bacteria can persist on such inanimate
surfaces for days or even weeks, forming resilient biofilms that are
notoriously difficult to eradicate and can facilitate rapid disease
transmission [3–5]. To mitigate these public health risks, the
development of advanced protective coatings that provide intrin-
sically antibacterial surfaces with durable efficacy has emerged as
an essential requirement in the evolution of modern electronics.
Nevertheless, engineering such coatings remains a formidable
challenge, as they must simultaneously satisfy a series of often-
conflicting property requirements: excellent optical transparency,
potent antibacterial activity, high biocompatibility for user safety,
robust mechanical durability against scratches and abrasion, as
well as intrinsic flexibility to meet the needs of foldable devices
[6, 7].
To achieve a surface that possesses all the required functions for
display coatings, one primary obstacle is to integrate antibacterial

functionality without compromising its optical properties. While
additives like metals (Ag, Cu, Zn), metal oxides (ZnO, Ag2O,
TiO2), and carbon-based materials are effective antibacterial
agents [8], increasing the filler loading to maximize antibacterial
efficacy often comes at the cost of optical clarity, as higher con-
centrations tend to promote particle aggregation and refractive
index mismatch. New emerging strategies, such as engineering
ultra-thin homogeneous films through advanced deposition or
sparse network structures [9, 10], can address these optical
losses, but creating a mechanically robust yet flexible physical
platform remains a challenge as the properties of hardness and
resilience are typically mutually exclusive. For instance, the
silver nanowire/chitosan films with optical transparency over
90% achieve remarkable stretchability, rendering them highly
suitable for skin-attachable electronics [11]. However, the sur-
faces of such materials are inherently vulnerable to mechanical
abrasion, a major drawback for high-touch display applications
that demand high durability. Consequently, many studies have
turned to inorganic components to improve mechanical stability.
Investigated materials range from relatively simple Zn-doped
silica films [12] and multi-component oxides such as SiO2/ZnO
[13] to highly-engineered superlattice nanofilms that exhibit
high transparency, exceptional hardness, good biocompatibility,
and photocatalytic antibacterial effects [14]. Unfortunately, these
approaches sacrifice flexibility for stiffness, resulting in inherent
brittleness and susceptibility to cracking under bending stress,
thereby limiting their use in flexible devices. An advanced
ZnO─PTFE composite system with alternating “soft” polymer
and “hard” inorganic oxide layers was designed to bridge this
gap, affording excellent mechanical robustness. However, the
flexibility of this composite coating (R = 5 mm) lags behind
the requirements for gapless foldable devices (typically ≤2 mm)
[15, 16], and its limited optical transparency (<90%) restricts
its utility in advanced display applications [17]. Furthermore,
the reported antibacterial surfaces with mechanically robust
inorganic oxide thin films as core components rely on high-
vacuum deposition techniques, such as atomic layer deposition
and sputtering, which impedes their widespread application in
touchscreen manufacturing, where cost-effectiveness and scala-
bility are critical considerations. Therefore, with the widespread
application of touchscreen displays, the simultaneous attainment
of all the merits mentioned above for an antibacterial coating with
a versatile and scalable method is still critically demanded.
Recently, perhydropolysilazane (PHPS), an inorganic polymer
consisting of a Si─N backbone and free of organic groups, has
demonstrated its superiority as a precursor for constructing
robust functional coatings via a convenient solution process.
Notably, by controlling the conversion process from PHPS to
inorganic silica, the mechanical properties of the resulting
coating can be well tuned, implying the potential for meeting
flexibility requirements [18, 19]. Moreover, the highly reactive
Si─H groups in PHPS provide a built-in chemical tool for the
in situ generation of metal nanoparticles [20]. It is therefore
highly attractive to exploit both the transformative characteristics
and the inherent reducing activity of PHPS to chemically anchor
antibacterial components within the coating matrix, thereby
enabling the construction of a robust antibacterial coating with
durable mechanical and optical properties. To accomplish this
design, a stable PHPS solution incorporating such antibacte-
rial metal nanoparticles is a prerequisite. However, due to the
inherent tendency of nanoparticles to aggregate in the liquid
phase, achieving high transparency in films deposited via a PHPS
solution remains a significant challenge [21].
In this work, by introducing the bifunctional coupling agent
3-mercaptopropyltrimethoxysilane (KH-590) into the mixture
of PHPS and AgNO3, we developed a stable and processable
precursor solution incorporated with highly dispersed silver
nanoparticles (AgNPs) for a robust, transparent, antibacterial
nanocomposite coating. In this synergistic system, the thiol group
of KH-590 ensures the formation of a stable and processable
liquid precursor by effectively stabilizing the nascent AgNPs.
Simultaneously, the silane moiety of KH-590 serves as both a
covalent “bridge” to the matrix and an internal catalyst for
the network transformation. This stable liquid precursor can be
converted into macroscopic thin films via a simple spray-coating
technique, followed by a low-temperature, moisture-induced
transformation (Figure 1a), ultimately yielding a covalently
anchored nanocomposite structure, in which AgNPs are homo-
geneously dispersed and firmly locked within a transparent
glass-like matrix (Figure 1b). This approach allows us to achieve
what we term the KPA coating (an acronym derived from its
key components: KH-590, PHPS, and AgNO3). Macroscopically,
the KPA coating is highly transparent and mechanically robust,
sustaining significant bending deformation without fracturing
(Figure 1c), as well as exhibiting potent antibacterial activity
(>99.99% efficacy against E. coli and >99.92% against S. aureus,
Figure 1d). A comparative radar chart highlights the superior
performance profile of the KPA coating in terms of mechani-
cal durability, flexibility, high transparency, ease of fabrication,
antibacterial functionality, and biocompatibility, demonstrating
seamless integration of these properties within a single system
(Figure 1e).
1.1
Preparation and Optimization of the KPA
Precursor Solution
The preparation of a stable, high-quality liquid precursor is
crucial for fabricating nanocomposite coatings. In our designed
precursor system, AgNO3 provides a source of antibacterial
AgNPs, while PHPS serves a dual role: as a precursor for the
coating matrix and as an endogenous reducing agent for silver
ions, owing to its Si─H groups. The KH-590 coupling agent
functions by stabilizing silver ions via thiol coordination, while
its alkoxy groups act as covalent “bridges” to the PHPS matrix. As
illustrated in Figure 2a, a critical kinetic balance exists within this
system: the thiol groups coordinate with Ag+ to form Ag-thiolate
complexes, which tempers the reduction potential. Concurrently,
Si─H groups mediate the reduction of these complexes to
form metallic Ag0 nanoparticles via a hydride-transfer pathway
accompanied by hydrogen gas (H2) evolution [23, 24]. Precise
stoichiometric control of these competing reactions is therefore
essential to achieve controlled nucleation and growth, prevent
undesired aggregation, and ensure the formation of a stable
precursor solution.
We systematically optimized the precursor formulation, using
ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy to monitor the quality
of the resulting AgNPs dispersion via its characteristic Surface
Plasmon Resonance (SPR) peak. First, while maintaining a

Next, we investigated the effect of Ag loading. A series of precur-
sors were formulated with varying amounts of AgNO3 (and thus
Ag+ contents), while maintaining the optimal AgNO3:KH-590
ratio of 1:1. Two distinct regimes were observed (Figure 2b). At low
to moderate Ag loadings (A-0.1 to A-1.0), all precursors exhibited
well-defined SPR peaks, signifying the successful formation of
stable and well-dispersed nanoparticles. Within this regime, the
SPR peak exhibited a progressive blue shift from ∼436 nm (A-
0.1) to ∼420 nm (A-1.0). This shift indicates that increasing the
reactant concentration within this range leads to the formation
of progressively smaller nanoparticles. However, at higher Ag
loadings (A-2.0 and A-5.0), the SPR peaks exhibited broadening
and a red shift, characteristic of nanoparticle aggregation.
Based on the initial spectral analysis, we investigated the long-
term colloidal stability of the promising precursor formulations
(samples A-0.1 to A-1.0) by monitoring them over a 7-day
period. This stability was confirmed quantitatively by UV-Vis
spectroscopy. As shown in Figure 2f, the absorption spectra
of the A-1.0 sample exhibited virtually unchanged features
over the 7-day period, with no significant shifts in the SPR
peak position or changes in bandwidth. Corresponding spec-
tral data for the other stable precursors (A-0.1, A-0.2, and
A-0.5), presented in Figure S2, display similar long-term stabil-
ity. Furthermore, as shown in the inset photographs in both
Figure 2f and Figure S2, all these precursor solutions remained
homogeneous and visibly transparent after 7 days of storage,
showing no sign of precipitation, in contrast to the A-5.0
sample (Figure S3). These results confirm that our strategy
can produce a series of colloidally robust precursor solutions
with tunable AgNP content, suitable for subsequent coating
applications.

1.2
Structural Characterization and Properties of
the KPA Coating
A solution with molar ratio of PHPS:AgNO3:KH-590 = 80:1:1
(A-1.0) was initially selected for coating fabrication. The forma-
tion of KPA coating proceeds via a moisture-induced pathway
(Figure 3a), wherein both the PHPS backbone and the silane
head group of the KH-590 coupling agent undergo hydrolysis to
form reactive silanol (Si─OH) intermediates. Co-condensation of
these silanols forms the Si─O─Si network while simultaneously
grafting the KH-590-stabilized AgNPs into the matrix via Ag─S
covalent bonds. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy
(Figure 3b) was used to monitor the reaction progress. The spectra
confirm the conversion through the disappearance of the Si─H
(∼2167 cm−1) and Si─N (∼830 cm−1) peaks and the concurrent
growth of the Si─O─Si network peak (∼1065 cm−1). Notably,

them into a functionally effective state. The absence of carbonyl
absorption bands in the FTIR spectrum (1600–1800 cm−1, Figure
S5) and the lack of solvent signals in the 1H NMR spectrum of
the coating extract (Figure S6) confirm the complete removal
of residual organic solvents. The nanoscale structure of KPA
was elucidated by scanning transmission electron microscopy
with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (STEM-EDS) mapping
(Figure 3c). The elemental map reveals a continuous Si─O
matrix hosting discrete, well-dispersed nanodomains. The perfect
overlay of the Ag and S signals within these domains provides
direct visual evidence that the KH-590 remains bound to the
AgNPs. The chemical nature of this anchoring was confirmed
by X -ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Figure 3d). The Si
2p and O 1s spectra, with primary peaks at 103.5 and 533.0 eV
respectively, confirm the formation of the SiOx matrix. The Ag 3d
spectrum shows the characteristic doublet for Ag0, while the S 2p
spectrum reveals a primary peak at ∼162.5 eV, the signature of an
Ag─S thiolate bond. These results confirm the covalent anchoring
of the AgNPs within the SiOx matrix.
The thickness of the coating was tunable via the number of spray
cycles, increasing linearly from ∼23 nm (1 cycle) to ∼185 nm
(9 cycles) (Figure 3e, inset). As indicated by the transmission
spectra, samples prepared with 1 to 6 cycles exhibit negligible
optical deviation from the bare PET substrate, where the 6-cycle
coating (∼122 nm) incurs a marginal transmittance loss of 1.3%
at 550 nm (Table S1). In contrast, extending the deposition to 9
cycles results in distinct spectral attenuation (3.8% loss), which
is attributed to light scattering arising from increased surface
roughness (Ra ≈16.8 nm; Table S1, Figure S7). Although this
thicker coating yields a superior water contact angle (81.2◦), the
loss in transparency renders it suboptimal. Consequently, the 6-
cycle coating was selected as the optimal candidate, striking a
balance between maximizing the material reservoir for durability
and preserving intrinsic optical transparency.
We further investigated the mechanical performance of the
coating cured at different temperatures (80◦C–250◦C). As shown
in Figure 3f, both hardness (H) and elastic modulus (E) increase
monotonically with temperature, which is attributed to the
densification of the inorganic network, as evidenced by solid-
state
29Si MAS NMR (Figure S8). The Q4 peak shifts from
-107 ppm (80◦C) to -110 ppm (150◦C), reflecting an increase in
average Si─O─Si bond angles [26], which is followed by peak
enhancement at higher temperatures, confirming the progressive
condensation of the siloxane network. Regarding the trade-off
between hardness and flexibility, the Ashby-style map (Figure 3g)
confirms that the coating processed at 80◦C already falls within
the high-performance benchmarks (H/E* >0.1, We >60%) [27],
balancing mechanical properties with the thermal stability limits
of standard flexible substrates (e.g., PET). The resulting coating
exhibits a hardness of 1.58 GPa—approximately sevenfold higher
than that of common polymers—while retaining a moderate
modulus of 12.7 GPa, which is an order of magnitude lower than
that of typical metals [28–30]. This combination yields a high
H/E* ratio of ∼0.12 and an excellent elastic recovery (We) of 72%,
which is attributed to the energy-dissipating cage-like structures
(Figure S9, 1130 cm−1) [31], embedded alkyl chains, and dynamic
hydrogen-bonding networks that facilitate localized molecular
motion [32, 33].
1.3
Antibacterial Efficacy and Biocompatibility
of the KPA Coating
The antibacterial activity was first visually assessed against both
E. coli and S. aureus using live/dead fluorescence microscopy
(Figure 4a). After 24 h of incubation, the surface of the KPA coat-
ing was dominated by red fluorescence, indicating widespread
bacterial mortality due to compromised membrane integrity. In
contrast, the control substrate fostered the growth of a dense
and viable biofilm, characterized by uniform green fluores-
cence. These results provide direct visual evidence of the potent
antibacterial nature of the KPA coating. To quantitatively analyze
the relationship between Ag loading and antibacterial efficacy,
coatings with varying silver contents were evaluated against E.
coli. As shown in Figure 4b, the silver-free sample exhibited an
antibacterial rate of 14.04% compared to bare PET. This result is
attributed to the higher water contact angle of the KPA coating
(∼78.7◦) relative to the bare PET substrate (∼69.6◦), as listed in
Table S1, which induces a slight physical anti-adhesion effect
rather than antibacterial capability. With the incorporation of Ag,
a dramatic improvement in antibacterial efficacy was observed.
The A-0.1 coating, with the lowest silver content, showed a
moderate efficiency of 82.73%. However, upon increasing the
Ag content to the A-0.2 level, the efficiency sharply rose to
over 99.99%. This high level of antibacterial performance was
maintained at all higher Ag loadings, establishing an “efficacy
plateau” for the A-0.2 to A-1.0 formulations. Corresponding
agar plate images, provided in Figure S10, visually confirm this
trend.
A critical aspect of the coating design is to achieve this
potent efficacy without inducing cytotoxicity. To assess the
biocompatibility under the most stringent conditions, the A-
1.0 formulation, which has the highest Ag loading within
this high-performance window, was selected for cytotoxicity
evaluation. The cytotoxicity of the KPA coating was assessed
using a 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bro-
mide (MTT) assay with L929 fibroblasts (Figure 4d). Cells were
cultured for 24 h with coating extracts at various concentrations.
Remarkably, even under the most stringent condition—exposure
to a 100% extract—cell viability remained exceptionally high at
104.5%, similar to the negative control. This demonstrates that
the KPA coating exhibited no cytotoxicity toward the fibroblasts.
Complementing these quantitative results, the corresponding
optical micrographs in Figure 4c showed that the fibroblasts
exhibited a healthy, spindle-like morphology indistinguishable
from the negative control. Fluorescence imaging further con-
firmed their high viability, revealing that the vast majority of
these cells exhibited uniform bright green fluorescence (live
cells). In contrast, the positive control (10% DMSO) induced
significant cell rounding and death, as evidenced by both the
optical cell image and the widespread red fluorescence in

1.4
Silver Release Kinetics and Service Life
Prediction
The time-dependent silver release profiles under physiologi-
cal conditions are shown in Figure 5a. In neutral water, the
cumulative release remains minimal, confirming the stability
of the SiOx matrix. In contrast, acidic sweat (pH 4.7) triggers
proton-promoted oxidative dissolution (Ag →Ag+), resulting
in a continuous upward trend over 72 h. This indicates that
dissolution is not merely confined to the surface but involves the
diffusion of solvated silver ions from the bulk reservoir through
the amorphous SiOx matrix to replenish the active interface.
Alkaline sweat (pH 8.0) is characterized by a sharp initial surge
during the first hour, followed by a markedly attenuated release
rate. This behavior is attributed to the initial etching of the SiOx
matrix by hydroxide ions (OH−), which is subsequently impeded
by the rapid formation of a Ag2O passivation layer, as illustrated
in the mechanistic schematic (Figure 5c). To investigate the
silver release behavior under rigorous daily usage, the coating
was subjected to accelerated cycling tests (8 h acidic immersion
followed by 1 h water rinse per cycle). The release profile reveals
an initial burst during the first few cycles, and then transitions
to a consistent, stable output (Figure 5b). This stabilization of the
depletion rate provides a basis for predicting the long-term service
life.
Based on the total silver reservoir (Mtotal ≈0.998 µg/cm2)
calculated in Note S1 (using Figure S13) and the established
kinetics, two predictive models were developed (Figure 5d). The
accelerated laboratory model simulates a worst-case scenario by
subjecting the coating to bulk immersion (10 mL) in acidic sweat,
representing a massive volumetric excess compared to realistic
thin-film exposure, followed by rigorous cleaning. Under these
extreme conditions, reservoir depletion is predicted within ∼10
cycles (days) (inset of Figure 5d; Note S1). In comparison, the
realistic saturated sweat film model is predicated on the physical
reality that sweat reaches a dynamic equilibrium between secre-
tion and evaporation, maintaining a constant, finite thin film
volume rather than accumulating indefinitely [37, 38]. Crucially,
the maximum cumulative silver depletion is determined by
the number of times this saturated film is physically removed.
Accordingly, this model projects the functional lifespan based
on the frequency of saturated sweat film removal. For heavy use
(100 wipes/day, e.g., public touch screens), the coating remains
effective for ∼2.6 months. Under typical use (40 wipes/day,
e.g., personal electronics), the lifespan extends to ∼6.5 months.
Finally, for light use (10 wipes/day, e.g., home furniture), the
service life exceeds 2 years, confirming the suitability of the KPA
coating for diverse long-term applications.
1.5
Performance of the KPA Coating in
Simulated Application Environments
To assess practical applicability in touch-based flexible elec-
tronics, we systematically evaluated the KPA coating under
rigorous mechanical, biological, and environmental stressors.
First, the mechanical robustness was evaluated. The flexibility
of the KPA-coated PET films was tested using a custom-built
bending apparatus (Figure 6a). In this setup, two glass slides
were hinged together face-to-face with adhesive tape, with their
minimum separation distance controlled by an incompressible
metal block. The film was fixed between the glass slides. Opening
the hinge kept the film flat, while closing it forced the film
to fold into a near-semicircular shape. The composite films
withstood 3000 bending cycles at a radius of 1.5 mm without
generating cracks or delamination. However, when the bending
radius was reduced to 0.5 mm, noticeable cracks appeared after
1500 cycles. Beyond flexibility, the surface abrasion resistance
was evaluated to ensure durability against daily wear using a
reciprocating abrader with a standard friction cloth under a 1 N
load (Figure 6b, inset). After 500 abrasion cycles, the uncoated
PET substrate was severely scratched (Figure S14), whereas the
KPA-coated surface remained smooth and largely intact (Figure
6b). This excellent wear resistance is attributed to the highly
crosslinked Si─O─Si network, which imparts a high hardness of
1.58 GPa and a high H/E* ratio (∼0.12), enabling the coating to
effectively resist scratching. To further challenge this durability,
we subjected the coating to more aggressive steel wool abrasion.
While the coating exhibited progressive surface wear from 100
to 5000 cycles (Figure S15), it remarkably sustained >99.96%
antibacterial activity even after 10000 cycles (Figure 6c). This
efficacy confirms that despite physical wear, the continuous
exposure of fresh AgNPs embedded within the matrix ensures
uninterrupted contact-killing performance.
Following the mechanical assessment, we proceeded to simulate
real-world contamination scenarios relevant to touch-based elec-
tronics. Figure 6d illustrates the antibacterial efficacy following
the accumulation of three distinct layers of fingerprints. Although
a slight decrease in the antibacterial rate against E. coli was
observed (from >99.99% to 99.67%) due to physical shielding by
lipid and protein residues, the coating retained a high level of
bacterial reduction. This retained activity is attributed to the dis-
continuous topology of fingerprint residues. Unlike a continuous
insulating film, this discontinuous structure allows AgNPs within
the interstitial spaces to remain exposed, thereby maintaining
effective contact-killing. Additionally, a cyclic “contamination-
cleaning” test (100 cycles, Figure 6e) confirmed that surface
activity is fully recoverable (>99.99%) via ethanol wiping. Finally,
considering the corrosive nature of skin contact, we evaluated
the stability of the coating in physiological microenvironments.
This test involved cyclic immersion in acidic artificial sweat
(pH 4.7) followed by vigorous oscillation rinsing and drying
(Figure 6g). The maintenance of >99.99% antibacterial activity
across multiple cycles demonstrates the chemical robustness of
the coating, which prevents rapid leaching of active silver species
in acidic microenvironments.

protocols simulating extreme cumulative stress. High-intensity
UV irradiation (100 h, 365 nm, 15 mW cm−2) applied at a distance
of 2 cm delivered a radiant dosage equivalent to >50000 h
of ambient indoor use, simulating several months of rigorous
outdoor aging. Similarly, the 24 h chemical immersion repre-
sented a worst-case accumulation exceeding 17000 instantaneous
ethanol wiping events or ∼300 accidental spills. Furthermore,
100 thermal shock cycles (−20◦C to 80◦C) were performed to

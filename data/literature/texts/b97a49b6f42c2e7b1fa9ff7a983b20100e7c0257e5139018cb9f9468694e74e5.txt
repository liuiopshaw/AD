INTRODUCTION
The United Nations' sustainability goal #3 (SDG-­3) is to 
promote good health and well-­being. The key to this is 
the control of infectious diseases. Unfortunately, due to 
antibiotic resistance, we are moving towards a predic-
tion of an apocalyptic post-­antibiotic life (WHO, 2023). 
According to a scary report in 2019, 1.27 million deaths 
worldwide were attributed directly to antimicrobial re-
sistance (AMR) bacteria and 4.95 million deaths 
were associated with AMR (Murray et al., 2022). This 
leads to a call for increased antimicrobial steward-
ship and directed policies around present and future 
uses, but also a call for alternatives to traditional an-
tibiotics (Dadgostar,  2019). A OneHealth perspective 
(Adisasmito et al., 2022; Shakoor et al., 2019) and the 
complete system cycle of a compound from synthesis 
to disposal are important factors to consider as we 
move towards new antimicrobial compounds and direc-
tions in antimicrobial practices in healthcare, industry, 
and agriculture.
Unfortunately, industry has avoided developing 
new antimicrobials as, due to the rapid onset of re-
sistance, the lifetime of a new compound is short and 
thus not financially viable. Regardless, there is now a 
large push within academic research towards explor-
ing various synthetic and natural compounds as well 
as other strategies to combat pathogenic bacteria to 
replace failing antibiotics (Plotniece et  al.,  2023). Of 
these is the consideration to return to historical medi-
cines, including so called “heavy” metals. Metal-­based 
antimicrobial strategies for solutions towards various 
United Nations sustainability goals (UN-­SDG) were 
proposed earlier (Turner, 2017). It was recognized that

Abstract
Nanoscience, a pivotal field spanning multiple industries, including health-
care, focuses on nanomaterials characterized by their dimensions. These ma-
terials are synthesized through conventional chemical and physical methods, 
often involving costly and energy-­intensive processes. Alternatively, biogenic 
synthesis using bacteria, fungi, or plant extracts offers a potentially sustain-
able and non-­toxic approach for producing metal-­based nanoparticles (NP). 
This eco-­friendly synthesis approach not only reduces environmental impact 
but also enhances features of NP production due to the unique biochemistry 
of the biological systems. Recent advancements have shown that along with 
chemically synthesized NPs, biogenic NPs possess significant antimicrobial 
properties. The inherent biochemistry of bacteria enables the efficient conver-
sion of metal salts into NPs through reduction processes, which are further 
stabilized by biomolecular capping layers that improve biocompatibility and 
functional properties. This mini review explores the use of bacteria to pro-
duce NPs with antimicrobial activities. Microbial technologies to produce NP 
antimicrobials have considerable potential to help address the antimicrobial 
resistance crisis, thus addressing critical health issues aligned with the United 
Nations Sustainability Goal #3 of good health and well-­being.

help with various goals. In the literature we see met-

organometallic compounds, metal chelates, and metal 
nanomaterials explored as antimicrobials and antimi-

Of the chemically produced metallo-­antimicrobials, 
nanomaterials, particularly nanoparticles (NPs), have 
seen considerable developments and implementa-
tions, specifically those made with silver (Ag). Ag NPs 
are showing increased use, finding them in many com-
mercial products and medical applications over the 
past decade. They are now considered the leading 
approach for developing antimicrobial therapies and 
creating new materials with enhanced antimicrobial ac-
tion (Khan et al., 2020; Mubeen et al., 2021). NPs may 
have antimicrobial properties themselves, as carriers 
of a drug, acting as antibiotic adjuvants, or as catalysts 
producing antimicrobial molecules, such as reactive 
oxygen species (ROS; Zielińska-­Górska et al., 2017). 
The antimicrobial activity of metal nanomaterials is 
not fully understood (Gharpure et  al.,  2020; Godoy-­
Gallardo et al., 2021; Slavin et al., 2017), and there are 
a lot of conjectures in the literature, which will not be 
addressed in this review other than to say that NPs 
may have disruptive effects on the membranes, which 
would be dependent on their size and cap chemistries. 
The suggested mechanisms underlying the antimicro-
bial effect of NPs beyond damaging the integrity of the 
cell wall and membrane that would cause leakage of 
essential molecules include disruption of the electron 
transport chain and uncoupling the proton motive force, 
interfering with cell division and cell signalling, induc-
ing DNA damage, inhibition of solute transporters, and 
deactivating specific enzymes (Ahmed et  al.,  2016; 
Lara et  al.,  2010; Li et  al.,  2010; Mgiba et  al.,  2024; 
Nisar et al., 2019). Additionally, they may simply pro-
vide a localized high concentration of the metal ions 
to the bacteria as the NPs decompose, releasing and 
oxidizing the metal atoms. For more information on the 
antimicrobial mechanisms of metal ions, see cited re-
views (Frei et al., 2023; Harrison et al., 2007; Lemire 
et al., 2013; Monych et al., 2019).
Because the free ions of metals and metalloids can 
be effective antimicrobials, their NP versions are usu-
ally found to be antimicrobial (Correa et  al.,  2020; 
Hoseinzadeh et  al.,  2017; Khan et  al.,  2020). In gen-
eral, the elements with antimicrobial properties are 
within the transition metals of the d-­block (V, Ti, Cr, Co, 
W, Ag, Cd, Ni, Cu, Zn, Tb, Au, and Hg) (Abdel-­Ghafar 
et al., 2022; Akbari et al., 2023; Anupong et al., 2023; 
Barani et al., 2021; El-­Bassuony & Abdelsalam, 2020; 
Jayarambabu et al., 2020; Kusrini et al., 2023; Martinez-­
Gutierrez et  al.,  2010; Masoudi et  al.,  2024; Matharu 
et  al.,  2020; Rajeshkumar et  al.,  2014; Vaseghi 
et  al.,  2018) and metalloids from groups 13 to 16 of 
the periodic table (Se, Sn, Sb, Te, Al, Ga, Ge, As, Pb, 
and Bi). (Chauhan et al., 2017; Khan et al., 2023; Park 
et  al.,  2023; Ramesh et  al.,  2022; Sadiq et  al.,  2009; 
Vazquez-­Munoz et al., 2020; Zonaro et al., 2015). For 
this reason, metal NPs, typically synthesized starting 
from salts of metals of Ag, Au, Se, Te, Zn, Ti, Ni, As, 
Sn, Fe, Pt, and Cd, have been explored for their anti-
microbial properties. The uses of these metal NPs offer 
potential advantages over conventional antimicrobial 
agents due to their physicochemical properties that lead 
to unique interactions and outcomes with biological sys-
tems (Khan et al., 2019; Wang & Wang, 2014).
SYNTHESIS OF NPs
The conventional production of NPs is performed 
by either of the two main approaches: bottom-­up or 
top-­down synthesis, through specific reagent-­based 
chemicals or with physical techniques including high 
temperatures (Schwarz et al., 2004). Top-­down synthe-
sis employs a destructive approach, starting with larger 
molecules or compounds, which are decomposed into 
smaller units, and then these units are converted into 
suitable NPs (Khan et al., 2019). This type of synthe-
sis involves expensive and energy-­intensive methods 
like mechanical milling, laser ablations, and sputtering 
(Bruna et al., 2019; Khan et al., 2019). For the past few 
decades, these physical–chemical methods have been 
the standard for nanostructure synthesis. However, 
these high energy costly methods that entail chem-
istries with harmful reducing agents and challenging 
waste management have increasingly become less de-
sirable when synthesis processes are scaled up (Salam 
et al., 2014). On the other hand, bottom-­up syntheses 
that form NPs from simpler substances are performed 
through methods like chemical vapour deposition, laser 
pyrolysis, and sol-­gel processes (Khan et  al.,  2019; 
Singh et al., 2018).
A drawback in the NP synthesis process, as pointed 
out above, is that it is either energy intensive or uses 
toxic chemicals. Thus, eco-­friendly, or even green 
synthesis approaches have been sought after. There 
has been a shift towards more sustainable practices, 
including entailing a biological (biogenic) route of bot-
tom-­up synthesis of NPs (Khan et  al.,  2019; Shah 
et  al.,  2024). Biogenic synthesis employs biological 
systems like bacteria, fungi, and plant extracts, offering 
a more sustainable, non-­toxic, and eco-­friendly alterna-
tive. Initially, the biogenic production of NPs primarily 
used plant material, often agricultural wastes, such as 
wheat chafe, banana peels, and various leaf extracts, 
which provided reductants including reducing sug-
ars and redox enzymes (Herlekar et al., 2014; Patil & 
Chandrasekaran, 2020; Sharma et al., 2019). As such, 
the development of these methods has led to biogenic 
nanoparticles (bioNPs) that are comparable and often

Biogenic NPs are being used for many purposes, like 
electronics, fluorescent probes, cancer treatment, anti-
fungal treatment, herbicides, pesticides, and more.
Microbes, both fungi (Bafghi et al., 2021; Pradhan & 
Turner, 2023) and bacteria (Barani et al., 2021; Pradhan 
& Turner, 2022; Presentato et al., 2020), have been ex-
plored to biogenically synthesize a variety of nanoma-
terials. Since around 2010, there has been a growing 
interest in using bacteria to synthesize metal-­based 
nanomaterials, driven by the potential for better control 
and reproducibility of NP production compared to plant 
extracts or fungi. This approach has shown consider-
able promise due to bacteria's rapid growth rates, low 
incubation costs, and the ease of genetic manipulation 
(Pantidos & Horsfall,  2014). Here, we will overview 
using bacteria to synthesize NPs as antimicrobials.
Synthesis of bacterial bioNPs
Bacteria utilize a wide array of reducing and oxidiz-
ing sources in the cell that can convert metal salts into 
nanomaterials, making them an excellent choice for 
optimized biogenic synthesis of metal NPs (Hosseini, 
Lashani, et  al.,  2023; Lemire et  al.,  2013; Pantidos 
& Horsfall,  2014). From the variety of bacteria spe-
cies, there is a diverse range of biochemistries show-
casing the potential for biotechnological control of the 
production and reproducibility of NP synthesis (Moon 
et al., 2010). Hence, investigating the antimicrobial prop-
erties specific to biogenically synthesized metal nano-
materials from bacteria, could be useful in this AMR era.
Most of the NP antimicrobial research has been fo-
cused on exploring their manufacturing or synthesis 
process. Researchers in the field are changing the ele-
ments in the metal core and varying the size and shape 
of the nanomaterials. Such factors in the biogenic NP 
synthesis process entail varying bacterial strain, growth 
media, incubation time, concentration of the metal salt, 
and culturing conditions (aeration, pH, salinity) during 
incubation. An important functional variable now appre-
ciated is the chemistry and nature of the surface coat-
ing or capping of the NP. All these parameters influence 
not only the NP stability but also their expected appli-
cation's efficacy (Abbaszadegan et  al.,  2015). From 
this, we see antimicrobial nanomaterial being produced 
that have antitumour, immunomodulating, antibacte-
rial, antiviral, antifungal, or antiparasitic properties. 
Further ideas include the example of capturing NPs in 
a polymer for antimicrobial coatings to prevent biofilms 
(Balaure & Grumezescu, 2020; Mohanta et al., 2023).
Metal exposure to bacteria is inherently toxic; hence, 
bacteria have evolved robust defence systems to mit-
igate damage, including metal ion efflux pumps and 
oxidation/reduction reactions mediated by enzymes 
and metabolites (Lemire et al., 2013). The predominant 
mechanism for NP synthesis by bacteria utilizes a re-
sistance mechanism of metal reduction. Additionally, 
there are organisms capable of employing dissimilatory 
metal reduction, a process in which the reduction of a 
metal ion is coupled with the oxidation of an enzyme 
(Pantidos & Horsfall, 2014). This yields stable and inert 
metallic precipitates of particles and crystals of the 
metal, often accumulating within the cell's cytoplasm 
(Hosseini, Hadian, et al., 2023). We also see metabolite 
reduction, such as by sulfate-­reducing bacteria (SRB), 
where the secreted hydrogen sulfite reacts to pro-
duce metal sulfur precipitates extracellularly (Dopson 
& Holmes, 2014). This bioprecipitation can be consid-
ered a metal resistance mechanism as it would reduce 
the concentration of the toxic form of the metal in the 
local environment to the less toxic form. Biotechnology 
wise, this biomineralized metal can then be harvested 
(Pantidos & Horsfall, 2014).

We see a variety of bacterial species and strains ex-
ploited for biogenic production of NPs from this survey. 
Some of the most common bacteria being used include 
Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter species, Bacillus spe-
cies, and Vibrio species, with little preference of spe-
cies for a given metal. This could be due to a strong 
understanding of these species' physiology and ge-
netics. For example, there has been a lot of research 
done on the Bacillus species due to their metal bioac-
cumulation abilities (Pantidos & Horsfall, 2014). While 
the Shewanella species have also been seen as a pre-
ferred bacteria for the synthesis of NPs as they have a 
high capacity for the reduction of metal and metalloid 
elements (Klonowska et al., 2005; Staicu et al., 2022; 
Wang et al., 2022).

Characteristics of bacterial bioNPs
When it comes to the basic analysis of metal-­based 
NPs, many methods are being used throughout the liter-
ature. These diverse methodologies for measurements 
of nanomaterials to characterize them typically include: 
X-­ray diffraction (XRD), X-­ray photoelectron, nuclear 
magnetic resonance, UV-­visible spectroscopy, Fourier 
transform infrared (FT-­IR) spectroscopy, zeta-­potential, 
energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), atomic force 
microscope (AFM), transmission and scanning electron 
microscopy (TEM/SEM), and dynamic light scattering 
(DLS). For example, XRD or electron diffraction from 
TEM is fundamental for identifying the atomic lattice 
(crystalline or amorphous) of metal-­based nanomate-
rials. The differential use of various protocols makes 
it challenging to compare results between publica-
tions. Particular discrepancies arise, especially in size 
measurements, due to differences in techniques from 
solid-­state measurements like TEM/SEM to aqueous 
suspension measurements like DLS. This is because 
DLS measures the hydrodynamic radius and includes 
the organic cap, giving a larger size than the NP metal 
core (Durán et al., 2016).
This variability highlights the need for standardized 
measurement protocols to ensure comparability and 
reliability of data across studies, particularly when aim-
ing to eventually assess the antimicrobial activities of

ANTIMICROBIAL ACTIVITY OF 
BIOGENIC NPs
The antimicrobial activity of metalloid NPs produced 
from bacteria seen in Table 1 was successful against 
both Gram-­negative and Gram-­positive bacteria. 
However, the concentrations at which these NPs were 
deployed to have antimicrobial activity against various 
pathogenic bacteria strains were quite high. Overall, 
the NP form of the metal, regardless of chemo or bio-
genic, is usually less antimicrobial compared to the free 
metal ion provided by the metal salt.
The antimicrobial mechanisms of metal NPs, regard-
less if they are chemically or biogenically synthesized, 
are not clear. Several mechanisms have been proposed, 
yet none are fully validated. A lot depends on the sta-
bility of the NP in the biological system: if it readily de-
composes, the NP simply becomes a delivery system 
to release locally a high concentration of metal ions that 
compose the NP core. If this is the case, the mecha-
nism of toxicity would follow that of the metal element, 
as overviewed in an earlier review (Lemire et al., 2013). 
If the particle is stable, things become more difficult to 
ascertain. Overall, the particle could steal electrons from 
the electron transport chain and potentially catalyse 
ROS directly or indirectly. Either way, the bioenergetics 
of the membrane become compromised. If the NPs can 
enter the cell and remain in NP form, one could imagine 
actions on DNA and enzymes. This is difficult to con-
ceptualize if we consider the sizes of NP metal cores + 
their cap, which can lead to the NPs being quite large. A 
25 nm core as evaluated by electron microscopy could 
have a large cap of biomolecules giving a hydrody-
namic radius over 150 nm (as seen in the few examples 
in Table 1 where both measurements were properly re-
ported). Thus, for most cases of bioNPs, it is unlikely that 
the NP gets into the bacterial cell compared to eukaryotic 
systems that could phagocytose particles of such sizes, 
although such mechanisms are often proposed, as sum-
marized by Parmar et al. (2022).
Approaches to evaluate biogenic NP 
efficacies
The assessment of antimicrobial activity in NPs has 
traditionally relied on the Kirby-­Bauer disc diffusion 
method, sometimes simply referred to as the zone 
of inhibition assay. This microbiological assay meas-
ures the diameter of the area where bacteria cannot 
grow around a disc containing the NPs. This assay 
was seen to be used by most literature exploring bio-
genic NPs as antimicrobials (Table 1). However, this 
method faces limitations, as NPs may not equally ad-
sorb into the material of the filter paper used. They 
may also adhere strongly and not release from the 
disk. Further, depending on the nature of the cap, the 
bioNPs may not freely diffuse through the agar. This 
limitation is also seen in another method literature 
uses; the agar well diffusion method, where the agar 
media plate has a hole in it containing the NP suspen-
sion. Either of these could lead to misleading results. 
Consequently, in more recently published articles, 
there is a shift towards using a form of broth dilution 
to determine the minimum inhibitory concentration 
(MIC), which provides the lowest concentration of NPs 
required to inhibit bacterial growth. This approach, 
often involving serial dilution and colony-­forming unit 
counts, offers a more accurate and reliable measure 
of antimicrobial efficacy. Given the deficits associ-
ated with disc diffusion methods, a broth dilution tech-
nique should be considered preferable for evaluating 
the antimicrobial properties of NPs.
The literature search for Table 1 also highlighted a 
trend emphasizing the diverse range of bacterial strains 
for both the synthesis of the NPs but also the patho-
genic strains used to test their antimicrobial properties. 
The use of pathogen reference cultures is essential 
in accurately evaluating the antimicrobial efficacy of 
NPs, serving as positive and negative controls, indi-
cator organisms, and identification standards. Testing 
against a wide range of laboratory strains without using 
standardized indicator strains complicates the ability 
for direct comparisons of NPs' antimicrobial activities 
between studies and laboratories. Reference strains 
are described and cited in national and international 
standards, including those published by the Clinical 
and Laboratory Standards Institute and United States 
Pharmacopoeia for the healthcare community and 
the pharmaceutical and biopharmaceutical industries. 
Examples of these strains are those obtained from 
ATCC or NTCC collections, which thankfully are being 
used more consistently throughout recent studies. 
These standardized strains provide a consistent bench-
mark for comparison, avoiding the variability introduced 
by genomic differences across strains of a given spe-
cies. This is critical for ensuring the comparability and 
reproducibility of results across studies. It is also good 
practice to evaluate the efficacy of the bioNPs against 
clinical isolates and antibiotic-­resistant strains. By em-
ploying standardized reference cultures, researchers 
can reliably compare antimicrobial effectiveness, en-
hancing the validity of their findings and facilitating the 
application of bioNPs in antimicrobial strategies.

As we consider what pathogens to target, we can look 
at the World Health Organization (WHO) priority group. 
It has emphasized the urgent need for new antibiotics 
targeting antibiotic-­resistant Gram-­negative bacteria, 
which pose a significant threat to global health. Gram-­
negative bacteria possess an outer membrane (OM) 
that serves as a formidable barrier against many anti-
biotics, preventing their passage and access to internal 
targets. This OM, characterized by lipopolysaccharides 
(LPS) and specialized porin proteins, selectively allows 
certain molecules to permeate, further complicating 
the entry of antibiotics (Breijyeh et  al.,  2020). While 
metal-­based NPs throughout the literature have shown 
broad-­spectrum antibacterial activity and a reduced 
likelihood for resistance development, most research 
has predominantly demonstrated the best effective-
ness against Gram-­positive bacteria. Chemically pro-
duced Ag NPs have certainly been explored for their 
efficacy against resistant Gram-­negative strains such 
as P. aeruginosa and E. coli. However, regarding bio-
genic production, the most current potential lies in Ag 
NPs produced by plant materials and extracts. For ex-
ample, Ag NPs made from pu-­reh tea leaves revealed 
exceptional antimicrobial activity against four species 
of Gram-­negative foodborne bacteria, including E. coli 
(Loo et al., 2018). Unfortunately, most studies explor-
ing bacterial bioNPs tend to be less effective on Gram-­
negative bacteria. This may have to do with how the 
bioNPs interact with the LPS or the OM. Regardless, 
there is great potential for bacterially produced antimi-
crobial bioNPs, yet increased research and develop-
ment are required to show that bacterially produced 
bioNPs can help meet this need.
The cap/coating of bioNPs
Metal NPs have a metal core and typically an organic 
molecule cap or shell. This cap is added by chem-
ists to provide thermodynamic stability either through 
steric or electrical repulsion. Such capping chemicals 
may be the chemicals the NPs are synthesized with 
or added chemicals such as amphiphiles/surfactants 
or polymers like polyethylene glycol. A key feature of 
biogenically produced nanomaterials is that the cap 
molecules are supplied by the organism that synthe-
sized them and thus comprise of biochemicals that 
can provide excellent electro-­steric thermodynamic 
stability characteristics (Piacenza et al., 2018). This 
study considers the types of biogenic capping mole-
cules, which include proteins, carbohydrates, nucleic 
acids, lipids, fatty acids, biosurfactants, and other 
metabolites. It is often considered that the capping 
agents of bioNPs are the molecules involved in the 
reduction of the metal ion to elemental form, initiat-
ing the NP formation. Such biomolecules are being 
identified through physical-­chemical methods as 
discussed above but could also start being discov-
ered through proteomics and metabolomics. Across 
the literature, it is widely recognized that the stabil-
ity and biocompatibility of biogenically synthesized 
NPs are very good and are attributed to the capping 
from the bacteria used to synthesize them. These 
biochemical capping molecules can give rise to new 
properties of the NPs compared to those chemically 
produced. Bacteria have a diverse range of biomole-
cules that could become part of this cap through their 
diverse metabolism (Piacenza et al., 2018; Pradhan 
& Turner, 2022). The biogenic capping not only en-
hances the stability of these NPs but also influences 
their physical properties, including shape, functional-
ity, and antimicrobial activity, underscoring the com-
plexity and variability in NP synthesis across different 
bacterial strains.
When it comes to the antimicrobial activity of bio-
genic nanomaterials, there is increasing evidence that 
the biochemical cap strongly enhances the antimicro-
bial activity. A study by (Piacenza et al., 2017) evalu-
ated the antimicrobial activity of SeNPs produced using 
Bacillus mycoides. The bioSeNPs were more antimicro-
bial than their chemically synthesized forms, and if the 
biomolecules were stripped off the bioNPs and used 
to cap the chemoNPs, the antimicrobial activity of the 
chemoNPs increased. A follow-­up on this explored how 
the SeNPs affected model lipid membranes (Piacenza 
et  al.,  2023). This study observed that the bioSeNPs 
produced by S.  maltophilia reflected that the organic 
material from the bioSeNPs induced similar membrane 
fluidity changes as the bioSeNPs, reinforcing the idea 
that the biochemical cap molecules may direct the ac-
tive properties of the bioNPs. More directed research is 
required in this area, as even when producing bioNPs 
with the same bacteria, the biochemical composition of 
the cap can vary between strains (Bulgarini et al., 2021).
Hints into the role of the cap could come from pro-
ducing a given element NPs in different biofactories 
and comparing efficacies. This was highlighted in a 
study comparing production hosts for Ag NPs where 
the antimicrobial activity was superior if the production 
microorganism are more closely related to the target 
pathogen (Singh & Mijakovic, 2022). This may be due 
to similar biomolecules in the cap and the surface of 
the pathogen allowing for greater interaction.
Moreover, there is a need for further research to 
fully understand the specific roles and compositions 
of the biomolecules in stabilizing and antimicrobially 
functionalizing the bioNPs. Although the stability of 
biogenically synthesized NPs is often attributed to 
the cap, many studies do not mention zeta potential, 
which is a good indicator of this stability. Zeta potential 
analysis evaluates the net charge distribution on NP 
surfaces and reflects the particles' ability to repulse 
each other electrostatically (Majeed et al., 2021). This 
plays a crucial role in confirming stability because,

along with the core metal of the bioNPs.
SUMMARY COMMENTS AND 
PROJECTIONS
Beyond using bacteria as a biocatalyst to produce 
metal nanomaterial as antimicrobials, we see some 
interesting developments using microbes to produce 
NPs for photodynamic and photothermic treatments 
(Chen et al., 2016; Qin et al., 2022; Shivashankarappa 
& Sanjay,  2019). Although such NPs are typically 
explored for cancer treatments, there are increas-
ing examples exploring their use for infection con-
trol and wound healing (Pormohammad et al., 2021). 
Extracellularly localized copper selenide NPs syn-
thesized from Shewanella oneidensis MR-­1 exhibited 
photothermal properties. In this process, the NPs 
are radiated with microwave or photoelectric energy, 
which leads to a localized increase in temperature 
around the NPs, leading to bacterial death (Wang 
et al., 2021). There is potential in this novel field of 
microbial biotechnology for applications towards 
sterilizing high-­touch surfaces and personal protec-
tive clothing and in other disease treatments. Further 
research into producing heteroatom biogenic NPs 
should lead to more examples to understand bacteri-
ally produced NPs full antimicrobial potentials.
As for the synthesis of NPs, there has been 
growing interest in the usage of green chemistry or 
eco-­friendly processes, offering a less harmful alter-
native to traditional methods. Green synthesis can 
not only reduce the overall cost of NP synthesis but 
could also facilitate large-­scale production (Sastry 
et al., 2013; Singh et al., 2018). According to the U.S. 
Environmental Protection Agency, “green chemistry” 
promotes the prevention of pollution at the molecular 
level and encourages innovative solutions to environ-
mental challenges (US EPA, 2013). It aims to min-
imize the generation of pollutants, reduce/eliminate 
high energy demands, and mitigate the adverse ef-
fects of chemical products and processes on human 
health and the environment by designing safer alter-
natives with reduced intrinsic hazards. Despite these 
clear definitions, the term “green synthesis” is often 
misused in the literature, particularly around biogen-
ically produced NPs. Unfortunately, bacterial syn-
thesis processes to date do not fit the tight definition 
of Green Chemistry, however, bacterial synthesis of 
metal NPs is certainly more “eco-­friendly” than chem-
ical or physical methods.
Ag NPs chemically produced are known for their 
broad-­spectrum antimicrobial properties; hence they 
are being used commercially in the health and medicine 
sector, including their incorporation into face masks to 
bolster protection against pathogenic microorganisms 
(Hiragond et al., 2018). Throughout the literature, pri-
marily Ag NPs, synthesized biogenically through plants 
are emerging as potent antimicrobial agents, crucial 
for addressing the WHO's crisis on antibiotic-­resistant 
Gram-­negative bacteria. However, scaling up produc-
tion methods from the laboratory research quantities 
is essential for evaluating their real-­world applicability 
to fight against AMR. Most studies are demonstrating 
synthesis at the mg scale. This is enough for some 
antimicrobial testing and characterization, but much 
more thought needs to be put into the realities of scal-
ing to produce 10–1000 kg quantities. One can imagine 
through using bacteria, scale can be reached through 
large bioreactors.
An exciting possibility for further development and 
meeting demand could be through biotechnological ap-
proaches. Although most antimicrobial bioengineered re-
search is focused on viruses, vaccines, and engineering/
functionalizing NPs to enhance antimicrobial activities. 
There is even a creative idea of cloneable nanomaterials 
(Hendricks et al., 2023), via cloning the key genes or op-
erons involved in the synthesis of a NP or relevant metal 
resistance determinants and traits (Dhanker et al., 2021). 
Such bioengineering and synthetic biology approaches 
would allow moving the manufacturing into a microbial 
species with technical advantages such as temperature, 
pH, and ionic strength tolerances in order to optimize the 
cellular nano factories.
From here we see considerable promise of bioNPs 
as antimicrobials, but there are cautionary concerns 
that awareness needs to be built. One needs to also 
consider the collateral effects of using metal-­based 
nanomaterials as antimicrobials. If we were to use NPs 
to the same degree as organic antibiotics, it could lead 
to disastrous amounts of metal pollution. Organic an-

to metal-­based nanomaterial post their primary applica-

cally synthesized, are eventually released into the liquid 
waste stream, ending up in wastewater treatment plants 
(WWTP). This means that the NPs enter aquatic or ma-
rine systems, resulting in ecosystem accumulations as 
they are not removed in the WWTP processing other 
than accumulating in the biomass sludge (Lazareva & 
Keller,  2014), which is still distributed as soil amend-
ments in agriculture, leading to ecosystem exposure. 
Furthermore, there are increasing examples of metal 
NPs exasperating AMR through selecting for antibiotic 
resistance in populations (Fang & Pan, 2024). Thus, like 
antibiotics, the use of biogenic nanomaterial as antimi-
crobials must be used with cautionary stewardship.
There is another issue of use that is often over-
looked, if the bioNP is to be used as an “antibiotic” 
whether oral or parenteral delivery, that given their 
size and nature of the cap, most NPs would be simply 
phagocytosed by cells of the immune system, causing 
a stress responses, inflammation, and tissue damage 
(Toscano & Torres-­Arias, 2023). This limits their use-
ful practical applications. However, for topical and an-
tiseptic, biocidal, and preservative purposes, bioNPs 
could be ideal.
Microbially produced NPs with antimicrobial prop-
erties emphasize the usage of diverse microorgan-
isms in the synthesis of these NPs, which not only 
advances towards achieving UN-­SDG-­3, but also 
signifies the importance of eco-­friendly production 
methods. As the field progresses from discovery to 
practical application, there is a critical need for stan-
dardized methods in both the characterization of bio-
genically produced NPs and the assessment of their 
antimicrobial efficacy. A key aspect of nanotechnol-
ogy is not just making small things; it is about con-
trolling the features of the NP. Microbial biotechnology 
has potential to shine here as the microbiologist has a 
large landscape of potential through the control of the 
biochemistry of a bacterium through strain, media, 
culturing conditions, and genomics, which will lead to 
NPs with different properties.

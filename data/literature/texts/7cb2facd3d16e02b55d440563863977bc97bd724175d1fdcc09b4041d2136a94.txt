ABSTRACT
The development of structurally precise, efficient, and tunable metal nanocluster-based photocatalysts serves as an ideal model
for elucidating intrinsic reaction mechanisms. However, deciphering the correlation between individual metal atoms alterations
in the metallic core and photocatalytic property modulation remains a formidable challenge. In this work, we employed an
atomically precise synthesis strategy to design and tailor alloy clusters Au12-xAgx (x = 4, 5, 6). Under light-specific activation,
these clusters efficiently catalyze oxygen to generate highly toxic reactive oxygen species (ROS), specifically hydroxyl radicals
(⋅OH). The catalytic performance exhibiting composition dependence, achieving effective regulation of redox properties through
gradient-composition adjustments in the metal core. With increasing Ag atom incorporation, the photocatalytic capability of the
alloy clusters is enhanced, exhibiting 99.9% broad-spectrum antibacterial efficacy against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative
bacteria. In infected murine models, nanocluster-embedded wound dressings exhibited excellent biocompatibility and safety,
enabling efficient and controllable photocatalytic antimicrobial action while mitigating excessive inflammation. The alloy clusters
Au12-xAgx exhibit composition-dependent properties, providing a good platform to investigate the relationship between structure
and function at the atomic level.

Introduction
Metal nanoclusters (NCs), as a class of nanomaterials with
atomically precise structures, have emerged as a frontier research
focus due to their unique quantum size effects and tunable
physicochemical properties [1–5]. Their ultrasmall size (<3 nm)
and high monodispersity endow these materials with a high
specific surface area, effectively exposing abundant active sites,
thereby rendering them highly valuable for studying catalytic
activity [6–9]. Notably, the pronounced heavy-atom effect of the
metallic nuclei facilitates energy transfer in excited states and
generates electrons and holes upon photoexcitation to participate
in photo-redox reactions, highlighting their immense potential
in photocatalysis [10–14]. Compared to conventional catalysts,
the atomic-level structural clarity of metal NCs provides an
ideal platform for deciphering structure–activity dynamics in

Importantly, the molecular-like properties of NCs exhibit high
sensitivity to their atomic-level composition, where even a
single-atom variation in the metal core or ligand shell is suffi-
cient to induce significant property changes [22–31]. Leveraging
this, alloying strategies offer a powerful approach for precise
performance modulation [32–34]. By constructing gradient-
composition metallic cores with multiple metals, the molec-
ular orbital arrangement and electronic structure of NCs can
be precisely and systematically regulated, thereby optimizing
their catalytic behavior [34–39]. Previous studies, including
our team’s work on Pt2M4 (M = Au, Ag, Cu) alloy clus-
ters, have demonstrated marked differences in enzyme-mimetic
catalytic activity with varying metal components [40]. Xie
et al. reported a template-synthesized AuxAg25-x alloy system (x
denotes a numerical range), which exhibited unique U-shaped
catalytic behavior in reactive oxygen species (ROS) genera-
tion [41]. However, existing research predominantly focuses
on macroscopic performance characterization of multi-atom
alloy systems, while the microscopic mechanism underlying
how specific x values impact catalytic activity remain under-
explored and warrant systematic investigation in future stud-
ies [42, 43]. Establishing clear structure–activity relationships
necessitates the development of alloy NC model systems with
identical surface ligands but gradually gradient atomic varia-
tions in the metal core, which will become a pivotal approach
to elucidate the role of metallic nuclei in photochemical
processes.
Herein, we designed and synthesized Au12-xAgx alloy nanoclus-
ters (ligated by levonorgestrel, denoted as L) at the atomic
scale as a photocatalytic system, and systematically investigate
the regulatory effects of metallic core composition on light-
responsive antibacterial performance. As illustrated in Scheme 1,
while maintaining an identical metallic framework, we achieved
controlled incorporation or removal of core metal atoms via a one-
pot co-reduction method, successfully preparing three distinct
clusters with sequential Au/Ag ratios, namely Au8Ag4, Au7Ag5,
and Au6Ag6. Variations in the Au/Ag ratio effectively modulate
the energy level structure of the clusters, thereby regulating
the energy barrier of the rate-limiting step in the photocatalytic
reaction and ultimately enhancing the photocatalytic activity.
Among them, Au6Ag6 exhibits superior oxygen reduction capa-
bility, enabling rapid generation of highly oxidative hydroxyl
radicals (⋅OH) under light irradiation. This alloy cluster demon-
strated favorable biocompatibility and broad-spectrum antibac-
terial efficacy against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative
bacteria, achieving a 99.9% eradication rate. Furthermore, in
vitro cytocompatibility and in vivo antibacterial assessments in
infected wound tissues confirmed its potential as a photocatalytic
wound dressing for efficient antimicrobial action and tissue
regeneration.

the prepared clusters possess a uniform size distribution, with
core sizes of approximately 1.0 nm (Figure S2). Dynamic light
scattering (DLS) tests suggested that the clusters exhibit excellent
monodispersity in deionized water and phosphate buffered saline
(PBS) (Figure S3). Energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) and
element mapping demonstrated homogeneous distribution of C,
O, Au, and Ag elements, with Au/Ag atom ratio shows a gradient.

at 3267 cm−1 and the emergence of characteristic metal-alkynyl
stretching vibrations at 1971, 2041, and 2035 cm−1, confirming
successful metal–ligand coordination (Figure 1k) [40]. The zeta
potential of the alloy clusters was measured to be approximately
-23 mV (Figure 1l). Subsequently, the optical properties of the
three clusters were characterized. Owing to the interaction
between the Au and Ag states in the alloy Au12-xAgx NCs, the
discrete energy levels of the clusters are altered, influencing the
electronic structure. As the Ag atoms are gradually incorporated,
the ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) characteristic absorption spectra
of the clusters undergo continuous changes (Figure 1j) [44].
Photoluminescence analysis showed strong green emission at 526
± 3 nm for all three NCs, with quantum yields of 77.16% (Au8Ag4),
69.23% (Au7Ag5), and 66.92% (Au6Ag6), and fluorescence lifetimes
of 3.34, 3.07, and 2.62 µs, respectively (Figures S7 and S8). These
NCs display outstanding photostability and thermal stability, as
evidenced by UV–vis monitoring and thermogravimetric analysis
(Figures S9 and S10). These results verify that we have success-
fully fabricated a series of alloy clusters by precisely regulating
the Au/Ag stoichiometric ratio.
2.2
Photocatalytic Performance of Clusters
To systematically elucidate the regulatory effects of the Au/Ag
stoichiometric ratio on photocatalytic performance, a multimode
catalytic activity evaluation system was established. As depicted
in Figure 2a, using methylene blue (MB) as a model pollutant,
negligible degradation of MB was observed in the control group
after 10 min of UV irradiation. However, in the presence of
Au12-xAgx clusters, the MB solution rapidly changed from dark
blue to light blue, indicating efficient oxidative degradation.
The characteristic absorption peak at 664 nm decreased signif-
icantly with increasing Ag content in the alloy nanoclusters.
Furthermore, when the concentration of Au6Ag6 ranged from
25 to 100 µg mL−1, MB was almost completely degraded within
10 min (Figure 2b). To further confirm the potent photocat-
alytic activity of Au12-xAgx, two additional typical chromogenic
substrates, 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) and 2,2′-azino-
bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) were also
employed. As illustrated in Figure 2c,d, under light irradiation,
colorless TMB and ABTS were oxidized to blue oxTMB and
green ABTS+, respectively, with characteristic absorption peaks
emerging at 652 and 420 nm. The color intensity deepened
with higher Ag content in the clusters, demonstrating that Ag
doping significantly enhances the redox capacity of the alloy
nanoclusters.
In order to further quantitatively assess the regulatory mecha-
nism of distinct Au/Ag stoichiometric ratios on catalytic kinetics,
we systematically monitored the kinetic behavior of Au12-xAgx
in the photocatalytic oxidation of TMB under light irradiation.
As depicted in Figure S11, the absorbance of oxTMB at 652 nm
increased gradually with the elevation of TMB concentration,
and the rate of this increase accelerated significantly as the
Ag content in the clusters rose. The catalytic efficiency of
the alloy nanoclusters was further evaluated via steady-state
kinetic analysis using the Lineweaver-Burk model (Figure S12). In
comparison with the kinetic parameters of other clusters, Au6Ag6
exhibited a higher maximum reaction rate (Vmax). Additionally,
based on the decay curves of MB absorbance over irradiation
time in the presence of different clusters (Figure S13), we further
calculated the initial generation rate of ROS. Within the initial
linear region of the absorbance-time curve, the degradation of MB
followed pseudo-first-order kinetics. The calculated initial ROS
generation rates for Au6Ag6, Au7Ag5, and Au8Ag4 were 1.06, 0.81,
and 0.41 µM min−1, respectively. This trend was highly consistent
with the results of TMB oxidation kinetics, indicating that Ag
doping accelerates dye degradation by enhancing the capacity
for ROS generation. These kinetic parameters directly correlate
the rapid photocatalytic dye degradation process with the intrin-
sic photoactivity of the nanoclusters, providing a quantitative

2.3
Exploration of Catalytic Mechanism
To elucidate the optical catalytic mechanism of alloy clusters,
we first conducted solid-state UV–vis analysis, revealing that Ag
doping can precisely regulate the band structure of the clusters.
The band gaps of Au8Ag4, Au7Ag5, and Au6Ag6, calculated via
the Kubelka-Munk function, were determined as 2.37, 2.34, and
2.47 eV, respectively (Figure S15 and Figure 3a). Ultraviolet
photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurements further showed
work functions of 2.21, 2.58, and 2.96 eV, with valence band
energies (EVB) at 6.48, 6.35, and 6.19 eV for the three clusters,
respectively (Figure 3b). Combined with these results, the elec-
tronic band structures of clusters are depicted in Figure 3c.
Macroscopically, the electronic band of this system is step-
like, with a highly negative conduction band (CB) potentials,
endowed photogenerated electrons with strong reducing power,
facilitating efficient ⋅OH generation for bacterial inactivation.

(ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to further elucidate
the relationship between the introduction of silver and the
reaction process (Figure 3d–f). The spectral results indicate
that the time-dependent peak observed at 1110 cm−1 can be
attributed to the O–O stretching vibration of surface-adsorbed
*OO molecules, confirming the effective adsorption of oxygen
[46–48]. Concurrently, the absorption peak corresponding to
*OOH detected at 1224 cm−1 exhibits a significant increase
in intensity and an earlier appearance with increasing silver
content in the clusters, indicating a markedly enhanced ability
for the conversion of O2 to *OOH [49–51]. Thus, the intro-
duction of silver effectively reduces the reaction energy barrier
for the transformation of O2 to *OOH, thereby improving the
overall reaction rate. These results provide a clear molecular-
level explanation for its enhanced ability to catalytically reduce
oxygen.
2.4
In Vitro Antimicrobial Performance
Investigation
Photocatalytic reactions, driven by light-triggered ROS genera-
tion, are widely exploited for antimicrobial applications due to
their ability to oxidatively disrupt bacterial membrane integrity,
enhance permeability, and induce DNA damage, ultimately
leading to cell death (Figure 4a). The photo-responsive in vitro
antibacterial performance and mechanism of Au12-xAgx was
systematically evaluated against Gram-positive bacteria Staphylo-
coccus aureus (S. aureus) and Gram-negative bacteria Escherichia
coli (E. coli) using ten experimental groups: Group I (PBS),
II (UV), III (L), IV (L + UV), V (Au8Agu4), VI (Au8Ag4 +
UV), VII (Au7Ag5), VIII (Au7Ag5 + UV), IX (Au6Ag6), and X
(Au6Ag6 + UV). Firstly, based on the time-dependent photo-
responsive antibacterial treatment of UV (360 nm, 30 mW cm−2),
the UV irradiation time was optimized to 10 min to exclude
intrinsic UV bactericidal effects (Figure S17). Spread plate assays
confirmed negligible bacterial inhibition by ligand L alone, even
at high concentrations (100 µg mL−1) (Figure S18). Subsequently,
the antibacterial ability of Au12-xAgx at different concentrations
under UV irradiation against S. aureus and E. coli was evaluated
(Figures S19 and S20). Notably, the Au6Ag6 (25 µg mL−1) +
UV exhibited nearly 99.9% broad-spectrum antibacterial effect
against both pathogenic bacteria (Figure 4d). Corresponding
quantitative analysis revealed that the antibacterial efficacy of the
Au7Ag5 + UV group and the Au8Ag4 + UV group decreased in a
gradient manner with the decrease of Ag content (Figure 4b,c).
To further explore the antibacterial mechanism, the morphology
of bacteria after treatment by different systems was observed by
SEM (Figure 4e). In contrast to the intact structures observed

2.5
In Vivo Antibacterial Efficacy and Toxicity
Evaluation
To ensure the biosafety of alloy NCs for infected wound healing
applications, systematic biocompatibility evaluations were con-
ducted. Hemolysis assays demonstrated that all NCs exhibited
hemolysis rates below the 5% safety threshold for biomaterials,
even at a high concentration of 800 µg mL−1 (Figure S22).
Cytotoxicity assessment using the CCK-8 assay revealed >95%
viability of NIH/3T3 mouse embryonic fibroblasts after 24-hour
exposure to varying concentrations of ligands or NCs (Figure S23).
The consecutive 5-day proliferation tracking experiment verified
that there was no significant difference in cell growth trend
between the treated and control group (Figure S24). Using dual
fluorescence staining with STYO 9 (live, green) and propidium
iodide (PI) (dead, red) further validated excellent biocompati-
bility, with viable cell ratios exceeding 99% across all groups
(Figure S25).
Based on these biocompatibility findings, a S. aureus-infected
full-thickness skin defect model was established in mice to
evaluate the in vivo photo-responsive antibacterial efficacy and
pro-healing mechanisms of the NCs. The entire in vivo treat-
ment process is depicted in Figure 5a. The alloy NCs were
fabricated into wound dressings resembling band-aids for light-
activated, controllable, and non-invasive antibacterial treatment
(Figure 5b). Different treatments were administered to the
wounds 1-day post-infection, and the photos of wound healing at
various time points were observed and documented (Figure 5c).
The wound closure evolution and quantitative analysis were
performed using Image J software (Figure 5d). At the conclusion
of the 11-day treatment cycle, after the treatment with only a single
UV irradiation (10 min) to remove the Au6Ag6 dressing, it was
observed that the wound was nearly completely closed, and new
skin was rapidly generated. The wound area of the Au6Ag6 +
UV group was merely 0.9%, significantly outperforming Ag7Ag5
+ UV group (2.2%) and Au8Ag4 + UV (6.6%) (Figure 5f). The
results indicated that the wounds contracted to varying extents
after treatment, and the wound healing effect of the Au12-xAgx +
UV group (groups VI, VIII, and X) was superior to that of the
other 7 groups. All experimental groups exhibited steady body
weight gain, further confirming systemic biosafety (Figure 5e). To
elucidate the correlation between the antibacterial effect in vivo
and wound healing, quantitative analysis of bacterial colonies
in the infected tissues was conducted. The bacteria from the
infected tissues treated differently on the third and 11th days
were inoculated into culture dishes to observe the number of
colonies formed (Figure 5h). Just as predicted, compared with the
blank group treated with PBS, the number of colonies decreased
gradually after Au12-xAgx + UV treatment. Colony-forming unit
(CFU) analysis showed that Au6Ag6 + UV treatment achieved
77.4% bacterial clearance by Day 3 and complete eradication by
Day 11 (Figure 5g), paralleling accelerated epithelialization rates.
These results demonstrate that the Au12-xAgx system enables spa-

3
Conclusion
In this work, a series of compositionally gradient-tunable
Au12-xAgx alloy NCs were successfully synthesized via an atom-
ically precise synthesis strategy. The variation in Au/Ag ratios
effectively modulates the electronic structure of active sites in the
alloy clusters, inducing a systematic shift in redox potentials and
enabling atomic-scale precision in photocatalytic performance
regulation. These nanoclusters exhibit light-triggered catalytic
activity with composition-dependent ROS generation, offering a
noninvasive, controllable, and highly efficient broad-spectrum
antibacterial strategy. The EPR, UPS, and ATR-FTIR analyses
reveal that Ag doping enhances the reducing capability of photo-
generated electrons and significantly lowers the reaction energy
barrier for the key intermediate *OOH. This effectively promotes
the generation of reactive oxygen species, thereby improving
the photocatalytic oxygen reduction performance of the system.
By establishing explicit structure-activity relationships through
atomically precise alloy clusters, this work provides both theoret-
ical and practical foundations for developing high-performance
photocatalytic materials.

Experimental Section
4.1
Synthesis of Precursors Au(tht)Cl Precursor
1.00 g of chloroauric acid (HAuCl4∙4H2O) was dissolved in 20 mL
of ethanol (C2H5OH), followed by the addition of 900 µL of
tetrahydrothiophene (C4H8S) solution. The mixture was stirred
vigorously, during which the solution color transitioned from
yellow to white turbidity. The resulting mixture was stirred in the
dark for 2 h, then sequentially washed three times with ethanol
and diethyl ether, respectively. After air-drying, the product was
stored at 4◦C in the dark for subsequent use.
4.2
Synthesis of Au12-xAgx Clusters (Au8Ag4,
Au7Ag5 and Au6Ag6)
Levonorgestrel (8 mg, 25 µmol) and Au(tht)Cl (5.33 mg,
16.67 µmol) were dissolved in 1.5 mL of dichloromethane. Under
magnetic stirring, 8 µL of triethylamine was added to the solution.
Subsequently, silver nitrate (AgNO3, 2.1 mg, 12.5 µmol) dissolved
in 2 mL of acetonitrile was introduced into the reaction system.
The reaction mixture was stirred in the dark for 1 h, then allowed
to stand at room temperature for natural evaporation over 3–
5 d, yielding green needle-shaped crystals of Au6Ag6. For the
synthesis of Au7Ag5 and Au8Ag4 clusters, an additional 2 mL
of acetone was added to the reaction system, and the molar
ratio of Au(tht)Cl to AgNO3 was adjusted accordingly, while
other reaction conditions remained identical. Specific conditions
are as follows: 4.66 mg (14.58 µmol) of Au(tht)Cl and 1.75 mg
(10.42 µmol) of AgNO3 were mixed, resulting in green block-
shaped crystals of Au7Ag5. 5.33 mg (16.67 µmol) of Au(tht)Cl
and 1.45 mg (8.33 µmol) of AgNO3 were mixed, yielding green
rod-shaped crystals of Au8Ag4.
4.3
Live/Dead Fluorescent Staining
The S. aureus and E. coli were divided into 10 groups (groups
I-X): (I) PBS, (II) UV, (III) L (16.25 µg mL−1), (IV) L + UV,
(V) Au8Agu4 (25 µg mL−1), (VI) Au8Ag4 + UV, (VII) Au7Ag5
(25 µg/mL), (VIII) Au7Ag5 + UV, (IX) Au6Ag6 (25 µg/mL), (X)
Au6Ag6 + UV, respectively. After treatment, incubation at 37◦C
for 10 h. Then, STYO 9 and PI solution was added and stained for
15 min at room temperature in the dark. After that, it was washed
three times with PBS and re-suspended in PBS, and the stained
bacteria were observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy.
4.4
Statistical Analysis
All the data are presented as the average value ± standard
deviation (SD), from triplicate experiments unless otherwise
stated. Differences between the two groups were examined by a
two-tailed Student’s t test. The significance of the difference was
noted with asterisks as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and
****p < 0.0001.

ABSTRACT: Escherichia coli bioﬁlms are a major causative agent of many intestinal
infections, and there is ongoing research aimed at E. coli bioﬁlm eradication. Gold
nanoclusters (AuNCs) conjugated with various surface ligands have been extensively
investigated for antimicrobial properties and provide a potential solution. There is little
known about their in vivo safety because current standards of nanosafety research involve
incubation of AuNCs with cells in vitro to conﬁrm biocompatibility. In addition to systemic
administration, nanosafety research on AuNC-based antimicrobials designed to treat
gastrointestinal infections must also consider the potential for inducing gastrointestinal
disorders. We report the design and application of two AuNCs coated with either hydroxyl
(AuNC@PEG-OH)- or amine (AuNC@PEG-NH2)-functionalized poly(ethylene glycol),
which enables the eradication of E. coli bioﬁlms. Gastrointestinal safety of AuNC@PEG-OH
and AuNC@PEG-NH2 was evaluated in healthy mice up to 35 days after administration by
oral gavage at a dose of 10 mg/kg (or 1 mg/mL) daily for 14 days. No changes were
detected in the histopathology of major organs, serum chemistry, hematology, and feces. Thus, oral administration of AuNCs is
unlikely to be of concern for systemic toxicity or in the induction of gastrointestinal illnesses. Further studies on increasing time
exposure and doses are necessary to determine whether toxicity occurs at higher doses or whether there is no adverse eﬀect limit.
KEYWORDS: gold nanocluster, gastrointestinal safety, oral administration, bioﬁlm, E. coli, persister cells
■INTRODUCTION
Ingested Escherichia coli is a leading cause of infections of the
gastrointestinal (GI) tract, such as travelers’ diarrhea, and a
major cause of diarrheal disease in the developing countries. E.
coli is usually contracted through ingestion of water
contaminated with human or animal feces. In cases of
infectious diarrhea, the majority of healthy patients will clear
the bacteria spontaneously and the oral rehydration solution
remains central to management.1 However, if the disease fails
to resolve early, in immunosuppressed patients or if
complications develop, oral antibiotics treatment is appro-
priate. Unfortunately, the management of GI infections with
oral antibiotics is controversial because of the associated
disruption of the GI microbiome homeostasis that can lead to
changes in several physiological functions such as digestion and
absorption, energy metabolism, immune system development,
and prevention of infections.2−6
While many of the acute E. coli GI infections are caused by
planktonic cells, awareness of subacute and chronic diarrhea-
associated infections due to bioﬁlms formed by E. coli has
increased in recent years.7−10 An E. coli bioﬁlm consists of a
bacterial colony embedded in an extracellular matrix of
polymeric substances (EPS). The increased expression of
resistance markers as the barrier for diﬀusion provided by the
EPS has made bioﬁlms recalcitrant to treatment with
antibiotics and may contribute to the development of chronic
diseases.11,12 There is, therefore, an unmet medical need for
new oral antimicrobial agents that eﬃciently kill bioﬁlms
without harming the intestinal microbiome.
Due to their lower systemic toxicity, faster kidney clearance,
and unique physicochemical properties, ultrasmall gold
nanoparticles (core diameter ≤2 nm), often called gold
nanoclusters (AuNCs), have been proved to be promising in
biomedical applications.13−15 For infections caused by multi-
drug-resistant bacteria (MDR), AuNCs have shown promise.16
AuNCs conjugated with various surface ligands have been
shown to be an eﬀective treatment of MDR Gram-negative
bacteria in vitro and in vivo, without induction of cytotoxicity to
mammalian cells. In addition, AuNCs have shown lower
propensity to induce microbial resistance as compared to
classical antibiotics.17,18 Comprehensive reviews about the
application of AuNC for bacterial detection and infection
therapy are available in the literature.19 Importantly, oral

AuNCs therefore represent an important class of antimicrobial

Despite the demonstrated potential of AuNCs as an

eradicate E. coli in vitro bioﬁlms of two AuNCs coated with
either hydroxyl (AuNC@PEG-OH)- or amine (AuNC@PEG-
NH2)-functionalized poly(ethylene glycol) and then demon-
strate the lack of toxicity when delivered in vivo by the
gastrointestinal route.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Synthesis and Characterization of AuNCs. AuNC@PEG-NH2
and AuNC@PEG-OH were synthesized and puriﬁed according to a
predeﬁned protocol, but we used a mixture of cell-penetrating peptide
and thiol-PEG rather than 6-mercaptohexanoic acid.23 Freshly
prepared aqueous solutions of HAuCl4 (20 mM, 1 mL) and thiol-
PEG-NH2 or thiol-PEG-OH (18 mg, 2 mL) and cell-penetrating
peptides YGRKKRRQRRR-(ß-ala)3-Cys-amide (18 mg, 2 mL) were
mixed in water (13.4 mL). After that, an aqueous NaOH solution (1
M, 1.2 mL) was added to the mixture. A freshly prepared NaBH4
solution (112 mM) was obtained by dissolving 43 mg of NaBH4 in 2
mL of NaOH solution (1 M), followed by the addition of 8 mL of
ultrapure water. After that, 0.4 mL of NaBH4 solution was added into
the solution, and AuNC@PEG-NH2 and AuNC@PEG-OH were
collected after 24 h. After synthesis, the solutions were dialyzed
(dialysis membrane, MWCO = 1000) for 2 days against Milli-Q
water, which was changed every 8 h to remove the unconjugated PEG
and YGRKKRRQRRR-(ß-ala)3-Cys-amide. The resulting AuNC@
PEG-NH2 and AuNC@PEG-OH were lyophilized and dried
completely before further use. The ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis)
absorption measurements were performed using an absorption
spectrophotometer (spectramMax M2, Molecular Devices, Down-
ington, PA). The emission spectra of as-synthetized AuNC@PEG-
OH and AuNC@PEG-NH2 were measured at excitation wavelength
λex = 365 nm.
In Vitro Bioﬁlm Eradication. Standard reference strains of E. coli
ATCC 25922 were used in this study. Bioﬁlms were formed on the lid
of a Calgary Bioﬁlm Device (CBD from Innovotech Inc. Edmonton,
Canada) and then transferred to a standard 96-well plate, in which
dilutions of their AuNC@PEG-NH2 or AuNC@PEG-OH were
prepared in PBS. The plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C,
after which the lid was removed, rinsed with PBS, and placed in a
second 96-well plate containing Lysogeny broth (LB). The bioﬁlm
was removed from the CBD lid by sonication for 15 min at 40 kHz
(CO-Z 6L professional ultrasonic cleaner). A new plate cover was
added, and the viability of the bioﬁlm was determined after 48 h of
incubation at 37 °C by reading the turbidity at 650 nm using a 96-well
plate reader (SpectraMax M2, Molecular Devices, Downington, PA)
and spot plating on LB agar plates to conﬁrm bioﬁlm eradication. The
minimal bioﬁlm eradication concentration (MBEC) was deﬁned as
the minimal concentration of AuNCs required to eradicate the
bioﬁlm. The capability of the cell-penetrating peptide to eradicate the
E. coli bioﬁlm was achieved as described above.
Animal Treatment and Sample Collection. All animal work
was approved by Stanford University’s Administrative Panel on
Laboratory Animal Care. The 10−12 week-old C57BL/6J mice were
purchased from Jackson Laboratories (Sacramento, CA) and housed
in Stanford University’s animal resource facility according to standard
guidelines in which food and water were provided ad libitum in a
room maintained at 12 h dark/light cycles. Female (n = 4 each group)
and male (n = 3 each group) C57BL/6J mice were divided into four
groups, including control (PBS), AuNC@PEG-NH2, AuNC@PEG-
OH, and Au3+. The treatments were administrated by oral gavage at a
dose of 10 mg/kg (i.e., 1 mg/mL) daily for 14 days. At 35 days post-
treatment, mice were sacriﬁced. Blood and organs were collected and
weighed for further investigation of the organ/body weight ratio,
serum chemistry, hematology, and histopathology. Based on the
absolute weight and body weight at necropsy, the organ-to-body
weight ratio (relative organ weight, ROW) was calculated. ROW =
(organ weight (mg)/body weight of the animal on sacriﬁce day (g)).
We selected organ weight data including those of the thymus, spleen,
heart, kidneys, liver, and testis from either the control group
(phosphate-buﬀered saline, PBS) or treated group animals. The
following organs including the esophagus, stomach, heart, lung, liver,
kidney, spleen, pancreas, thymus, small intestine, colon, bladder, tests,
and ovary were examined for potential histological alteration.
During the 35 day study, the body weights of animals were
measured every 2 days.
In Vivo Toxicity. Blood samples and the major organs were
subjected to toxicity analysis. An inferior vena cava blood collection
was performed at the sacriﬁce. Blood (150 μL) was placed in a
K2EDTA tube for hematological analysis, and the left blood sample
was placed in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube for serum extraction.
The serum was separated by centrifuging the blood to remove the
cellular fraction for liver and renal function testing. All organs were
preserved, ﬁxed in 10% neutral formalin buﬀer, processed into paraﬃn
embedding, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for pathological
analysis using a light microscope. The studies and histology were
blinded, and a sample size calculation was performed to achieve
statistical power.
Statistical Analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard
deviation. In the case of signiﬁcance (P < 0.05), the data were tested
using the unpaired (independent) T-test in GraphPad Prism to
determine which groups are signiﬁcantly diﬀerent from each other.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Characterization of AuNCs. PEG coating is a strategy to
prevent agglomeration upon oral administration. It has
previously been demonstrated that AuNCs protected by PEG
(5 kDa) can be observed as primary, unagglomerated particles
throughout the gastrointestinal tract and feces of dosed
animals.24 To ensure the stability of the AuNCs at the gut
pH, we passivated them with either thiol-PEG-NH2 or thiol-
PEG-OH. Figure 1a shows the absorption spectra of AuNC@
PEG-OH and AuNC@PEG-NH2. The ultraviolet−visible
(UV−vis) spectra of both PEG-coated AuNCs show a
monotonous decrease from UV to the visible region, similar
to the PEG-protected AuNCs previously reported.25 Spherical
gold nanoparticles with diameters between 5 and 100 nm are
well known to exhibit a distinct surface plasmon resonance
(SPR) between 520 and 570 nm depending on the size. The
SPR disappears for ultrasmall particles (core diameter ≤2
nm). Thus, the absence of SPR in the UV−vis spectra in
Figure 1A demonstrates that AuNC@PEG-OH and AuNC@
PEG-NH2 are ultrasmall with a core diameter ≤2 nm. AuNCs
are a transition state between gold atoms and gold nanocryst-
als, and there is no evidence for the nucleation step of gold
nanoclusters as they are still considered as molecules (or
molecule-like compounds). Under light irradiation, AuNCs can
emit ﬂuorescence, similar to the small organic dyes. AuNC@
PEG-NH2 and AuNC@PEG-OH exhibit a similar emission
peak at 438 nm (emission energy 2.8 eV) (Figure 1B,C). While
the issue of how to exactly measure the size of AuNCs poses a
big challenge,26 the emission wavelength of ﬂuorescence allows
for reasonable estimates of how many gold atoms compose a
single nanocluster.27−31 According to the correlation of the
number of gold atoms, N, per cluster with emission energy,

In Vitro Bioﬁlm Eradication by AuNCs. Addressing the
problem of antimicrobial resistance of bioﬁlm-producing E.
coli, we tested whether AuNC@PEG-OH and AuNC@PEG-
NH2 aﬀected bioﬁlms. In vitro estimation of the minimum
bioﬁlm eradication concentration (MBEC) has served as the
primary testing model for predicting clinical failure and clinical
success of antimicrobial therapy against bioﬁlm bacteria.34,35
To this end, we treated 48 h old bioﬁlms of E. coli with both
AuNCs for 24 h and determined the MBEC.36 The treatment
of the E. coli bioﬁlm with AuNC@PEG-OH and AuNC@PEG-
NH2 leads to an MBEC value of 1 mg/mL (Figure 2). It has
previously been observed that the surface ligand chemistry of
AuNCs determines their antimicrobial ability against plank-
tonic bacteria.37 It was mentioned that the observed
antimicrobial properties could not be due to the ligand itself.
The antimicrobial ability of their AuNCs was achieved through
generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which could
interfere with the regular bacterial metabolism and sub-
sequently kill the cells. The presence of positively −NH2 and
neutrally charged −OH groups decreases the antimicrobial
ability of this kind of AuNCs, as their ROS-preserving
capability weakens. In contrast to the positively and neutrally
charged groups, the negative −COOH could better preserve
the generated ROS, resulting in higher antimicrobial ability
against the metabolically active planktonic bacteria in
suspension. Of particular note, reducing the metabolic activity
of the bacterial cell by decreasing the exposure temperature to
4 °C signiﬁcantly suppresses the ROS production induced by
anionic AuNCs.23
The current study has been unable to demonstrate the
surface ligand eﬀects of AuNCs on E. coli bioﬁlm cells. A
possible explanation for this might be that in our design
AuNCs are protected by a biligand (thiol-PEG-NH2 or thiol-
PEG-OH and a cell-penetrating peptide). Thus, AuNCs could
exert their antibacterial activities by penetrating bacterial cell
membranes and targeting intracellular DNA rather than
through ROS generation. More studies on the molecular
mechanisms are needed. However, our results clearly
demonstrated that the interchange between PEG-NH2 and
PEG-OH has no eﬀect on the antimicrobial ability of AuNCs.
The killing eﬃcacy of two types of AuNCs (i.e., MBEC value
against bioﬁlm cells) is similar, suggesting that the cell-
penetrating peptide could be the ligand that interacts with
intracellular DNA and determines the antimicrobial ability. It
has been shown that a cell-penetrating peptide could exert
antimicrobial activity against planktonic E. coli by targeting
intracellular DNA.38 As shown in our control experiment in
Figure S1, the cell-penetrating peptide did not show any
bioﬁlm eradication at a concentration of up to 4000 μg/mL.
This study conﬁrms that the entire entity of AuNCs was
required for bioﬁlm eradication. The bioﬁlm eradication
eﬃcacy could probably be attributed to the more compact
and protruding orientation of the cell-penetrating peptide on

It is worth noting that the antimicrobial nanoparticle needs
to penetrate the bioﬁlm barrier to kill the bacterial cells that
reside inside the barrier.39 Previous research has shown that
the antimicrobial ability of anionic AuNCs through ROS
generation could eﬀectively kill the bacteria within the bioﬁlm
using the spheroids (e.g., the metabolically active bacteria/agar
mixture).37 The physical barrier produced by spheroids is
radically diﬀerent from that of the self-produced bioﬁlm
matrix.40 Rotello and colleagues studied the diﬀerent
interactions of an E. coli bioﬁlm with, respectively, neutral,
anionic, and cationic quantum dots (QDs) (d = 7.5−24 nm).41
They reported that both neutral and anionic QDs were not
able to penetrate the self-produced bioﬁlm matrix, while
cationic ones were able to penetrate and diﬀuse within the
bioﬁlm matrix. Furthermore, the bacterial cells that reside
inside the bioﬁlm can switch from metabolically active bacteria
(susceptible cells) to dormant bacterial cells, the so-called
persister cells. Persister cells use superoxide dismutases to
survive antimicrobial actions of ROS.42 The dormant persister
cells have been implicated in treatment failure of infection
associated with bioﬁlms.43 Thus, mimicking the bioﬁlms with
the spheroids may result in inaccurate estimation of the
physical barrier produced bioﬁlms and disregard the presence
of persister cells. Based on the antimicrobial mechanic and
surface properties, the preceding anionic AuNCs are not the
good design to target dormant persister cells that reside inside
the bioﬁlms. This highlighted the diﬀerence in the current
design of AuNCs compared to those published previously.16,37
In this work, for the ﬁrst time, we demonstrate the eradication
of bioﬁlms and associated persister cells using AuNCs. Taken
together, these results demonstrate the potential application of
AuNC@PEG-OH and AuNC@PEG-NH2 as an alternative to
oral antibiotics for treating diarrhea-associated bioﬁlms formed
by E. coli. It will be useful in the future to look at how the
current therapy aﬀects commensal bacteria. Antibiotic-induced
release of endotoxin can have immediate adverse eﬀects on the
patient and is a major problem in deciding whether to use
antibiotic therapy.44 The AuNCs target lipid A of lip-
opolysaccharide to deactivate endotoxicity by compacting its
packing density,45 suggesting that the current AuNCs are
unlikely to induce endotoxin release. We recognize the
limitations of using laboratory strains to study clinical disease.
The E. coli strain used in this study is validated over a large
number of preclinical studies in detecting antimicrobial
eﬃcacy; however, another avenue of potential research
includes testing directly on clinical isolates. Further studies
are necessary to conﬁrm the antibioﬁlm activity of AuNC@
PEG-OH and AuNC@PEG-NH2 after oral administration in
vivo models of the bioﬁlm formed by E. coli in the GI tract.
Body Weight Loss and Organ Weight Changes. The
organ-to-body weight ratio is one of the most sensitive drug
toxicity indicators. Currently, there is no data supporting
AuNC safety on the potential change in organ weights.
Furthermore, the role of animal sex in the toxicity of AuNCs is
poorly understood. Therefore, this study was conducted to
demonstrate the safety of oral gavage of AuNCs on changes in
organ weights and by taking into account the gender. Figure 3
shows the animal treatment design and timeline of the GI
toxicity used in the study. According to the measured body
weight, no statistical signiﬁcance in body weight loss was seen
among groups or between genders (Figure 4). There was no
remarkable change in the fecal form (i.e., presence of diarrhea)
recorded during the observations. Some relative organ weight
(ROW) diﬀerence was noticed in the treated female groups (n
= 4 each group) compared to control, but there were no
signiﬁcant diﬀerences in the male groups (n = 3 each group).
The ROWs of the spleen and liver were signiﬁcantly increased
in AuNC@PEG-NH2 and AuNC@PEG-OH treatment groups,
respectively (Figure 5 and Table S1). In addition, the ROW of
the heart was signiﬁcantly decreased in Au3+ treatment groups
compared to that in the control group (Figure 5). The society
of toxicologic pathology considers that ROW changes not
accompanied by any histopathological changes or clinical
chemistry changes are not adverse and so we do not consider
these as identiﬁers of toxicity without corroboration with
histopathological data presented henceforward.46

Histopathological Alteration of Gastrointestinal Tract
Organs. Light microscopy examination of hematoxylin−eosin
staining sections of organs including the esophagus, stomach,
heart, lung, liver, kidney, spleen, pancreas, thymus, small
intestine, colon, bladder, testis, and ovary of control and
treated groups showed normal histology and absence of any
histopathological lesions (Figure 6 and Table S2). Addition-
ally, macroscopic examination of organs of treated mice
revealed no abnormalities in the color or texture when
compared with the organs of the control group. Although
ROW changes (liver, spleen, and heart) have been observed
for treated female mice, the histopathological examination
showed no diﬀerence when compared with the control group.
These results suggest that oral gavage of AuNC@PEG-NH2,
AuNC@PEG-OH, and Au3+ does not induce adverse eﬀects.
The nanoparticle size has been found to impact the in vitro
cytotoxicity.47 Therefore, the eﬀect of size was also compared
for oral toxicity. Elbakary and colleagues have reported
histological changes in lung tissues after oral gavage with
citrate-coated gold nanoparticles (10−20 nm) at a dose of 0.4
mg/kg daily for 14 days (a dose 25 times lower than the one
used in this study).48 In contrary to this ﬁnding, the eﬀect of
AuNC@PEG-NH2 and AuNC@PEG-OH on the histological
lung tissues of mice revealed normal lung architecture with a
spongy structure, normal clear alveoli, and thin interalveolar
septa (Figure 6). It can thus be suggested that the size plays a
key role in the oral toxicity of gold nanoparticles and smaller
sizes (core diameter ≤2 nm) are much less toxic than bigger
nanoparticles. No statistically signiﬁcant diﬀerences were
observed between the groups, suggesting that there were no
ligand eﬀects on the toxicity of AuNCs administered orally. It
is now well established from a variety of studies that AuNCs
(below 6 nm) escape the reticuloendothelial system and are
primarily metabolized by renal clearance.49,50 Stevens and
colleagues demonstrated that AuNCs could be completely
cleared from the body through the liver and kidneys.51 Of
particular note, the combination of low oral absorption and
ﬁrst-pass metabolism limits the bioavailability of AuNCs (less
than 7%).52 Therefore, after oral administration, the main
pathway for AuNC excretion from the body is through the
fecal route.
Hematology Analysis In Vivo. In its metallic form, gold
(Au0) is not toxic, which is why many consumable gourmet
products have received a U.S. Food and Drug Administration
(FDA) certiﬁcation. Besides gourmet products, nonmetallic
state of gold (aurothiomalate, Au+) is used in the oral
treatment of rheumatoid arthritis. Myeloperoxidase generates
the oxidizing agent hypochlorous acid during the oxidative
burst associated with inﬂammation and can oxidize Au+ to
Au3+, the parent element of AuNC@PEG-NH2 and AuNC@
PEG-OH.53 Wet chemical synthesis of AuNCs involves the
reduction of Au3+ ions to Au0 atoms, which upon nucleation
and growth form AuNCs. The AuNCs ≤2 nm are no longer
metallic but exhibit energetically separated electronic states
and behave as molecular ligands.54 The present study was
designed to determine whether the AuNC form increases the
hematological toxicity of Au0 or Au3+. To this end, we
evaluated the changes in hematological markers of in vivo
toxicity including erythrocytes, leukocytes, and related
parameters after exposure to AuNC@PEG-NH2, AuNC@
PEG-OH, and Au3+. This study is the ﬁrst to quantify the
hematological change in mice after exposure to an oral
formulation of AuNCs.

■CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we synthesized two diﬀerently charged AuNCs
functionalized with poly(ethylene glycol) (AuNC@PEG-NH2
and AuNC@PEG-OH) and studied their antibioﬁlm activity
and potential GI toxicity after oral gavage in healthy mice.
AuNC@PEG-NH2 and AuNC@PEG-OH displayed a pro-
nounced antibioﬁlm activity. When administrated orally, the
AuNC form does not increase the toxicity of the parent
element (i.e., Au3+). When administrated through the GI route,
these AuNCs do not prompt signiﬁcant changes in the
hematology, GI tract organs, or liver and kidney function.
Thus, our data raise the possibility that the in vivo toxicity of
AuNCs is dependent on the exposure routes.

A B S T R A C T
Cationic polymers hold strong promise for treating bacterial infections. Most reported cationic antibacterial 
polymers incorporate nitrogen-containing moieties, including primary, secondary, or quaternary amine group, 
whereas phosphonium-based antibacterial polymer was less reported and their structure–activity relationships 
have not been systematically elucidated. In this work, a series of polymers bearing different quaternary phos­
phonium side chains (CP-n) were synthesized. Among these polymers, the polymer with tripropylphosphine side 
group (brief named as CP-3) shown best antibacterial activity (MIC = 2 μg/mL against S. aureus) and highest 
selectivity index (SI = 100). Mechanistic study further demonstrated that, compared with the zwitterionic 
phosphocholine (DOPC) in mammalian membranes, CP-3 can selectively bind to the anionic bacterial-specific 
phospholipids phosphatidylglycerol (POPG) through electrostatic interactions, thereby effectively disrupt bac­
terial membrane integrity. Due to the unique bacterial phospholipids targeting ability and membrane damaging 
mechanism, CP-3 demonstrates rapid bactericidal kinetics with low tendency to induce bacterial resistance. 
Notably, CP-3 effectively inhibits S. aureus biofilm formation and disrupts mature S. aureus biofilms. Further­
more, CP-3 demonstrated strong therapeutic potential in MRSA-induced lung infection and peritonitis. Hence, 
this work provides a new strategy for the design of phosphonium-based antibacterial polymers.
1. Introduction
Bacterial infections, including those caused by antibiotic-resistant 
organisms, impose a substantial burden on global health and econo­
mies, with drug-resistant pathogens accounting for an estimated 4.95 
million deaths associated with antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in 2019, 
including 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to AMR [1–3]. 
However, the rate of antibacterial innovation has failed to match the 
accelerating rise in bacterial resistance. The WHO reviews and inde­
pendent pipeline audits consistently report a scarcity of truly novel, 
first-in-class agents advancing against priority pathogens, thereby 
widening the gap between clinical need and available therapies [4–7]. 
Beyond

antibiotics owing to extracellular-matrix shielding nutrient limitation

and these features complicate eradication and drive chronic relapsing 
disease [8–12]. Collectively, there is an urgent need for novel 
antibacterial agents with a reduced propensity for bacterial resistance 
development and effective bacterial biofilm eradication ability.
The bacterial cytoplasmic membrane performs essential functions in 
barrier integrity, energy transduction, nutrient transport, and stress 
sensing, and is therefore a critical determinant of viability [13–15]. 
Phospholipids are the principal constituents of membranes [16,17]. 
Notably, the phospholipid composition of bacterial membranes differs 
markedly from that of mammalian cell membranes [18,19]. Quantita­
tively, negative charged 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-phosphatidylglycerol 
(POPG) constitutes roughly 20–25% of total phospholipids in 
Gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli and can reach 50–60% in 
Gram-positive species like Staphylococcus aureus [20–22]. Owing to the 
high levels of POPG, bacterial membranes generally display obvious 
negative charge. In sharp contrast, the exoplasmic leaflet of mammalian 
plasma membranes is enriched in zwitterionic choline-headgroup 
phospholipids, predominantly phosphatidylcholine (DOPC) and sphin­
gomyelin, yielding an approximately neutral net surface charge 
[23–25]. Considering this distinctive difference in membrane charge of

bacterial infection models.

2.2. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal 
concentration (MBC) measurement
MICs of the CP-n and antibiotics (vancomycin or norfloxacin) against 
bacteria were determined by the broth microdilution method. Bacterial 
cultures were incubated in MH broth at 37 ◦C overnight. The bacteria 
were washed with PBS two times and adjusted to 1 × 106 CFU mL−1. 
Then, the bacterial solution was incubated with each agent at 37 ◦C for 
24 h. The MIC was the lowest concentration with no increase in OD600. 
Broth with bacteria but without drug served as the negative control. 
Each MIC measurement was performed in triplicate.
The MBC assay was conducted according to previously described 
literature. After the MIC assay, 100 μL bacterial solution from each 
selected well of the 96-well plate was spread evenly onto the surface of 
MH agar plates. The plates were incubated at 37 ◦C for 24 h, followed by 
colony counting. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration at 
which no colonies were observed.
2.3. Zeta potential test
The bacteria were cultured at 37 ◦C overnight. The bacteria were 
washed twice with PBS, and the suspension was adjusted to OD600 = 0.5. 
The polymer solutions with different concentrations were incubated 
with the bacterial suspension in microcentrifuge tubes for 30 min. The 
suspension was removed by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS, and 
resuspended in PBS. 1 mL of the bacterial suspension was transferred to 
the sample well, and the zeta potential was determined using a Malvern 
Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, UK).
2.4. Mouse lung infection
The infection model was established according to previously re­
ported procedures, with minor modifications. Briefly, female ICR mice 
(18–22 g) were rendered neutropenic by intraperitoneal cyclophos­
phamide (150 mg/kg on day −5 and 100 mg/kg on day −3). On day 0, 
mice were anesthetized and intratracheally instilled with 50 μL of MRSA 
suspension (1 × 107 CFU/mL). At 24 h post-infection, mice were 
randomized into three groups and given a single intratracheal dose of 
PBS, CP-3 (1 mg/mL), or vancomycin (1 mg/mL). At 24 h post- 
treatment, lungs were harvested, homogenized, serially diluted, and 
plated on MH agar, colonies were counted after incubation at 37 ◦C for 
14 h to quantify bacterial burden (CFU per gram of lung). Representative 
lung tissues were further processed for histopathological evaluation 
using standard staining protocols. For survival analysis, infected mice 
were treated as above and monitored daily for up to 8 days, with survival 
recorded.

3.3. Antibacterial mechanism assay
It has been reported that cationic polymers exert bactericidal effects 
primarily by compromising bacterial membrane integrity [48,49]. The 
antibacterial mechanism of CP-3 was first examined by zeta-potential 
measurements. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, the bacterial surface poten­
tial was progressively shifted toward positive values with increasing 
CP-3 concentrations, indicating that cationic CP-3 can effectively bind­
ing to negatively charged bacteria surface through electrostatic in­
teractions. Membrane depolarization was then assessed using DiSC3(5) 
as a membrane-potential probe. As shown in Fig. 3C and D, fluorescence 
intensity was markedly increased in a concentration-dependent manner, 
demonstrating that CP-3 induced bacterial membrane depolarization, 
which mainly attributable to insertion of hydrophobic side chains into 
the bilayer and consequent structural disruption [50]. Bacterial mem­
brane damage was evaluated by quantifying the release of intracellular 
constituents. Following exposure to CP-3 across a range of concentra­
tions, substantial amounts of DNA, ATP, K+, and protein were detected 
in the bacterial supernatants (Fig. 3E–H).
To further confirm the membrane-disruption mechanism of CP-3, 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron micro­
scopy (TEM) were employed. As demonstrated in Fig. 3I and J, clear 
edges and intact surfaces were observed for PBS-treated bacteria. In 
sharp contrast, CP-3 treated bacteria were characterized by pronounced 
membrane collapse and deformation with concomitant cytoplasmic 
leakage. These morphological features are consistent with CP-3 exerting 
bactericidal activity through membrane disruption. Live/dead staining 
assay was also conducted to confirm the membrane-lysis ability of CP-3. 
SYTO 9 labels nucleic acids in cells with either intact or damaged 
membranes, whereas PI penetrates only compromised membranes, 
binds DNA, and yields red fluorescence [51]. Strong red fluorescence 
was observed after 2 min of co-incubation of CP-3 and bacteria (Fig. 3K), 
further demonstrating the rapid and effective bacterial membrane-lytic 
ability of CP-3. Taken together, the above results suggest that the anti­
bacterial mechanism of CP-3 mirrors that of many natural or synthetic 
AMPs, the positively charged CP-3 engages the negatively charged 
bacterial membrane through electrostatic interactions, leading to 
membrane disruption, permeabilization, and subsequent lysis (Fig. 3L).
In contrast to normal mammalian cells, whose plasma membranes 
are rich in zwitterionic phospholipids such as DOPC, bacterial cyto­
plasmic membranes contain higher levels of the anionic lipid POPG 
(Fig. 4A and B) [16,52]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the cationic 
CP-3 can disrupt the integrity of bacterial membrane through binding to 
negatively charged POPG. CP-3 was co-incubated with POPG and DOPC 
at varying molar ratios to assess phospholipids-dependent changes in 
antibacterial activity. Bacterial viability was quantified using the resa­
zurin sodium salt assay, in which resazurin (blue) is metabolically 
reduced by live cells to fluorescent resorufin (pink) in the presence of 
bacterial growth. As shown in Fig. 4C, with increasing fractions of POPG 
in the mixed solution, a gradual increase in MIC value was detected of 
CP-3. When the phospholipid to CP-3 molar ratio was 10:1, the MIC 
values of CP-3 against S. aureus and E. coli increased by 8- and 16-fold, 
respectively (Fig. 4D and E). However, after co-incubation of DOPC with 
CP-3, the antibacterial activity of CP-3 showed no change (MIC values 
consistently remained at 2 μg/mL). The above result indicated that CP-3 
can effectively bind to POPG on bacterial membranes, thereby exhibit­
ing strong bacterial membrane-lytic activity.
In order to confirm the interaction between CP-3 and phospholipid, 
Cy5–CP-3 was incubated with different concentrations of POPG or 
DOPC, and fluorescence changes were recorded. As shown in Fig. 4F, an 
obvious blue shift (~8 nm) of the emission maximum (λem) was 
observed for Cy5–CP-3 in POPG mixed solution relative to water. It was 
inferred that, upon co-incubation with POPG, the Cy5 moiety conju­
gated to CP-3 was relocated from a less polar environment to the more 
polar surface of POPG. Moreover, as the concentration of POPG lipo­
somes was increased, the fluorescence intensity of Cy5–CP-3 was 
markedly decreased, which was consistent with Dexter quenching 
mechanism [53]. Since Dexter quenching occurs only when the donor 
and acceptor are sufficiently close to allow orbital overlap, effective 
binding of Cy5–CP-3 to POPG was thereby confirmed. In contrast, no 
changes in either λem or fluorescence intensity were detected when 
Cy5–CP-3 was incubated with DOPC over a range of concentrations, 
indicating the weak interaction between CP-3 and DOPC (Fig. 4G). To 
further verify the binding ability between CP-3 and phospholipids, ITC 
assay was conducted. As indicated in Fig. 4H and I, CP-3 displayed a 
strong interaction with POPG (Kd = 6.21 × 10−5 M). In contrast, the 
interaction between CP-3 and DOPC was weak and did not produce a 
measurable Kd. Taken together, the strong interactions of CP-3 with 
bacterial-specific phospholipids POPG, combined with its weak affinity 
for DOPC, are the main reasons for its potent antibacterial activity and 
low cytotoxicity (Fig. 4J). Considering the differences in lipid compo­
sitions between E. coli and S. aureus, we used different lipid ratios of 
POPE and POPG to simulate the inner membrane of E. coli and the cell 
membrane of S. aureus for dye leakage assays. As shown in Fig. S7, CP-3 
rapidly induced dye leakage from liposomes mimicking the S. aureus 
membrane structure within 30 min. In contrast, CP-3 showed limited 
dye leakage in the E. coli mimicking liposomes, demonstrating its 
weaker disruptive ability. These results further support the observation 
that the polymer exhibits stronger antibacterial activity against S. aureus 
and slightly weaker activity against E. coli due to its limited effect on the 
inner membrane.
Owing to the distinctive membrane lytic mechanism, the time kill 
kinetics of CP-3 against bacterial were characterized. As shown in 
Fig. 4K and L, compared with the clinically used antibiotics vancomycin 
and norfloxacin, CP-3 exhibits rapid bactericidal kinetics, resulting in a 
100% killing rate within 30 min. Compared with intrinsic resistance, 
acquired resistance, often driven by the excessive use of antibiotics and 
other antimicrobials, represents the greater clinical threat [54,55]. 
Accordingly, the potential of CP-3 to induce bacterial resistance under 
continuous exposure was investigated. Vancomycin and norfloxacin 
were used as control groups. Cultures were maintained for 20 sequential 
passages at subinhibitory (sub-MIC) concentrations of CP-3 or antibi­
otics, with MIC values measured at each passage. As displayed in Fig. 4M 
and N, after 20 serial passages under continuous exposure, CP-3 did not 
induce resistance in either S. aureus or E. coli, whereas the MICs of 
vancomycin and norfloxacin increased to 16-fold and 8-fold, respec­
tively. In addition, we found that CP-3 treated S. aureus and E. coli did 
not exhibit cross-resistance to common antibiotics. In contrast, S. aureus 
treated with vancomycin showed moderate resistance to teicoplanin and 
daptomycin, while E. coli treated with norfloxacin demonstrated a 
certain level of cross-resistance to ampicillin (Fig. S8). Owing to the 
potent antibacterial activity, rapid time–kill kinetics, and low tendency 
to induce bacterial resistance, CP-3 could be considered as a potential 
antibacterial candidate.

3.5. In vivo antibacterial activity
Due to the potent in vitro antibacterial activity of CP-3, the thera­
peutic efficacy of CP-3 was investigated in a MRSA induced mouse lung 
infection. Mice were immunosuppressed via intraperitoneal cyclophos­
phamide administration on days −5 and −3. On day 0, MRSA was 
delivered intratracheally to establish pneumonia, followed by intra­
tracheal CP-3 treatment on day 1. After 24 h, the lung tissue was 
collected (Fig. 6A). H&E staining showed extensive alveolar disruption 
and septal thickening in the PBS group, consistent with severe lung 
injury (Fig. 6B). By comparison, CP-3 and vancomycin treatment largely 
preserved alveolar architecture and airspace integrity, indicating 
markedly reduced infection-induced tissue damage. The antibacterial 
efficacy was quantified using the standard plate dilution method 
(Fig. 6C). Compared to the PBS treated group, CP-3 significantly 
reducing the bacterial load by approximately 2.71- log CFU, indicating 
its excellent antibacterial ability in vivo. In addition, intratracheal 
administration of CP-3 at the therapeutic dosage caused no obvious 
pathological changes in lung tissues of uninfected healthy mice 
compared with PBS, suggesting good local pulmonary compatibility 
(Fig. S9).
The degree of pulmonary inflammation serves as a key measure of 
lung tissue recovery. The pulmonary expression of IL-6 and TNF-α was 
analyzed by immunofluorescence staining. As shown in Fig. 6D–G, 
compared with the PBS group, both CP-3 and vancomycin treatment 
markedly reduced the levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and 
TNF-α. The survival ratio of the mouse was monitored over an 8-day 
period. As shown in Fig. 6H, CP-3 markedly improved survival, 
yielding a 90% survival rate compared with 20% in the PBS group. The 
in vivo safety of CP-3 was also assessed. As shown in Fig. 6I, the mice 
showed a slight increase in body weight during treatment, and serum 
biochemical indices remained within normal physiological ranges at the 
end of dosing (Fig. 6J–K). Collectively, these results suggest favorable in 
vivo biocompatibility and safety of CP-3.
Next, the therapeutic potential of CP-3 in mouse peritonitis was 
conducted, which is much more challenging. Mice were immunosup­
pressed with cyclophosphamide and then challenged by intraperitoneal 
injection of 200 μL of MRSA (Fig. 7A). After four consecutive treatments, 
bacterial burdens in major organs, blood, and peritoneal fluid were 
quantified. As shown in Fig. 7B–H, both vancomycin and CP-3 resulted 
in marked reductions in bacterial loads relative to PBS. Survival over a 
15-day period was assessed. As depicted in Fig. 7I, CP-3 significantly 
prolonged survival, with an 80% survival rate at day 15 compared with 
20% in the PBS group.
To assess the in vivo biocompatibility of CP-3, the body weight of the 
mice was monitored. As demonstrated in Fig. 7J, no significant weight 
loss was observed in CP-3 treatment group, indicating a negligible sys­
temic toxicity of CP-3. Additionally, hematoxylin–eosin staining of 
major organs after different treatments was also conducted and no 
obvious histopathological abnormalities was observed in the CP-3 
treated group (Fig. 7K). Finally, serum biochemical analyses were per­
formed to further evaluate the in vivo safety of CP-3. As shown in 
Fig. S10, no abnormalities were detected in the biochemical parameters 
of CP-3 treated mice, further supporting the favorable in vivo safety 
profile of CP-3. The above results demonstrated that CP-3 is a promising 
antibacterial agent for the treatment of bacterial infection with low 
toxicity in vivo.
4. Conclusion
In this study, a series of polymers with different quaternary phos­
phonium side chains were synthesized and evaluated for the treatment 
of bacterial infections. Among the synthesized polymers, the optimal 
polymer CP-3 (bearing tripropylphosphine side group) shown best 
antibacterial ability in vitro. CP-3 was found to bind anionic bacterial 
phospholipids POPG preferentially rather than the neutral phospho­
lipids DOPC present in mammalian membranes. This phospholipids 
selectivity ability of CP-3 was considered a principal driver of its potent 
antibacterial activity and low cytotoxicity. In addition, CP-3 effectively 
inhibits bacterial biofilm formation and disrupts mature bacterial bio­
films. In MRSA induced mouse lung infection and peritonitis, CP-3 
shown significant therapeutic ability while exhibiting negligible in 
vivo toxicity. This work provides a novel strategy for development of 
phosphonium-based polymer with potent antibacterial ability.

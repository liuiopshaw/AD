Single-atom nanozymes (SAzymes) are considered promising alternatives to 
natural enzymes. The catalytic performance of SAzymes featuring homoge-
neous, well-defined active structures can be enhanced through elucidating 
structure-activity relationship and tailoring physicochemical properties. How-
ever, manipulating enzymatic properties through structural variation is an 
underdeveloped approach. Herein, the synthesis of edge-rich Fe single-atom 
nanozymes (FeNC-edge) via an H2O2-mediated edge generation is reported. 
By controlling the number of edge sites, the peroxidase (POD)- and oxidase 
(OXD)-like performance is significantly enhanced. The activity enhancement 
results from the presence of abundant edges, which provide new anchoring 
sites to mononuclear Fe. Experimental results combined with density func-
tional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that FeN4 moieties in the edge sites 
display high electron density of Fe atoms and open N atoms. Finally, it is 
demonstrated that FeNC-edge nanozyme effectively inhibits tumor growth 
both in vitro and in vivo, suggesting that edge-tailoring is an efficient strategy 
for developing artificial enzymes as novel catalytic therapeutics.

behavior remains unclear.
Herein, we developed edge-rich Fe-based SAzymes where 
FeN4 moieties are embedded in the edge sites of N-doped 
mesoporous carbon nanoparticles (FeNC-edge) via an H2O2-
mediated edge generation. The introduction of edges to the 
carbon matrix and modification of the electron density at the 
Fe active center led to a remarkable enhancement of various 
enzymatic activities, including peroxidase (POD)-, oxidase 
(OXD)-, and catalase (CAT)-like activities. The POD- and OXD-
like catalytic efficiencies (kcat/Km) of FeNC-edge were 9.0- and 
1.3-fold higher, respectively, than those of its counterpart devoid 
of edge sites (FeNC). Density functional theory (DFT) calcula-
tions reveal that the activity enhancement results from the 
increased electron density of the Fe atom and open N atoms 
in edge-located active sites. We demonstrated that owing to its 
superior activities, FeNC-edge can effectively generate OH∙ 
and O2−∙ from both H2O2 and O2, disrupting cellular redox 
homeostasis and facilitating tumor apoptosis, leading to excel-
lent therapeutic effects both in vitro and in vivo. Unravelling 
the dependency of enzymatic activity on structural properties 
potentially opens up new avenues for the rational design of 
SAzymes as therapeutic agents for treating reactive oxygen spe-
cies (ROS)-related diseases and disorders, such as tumor and 
inflammation.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Synthesis and Structural Characterization of FeNC-Edge
N-doped mesoporous carbon nanoparticles (NC) were synthe-
sized via polymerization using phenol and melamine as the 
source of carbon and nitrogen, respectively, which was followed 
by pyrolysis under vacuum (Figure S1, Supporting Informa-
tion).[9] To engineer defects on the carbon support, H2O2-etching 
was performed,[3b,10] whereby H2O2 is decomposed to OH∙ 
under a hydrothermal process at 180 °C, and the highly reactive 
OH∙ can attach to and detach from the surface of mesoporous 
carbon, generating abundant edge sites (NC-edge) (Figure S2, 
Supporting Information).[11] The generated edges can serve as 
anchoring sites for Fe atoms because defective domains pos-
sess a lower work function, which enhances their reducing 
capacity in comparison with that of perfect hexagonal sites.[12a] 
Furthermore, the formation energy of FeN4-edge is lower than 
that of FeN4-plane, indicating that presence of graphitic edge 
facilitates the formation of Fe active sites at the edges.[12b] After 
the H2O2-treatment, Fe precursors were adsorbed onto the 
carbon matrix. Then, FeNC-edge was obtained after annealing 
at 900 °C under an argon atmosphere (Figure 1a). The trans-
mission electron microscopy (TEM) image of FeNC-edge shown 
in Figure 1b depicts the well-synthesized mesopore structure of 
the carbon matrix and the absence of aggregated Fe species. 
The high-angle annular dark-field imaging scanning transmis-
sion electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image (Figure  1c 
and Figure S3, Supporting Information) shows single Fe atoms 
atomically dispersed on the carbon support without aggre-
gation, which was further confirmed by elemental mapping 
using energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) (Figure  1d). Simi-
larly, edge-deficient FeNC also has a mesoporous structure and 
single-atom active sites, as confirmed by the TEM, HAADF-
STEM, and EDS results (Figures S4 and S5, Supporting Infor-
mation). Furthermore, in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns 
of both samples, only two carbon peaks were observed, and sig-
nals for iron or iron oxide nanoparticles did not appear (Figure S6, 
Supporting Information).
2.2. Edge Formation in Carbon Matrix through H2O2-Etching
The etching conditions were optimized by controlling the 
H2O2-treatment time to adjust the degree of defect forma-
tion (Figure S7, Supporting Information). First, we explored 
the edge formation mechanism. The X-ray photoelectron 
spectroscopy (XPS) analysis indicated that pyrrolic-N was 
dominant after the H2O2-etching (Figure S8, Supporting 
Information). Initially, OH∙ generated during the hydro-
thermal process was attached to the carbon support, forming 
pyridonic-N, the peak of which overlaps with the pyrrolic-N 
peak in the N 1s XPS spectrum.[13] However, after annealing 
at 900 °C, the intensity of the pyrrolic-N peak decreased due 
to the elimination of the O–H functional groups at high tem-
perature, which was confirmed by the O 1s XPS spectra. An 
increase in the oxygen content from 2.42 to 9.70 at % after 
the H2O2-etching was followed by a decrease to 4.43 at % after 
the annealing (Table S1, Supporting Information). On the 
other hand, the nitrogen content increased because carbon 
was etched and removed in the oxidative etching process, 
and therefore, the relative amount of nitrogen increased,[11,14] 
indicating the successful construction of edge sites (Table S2, 
Supporting Information). Furthermore, the population of N 
species in FeNC-edge changed considerably compared to that 
of FeNC. With increasing the treatment time, the percentage 
of defective nitrogen bonded with two carbon atoms at the 
edge sites of the carbon support (pyridinic- and pyrrolic-N) 
gradually increased, whereas the percentage of graphitic 
nitrogen decreased (Figure 2a,b and Figure S9, Supporting 
Information). In addition, the C 1s XPS spectra indicated 
that the defective carbon content also increased from 11% to

edge samples were evaluated based on equivalent amount of 
Fe active sites, catalyzing the oxidation of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylb-

activities among the synthesized nanozymes (NC, NC-edge,

Information). In the case of the center site: (1) the adsorbed 
H2O2 dissociates into 2OH* at the Fe site, (2) one OH* desorbs 
from the surface as an OH radical (OH∙), (3) the remaining 
OH* is protonated to form H2O, and (4) the produced H2O 
desorbs to complete the catalytic cycle. The rate determining 
step for the catalytic cycle at the center FeN4 sites is the desorp-
tion of OH*, requiring 0.94 eV. However, the edge FeN4 site 
offers edge N atoms as additional sites, and the catalytic cycle 
using these additional sites was found to be energetically more 
favorable. The edge N atoms were readily protonated, followed 
by the desorption of OH* and the formation and desorption 
of H2O. In this catalytic cycle, the formation of H2O* from 
co-adsorbed OH*-H* determines the overall rate, requiring 
0.83 eV, which is ≈0.1 eV more favorable compared to that of 
the center FeN4 site. It should be noted that the N atoms of the 
central FeN4 site are also capable of accommodating H atoms, 
but this reaction pathway has a significantly larger energy bar-
rier (2.17 eV) compared to that of the most favorable reaction 
pathway (Figure S25, Supporting Information). This difference 
in the reaction pathways was attributed to the high electron 
density of the N atoms at the edge sites (Figure 3f), which facil-
itates electron donation to the H+ ion and enables the occur-
rence of a new reaction pathway at the edge FeN4 sites. We also 
investigated the mechanism of CAT-like activity.[20] Similar to 
the POD-like reaction, we demonstrated that FeNC-edge has 
lower energy barriers for CAT-like reaction, which is in agree-
ment with the experimental results (Figure S26, Supporting 
Information).
By engineering the local coordination environment and 
electronic structure of the Fe center, the enzymatic activity of 
SAzymes was significantly enhanced. This finding indicates 
that well-defined SAzymes can be modified rationally, and the 
established structure-activity relationship can be harnessed to 
develop novel catalytic-bio therapeutics.
2.5. Catalytic Therapeutic Efficacy of FeNC-Edge In Vitro 
and In Vivo
Encouraged by the outstanding enzymatic activity of FeNC-
edge, this nanozyme was investigated as an effective thera-
peutic agent at the cellular level. First, for biocompatibility, 
FeNC-edge was PEGylated with DSPE-PEG via hydrophobic 
interactions. The number-average hydrodynamic diameter was 
109.6 ± 19.6 nm, as determined based on dynamic light scat-
tering analysis (Figure S27, Supporting Information). TEM 
image of PEGylated FeNC-edge and the change in the zeta 
potential and the Fourier transform infrared (FT–IR) spectra 
indicated successful PEGylation (Figures S28, S29, and S30, 
Supporting Information). Hereinafter, all of the nanozymes dis-
cussed refer to PEGylated nanozymes, unless otherwise stated. 
After 3 h of incubation with Cy3-labeled FeNC-edge, confocal 
laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images were collected, 
which indicated efficient cellular uptake of the nanoparticles 
by endocytosis and colocalization into lysosome (Figure S31, 
Supporting Information). As shown in Figure 5a, FeNC-edge 
strongly inhibited tumor cell viability, while edge-deficient 
FeNC showed moderate cytotoxicity. The superior cytotoxicity 
of FeNC-edge was further confirmed by live/dead cell staining 
analysis (Figure 5b). To elucidate the difference in cytotoxicity, 
intracellular ROS levels were measured using 2′,7′-dichloro-
dihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) as an ROS detector 
(Figure 5c,d). The elevated fluorescence intensity in the FeNC-
edge group indicated tremendous intracellular oxidative stress, 
which is the principal cause of its superior cytotoxicity. Due 
to acidic specificity, FeNC-edge catalytically generated ROS 
in acidic lysosome, inducing lysosomal membrane permea-
bilization (Figure S32, Supporting Information). After that, 
the increased intracellular ROS oxidized the lipid membrane 
(Figure S33, Supporting Information) and depolarized the 
membrane potential of mitochondria (Figure  5e,f), thus trig-
gering severe apoptosis. In the case of Fe-free nanozymes, the 
cytotoxicity and intracellular ROS generation were much lower 
than those of nanozymes containing Fe centers (Figures S34 
and S35, Supporting Information), indicating that FeN4 sites 
are responsible for the enzymatic activity, which is consistent 
with the results discussed above.
To assess the therapeutic efficacy in vivo, we first investi-
gated the bio-distribution of nanoparticles administrated intra-
venously. FeNC-edge was mainly distributed in lung, liver, and 
spleen, and some of nanoparticles were accumulated in tumor 
24 h post-injection (Figure S36, Supporting Information). 
Next, we evaluated the therapeutic effect in animal models. 
FeNC-edge was administrated by intravenous injection 0, 3, 
and 7 d after establishing the 4T1-tumor-bearing mice model. 
Significant tumor growth inhibition was observed in the FeNC-
edge mice compared to the control and FeNC-treated group 
(Figure 5g). The tumor growth inhibition rate of FeNC-edge was 
calculated to be 85%, which is higher than that of FeNC (38%) 
(Figure 5h). Their therapeutic efficacy was further corroborated 
by the image of tumor tissues and TUNEL-stained tumor tissue 
after the whole treatment (Figure S37, Supporting Informa-
tion). We studied the biocompatibility of FeNC-edge by moni-
toring the body weight of the mice throughout the treatment. 
The body weights of mice in all groups did not change signifi-
cantly, implying non-toxicity (Figure 5i). In addition, the H&E 
staining images of the main organs did not differ significantly 
between the control and FeNC-edge groups in both Balb/C 
nude mice model and C57BL/6 mice model (Figure S38, Sup-
porting Information). For serum chemistry, various functional 
markers were analyzed and no visible toxicity was observed 
after the treatment (Figure S39, Supporting Information). 
The elimination blood half-life (t(1/2)) was found to be 0.38 h 
(Figure S40, Supporting Information), further confirming the 
biosafety of FeNC-edge. For clinical translation, future work

3. Conclusions
Single-atom catalysts have homogeneous and well-defined 
active centers on a molecular level, which is rarely found in 
traditional heterogeneous catalysts.[3a,4c,d] This unique property 
allows for their structural fine-tuning to maximize the catalytic 
performance.[5a,c,d] However, this property has rarely been con-
sidered for optimizing the potency of catalytic therapeutics. 
Therefore, we adopted a defect engineering strategy to enhance 
the multi-enzymatic activity of SAzymes and establish the rela-
tionships between structure and bio-catalytic activity. Applying 
an H2O2-mediated edge generation, we developed edge-rich 
Fe-based SAzymes, which displayed outstanding POD-, OXD-, 
and CAT-like activities. The introduction of abundant edge 
sites increases the electron density of the Fe active center at the 
edges, facilitating electron transfer, and the open N sites offer 
an energy-favorable new reaction route, as confirmed by experi-
mental results and DFT calculations. The superior enzymatic 
activity of FeNC-edge achieved highly effective tumor inhibi-
tion in a 4T1-tumor-bearing mice model compared with that of 
edge-deficient SAzymes. These findings provide guidance for 
the rational design of SAzymes by revealing the dependency 
of enzymatic activity on structural properties, thus enabling 
researchers to develop high-performance artificial enzymes 
with significant potential for replacing traditional therapeutics 
in practical applications.
4. Experimental Section
Chemicals: All reagents and solvents were obtained commercially 
and used without further purification. Melamine, phenol, Pluronic F-127, 
formaldehyde solution (37 wt% in H2O), iron (III) chloride hexahydrate, 
hydrogen peroxide (30 wt% in H2O), 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide, 
Thiazolyl Blue Tetrazolium Bromide, and 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein 
diacetate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). 
Sodium hydroxide (beads, 98.0%), ethanol (99. 9%, anhydrous), and 
methanol (99.8%) were purchased from Samchun Chemical (Seoul, 
Korea).
Synthesis of N–Doped Mesoporous Carbon Nanoparticles (NC): We 
modified our previous method slightly for the synthesis of FeNC-edge.[9] 
In a typical synthesis of the standard catalyst, phenol (0.6 g), melamine 
(2.4 g), and aqueous NaOH (0.1 M, 15 mL) were mixed and stirred. 
After the solution was heated to 50 °C, formaldehyde solution (4.2 mL, 
37 wt%) was added and the mixture was stirred at 70 °C for 30 min. Then, 
Pluronic F127 (0.96 g) dissolved in distilled water (15 mL) was added, 
and the resulting mixture was stirred at 66  °C for 2 h. Distilled water 
(50 mL) was then added to dilute the solution, and heating was stopped 
after 17 h at 66 °C. After cooling to room temperature, the resulting 
solution (13.5 mL) was diluted with distilled water (57.5 mL), transferred 
to a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 120 °C for 24 h. The resulting 
polymer was washed with distilled water three times via vacuum 
filtration and dried under vacuum. Carbonization was performed under 
vacuum. For sufficient soft-template removal, a stepwise carbonization 
process (5 °C min-1 up to 350 °C, 2 h aging, 5 °C min-1 up to 600 °C, 
and 1 °C min-1 up to 900 °C, 1 h aging) was applied.
Synthesis of Fe SAzymes: For FeNC-edge synthesis, N–doped 
mesoporous carbon nanoparticles (90 mg) were dispersed in DI 
water (100 mL). Then, H2O2 (500 µL) was added and the mixture was 
transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 180 °C for 16 h. 
After cooling to room temperature, FeCl3·6H2O (727 µl of 3 mg ml-1 
in DI water) was added and the mixture was stirred under room 
temperature for 24 h. The mixture was then dried at 100 °C in an oven 
overnight. Subsequently, the dried powder was annealed at 900 °C for 
1 h under an argon atmosphere. FeNC was obtained using the same 
procedure, omitting the H2O2 treatment. NC-edge were obtained 
using same procedure as that for FeNC-edge without the addition of 
FeCl3·6H2O.
Characterization: Transmission electron microscopy images were 
obtained using a JEM-2100 (JEOL) microscope operated at 200 kV. 
EDS mapping was conducted in the STEM mode employing a single 
drift detector (X-MAXN, Oxford Instruments). Cs-corrected scanning 
transmission microscopy (Cs-STEM) imaging was performed at 200 kV 
using a spherical aberration-corrected JEM ARM-200F microscope (Cold 
FEG Type, JEOL), installed at the National Center for Inter-University 
Research Facilities at Seoul National University. X-ray diffraction (XRD) 
patterns were obtained using a SmartLab (Rigaku) diffractometer with 
Cu Kα radiation. The Fe content was characterized employing inductively 
coupled plasma–absorption emission microscopy (OPTIMA 8300, 
Perkin-Elmer). The XPS measurements were conducted using a K-Alpha+ 
XPS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at the Korea Basic Science 
Institute (KBSI). The X-ray source was 1,486.6 eV monochromated Al-Kα 
radiation. ESR measurements were performed at 10 K using a Bruker 
EMX plus 6/1 spectrometer operating in the X-band (9.64 GHz) and 
100 kHz magnetic field modulation (modulation amplitude, 10 G). The 
temperature was controlled using an Oxford Instrument ESR900 liquid 
He cryostat with an Oxford ITC 503 temperature controller. Spectra were 
recorded at 1 mW microwave power. Magnetometry experiment were 
performed at KBSI using a MPMS 3 (Quantum design) under 2 K. The 
data was normalized to iron contents.
X-Ray Absorption Spectroscopy Measurements: XAFS spectra were 
acquired using the 8C nano-probe XAFS beamline (BL8C) of Pohang 
Light Source II. The incident beam was monochromatized by a Si 
(111) crystal and detuned by 30% to minimize higher harmonics. 
Energy calibration was performed using a Fe reference foil. XAFS 
spectra were collected in both transmission and fluorescence modes; 
however, since fluorescence was prone to self-absorption effects, only 
transmission mode results were discussed. The obtained spectra 
were processed using Demeter software.[21] Fourier transforms of the 
theoretical calculations were performed using the ARTEMIS program. 
The theoretical calculation of the phase shifts and back scattering 
amplitudes for specific atom pairs was obtained using the FEFF[22] 
based on the structures optimized by the DFT calculations (for 
details see computation section). The S02 parameter was determined 
from the fit of the Fe standard and fixed for the EXAFS fits. The 
theoretical models used for FeNC and FeNC-edge were typical for 
FeNC structures. The EXAFS spectrum was modelled over a Fourier 
transform range of 3 – 9 Å-1 with a Hanning window applied between 
1.0 and 2.9 Å. Additionally, to avoid over-parametrization, multi-edge 
fitting was used, and Nyquist criterion was considered for the EXAFS 
fits. Wavelet transforms were obtained with HAMA Fortran code, using 
Morlet wavelet (ĸ = 7, σ = 1).[23]
Evaluation of Peroxidase- and Oxidase-Like Activities: POD- and OXD-
like activities of nanozymes were measured using TMB as a substrate 
at 25 °C under acidic condition (pH 5.5, sodium acetate buffer). The 
absorbance of oxTMB was recorded at 652 nm using a PerkinElmer 
Victor X4 multiple plate reader. In the case of POD-like activity, 
absorption was measured in the presence of H2O2. For kinetic assays 
of FeNC and FeNC-edge, the experiments were conducted in reaction 
solutions containing different concentrations of H2O2 or TMB. The 
absorbance at 652 nm was recorded to obtain the reaction-time curve. 
The initial rate of change in absorbance was acquired by calculating the 
slope of the initial linear portion of the reaction-time curve. A Michaelis-
Menten curve was obtained by plotting the calculated initial rate as a 
function of the substrate concentrations. For poisoning the Fe active 
sites by SCN−, after co-incubation of FeNC-edge and SCN− for 10 min, 
TMB or TMB and H2O2 were added, and the absorption of oxTMB was

Cell Viability Assay: HeLa cells were seeded in 96-well plates with a 
density of 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, 
the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing nanozymes 
of different concentrations. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, the 
supernatant was removed and 100 µL of MTT solution was added into 
each well and after another 2 h of incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm 
was obtained using a PerkinElmer Victor X4 multiple plate reader.
Live/Dead Cell Staining: HeLa cells were seeded in glass-bottom cell 
culture dishes and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the medium was 
replaced with fresh medium containing 2.5 µg mL-1 (Fe concentration) 
of FeNC or FeNC-edge. After incubation of 24 h, HeLa cells were rinsed 
with PBS twice, followed by staining with calcein AM and PI (Thermo 
Fisher Scientific). The resulting cells were observed by confocal laser 
scanning microscope (CLSM).
Intracellular ROS Assay: The ROS generation was monitored using 
2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) probe. Briefly, 
HeLa cells were incubated with 0.16 µg mL-1 (Fe concentration) of 
FeNC or FeNC-edge for 3 h. The cells were stained by H2DCFDA and 
incubated for 20 min at 37 °C, followed by washing with PBS for twice. 
The fluorescence signals were measured by CLSM.
Mitochondria Membrane Depolarization Analysis: HeLa cells were 
seeded in glass-bottom culture dishes for 24 h. Subsequently, the 
medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 1.56 µg mL-1 
(Fe concentration) of FeNC or FeNC-edge. After incubation for 24 h, 
cells were washed with PBS twice, followed by staining with JC-1 Dye 
(Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Probe) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 
The resulting cells were observed by CLSM.
In Vivo Therapeutic Efficacy of FeNC-Edge: This study was approved 
by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC; No. SNU-
210729-3) and was performed in accordance with IACUC guidelines of 
Seoul National University and the National Institute of Health Guide for 
the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The researchers who carried 
out surgery, managed the animal, and measured the tumor volume were 
blinded each other. 8-week-old immune-deficient mice (male Balb/c 
nude mice) were anesthetized with 1.5/2% isoflurane/oxygen (v/v). 
Then, the 4T1-Luc2 (PerkinElmer Inc., USA) cells were subcutaneously 
implanted (1 × 106 cells/60 µL matrigel/mouse) near the back of the 
nude mice for the development of tumor model. After establishing of 
tumor-bearing mice model, phosphate buffer saline, 15 mg kg-1 of FeNC, 
and FeNC-edge were administrated three times for 14 d by intravenous 
injection into control, FeNC, and FeNC-edge group, respectively. To 
evaluate therapeutic efficacy, we used the bioluminescence imaging 
(BLI) technique. For the in vivo BLI analyses, live mice were imaged 
simultaneously for 5 min using the IVIS system, and the regions of 
interest were quantified with photon flux (p/s) using Living Image 
software v4.7.3 (PerkinElmer, USA).

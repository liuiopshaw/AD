A B S T R A C T   
Photocatalysis is reported to be an effective method for the removal of organic toxic pollutant present in the 
environmental waters and industrial effluents. NiMoO4/Ag2MoO4 nanocluster (NMO/AMO) was synthesized for 
its multiple applications such as photocatalytic property under visible light irradiation and antimicrobial activity. 
The structure and the morphological features of NMO/AMO was determined by high-resolution transmission 
electron microscope (HRTEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), fourier-transfer infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), photo­
luminescence (PL), energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDAX), BET isotherm and UV–visible diffuse reflec­
tance spectroscopy (UV–vis DRS). EDAX and XRD confirm the formation of NMO/AMO and its crystalline nature. 
PL of NMO/AMO showed that the intensity was lower than individual AMO and NMO, which indicates its 
reduced recombination of charge carriers. Effective photodegradation of methylene blue (MB) dye was noticed 
by using NMO/AMO. NMO/AMO nanocluster showed 84.5% degradation at 1st cycle and 82.2% at 6th cycle 
which confirm its reusability. Here, the photocatalytic activity was enhanced mainly due to h+ and OH., and it 
follows pseudo first order kinetics. The antimicrobial activity of NMO/AMO nanocluster, AMO and NMO 
nanoparticles were performed against Escherichia coli and Bacillus subtilis at different concentrations. The NMO/ 
AMO nanocluster showed effective antimicrobial activity than the individual AMO and NMO nanoparticles. 
Hence, the fabricated NMO/AMO nanocluster could be used as an effective candidate for high potential envi­
ronmental application such has industrial water treatment, antibacterial agent etc.   
1. Introduction 
Recent studies focused on the removal of pollutants from water 
sources which is one of the major problems that demand attention. 
Nanoparticles (NPs) were widely used for variety of applications for 
environmental remediation including pollutant detection, pollutant 
removing, antimicrobial agents etc., [1–8]. Organic dye bearing efflu­
ents was considered to be a major contaminant that poses hazardous 
effect to environment, human and other living organisms. Most of the 
organic dye pollutants originated from the effluents of textile, foodstuff, 
and leather industries [9]. The organic dye effluent causes the restriction 
of sunlight to reach inside the water bodies and it also increased the 
biological oxygen demand [10]. Some organic dyes are carcinogenic and 
toxic in nature [11,12]. Various conventional methods are available for 
the removal of pollutants such as physicochemical methods including 
chemical precipitation, electro-coagulation, and activated carbon 
[13–15]. The available methods for the removal of organic dye pollutant 
were not effective for the complete removal of dyes. The conventional 
techniques result in conversion of organic dye into other forms and the 
formed by-product may cause increased toxicity. Hence, it is necessary 
to adopt a new method for the effective removal of toxic organic dye for 
complete mineralization. 
One of the important methods for the removal of the organic dye was 
photocatalytic degradation under visible light irradiation. The photo­
catalysis is an effective method that not only decolorizes the dye effluent 
but also for the complete removal of toxic compound [16,17].

Photocatalysis have high potential applications as it uses solar light as 
energy source [18–20]. The catalysts mainly made up of transition metal 
complexes or semiconductor is being studied recently [21,22]. The 
heterogeneous catalytic material forms a solid-state support typically 
include organic polymers porous ordered materials and amorphous sil­
ica, such as mesoporous silicates or zeolites and more recently, 
metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) [23–30]. The heterogeneous catalyst 
pose better efficiency in photocatalytic performance compared to ho­
mogeneous catalyst due to enhanced charge separation [31–36]. 
The heterogeneous catalyst of type XMO-XMO (X = Ag, Ba, Ni) were 
reported for several applications including excellent photocatalytic 
degradation, inactivation of the metabolic process of the microbes, etc. 
[37]. The band gap energy of NiMoO4 NPs was reported to be in the 
range of 2.88–3.18 [38–40] and for Ag2MoO4 NPs it is in the range of 
3.26–4.39 eV [41–43]. The present investigation focused on the syn­
thesis of NiMoO4/Ag2MoO4 nanocluster to achieve narrow band gap 
energy that facilitates enhanced visible light harvesting. Silver possess 
enhanced antimicrobial activity, so the prepared probe can also be used 
as an antimicrobial active material [44]. Thus, the present work offered 
on the evaluation of photocatalytic and antimicrobial activity of the 
prepared Ag2MoO4/NiMoO4 nanocluster and its associated catalytic 
mechanism. 
2. Experiment 
2.1. Material 
NaOH, NiCl2, Na2MoO4, AgNO3, EDTA, methylene blue, isopropyl 
alcohol, formic acid and benzoquinone were obtained from Merk, 
German. All the chemical are in analytical grade and used directly. 
2.2. Catalyst preparation 
2.2.1. Preparation of NMO nanoparticle 
The NiMO4 nanoparticle was synthesized by reflux condensation 
method as follow. To the 0.1 M of NiCl2 solution (50 mL), Na2MoO4 of 
0.1 M was mixed in magnetic stirrer for 15 min. Thereafter, 25 mL of 0.4 
M of NaOH was added under stirring and heat at 80 ◦C for 15 min. Then, 
the solution was refluxed at 100 ◦C for 1 h. The solution was centrifuged 
and the pellet was collected and the pellet was washed with both water 
and ethanol twice. Then, the pellet was dried in a hot air oven. The 
synthesized NMO nanoparticles were calcinated using a muffle furnace 
for 60 min at 500 ◦C. 
2.2.2. Preparation of NMO/AMO nanocluster 
The NMO/AMO nanocluster was prepared as follow. The prepared 
NMO NPs (0.1 M) was weighed and added to 50 mL Na2MoO4 (0.1 M) 
solution. AgNO3 (0.2 M) was added to the above suspension and the 
mixture was stirred for 10 min. Then, the reaction mixture was sonicated 
for 15 min using an ultrasonic processor (Sonics,USA). Thereafter, the 
solution was centrifuged and washed twice with both water and ethanol. 
The pellet was dried in hot air oven to remove the moisture in the pellet. 
AMO particles was prepared by same procedure without NMO and 
ultrasonication. 
2.3. Characterization of NMO/AMO nanocluster 
The formation of NMO/AMO nanocluster was confirmed by the X- 
ray diffraction pattern analyzed by using Bruker D8 Advance. The 
morphology and the structure of the nanocluster were studied by high- 
resolution transmission electron microscopy (Jeol/JEM 2100) and 
Scanning Electron microscopy (Carl Zeiss microscopy ltd, UK). The 
elemental analysis of the NMO/AMO nanocluster was analyzed by EDAX 
(Carl-Zeiss, Germany). The vibrational stretching of the prepared sample 
was studied by the Fourier-Transfer Infrared Spectroscopy (Thermo 
Fisher Scientific Nicolet IS50 machine). The isotherm, pore size and pore 
volume of the nanoparticles were studied by BET surface area analyzer 
(Quantachrome Instrument, Autosorb IQ series). The UV–visible DRS 
analysis was performed to study the absorption and reflection spectra of 
the solid NMO/AMO nanocluster (Perkin–Elmer Lambda 750). The 
photoluminescence of the NMO/AMO nanocluster was studied by using 
photoluminescence spectrophotometer (FLS 1000, Edinburgh In­
struments). The chemical composition and oxidation state of the ele­
ments were studied by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Thermo-VG 
Scientific Escalab 250 spectrometer). 
2.4. Experimental procedure for the photocatalytic degeneration of MB 
dye 
The photocatalytic effect of NMO/AMO nanocluster was studied by 
MB dye degradation under the irradiation of 500 W tungsten halogen 
lamp. The prepared NMO, AMO and NMO/AMO nanocluster (3 mg) was 
sonicated in 20 mL water for 45 min. Then the sonicated nanomaterial 
solution of 20 mL was mixed with 10 mg/L of MB dye and the solution 
was kept under the visible light irradiation. Small aliquot from the re­
action mixture was taken in every 20 min time intervals and the 
UV–visible absorption spectra were recorded in the range of 500–750 
nm. The efficiency of the degradation was calculated using the equ. 1

The reusability of the NMO/AMO nanocluster was studied in detail

ability study of the photocatalyst was performed by the collecting the

In order to understand the photocatalytic mechanism, the free

EDTA (1 mM), IPA (0.2 mL alcohol/20 mL dye) and benzoquinone (1 
mM) to the photocatalytic reaction mixture separately. 
2.5. Antimicrobial activity 
The antimicrobial activity of the NMO/AMO nanocluster was per­
formed against E. coli and B. subtilis. The bacterial culture was inoculated 
in sterilized LB broth and incubated at 37 ◦C for 18 h. Thereafter, the 
culture was centrifuged and the pellet was washed with 0.75 mM of 
saline water. The bacterial cell number was adjusted to 1 × 108 CFU/mL 
and interacted with different concentrations of particles. The samples 
were interacted for 4 h, thereafter, the number of colony forming unit 
left in the interaction mixture was determined by dilution plate count 
method. The antimicrobial efficiency was estimated by calculating 
percentage of relative inhibition as given in Equ. 3.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Characterization 
3.1.1. XRD 
The formation of NMO/AMO nanocluster and crystalline nature were 
primarily characterized by XRD analysis. Fig. 1a shows the XRD analysis 
of the AMO, NMO and NMO/AMO nanocluster. The XRD peaks at 18.7◦, 
33.6◦, 37.9◦and 59.2◦indicates the NiMoO4 plane and the spectrum at 
16.69◦, 26.57◦, 34.73◦, 47.27◦and 55.30◦confirms the Ag2MoO4 lattice 
plane. XRD spectrum of NMO/AMO nanocluster showed both the NMO 
and AMO nanoparticles peak, which indicates the NMO/AMO nano­
cluster formation. The results showed the nanocluster didn’t show major 
difference in the peak, which indicates that the NMO did not enter the 
AMO lattice crystal plane. 
The elemental analysis was performed to confirm the presence of Ni, 
Mo, O and Ag in the synthesized particle. The EDAX analysis of the 
NMO/AMO nanocluster was shown in Fig. 1b. The results showed the 
presence of Ni, Ag, O, Mo in the prepared nanocluster.

3.2. Photocatalytic activity 
3.2.1. Photocatalytic activity of AMO and NMO nanoparticles 
The photodegradation of MB by AMO and NMO nanoparticles was 
estimated separately under the irradiation of visible light. The UV–vi­
sible absorption spectrum of photocatalytic degradation of MB dye by 
NMO/AMO nanocluster is shown in Fig. 5a. The degradation efficiency 
was calculated to be 58.7% and 56.8% in 180 min by AMO and NMO 
nanoparticles respectively, whereas the photocatalytic degradation of 
MB dye by NMO/AMO nanocluster was 84.5%. The degradation of MB 
dye by NMO/AMO nanocluster was 1.4 times greater than AMO and 
NMO nanoparticle. Fig. 5b shows the comparison of the half life period 
of MB by AMO, NMO and NMO/MNO nanocluster. The half life period of 
MB by AMO and NMO was found to 145 min and 135 min, whereas the 
half life period was 70 min by NMO/AMO (Fig. 5b). The results showed 
that the catalytic property was efficient after the formation of hetero­
junction than individual nanoparticle. 
The kinetic of photocatalytic activity of NMO/AMO nanocluster for 
the degradation of MB dye was calculated (Fig. 5c). The degradation rate 
of AMO, NMO and NMO/AMO was 0.0794, 0.0315 and 0.1927 min−1 
respectively. The degradation rate of NMO/AMO nanocluster was 2.42 
and 6.11 times greater than AMO and NMO nanoparticle. The regression 
coefficient of the nanocatalyst was 0.95, thus, the nanocatalyst follows 
the pseudo first order kinetics.

3.3. Reusability 
The reusability of the NMO/AMO nanocluster was studied by per­
forming six cycle test. Here, the photocatalytic activity was performed 
by using the same catalyst, the nanoparticle was collected from each 
cycle and the particle was used for the next cycle. The photodegradation 
efficiency of NMO/AMO nanocluster for six cycles was displayed in 
Fig. 6a. The photocatalytic degradation of the NMO/AMO nanocluster 
was 84.5%, 84%, 83.7%, 83.4%, 83.1% and 82.1% for the six consec­
utive cycles. The photocatalytic activity of NMO/AMO nanocluster after 
six cycle was almost similar first, indicates its stability. The comparison 
of photocatalytic activity and reusability of the present work with other 
research work is shown in Table 2. Dutta et al. [49] studied the photo­
catalytic activity of FeSe nanoparticle and they received 88% degrada­
tion and 93.1% of reusability efficiency. The present study shows the 
enhanced photocatalytic activity of nanocluster with effective degra­
dation of 84.5% and 97.6% of reusability efficiency under visible light 
irradiation. 
3.4. Structural stability 
The structural stability is an essential factor for the photocatalytic 
activity of a nanomaterial. The structural stability of NMO/AMO 
nanocluster was determined by XRD analysis after 6th cycle. The XRD 
peaks and its intensity of the NMO/AMO before 1st cycle and after 6th 
cycle were remained almost same (Fig. 6b). The results showed that the 
particle didn’t undergo photocorrosion and the particle was stable even 
after six cycles. SEM image of NMO/AMO nanocluster after sixth cycle 
was taken and it is shown in Fig. 3b. XRD and SEM image which confirm 
the stability of reused nanocluster. 
3.5. Scavenging 
The mechanism of photocatalytic activity of NMO/AMO nanocluster 
was determined by radical scavenging assay. Fig. 6c shows the scav­
enging study of free radicals during photocatalytic degradation of MB 
dye. The scavenging of free radicals was performed by adding AgNO3, 
EDTA, IPA and benzoquinone to the photocatalytic reaction mixture. 
The addition of AgNO3, benzoquinone, IPA and EDTA resulted in 
reduced the MB degradation as 73.3%, 76%, 33.9% 74.2% and 30.1% 
respectively. The results showed that O2−. and e−had been scavenged 
almost equally and hydroxy radicals and holes were responsible for the 
photocatalytic activity of NMO/AMO nanocluster. 
3.6. Photocatalytic mechanism of NMO/AMO nanocluster

dation of MB dye by NMO/AMO nanocluster. The NMO/AMO nano­
cluster is fabricated by transmission metal and semiconductor which 
contains valance band and conduction band electrons. The mechanism 
of the degradation is explained as follow. The effective degradation of 
MB dye by h+ and e-pair was determined by the band edge position of 
the direct migration of the photodegraded electron and hole. Here, the 
conduction band and valance band were determined by Mulliken elec­
tronegativity. The valance band (VB) and conduction band (CB) of AMO 
were −0.29 eV and 3.1 eV respectively, and the valance band and 
conduction band of NMO were 0.4 eV and 2.9 eV respectively. Here, the 
electron in the valance bond excited to the conduction band when it gets 
irradiated under visible light. The conduction band electron from the 
AMO will transfer to NMO in order to balance the Fermi level between 
AMO and NMO nanoparticle. Here, the ECB of NMO (+0.43 eV vs NHE) is 
less negative than O2/.O2−(−0.33eV), the photogenerated electron on 
the surface of NMO cannot react with dissolve O2 to generate .O2 [50].

and the h+ possess a strong oxidation ability to degrade the organic 
pollutant owing to its high potential [51]. The EVB of NMO (2.9 eV vs

3.7. Antimicrobial activity 
The antimicrobial activity of NMO/AMO nanocluster was evaluated 
against E. coli and B. subtilis and the result is shown in Fig. 8. The per­
centage of inhibition of NMO nanoparticle on E. coli was 6.2% for 0.1 
mg/L and 25.9% for 50 mg/L and on B. subtilis was 5.12% for 0.1 mg/L, 
21% for 50 mg/L. The percentage of relative inhibition of AMO 
nanoparticle on E. coli was 8.7% for 0.1 mg/L and 42% for 50 mg/L and 
on B. subtilis was 7.9% for 0.1 mg/L and 32.5% for 50 mg/L. The % of 
inhibition of NMO/AMO nanocluster was calculated to be 9.3% for 0.1 
mg/L and 75.2% for 50 mg/L on E. coli and 9.5% for 0.1 mg/L and 
66.5% for 50 mg/L on B. subtilis. Here, the heterojunction formation 
showed effective antimicrobial activity than individual AMO and NMO 
particles. Table 3 show the comparison of antimicrobial activity of 
NMO/AMO nanocluster with other research work. The study suggests 
the multifunctional application of the prepared photocatalyst. 
4. Conclusions 
NMO/AMO nanocluster was fabricated by sonochemical method and 
the formation of the nanocluster was confirmed by XRD, EDX and XPS 
analysis. The photodegradation of the nanocluster was found to be 
effective and it showed the MB degradation of 84.5% in 120 min. The 
nanocluster exhibited efficient reusability and excellent photostability. 
In addition, the nanocluster showed effective antimicrobial activity 
against E. coli and B. subtilis. The results indicated that the formation of 
heterojunction increased its photocatalytic and antimicrobial proper­
ties. The prepared nanocluster can be used effectively for the degrada­
tion of organic dye from the industrial effluents.

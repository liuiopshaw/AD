A B S T R A C T   
Efforts to expedite the development of novel antimicrobial agents without drug resistance are imperative to 
combat drug-resistant bacterial infections. In this study, Cu nanoclusters (NCs) with aggregation-induced 
emission (AIE) were designed by manipulating the pH to control the aggregation of Cu(I) complexes on the 
surface of a Cu(0) core. The resulting Cu NCs exhibited excellent photodynamic antimicrobial activity against a 
wide range of bacteria, particularly methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), through a synergistic 
interplay of intrinsic and photodynamic antimicrobial mechanisms. This was achieved by the copious generation 
of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that are lethal to bacteria. In addition to that, these Cu NCs displayed minimal 
cytotoxicity and excellent biocompatibility, enabling the promoted healing of MRSA-infected wounds in mice 
through eliminating bacterial infection, and alleviating inflammation without causing systemic harm or 
impairing blood and liver functions. This study represents the first successful development of AIE-featured Cu 
NCs-based photodynamic antimicrobials, which may shed light on the design of other AIE-featured metal NCs, 
contribute to a better understanding of NC’s luminescence fundamentals, and add fuels to the development of 
metal NCs-based nanomedicine.   
1. Introduction 
Luminescent metal nanoclusters (NCs) with metal cores smaller than 
3 nm have emerged as a novel class of luminophores due to their ul­
trasmall size [1–4], customizable photoluminescence (PL) [5,6], 
controllable surface chemistry [7–9], and favorable biocompatibility 
[10,11]. These novel materials have found widespread applications in 
sensing [5,12,13], imaging [14–16], disease diagnosis [17,18], and 
optoelectronics [19–22]. The understanding of the PL fundamentals of 
metal NCs has fueled the creation of various luminescent metal NCs that 
address critical challenges across diverse disciplines [23]. One notable 
advancement is the introduction of aggregation-induced emission (AIE, 
pioneered by Tang’s group in 2001) to metal NCs to investigate their PL 
properties and expand their application territory [24]. Traditional 
luminophores, while displaying excellent PL properties in dilute solu­
tions, suffer from aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ) at high con­
centrations [25,26], greatly curtailing their applications in biological 
systems. In comparison, AIE-typed luminophores are nonemissive or 
weakly emissive in dilute solutions but exhibit intensive emission in the 
aggregate state [27]. Their working fundamentals has been clearly 
identified to be the restriction of intramolecular motion (RIM), which 
resulted in configuration rigidification with the suppression of non­
radiative decay pathways, contributing to PL enhancement [28]. The 
development of AIE has significantly improved materials design and its 
applications in precision medicine and light-based healthcare [29]. For 
example, by modulating the aggregation level of M(I)-ligand motifs on 
the metal NCs’ surface (M denotes metal), a range of luminescent Au, 
Ag, and their alloy NCs with AIE characteristics have been synthesized 
[30–33]. These NCs exhibit strong PL, low background noise, prolonged 
PL duration, fair photostability, biocompatibility, large Stokes shift, and 
higher temporospatial resolution, making them highly appealing for 
biomedical applications such as bioimaging and photo-involved therapy 
[10,34–36].

Au and Ag and belongs to the same group in the periodic table of ele­
ments, Cu NCs with comparable properties to Au/Ag analogues but at a

NCs has been hindered by the metal’s susceptibility to oxidation and the 
lack of suitable synthetic methods, including the design of AIE-featured 
Cu NCs and the manipulation of their PL [38]. Consequently, there is an 
urgent need to design AIE-featured Cu NCs with adjustable PL properties

also for expanding the repertoire of metal NCs to cater to diverse

In recent years, various antimicrobial nanomaterials have been 
developed for different antimicrobial scenarios [39–42]. Among them, 
metal NCs, particularly Au and Ag NCs, have shown promise as valuable 
alternatives for combating drug-resistant microbes, demonstrating 
broad-spectrum microbial killing capabilities without inducing drug 
resistance [3,43–45]. This contrasts with the limitations of antibiotics, 
which are often confined to narrow-spectrum bactericidal attribute and 
prone to triggering microbial drug resistance. However, the progress of 
NCs-based antimicrobials is hindered by several challenges. Firstly, the 
scarcity and high cost of Au (over 6500 times more expensive than Cu) 
and Ag (more than 72 times pricier than Cu) restrict the widespread 
application of Au and Ag NCs in antimicrobial settings. Secondly, the 
antimicrobial mechanism of these NCs primarily involves the release of 
antimicrobial species or self-consumption through cell endocytosis, 
limiting their efficacy in prolonged and multi-mode antimicrobial sce­
narios [46]. Moreover, existing research on NCs-based antimicrobials 
predominantly focuses on assessing their antimicrobial efficacy against 
common bacteria, with a dearth of studies on combating drug-resistant 
bacteria in vivo [47]. In addition, in contrast to several emerging AIE- 
featured Au NCs and AuAg NCs showing great efficacy in long-lasting 
photodynamically antimicrobial applications [31,43], AIE-featured Cu 
NCs as photodynamic antimicrobials are seldomly applied for both in 
vitro and in vivo antimicrobial applications due to the challenging syn­
thesis process [38,48]. Of note, the AIE property of metal NCs plays a 
crucial role in providing long-lasting antimicrobial activity through a 
photodynamic bactericidal route rather than a self-consumption- 
mediated bactericidal pathway [43]. This property also significantly 
increases the photodynamic antimicrobial efficiency by enhancing light 
absorption, promoting the generation and separation of charge carriers, 
and facilitating surface bactericidal reactions with minimal side effects, 
such as dosage-dependent cytotoxicity and metabolic burden [10,49]. 
Given the abundance and cost-effectiveness of Cu, an essential human 
health trace element, coupled with the crucially important AIE property 
and strong antimicrobial activity of Cu NCs, it is highly desirable to 
develop Cu NCs-based antimicrobial agents with AIE, capable of 
exhibiting photodynamic antimicrobial properties and inherent anti­
microbial activity against drug-resistant bacteria in vivo [11]. Never­
theless, despite the critical importance of investigating AIE-featured Cu 
NCs-based antimicrobials and their impact on wound healing against 
multi-drug resistant bacteria in vivo, such research remains unim­
plemented, underscoring the need to address this research gap. 
Herein, we report the design of AIE-featured Cu NCs with tailorable 
PL for using as photodynamic antimicrobial agents to promote healing of 
wounds inflected by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA; 
see Scheme 1). The design involved inducing controlled aggregation of 
Cu(I)-thiolate motifs on in situ generated Cu(0) cores to form Cu(0)@Cu 
(I)-thiolate core–shell NCs at an elevated temperature, resulting in 
strong PL generated by the AIE of Cu(I)-thiolate motifs on the NC sur­
face. By varying the reaction pH from 3 to 12, the PL of the Cu NCs could 
be adjusted from blue to cyan due to the modulation of the aggregation 
degree of Cu(I)-thiolate motifs. The as-prepared AIE-featured Cu NCs 
showed excellent photodynamic antimicrobial activity against broad- 
spectrum bacteria and MRSA, with a bactericidal rate exceeding 98 %. 
This antimicrobial activity was attributed to the photodynamic pro­
ducibility of abundant reactive oxygen species (ROS), including H2O2 
(via O2 reduction) and •OH (via H2O2 or H2O oxidation). Importantly, 
the cytotoxicity of the Cu NCs to mammalian cells was found to be 
negligible, with a cell viability of > 95 % observed in mouse fibroblasts 
L929 cells used as the cell model. In in vivo experiments, the Cu NCs were 
found to efficiently treat MRSA-infected wounds by eradicating bacterial 
infection, and relieving inflammation in the skin wound section. This 
study represents the first successful preparation of AIE-featured Cu NCs- 
based photodynamic antimicrobials, laying the groundwork for future 
applications of Cu NCs in antimicrobial therapies. 
2. Results and discussion 
2.1. Characterizations of AIE-featured Cu(I)-thiolate motifs and Cu NCs 
It is straightforward to synthesize AIE-featured metal NCs by 
condensing Cu(I)-thiolate motifs (or complexes) on in situ generated Cu 
(0) cores, and therefore the prerequisite of synthesizing such Cu NCs is 
the production of AIE-featured Cu(I)-thiolate motifs [48]. The synthesis 
of AIE-featured Cu(I)-thiolate motifs was achieved by simply reacting 
CuSO4 as Cu precursor with tri-peptide glutathione (GSH) as protecting 
ligand. The formula of Cu(I)-GSH motifs was characterized using elec­
trospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS). As shown in Figure S1, 
the ESI-MS analysis reveals two sets of intense peaks at m/z ~ 1060, and

The AIE phenomenon of Cu(I)-GSH motifs can be demonstrated 
through a solvent-induced aggregation strategy. Particularly, the solvent 
polarity is manipulated by adjusting the volume ratio of dimethyl 
formamide (DMF) to water (i.e., fDMF = volDMF/volDMF+water) to regulate 
the aggregation state of Cu(I)-GSH motifs (Fig. 1a). At low pH (e.g., pH 
= 3.5), the as-synthesized Cu(I)-GSH motifs exist in a neutral, loosely 
structured state in the water solution (electric constant of ~80.1). This 
neutrality is attributed to the molecular structure of GSH, which con­
tains one amino group, two. 
carboxyl groups, and one thiol group, with PKa values of 9.86, 2.12, 
3.53, and 8.66, respectively (refer to Supplementary Note 2 and 
Figure S3 for further details). Notably, the neutrally charged Cu(I)-GSH 
motifs exhibit opalescence in room light but do not demonstrate PL 
under UV light (Fig. 1b, left items). Upon the introduction of less polar 
DMF (electric constant of ~36.7) into the Cu(I)-GSH motifs solution, 
which ranged from fDMF = 30 % to 85 %, the disruption of the hydration 
shell by DMF prompts the loose-structured Cu(I)-GSH motifs to aggre­
gate, thereby resulting in moderate PL (middle part of Fig. 1a and 
Fig. 1b). Further increase in the DMF-to-water ratio, such as at fDMF = 90 
%, leads to the transformation of Cu(I)-GSH motifs into smaller and 
denser aggregates (Fig. 1a, right part) as verified by dynamic light 
scattering results (DLS, Figure S4), ultimately showcasing strong PL 
(Fig. 1b). This transition to a solid-state with the highest aggregation 
level that exhibits intensified PL reinforces the AIE characteristic of the 
Cu(I)-GSH motifs (right panel of Fig. 1b).

(I)-GSH aggregates. As the fDMF increases, a gradual reduction in back­
ground scattering at wavelengths < 900 nm is observed. This decrease in 
background scattering is attributed to the reduced size of the aggregates, 
indicating a higher degree of aggregation for the Cu(I)-GSH motifs. The 
PL emission spectra (Fig. 1d) of the aggregates exhibited two interesting 
phenomena: 1) The increase in fDMF results in an enhancement in the PL 
intensity of Cu(I)-GSH aggregates, which can be attributed to the

In addition, the aggregates also exhibited a broad excitation band 
(Figure S5) with a dominant peak at 345 nm corresponding to their 
absorption peak (Fig. 1c). The large Stokes shift (>260 nm) indicates 
that the emission from aggregated Cu(I)-GSH motifs was primarily 
phosphorescence, supported by PL lifetime measurements revealing 
average lifetimes of 5.94 μs, 9.67 μs, and 11.62 μs for DMF fractions of 0 
%, 50 %, and 80 %, respectively (Fig. 1e). The microsecond-scale PL 
emission from the aggregates can be attributed to ligand-to-metal charge 
transfer (LMCT) or ligand-to-metal–metal charge transfer (LMMCT) 
from the sulfur atom in the GSH ligands to the Cu atoms, followed by 
radiative relaxation through a metal-centered triplet state. The extended 
PL decay time, coupled with the increasing fDMF, aligns well with the 
intensified metalophilic Cu(I)···Cu(I) interactions among the Cu(I)-GSH 
motifs due to higher aggregation. To validate the AIE property of Cu(I)- 
GSH aggregates, ethanol (dielectric constant of ~25.3) was utilized 
instead of DMF for the solvent-induced aggregation experiment, 
revealing a similar AIE phenomenon exhibited by Cu(I)-GSH aggregates 
(Figure S6). 
The dispersive nature and large hydrodynamic diameter of the Cu- 
GSH complexes limit their practical applicability and may potentially 
increase their biotoxicity due to the hazards of organic solvents, despite 
their possession of the AIE effect. Fortunately, the successful synthesis of 
AIE-featured Cu(I)-GSH complexes has provided insight into exploring a 
synthetic route for producing AIE-featured Cu NCs in water. To syn­
thesize AIE-featured Cu NCs involving the protection and condensation 
of Cu-GSH complexes on the Cu NCs’ surface (Fig. 2a), an elevated 
temperature of 80 ℃ was employed to facilitate the adjustment of the 
reducing-cum-protecting dual functionality of GSH. Additionally, pH 
was utilized to regulate the aggregation of Cu(I)-GSH complexes on the 
in-situ generated Cu(0) core. Consequently, blue-emitting Cu NCs (B-Cu 
NCs for short) and cyan-emitting Cu NCs (C-Cu NCs) with AIE could be 
produced after 24 h reaction at 80 ℃ in water at pH = 3 and 12, 
respectively. As shown in Fig. 2a, the modulation of PL in AIE-featured 
Cu NCs is directly dependent on the control of the aggregation level of 
their Cu(I)-GSH complexes through pH adjustments. Specifically, under 
acidic conditions at pH = 3, all Cu(I)-GSH complexes exist in a neutral 
state, facilitating closer aggregation and promoting enhanced metal­
ophilic Cu(I)···Cu(I) interactions within the motifs. These results in the 
emission of blue PL characterized by a higher energy output. In contrast, 
under alkaline conditions at pH = 12, the Cu(I)-GSH complexes carry 
negative charges, leading to a looser aggregation on the surface of the 
NCs due to electrostatic repulsion between motifs. Consequently, the 
metalophilic Cu(I)···Cu(I) interactions are less intensified, giving rise to 
a cyan PL with a lower emission energy. The proposed synthetic 
mechanism is detailed in Figure S7 and Supplementary Note 3. 
Of note, the coverage of Cu NCs by GSH ligands was confirmed by 
Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) over both B-Cu NCs and 
C-NCs using GSH as a reference. Figure S8 displays the results, revealing 
an evident −SH characteristic peak at 2500 cm−1 in GSH, which, how­
ever, is absent in the samples of B-Cu NCs and C-NCs. This absence 
implies the formation of Cu(I)-S bonds on the surface of the NCs. 
Furthermore, the GSH ligands confer excellent water solubility and a 
negatively charged surface to the NCs. The Zeta potential measurements 
of B-Cu NCs and C-NCs yielded values of −7.38 mV and −16.1 mV 
(Figure S9), respectively, supporting the coverage of Cu NCs by GSH. To 
analyze the Cu-to-GSH ratio of both Cu NCs, we conducted thermo 
gravimetric analysis (TGA). TGA results reveal that the molar ratio of Cu 
ions-to-GSH to be 1: 0.37 for C-Cu NCs based on the weight ratio of GSH 
to Cu species in these Cu NCs (i.e., 58.5 % for C-Cu NCs, see Figure S10). 
The high Cu-to-GSH ratio indicates the unique structure of Cu NCs 
consisting of a small Cu(0) core with few Cu atoms surrounded by 
abundant Cu(I)-GSH motifs, which is consistent with the finding in the 
case of AIE-featured GSH-protected Au NCs [55]. 
The optical properties, size and oxidation states of the as-fabricated 
Cu NCs were characterized by UV–vis absorption spectroscopy, PL 
spectroscopy, transmission electron microscope (TEM), and X-ray 
photoelectronic spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Fig. 2b and c, these 
UV–vis absorption spectra reveal a shoulder absorption band at 375 nm 
and a dominant peak at 730 nm for B-Cu NCs, while for C-Cu NCs, the 
corresponding peaks are at 375 nm and ~ 616 nm. The molecule-like 
absorptions observed suggest the successful synthesis of Cu NCs rather 
than Cu nanoparticles, which typically exhibit absorptions at ~ 574 nm.

The as-synthesized B-Cu NCs and C-Cu NCs exhibit blue and cyan PL, 
with emission peaks of 440 nm and 500 nm, respectively (Fig. 2b and c). 
Furthermore, the quantum yield (QY) of B-Cu NCs and C-Cu NCs was 
determined to be 1.46 % and 1.29 %, respectively. Interestingly, despite 
their distinct sizes and PL emissions, both B-Cu NCs and C-Cu NCs share 
the same excitation peak at 375 nm (Fig. 2b and c). The same excitation 
results suggest that the PL of these Cu NCs originates from the condensed 
Cu(I)-GSH motifs on the NCs’ shell, rather than from the Cu(0) core. The 
aggregation degree of Cu(I)-GSH motifs on the NCs’ shell was shown to 
significantly influence PL emissions by affecting metalophilic Cu(I)···Cu 
(I) interactions. This deduction was supported by the discovery that both 
types of NCs exhibited microsecond-scale PL lifetimes, with 6.65 μs for 
B-Cu NCs and 6.40 μs for C-Cu NCs (Fig. 2f), which are consistent with 
those of Cu(I)-GSH complex aggregates. This PL lifetimes of such two Cu 
NCs are over seven times longer than that of recently reported AIE- 
featured PEG-condensed Cu(I) aggregates (0.82 μs; Table S1) [48], 
suggesting that both Cu NCs may exhibit higher photodynamic antimi­
crobial efficacy. Additionally, the inheritance of the AIE property by 
both B-Cu NCs and C-Cu NCs was also supported by these findings. 
As known, such AIE-featured Cu NCs should possess a large pro­
portion of Cu(I) species due to the existence of abundant surface Cu(I)- 
GSH aggregates, which was examined by XPS analysis. The Cu 2p3/2 
spectra of the B-Cu NCs and C-Cu NCs were deconvoluted into Cu(I) and 
Cu(0) components with binding energies of 933.0 eV and 932.7 eV, 
respectively [55]. In Fig. 2g, it is evident that the oxidation state of Cu 
species in B-Cu NCs (blue curve) and C-Cu NCs (cyan curve) falls be­
tween that of Cu(I)-GSH complexes (black curve) and the large Cu(0) 
film (pink curve). Specifically, it is noteworthy that about 62.1 % of all 
Cu species in the B-Cu NCs were found to be Cu(I), which is a higher 
proportion compared to C-Cu NCs, where the Cu(I) content was 59.9 %. 
This distinction in Cu(I) content between the B-Cu and C-Cu NCs un­
derscores our previous inference that the more potent reducing ability of 
GSH at pH = 12 facilitated the conversion of Cu(I) species into Cu(0), 
resulting in a larger Cu(0) core in the C-Cu NCs. The unique Cu(0)@Cu 
(I)-GSH structure of the AIE-featured Cu NCs is crucial in addressing the 
poor stability issue commonly encountered with conventional Cu NCs 
due to the susceptibility of Cu species to oxidation. One notable example 
is the noteworthy stability demonstrated by the C-Cu-NCs, as evidenced 
by the PL decline of < 5 % observed across diverse conditions 
(Figure S11), such as in water at 4 ℃ for 30 days, in 1 M NaCl, and in a 
common phosphate buffered saline solution (pH = 7.4). Taking all the 
characterization results together, the Cu NCs exhibit ultrasmall size (<2 
nm), typical AIE property, good hydrophilicity, and marked stability, 
making them an ideal platform for biomedical applications. 
2.2. Cytotoxicity of AIE-featured Cu NCs 
Investigating the impact of AIE-featured Cu NCs on cell viability and 
migration is crucial for evaluating their potential for biological appli­
cations. In this study, both Live/Dead staining and 3-[4,5- 
dimethylthiazole-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assays 
were conducted using L929 mouse fibroblast cells as a model. The re­
sults of Live/Dead staining (Fig. 3a and c, Figure S12a and Figure S12c) 
revealed that L929 cells treated with C-Cu NCs and B-Cu NCs exhibited 
high viability, indicated by the predominant green staining, suggesting 
minimal cytotoxicity associated with these Cu NCs. Complementarily, 
MTT assay results (Fig. 3b and d, Figure S12b and Figure S12d) provided 
quantitative data on the cytotoxicity of the Cu NCs. Notably, both C-Cu 
NCs at [Cu] concentrations < 400 μM and B-Cu NCs at [Cu] ≤400 μM 
were observed to enhance L929 cell growth. Particularly, when [Cu] 
equaled 400 μM, C-Cu NCs demonstrated a L929 cell viability rate of 
approximately 94.5 %. This starkly contrasts with the significant cyto­
toxic impacts shown by Cu2+ ions, as they exhibited a L929 cell viability 
of only around 15.2 % at [Cu] = 400 μM. The two Cu NCs are also not 
biologically toxic to the L929 cells under light illumination, where all 
the cell viability rates are beyond 100 % (Figure S12). Regarding the cell 
migration test, as depicted in Fig. 3e, L929 cells in the C-Cu NCs + Light 
group exhibit the highest migration capability, which proves that the C- 
Cu NCs play a vital role in promoting cell migration and wound re- 
epithelialization under light illumination. The exceptional biocompati­
bility of these Cu NCs can be attributed to two key factors. Firstly, the tri- 
peptide GSH ligand contributes antioxidative properties, enhancing 
biocompatibility. Secondly, the strong binding of thiol groups with Cu(I) 
ions, along with the thick Cu(I)-GSH shell, effectively shields the NCs 
from releasing toxic Cu2+ ions, further bolstering biocompatibility. 
2.3. In vitro antimicrobial test and corresponding antimicrobial 
mechanism of c-cu NCs 
C-Cu NCs were selected as a model for photodynamic antimicrobial 
demonstration. Despite their relatively weak PL signal, the prepared C- 
Cu NCs exhibited exceptional photodynamic antimicrobial activity 
against Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Gram-negative 
Escherichia coli (E. coli), MRSA, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa 
(P. aeruginosa). This antimicrobial effectiveness can be attributed to the 
intriguing AIE trait and the inherent antimicrobial properties of Cu 
species. Firstly, concentration-related antimicrobial experiments of B-Cu 
NCs and C-Cu NCs were conducted, and the experimental results are 
shown in Figure S13. From the result, it can be seen that the half min­
imum inhibitory concentration (MIC50) is about 0.1 mM for Cu NCs, 
while the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) is nearly 0.25 
mM. This MBC is half than that of AIE-featured Au NCs (MBC = 0.5 mM) 
[43], suggesting that the Cu NCs have a stronger antimicrobial effect due 
to their inherent antimicrobial properties compared to Au NCs. As 
illustrated in Fig. 4a, the photodynamic antimicrobial activity of C-Cu 
NCs ([Cu] = 0.2 mM) was significantly high against S. aureus, E. coli, 
MRSA, and P. aeruginosa under visible light irradiation (60 min, 100 
mW/cm2) in an air atmosphere, with antimicrobial rates exceeding 98 % 
(Fig. 4b). Importantly, the antimicrobial rates of C-Cu NCs in the 
absence of light exposure were less than 49 % (Fig. 4a and b), indicating 
the critical role of light in enhancing their activity. A control experiment 
ruled out the possibility of the bacteria-killing effect of visible light itself 
(light control: < 1.3 %). Therefore, two conclusions can be derived from 
these findings: (1) C-Cu NCs possess inherent antimicrobial activity due 
to the antimicrobial properties of Cu species, even without light expo­
sure; (2) the AIE property of C-Cu NCs enhances their photodynamic 
antimicrobial activity by facilitating visible light absorption, prolonging 
carrier lifetime, ensuring good water solubility, and facilitating rapid 
carrier transfer between C-Cu NCs and bacteria. Moreover, C-Cu NCs 
demonstrated exceptional bactericidal activity against MRSA under 
visible light irradiation, indicating superior photodynamic antimicrobial 
efficacy and the ability to overcome the short-acting nature inherent in 
traditional antibiotic agents, particularly against drug-resistant bacteria. 
The AIE-featured Cu NCs, including but not limited to C-Cu NCs, exhibit 
potent photodynamic antimicrobial activity, as evidenced by the strong 
performance of B-Cu NCs in this role (Figure S14).

2.4. In vivo photodynamic therapy of MRSA-infected wound 
To assess the in vivo photodynamic therapeutic effectiveness of Cu 
NCs for MRSA-infected wounds, a carbomer gel containing C-Cu NCs 
was developed for easy wound management. Prior to the in vivo wound 
treatment, the physicochemical properties of C-Cu NCs-incorporated 
carbomer gel (C-Cu NCs-gel) such as the injectability, AIE properties, 
and antimicrobial activities was examined. The injectability of C-Cu 
NCs-gel is illustrated in Figure S21, where the methylene blue-stained 
gel can be smoothly injected out from a syringe (specifications/mm: 
0.6 × 25) while maintaining its solid form when in a water solution. The 
excellent hydrophilicity of GSH ligands ensured the uniform and stable 
dispersion of AIE-featured Cu NCs within the carbomer gel, as proven by 
the C-Cu NCs-gel (Figure S22). Additionally, the C-Cu NCs-gel inherited 
the AIE characteristic from the Cu NCs, as demonstrated by Figure S23, 
which shows no significant change in the PL emission even with the 
introduction of additional water and ethanol into the gel. This indicates 
effective protection of the Cu NCs within the gel matrix. To evaluate the 
impact of the gel on antimicrobial performance, antimicrobial experi­
ments were conducted using C-Cu NCs and C-Cu NCs-gel against various 
bacteria strains such as E. coli, S. aureus, MRSA, and P. aeruginosa under 
different conditions. Figure S24 shows that the C-Cu NCs-gel exhibits a 
slightly higher antibacterial rate compared to pristine C-Cu NCs at a 
concentration of [Cu] = 0.1 mM, suggesting that the antimicrobial ac­
tivity of C-Cu NCs remains potent after gel formation. Fig. 5a depicts the 
comprehensive evaluation process of photodynamic therapy applied to 
mice with an MRSA-infected wound using the C-Cu NCs-gel. As illus­
trated in Fig. 5b, through monitoring the wound healing progression and 
examining the haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained infected skin 
wound tissues, we found that the C-Cu NCs-gel with visible light expo­
sure (C-Cu NCs + Light group) exhibited superior therapeutic perfor­
mance for MRSA-infected wounds compared to other groups, including 
PBS, carbomer gel without C-Cu NCs embedment under visible light 
exposure (Gel + Light group), C-Cu NCs-gel without visible light expo­
sure (C-Cu NCs group), and even vancomycin (Van; the current most 
effective advanced antibiotic for MRSA eradication). The analysis of 
wound imprinting and physiological changes in the mice in terms of the 
infected wound area, body weight, and healing rate, further confirmed 
that the C-Cu NCs + Light group outperformed the other groups 
(Fig. 5c–e). For instance, on day 11, the wound areas in the PBS, Light, 
Van, C-Cu NCs, and C-Cu NCs + Light groups were as follows: 5.77 ±
0.42 mm2, 4.48 ± 0.37 mm2, 0.85 ± 0.09 mm2, 0.72 ± 0.04 mm2, and 
0.4 ± 0.04 mm2, respectively (Fig. 5c). Notably, the C-Cu NCs + Light 
group exhibited a more potent therapeutic effect compared to the C-Cu 
NCs group (Fig. 5b–e), which can be attributed to the integration of 
intrinsic antimicrobial attribute of ultrasmall Cu NCs with their excel­
lent photodynamic therapy performance. Furthermore, the therapeutic 
groups containing C-Cu NCs-gel effectively suppressed wound infection 
and accelerated wound healing compared to the Van group (Fig. 5b–e), 
highlighting the promising potential of Cu NCs-based antimicrobial 
agents for clinical applications. 
During the AIE-featured Cu NCs therapy process, the skin remodeling 
and regeneration play a crucial role in wound treatment. The histo­
pathological changes of the MRSA-infected wound site at the end of 
treatment were exhibited through Fig. 5b (right panel). It is evident that 
the epidermal and dermal structures of infected skin tissues in the PBS 
and Gel + Light groups are severely damaged, while the infected wound 
tissue in the Cu NCs group, C-Cu NCs + Light group, and Van group 
exhibit intact regenerated epithelial tissue and returned to normal his­
tological morphology. Meanwhile, as shown in the H&E staining (right 
panel, Fig. 5b), the wound tissue in C-Cu-NCs group and C-Cu-NCs +
Light group were almost completely re-epithelialized, which is the same 
as that in the Un-inf group. These results demonstrated that the Cu-NCs 
could eradicate bacteria and speed up MRSA-infected skin wound 
healing [57]. The C-Cu NCs-gel, benefitted from the excellent antimi­
crobial activity of Cu NCs and the good anti-oxidative property of their 
GSH ligands, exhibited a remarkable anti-inflammatory effect, as evi­
denced by the levels of key inflammatory factors like IL-6, IL-1β, and 
TNF-α. These inflammatory factors were assessed post-antimicrobial 
treatment in the animal experiment and the results were presented in 
Fig. 5f–h. By day 11, the levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in mice treated 
with C-Cu NCs + Light and Van tended to be within the normal range, 
similar to that of the Un-inf group, indicating eradication of the MRSA 
infection in the treatment group without inflammatory stress (Fig. 5f–h). 
Therefore, the Cu NCs-gel demonstrated excellent photodynamic per­
formance in promoting wound healing induced by MRSA. 
After the treatment experiment, the mice were euthanized and 
dissected to assess the biosecurity of C-Cu NCs-gel on their major organs 
(i.e., heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney). Histopathological evaluation 
of H&E staining in the major organs of mice in each group (Fig. 6a) show 
no significant differences, indicating that the C-Cu NCs-gel do not cause 
damage to the major organs. Moreover, a comprehensive assessment of 
the blood and liver function of mice treated with PBS and C-Cu NCs-gel 
was conducted, and the corresponding data was presented in Fig. 6b. 
The results of routine blood tests revealed that the white blood cell 
count, neutrophil count, and red blood cell count are not impacted 
following C-Cu NCs-gel therapy, thus confirming its safety and low 
toxicity. Furthermore, the liver function test indicated no significant 
differences between the treated mice and healthy mice. The measured 
values of alkaline phosphatase, alanine aminotransferase, and albumin 
are all within the reference range, suggesting that the liver function of 
the mice in the treatment group are essentially normal. Collectively, 
these findings demonstrate that C-Cu NCs-gel exhibits good biosafety 
when used to treat MASA-infected wounds. 
3. Conclusion 
In summary, the AIE-featured Cu(I)-GSH complexes and AIE- 
featured Cu NCs have been prepared through altering the polarity and 
pH of solvent, which in turn enhanced the aggregation extent of Cu(I)- 
GSH complexes and regulated the metalophilic Cu(I)···Cu(I) in­
teractions on the surface of Cu(0) core. The PL emission of the Cu NCs 
could be regulated based on adjusting the aggregation of surface com­
plexes for the first time. The two types of Cu NCs produced showed 
significant visible light absorptions (>600 nm), typical AIE property 
including strong PL (QY of 1.46 % for B-Cu NCs and 1.29 % for C-Cu 
NCs) and microsecond-scale PL lifetimes (≥6.40 μs). All these properties 
have enabled the AIE-featured Cu NCs to be excellent photodynamic 
antimicrobial agents. Experimental results validated that the Cu NCs 
possess excellent biocompatibility, where the cell viability rates are 
beyond 100 % due to the GSH ligand. The exciting antimicrobial effi­
ciency of B-Cu NCs and C-Cu NCs are all around 99 % with [Cu] = 0.2 
mM due to the synergistic coupling of both photodynamic and intrinsic 
antimicrobial actions. Furthermore, the Cu NCs-gel was also successfully 
prepared and applied in the photodynamic treatment of skin wound 
infected with MRSA in mice. After 11 days treatment, the Cu NCs-gel 
demonstrated effective photodynamic therapy for the MASA-infected

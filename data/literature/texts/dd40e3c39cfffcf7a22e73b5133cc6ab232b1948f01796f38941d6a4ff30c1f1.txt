A B S T R A C T   
In this paper, the antibacterial 2D covalent organic framework (COFTGTp) containing guanidyl cation was syn­
thesized and used as a carrier to deposit AgNPs on N and O groups to form nanocomposites to avoid AgNPs 
aggregation and achieve excellent antibacterial effect. The Ag content in Ag/COFTGTp was 10.3% determined by 
ICP. TEM can clearly observe that AgNPs were uniformly dispersed in COFTGTp. Ag/COFTGTp was stable after 
being evenly dispersed in water with low cytotoxicity and hemolysis rate to erythrocytes. In bacteriostatic study, 
the minimum inhibitory concentration of Ag/COFTGTp for E. coli and S. aureus were 100 μg/mL and 50 μg/mL, 
respectively. Finally, the morphology of the bacteria was observed by SEM, confirmed the main sterilization by 
Ag/COFTGTp was destroying the cell wall of the bacteria.   
1. Introduction 
Today antibiotic resistance and incurable bacterial infections are 
serious threaten to mankind, because there are few novel antibiotics can 
keep up with the evolution of antimicrobial resistance. So, it is necessary 
to develop other antibacterial way to realize innovative antimicrobial 
methods (S¸en Karaman et al., 2020). AgNPs are the most widely used 
metal nanoparticles to annihilate microbes including drug-resistance 
bacteria, which are always existed in various consumer products, as 
well as in medical applications (Tortella et al., 2020). Silver used as 
medicine can be traced back to 1881 to prevent neonatal eye infections, 
and later used for internal disinfection in 1901 (Gayathri et al., 2015). 
However, the high surface energy of AgNPs leads to agglomerate and 
deposition, which in turn reduces antibacterial effect, and the

uncontrollable oxidation behavior of AgNPs would lead to the sudden 
release of Ag+ ions, which may cause cytotoxicity, affect stability and 
long-term use, even cause environmental hazards, and increase health 
and safety risks (Tortella et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2014; Hadrup et al., 
2012). In fact, AgNPs in vivo will induce apoptosis, oxidative stress and 
mitochondrial disruption, particularly in the central nervous system (Hu 
et al., 2020). Y. Zhang et al. (2018) and W. Zhang et al. (2018) studied 
the influence of AgNPs on lens’ development of zebrafish embryos, 
perhaps which will defect cranial placode. Pauksch et al. (2014) found 
that AgNPs mainly affect human mesenchymal stem cells and osteo­
blasts, and were cytotoxic when the concentration of AgNPs reaches to 
10 µg/g or higher. Sung et al. (2008) showed that after the rats were 
given silver nanoparticles with a diameter of 18 nm for 90 consecutive 
days, corresponding inflammatory manifestations occurred in the lungs 
accompanied by lung function damage. Therefore, the introduction of 
suitable carriers for AgNPs anchoring and depositing can effectively 
improve the long-term sustainability of single AgNPs, and avoid the 
aggregation to ensure biological safety and antibacterial efficacy. 
Now, researchers focused on how to anchor AgNPs on proper carriers 
to improve their dispersion. Some materials with micro/nanopores and 
smooth titanium surfaces have been used to deposit AgNPs, which can 
reduce the risk of infection during surgery (Y. Zhang et al., 2018; W. 
Zhang et al., 2018). Among them, graphene oxide rich in 
oxygen-containing groups can provide sufficient active points for AgNPs 
depositing, and the obtained Ag/GO composites to prevent AgNPs 
aggregating, thus improving the stability and continuous antibacterial 
effect (Li et al., 2015). The kind of Ag-RGO nanocomposite with high 
antibacterial ability and low toxicity can be used to against E. coli 
(Moghayedi et al., 2017, 2020). And g-C3N4 sheets were also proper one 
for AgNPs depositing, because there was strong interaction between Ag 
atoms and –C–N, –C––N bonds, resulting in successful AgNPs 
anchoring on g-C3N4 sheets (Liu et al., 2016). AgNPs were dispersed into 
the nanoporous carbon nitride (NCN) matrix physically by in-situ ul­
trasound (NCN@Ag) to avoid AgNPs accumulating and enhance anti­
bacterial activity by destroy the membrane of bacterial cells (Wahab 
et al., 2021). The emerging organic framework (COF) materials with 
well-designed structure according to the requirement had also been be 
used for AgNPs or other noble metal NPs depositing (Pachfule et al., 
2014; Mullangi et al., 2015; Ding et al., 2011). Su et al. used the solution 
infiltration method to synthesize TPHH-COF rich in N and O functional 
groups to load AgNPs for catalytic degradation (Wang et al., 2019). 
Bhunia et al. (2012) synthesized a Schiff base covalent porous material 
(CIN-1) through the condensation reaction between rigid triamines and 
dialdehydes, which is rich in N and O active sites to bind PdNPs firmly 
on its surface. Esteves et al. synthesized an imine-linked covalent 
organic framework COF-300 modified by Pd(OAc)2 named as Pd 
(OAc)2@COF-300 to catalyze the Suzuki coupling reaction efficiently 
(Du et al., 2017). 
Therefore, in this study, we chose COFTGTp rich in N and O atoms as 
carrier to reduce Ag+ into AgNPs on the skeleton during the reduction 
process to form Ag/COFTGTp nanocomposite. COFTGTp was synthesized by 
triaminoguanidine chloride (TG) and 1,3,5-triformyl-phloroglucinol (Tp) 
through solvothermal method (Mitra et al., 2016) with antibacterial 
properties, the active sites N and O atoms on the skeleton as the binding 
sites will anchor AgNPs to avoid aggregating and promote dispensing 
evenly. Ag/COFTGTp nanocomposite had the following advantages: Firstly, 
AgNPs deposited on COFTGTp can enable the better dispersion and stability 
of AgNPs in aqueous solution, thereby ensuring the antibacterial properties 
with good biosecurity. Secondly, COFTGTp was a cationic antibacterial 
material, which can adhere on the membrane of gram-negative bacteria to 
assistant AgNPs to annihilate this kind of bacteria efficiently. Thirdly, 
Ag/COFTGTp can both kill gram-positive bacteria and gram-negative bac­
teria, realizing broad-spectrum antibacterial. Ag/COFTGTp nanocomposite 
is expected to become a new kind of broad-spectrum and efficient anti­
bacterial material, which can solve the problem of antibiotic abuse and 
annihilate drug-resistant bacteria (Scheme 1). 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials 
Guanidine hydrochloride (chemical purity 98%), phloroglucinol 
(99%) were purchased from Aladdin; 1,4-dioxane, hydrazine hydrate 
(analytical purity 85%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Re­
agent Co., Ltd.; Hexamethylene Tetraamine (analytical purity) was 
purchased from Tianjin Fuchen Chemical Reagent Factory; silver nitrate, 
sodium borohydride (96%), trifluoroacetic acid, hydrochloric acid, N,N- 
dimethylacetamide, ethanol, dichloromethane, anhydrous sodium sul­
fate, absolute and acetone were purchased from Beijing Chemical Plant. 
DMEM medium (high sugar), thiazole blue, dimethyl sulfoxide, acetone, 
glutaraldehyde, LB broth medium, MH medium, LB nutrient agar, MH 
nutrient agar, live bacterial staining kit were purchased from Qingdao 
Haibo Biological Technology Co., Ltd. The strains of S. aureus (ATCC 
25923) and Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC 25922) were obtained from 
Luwei Technology (Shanghai, China). All solvents and materials used in 
bacterial cultures were autoclaved (120 ◦C, 20 min) before use. Mouse 
embryonic cells (NIH3T3) were provided by Northeast Normal 
University. 
2.2. Synthesis and characterization 
The synthetic routes of the two monomers TG and Tp were shown in 
Fig. S1, aldehydes on Tp and amino groups on TG reacted with each 
other to form Schiff base, and then reacted under 120 ◦C in sealed tube 
for 72 h to form COFTGTp. The functional groups of TG and Tp were 
characterized by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) through infrared 
transmittance mode and shown in Fig. S2. Through condensation be­
tween amino groups on TG and aldehydes on Tp, COFTGTp will be ob­
tained according to reference (Wang et al., 2019) and characterized by 
13C CP-MAS solid state NMR and particle size distribution shown in 
Figs. S3 and S4. Solid-state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) 
spectrum was recorded on Bruker AVANCE III 400WB. Then AgNPs can 
be anchored on the active sites such as N and O atoms of COFTGTp 
skeleton. First 75 mg COFTGTp was grind in an agate mortar with 1 mL of 
methanol for 30 min, then grinded COFTGTp was collected with 45 mL 
methanol and placed in a 100 mL round bottom flask to disperse thor­
oughly in an ultrasonic cell crusher for 20 min. After sonication, 12 mL 
of 8.75 mg/mL colorless AgNO3 solution was added to the COFTGTp so­
lution, the color of the mixed solution changed from brown-yellow to 
earthen yellow immediately, and then rotary evaporation with the 
temperature setting to 50 ◦C. After the liquid in the flask was completely 
removed, 30 mL of water was added in to centrifuge (9000 rpm, 5 min) 
twice to remove excess AgNO3. Finally, the precipitate was dispersed in 
a mixed solution of 30 mL methanol and water (volume ratio 3:2), then 
15 mL of NaBH4 methanol solution (227 mg NaBH4) was slowly added 
dropwise to the solution under magnetic stirring for 2 days, then washed 
with water and methanol 3 times to remove free AgNPs, and finally dry 
with a vacuum drying oven to obtain Ag/COFTGTp nanocomposite. 
The preparation of AgNPs as control group was the same to the 
above. Firstly, 105 mg of AgNO3 was saluted in 30 mL of water, vor­
texed for 1 min, and 15 mL of NaBH4 methanol solution (227 mg) was 
slowly added dropwise to the solution under magnetic stirring for 2 
days. Then the AgNPs were washed with water and methanol for three 
times, and finally dried in a vacuum drying oven. 
The structure of Ag/COFTGTp was characterized by FT-IR, X-ray 
diffraction (XRD) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, 
ESCALab220i-XL,VG Scientific, England). FT-IR spectra were recorded 
on Bruker VERTEX70 using the KBr pellet method and the test wave­
length range was 500–4000 cm−1. XRD study was performed by a Wide- 
angle X-ray diffractometer (D8ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany) using Cu- 
Kα radiation. XPS was used for testing elements content and the state of 
Ag on skeleton of COFTGTp, and analyzed by XPSPEAK software. The 
particle size of Ag/COFTGTp was measured by zeta potential/BI-90Plus

2.3. Antibacterial experiment and biocompatibility study of Ag/COFTGTp 
The antibacterial performance of Ag/COFTGTp was evaluated by the 
plate counting method and the living and dead staining experiment. 
First, Ag/COFTGTp and bacteria were co-cultivated for 3 h, and then 
spread on MH or LB nutrient agar to let bacteria growth to form colonies 
on the Petri dish. Second, bacterial colonies were calculated to confirm 
the antibacterial effect of Ag/COFTGTp. The living and dead staining 
experiment was carried on by co-cultivated bacteria with Ag/COFTGTp, 
COFTGTp (0.5 mL, 2 mg/mL) and AgNPs (0.5 mL, 0.2 mg/mL) for 4 h. 
4 h later, the mixture was transferred for centrifugation (4000 rpm, 
5 min), discarded the supernatant, and 20 μL of the obtained bacterial 
suspension mixed with 10 μL of PI and SYTO9 simultaneously and co- 
incubated to stain for 20 min in dark for alive and dead bacterial 
observation with a laser microscope. The morphology of bacteria after 
treatment with Ag/COFTGTp was observed by scanning electron 
microscope (SEM), PBS, COFTGTp and AgNPs were used as control. After 
the bacteria co-cultivated with PBS, COFTGTp, AgNPs and Ag/COFTGTp, 
2.5% glutaraldehyde solution was used to fix the bacteria for 1 h, then 
dehydrated the bacteria by ethanol solution of 40%, 60%, 80%, and 
100% for 10 min, respectively for SEM observation. 
The biocompatibility of Ag/COFTGTp was studied by cytotoxicity of 
mouse embryonic fibroblasts (NIH3T3). The NIH3T3 cells were diluted 
in DMEM medium and inoculated in 96-well plates with 5000 cells per 
well for 24 h, then 20 μL Ag/COFTGTp (2, 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.625 mg/ 
mL) was added in each well, 100 μL AgNPs (0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.025, 
0.0125, 0.00625 mg/mL) and 100 μL PBS were used as control. After 
that, the samples were cultivated for another 24 h and remove all of 
liquid thoroughly, and then 10 μL of CCK-8 and 100 μL DMEM medium 
was added in and cultivated for 1 h to test absorption at 490 nm by Elisa. 
For hemolysis test, 4% rabbit red blood cells purchased from Shanghai 
Gu duo Biological Technology Co., Ltd. 100 μL of COFTGTp and Ag/ 
COFTGTp solution (400, 200, 100, 50, 25, 12.5 μg/mL) and AgNPs so­
lution (40, 20, 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25 μg/mL) were added in 100 μL red blood 
cell suspension to incubate at 37 ℃ for 12 h, respectively, and 100 μL of 
0.2% Triton X-100 solution as the positive control and 100 μL PBS as the 
negative control. After that, the red blood cells were centrifuged again 
and the sub natant was transferred to a 96-well plate and detected by a 
microplate reader at 570 nm, if the hemolysis ratio was below 5%, the 
material will not lead to severe hemolysis according to ISO 10993- 
4:2002 (Zhang et al., 2009).

peak near 1075 cm−1 assigned to C-N enhanced from weak to media 
during the Ag+ reduction (Mazumder et al., 2019). In addition, the new 
peak appeared at 507 and 671 cm−1 assigned to Ag–O bond indicated 
Ag coordinating with O on the skeleton of COFTGTp (Bhagyaraj and 
Krupa, 2020). The particle size of the Ag/COFTGTp were tested and listed 
in Fig. 1c and d. The size of COFTGTp was about 850 nm, while for AgNPs 
was 200 nm, and for Ag/COFTGTp was 950 nm. As part of the Ag+ co­
ordinated with the residual -NH2 and C––O at the edge, the particle size 
of Ag/COFTGTp was somewhat increased. The zeta potential of COFTGTp 
was about +29 mV and that of AgNPs was −34 mV, and for Ag/COFTGTp 
was −18 mV. After AgNPs were loaded, the potential dropped from 
+28 mV to −18 mV, indicating negatively charged AgNPs successfully 
deposited on the skeleton of COFTGTp. 
XPS analyzed the presence and state of AgNPs on the skeleton of 
COFTGTp. In the range of 0–1000 eV, compared with COFTGTp, the full 
scan spectrum of Ag/COFTGTp showed the presence of C, N, O and Ag 
(Fig. 2a). The high-resolution XPS spectrum of Ag 3d energy level 
(Fig. 2b) consisted of three parts: Ag0, Ag⋅⋅⋅N-C and Ag⋅⋅⋅O––C. The 
peaks at 368.5 eV and 374.5 eV indicated the presence of metallic silver 
(Liu et al., 2017). As shown in Fig. S8, C–N, C–O and Ag–O bonds 
have peaks at 398.6 eV, 533.1 eV, 531.8 eV. The spin energy difference 
between these two peaks was 6.0 eV, indicating that Ag existed in a 
zero-valent state in Ag/COFTGTp. According to the literature (Yan et al., 
2018), the peaks at 375.4 eV and 369.4 eV can be attributed to Ag⋅⋅⋅N, 
and the peaks at 373.4 eV and 367.4 eV can be attributed to Ag⋅⋅⋅O. The 
difference in spin energy was also 6.0 eV, which indicated that Ag in 
Ag⋅⋅⋅N and Ag⋅⋅⋅O also existed in the form of Ag0. Compared with 
metallic Ag, the slight change in binding energy indicated that the Ag0 
binding site was tightly bound to the N and O positions in COFTGTp. 
Fig. S9 also showed that Ag+ has been reduced to Ag0. The nitrogen and 
oxygen atoms in Ag/COFTGTp contributed a lot of active sites for 
anchoring AgNPs. TEM spectra can be used to intuitively observe the 
existence of AgNPs on COFTGTp as shown in Fig. 2c and d. COFTGTp had 
an obvious flake structure (Fig. 2c), and the existence of AgNPs also 
made COFTGTp more thoroughly stripped into slices with AgNPs 
dispersed in it evenly (Fig. 2d). The size of AgNPs was about 200 nm and 
uniformly distributing on COFTGTp tablets, indicating that this method 
can avoid AgNPs aggregating, fully improved the dispersion of AgNPs to 
maintain its antibacterial efficiency. From Table S1, the content of Ag 
loaded in Ag/COFTGTp was accurately measured by ICP about 10.3% 
(w/w). In order to evaluate the stability of Ag/COFTGTp in aqueous so­
lution, Ag/COFTGTp was dispersed in water to observe the Tyndall 
phenomenon with laser irradiation, the result was shown in Fig. 2e. 
When a beam of red laser passed through the newly prepared 
Ag/COFTGTp aqueous solution, a clear optical path can be seen, indi­
cating that Ag/COFTGTp had be uniformly dispersed in the water in a 
colloidal state, which was caused by the hydrophilic skeleton of 
COFTGTp. After standing for 12 h, red laser could still pass through the 
solution to form a clear optical path. According to the Tyndall phe­
nomenon, the solution still existed in a colloidal state, indicating 
Ag/COFTGTp was stably dispersed in water to confirm Ag/COFTGTp was 
stable in aqueous solution. After the Ag/COFTGTp store for at least one 
month, we conducted FTIR test as Fig. S10, found there was no obvious 
difference with Fig. 1b, proving that the material had good stability. 
The specific surface area and porosity of COFTGTp and Ag/COFTGTp 
were measured by N2 adsorption and desorption experiments, and the 
results were shown in Figs. S5 and 3a and b. Both adsorption isotherms 
of COFTGTp (Fig. S5a) and Ag/COFTGTp (Fig. 3a) followed a type II 
reversible adsorption isotherm. The BET specific surface area and pore 
size of COFTGTp were 152.8 m2 g−1 and 4 nm, respectively. For Ag/ 
COFTGTp, the BET surface area was significantly reduced to 81.69 m2 
g−1, and the pore size was slightly reduced to 3.4 nm, which indicated 
that AgNPs were not loaded in the holes of COFTGTp, but on it through 
coordination. The AgNPs covered some holes of COFTGTp led to a sig­
nificant decrease in the specific surface area and a small decrease in the 
pore size. The content of Ag was quantitatively and qualitatively 
determined by TG and UV, as shown in Figs. S6 and S7. In Fig. S6, 
COFTGTp had a characteristic absorption peak at 397 nm, and Ag/ 
COFTGTp had two characteristic absorption peaks at 292 nm and 440 nm 
(Zhao et al., 2006). Fig. S7 showed the TG curves of COFTGTp and 
Ag/COFTGTp, at 800 ℃, the remaining mass percentages in COFTGTp and 
Ag/COFTGTp were 35.18% and 38.25%, respectively, the results of UV 
and TG indicated AgNPs deposited on the skeleton of COFTGTp 
successfully. 
The release of Ag+ form Ag/COFTGTp within 24 h and two weeks 
were shown in Fig. 3c and d. It can be seen from Fig. 3c that the cu­
mulative concentration of Ag+ released from Ag/COFTGTp at 24 h was 
1.34 ppb, and the calculated release amount was about 0.14%. While for 
Ag/COFTGTp, the calculated release amount of Ag+ from Ag/COFTGTp 
within a week was 8%, indicating that the release rate of Ag+ was sus­
tained and belonged to a long-term release. 
3.2. Antibacterial results 
Plate counting method was used to evaluate the antibacterial per­
formance of Ag/COFTGTp. By diluting the bacterial solution and then 
spreading it on the agar plate for cultivation, each single bacterium will 
multiply into a colony visible to the naked eyes, and the number of 
bacteria in the original bacterial solution can be reflected by the number 
of colonies. Gram-negative E. coli and gram-positive S. aureus were 
selected for antibacterial experiments, and the results were shown in  
Figs. 4 and 5, PBS was used as control. From Fig. 4, the antibacterial 
effect of AgNPs increased slightly with the increase of the AgNPs con­
centration. When the concentration of AgNPs was 20 μg/mL, the bac­
terial inhibition rate was only 41% due to the aggregation of AgNPs. The 
bacteriostatic effect of COFTGTp was better than that of AgNPs, the 
bacterial inhibition rate was 79% when the concentration of COFTGTp 
was 100 μg/mL, and when the concentration of COFTGTp increased to 
200 μg/mL, the bacterial inhibition rate was up to 100%. For Ag/ 
COFTGTp, when the concentration was 100 μg/mL, bacterial inhibition 
rate reached to 100%, which was significantly better than that of the 
AgNPs and COFTGTp. The results indicated when AgNPs was deposited 
on the skeleton of COFTGTp to form Ag/ COFTGTp, the antibacterial effect 
on E. coli can be effectively improved. From Fig. 5, same to that of Fig. 4, 
the antibacterial effect on S. aureus of samples increased slightly with 
the increase of concentration. When concentration of AgNPs was 
200 μg/mL, the bacterial inhibition rate was only 58%, at the same 
concentration, COFTGTp was better than AgNPs with the bacterial inhi­
bition rate up to 100%, even concentration of COFTGTp was 50 μg/mL, 
the bacterial inhibition rate can reach to 83%. While for Ag/COFTGTp, 
when the concentration was 50 μg/mL, the bacterial inhibition rate can 
reach to 100%, which was significantly higher than AgNPs and COFTGTp.

3.3. Live and dead staining 
Using the different structure of the cell walls of dead and living 
bacteria, the antibacterial effect of AgNPs, COFTGTp and Ag/COFTGTp can 
be observed by live and dead staining directly, PBS groups were used as 
control. We used PI to stain dead bacteria and SYTO9 to stain both live 
and dead bacteria. The results were shown in the Fig. 6. The photos of 
the PBS group of the two bacteria (gram-negative E. coli than gram- 
positive S. aureus) were all green, indicating both bacteria were living. 
After cultivating with AgNPs, some of the bacteria dead, and from the 
photos, the dead number of S. aureus was slightly more than that of 
E. coli. After COFTGTp treatment, the two kinds of bacteria were signif­
icantly dead to exhibit more red point on the photos, the dead bacteria 
were more than that of the AgNPs. While after Ag/COFTGTp treatment, 
almost all the S. aureus died, and only a few E. coli were in living state as 
some yellow points observed in the Ag/COFTGTp photo in Fig. 6a. The 
above results indicated that Ag/COFTGTp had the best antibacterial ef­
fect, and its bacterial inhibition rate on S. aureus was stronger than that 
on E. coli, consisted with the result of plate counting experiment. 
3.4. Morphologies of bacteria 
In order to further confirm the antibacterial effect of Ag/COFTGTp, the 
morphologies of the bacteria after inhibiting by AgNPs, COFTGTp and Ag/ 
COFTGTp were directly observed, PBS was used as control, and the results 
were shown in Fig. 7. In Fig. 7a, the E. coli in PBS group had a whole rod- 
shaped and the surfaces didn’t damage. While E. coli in AgNPs and COFTGTp 
groups showed some slight damage on their surface. And the number of 
damaged E. coli in COFTGTp group was more than that in the AgNPs group. 
Almost all of E. coli in Ag/COFTGTp group were damaged, and holes 
appeared on the surfaces, which will induce leakage of the contents, 
destroy the physiological metabolism of E. coli to kill them. The result 
indicated that the Gram-negative bacteria were effectively inhibited by Ag/ 
COFTGTp. In Fig. 7b, like that of E. coli, the morphologies of S. aureus in PBS 
were full and intact with smooth surface. While after S. aureus treated with 
AgNPs and COFTGTp, the morphologies were slightly shrunk, and there was 
a small amount of damage on the surface. Among them, more S. aureus in 
the COFTGTp group deformed than that of AgNPs group. While S. aureus in 
Ag/COFTGTp group, the morphologies significantly changed, the leakages 
were shown on the surface, some S. aureus turn into pie-shaped were like 
red blood cells. The result showed that Ag/COFTGTp can effectively inhibit 
gram-positive bacteria. The observation of SEM was same to the analyses of 
plate counting and live-dead staining experiments, and all of them can 
prove that Ag/COFTGTp had perfect antibacterial effect on E coli and 
S. aureus to be a broad-spectrum antibacterial. 
3.5. Biological safety 
Mouse embryonic fibroblasts (NIH3T3) were used to test the cyto­
toxicity of COFTGTp, AgNPs and Ag/COFTGTp. The results were shown in  
Fig. 8a. As the concentrations of COFTGTp, AgNPs and Ag/COFTGTp 
increasing, the cytotoxicity also increased, and the cell survival rate 
decreased accordingly, but all of them were in a biosafety state. When 
the concentration of Ag/COFTGTp reached to 200 μg/mL, the cell sur­
vival rate was still above 85%, indicating that Ag/COFTGTp had good 
biocompatibility. 
In in vitro hemolysis test, if the compatibility with red blood cells was 
low, red blood cells will be destroyed to induce the contents flow out, 
and hemolysis will occur. Hemolysis test by COFTGTp, AgNPs and Ag/ 
COFTGTp was performed by rabbit red blood cells. The result was shown 
in Fig. 8b. With the increase of the concentration of AgNPs, COFTGTp and 
Ag/COFTGTp, the hemolysis rates were increased slightly, but the overall 
hemolysis rates remained below 3%. While the hemolysis rate of Ag/ 
COFTGTp group increased with the increase of the concentration. 
Compared with AgNPs and COFTGTp groups, the hemolysis rate of Ag/ 
COFTGTp was larger, because AgNPs tended to accumulate in aqueous 
solution and reduced the contact area with red blood cells, while Ag/ 
COFTGTp can significantly improve the dispersibility of AgNPs, increase 
the contact area with red blood cells to destroy their cell membrane, and 
then caused the contents leak out to increase the hemolysis rate. But 
even when the concentration of Ag/COFTGTp reached to 200 μg/mL, the 
average hemolysis rate was less than 5%, according to ISO, which can’t 
be regarded as hemolysis (Zhang et al., 2009), indicating that 
Ag/COFTGTp had low hemolysis rate with good biocompatibility.

4. Conclusion 
In this study, a 2D COFTGTp was synthesized and depositing AgNPs on 
its skeletons successfully by reducing Ag+ bonding on N and O active 
sites to form Ag/COFTGTp. The Ag content in Ag/COFTGTp was 10.3% 
calculated by ICP. From TEM of Ag/COFTGTp, it can be clearly observed 
that AgNPs were evenly dispersed in COFTGTp, the problem of AgNPs 
aggregation had been solved effectively. And the Ag/COFTGTp was stable 
and well dispersed in aqueous solution for 12 h observation. The mini­
mum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for E. coli and S. aureus were 
100 μg/mL and 50 μg/mL, respectively, especially for S. aureus. Ag/ 
COFTGTp can destroy cell walls of bacteria to annihilate them with low 
cytotoxicity and hemolysis. As a result, this study fully demonstrated 
that Ag/COFTGTp had an effective antibacterial performance, stable 
physical and chemical properties, and excellent biocompatibility, which 
will be a potential antibacterial to use as wound dressing material.

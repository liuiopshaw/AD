SUMMARY
Expressed in the entorhinal cortex (EC), the cholecystokinin (CCK) B receptor (CCKBR) plays an important 
role in memory and learning. Here, we identify that CCKBR-Gs and -Gq signaling, rather than CCKBR-Gi 
signaling, are beneficial for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) treatment. Clinically, patients with more severe AD asso-
ciated with lower CCKBR-Gq activity. The cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of CCKBR in com-
plex with the endogenous agonist sulfated CCK8 (CCK8s) and 3 different G protein subtypes revealed that 
distinct receptor conformations contribute to selective G protein bias. Leveraging these structural insights, 
we rationally develop synthetic CCKBR agonists, including a Gi-biased agonist (z-44) and a Gq-biased 
agonist (3r1). Notably, 3r1 demonstrates therapeutic potential by ameliorating cognitive decline in 5×FAD 
mice, reducing the number of amyloid-β plaques, and promoting long-term potentiation (LTP) via upregula-
tion of the α-secretase (ADAM10) and the calcium signaling molecule PLCB4. Our findings suggest that 
synthetic biased agonists targeting CCKBR-Gq signaling have therapeutic potential for AD.

lead to memory impairment. Cholecystokinin (CCK) is one of the 
most abundant neuropeptides in the brain and is distributed in 
the neocortex, amygdala, thalamus, hippocampus, and entorhi-
nal cortex (EC). 3–6 CCK is involved in neurotransmission and the 
modulation of many brain functions, such as emotion, learning 
and memory, anxiety, and fear. 7–12 Importantly, the EC is the 
brain region with enriched CCK, 13,14 and it acts as a mediator 
for the relay of information between the hippocampus and the 
neocortex. Moreover, most cells in the EC are CCK-positive. 15 
The importance of CCK signaling in learning and memory is high-
lighted by the fact that Cck knockout mice perform worse in the 
passive avoidance task and show impaired spatial memory in the 
Morris water maze (MWM) test. 16 In our previous studies, we 
have reported that the EC plays an important role in encoding 
and retrieving artificial visuo-auditory memory traces in the audi-
tory cortex 17 and that the activation of CCK-ergic neurons from 
the EC induces long-term potentiation (LTP) and neuroplasticity. 
The interaction between CCK and the CCK B receptor (CCKBR) 
is essential for high-frequency stimulation-induced LTP and is 
important for sound-sound associative memory, an important 
aspect of learning and memory. 18 There are many CCK variant 
peptides in the brain that can activate multiple G protein subtype 
signals—including, but not limited to, Gs, Gq, and Gi 19–21 —with 
the sulfated CCK8 (CCK8s) variant being the most abundant 22,23 
(Figure S1A).
Notably, the EC is the region of the brain that initially exhibits 
the gradual accumulation of neurofibrillary tangles and neuropil 
threads, noticeable atrophy, and neuronal loss, which are path-
ological hallmarks of the early stages of Alzheimer’s disease 
(AD). 24–26 In previous behavioral studies, we reported that 
repeated administration of CCK8s could enhance spatial 
learning and memory in elderly rats. 27 We therefore hypothe-
sized that complementation of CCK signaling by exogenous 
CCK8s may have beneficial effects in ameliorating AD progres-
sion. 28–30 In the present study, we investigated CCKBR activity 
changes during AD and the effects of the administration of 
CCKBR agonists on AD development, analyzed the contribu-
tions of specific downstream-biased signaling pathways, and 
then determined the structures of CCK8s-CCKBR bound to
three different transducers. Further structural analysis and phar-
macological profiling revealed the structural basis of CCKBR-
biased signaling, which facilitates the design of both Gi- and 
Gq-biased CCKBR agonists with receptor selectivity and strong 
potency. The application of the Gq-biased CCKBR agonist 3r1, 
but not the Gi-biased agonist z-44, had beneficial effects on 
learning and memory impairments in AD model mice.
RESULTS
CCK alleviates memory decline by activating the CCKBR 
in the brain
Because EC is one of the earliest regions that exhibits AD pathol-
ogy, 24–26 we investigated how CCK signaling changes in the brain 
during AD development. Importantly, the expression of CCK in the 
EC region of 5×FAD mice in the late stage of AD was significantly 
decreased by approximately 90% compared with that in their 
age-matched non-carrier littermates, and the expression of the 
CCKBR was conversely upregulated in the hippocampus and 
cortical regions (Figures 1A and S1B–S1D). Consistent with these 
observations, learning and memory abilities were decreased, and 
LTP in the hippocampus and cortical regions was attenuated, 31 in 
CCKBR knockout mice (Cckbr − /− ) compared with their age-
matched non-carrier littermates (Cckbr +/+ ), suggesting a potential 
correlation between impaired CCK signaling and memory loss 
during AD progression (Figures 1B, 1C, and S1E).
We hypothesized that the upregulation of CCKR activities by 
the introduction of exogenous CCK8s may have beneficial ef-
fects on slowing AD progression and ameliorating neuronal 
injury. Because of the inability of CCK8s to cross the blood-brain 
barrier (BBB) after administration, and the considerable brain 
damage caused by long-term epidural administration, we first 
examined the effects of CCK8s on amyloid-β (Aβ)-induced death 
of primary cortical neurons in vitro. The transformation of Aβ 
monomers to neurotoxic aggregates is one of the key steps dur-
ing AD progression. Whereas Aβ (1–42) (Aβ42) treatment signifi-
cantly decreased the primary cultured cortical neurons’ viability, 
CCK8s effectively rescued the reduction in neuronal viability 
induced by Aβ42 in vitro (Figures S1G and S1H). Moreover, the

It is known that patients with AD have cerebral neurometabolic 
disorders with significant changes of protein or polypeptide 
components in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). 36–39 We next designed 
a cross-sectional study of CSF-induced CCKBR activity 
involving 78 patients diagnosed with AD, excluding those with 
other neurological diseases. The patients were divided into two 
groups based on the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) global 
score of AD 40 (39 cases of mild impairment and 39 cases of se-
vere impairment) (Figures 1I and 1J). We enriched the polypep-
tide protein components (PPCs) of CSF and measured changes 
in their ability to activate Gi or Gq signaling of CCKBR. Impor-
tantly, the activation of CCKBR-Gq signal by CSF polypeptide 
components in patients with severe AD was significantly lower 
than that in patients with mild AD. In contrast, the activation of 
CCKBR-Gi signal was increased in patients with severe AD 
compared with those with mild AD (Figures 1K, 1L, and S1Q– 
S1U). These data suggest that Gq activity of CCKBR might be 
downregulated during AD development.
Without knowledge of the exact endogenous agonists of 
CCKBR during AD development, we performed mass spectrom-
etry (MS)-based quantification of CCK peptides (CCK8s and 
CCK4), two known CCKBR agonists in the normal brain, 22,23,41 
in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients with mild AD and se-
vere AD. The results showed that CCK8s levels in the CSF of pa-
tients with severe AD were significantly lower than those in pa-
tients with mild AD, whereas CCK4 levels in the CSF of 
patients with severe AD were significantly higher. The biased 
properties of CCK8s and CCK4 may explain why the Gq activity 
of CCKBR stimulated by the CSF PPCs was weaker in cases of 
severe AD (Figure 1M).

of CCK8s-CCKBR-Gq, CCK8s-CCKBR-Gs, and CCK8s-CCKBR-

Different binding patterns of CCK8s guide G protein bias 
In all three structures of CCK8s-CCKBR coupled with different G 
protein subtypes, CCK8s represents an inverted ‘‘fish hook’’ and 
is inserted deeply into the receptor core, with its C-terminal F8 
sitting at the bottom of the pocket (Figures 2B–2F and S3A– 
S3F). Therefore, elucidating the contribution of specific CCK8s 
interaction patterns to the coupling selectivity of different G pro-
tein subtypes may facilitate biased CCKBR agonist design. 
Through alanine mutation scanning, we found that mutations 
of the first three amino acids of CCK8s, including D1, Y2, and 
M3, impaired Gs activation more significantly than Gi and Gq 
activation (Figures 2H and S3H–S3J). The first four residues 
occupied the top region of the pocket (region T) (Figures 2C 
and 2D). Notably, Y2 and M3 of CCK8s formed stronger interac-
tions with P114 2.64 , Q204 ECL2 (extracellular loop 2), and 
H364 ECL3 in the CCK8s-CCBR-Gs complex structure than in 
the CCK8s-CCBR-Gq and CCK8s-CCBR-Gi complex structures

Structure-based rational design of 
Lyz-866, a Gi-biased CCKBR 
agonist

may help elucidate the functions of selec-
tive CCKBR-Gi signaling. Because deleting the four N-terminal 
residues of CCK8s completely abolished CCKAR activation but

G-protein-subtype-biased properties (Figures 3A, 3B, and

tion of the CCK4 N terminus increases the number of hydrogen 
bonds between CCK4 and H364 ECL3 of the CCKBR 
(Figure 3C). Consistent with this prediction, the potency of Ac-
CCK4-induced CCKBR activation of Gi signaling increased 
approximately 2.5-fold (Figures S4C and S4D; Table S1). In

Development of an exclusive Gi-biased CCKBR agonist, 
z-44
We noticed that Lyz-866 not only interacts with H376 7.39 and 
Y380 7.43 —residues that activate the Gi pathway—but also main-
tains contact with H207 ECL2 , a residue that specifically contributes 
to Gq activity in response to CCK8s stimulation. We therefore pro-
pose a strategy in which the two N-terminal residues of Lyz-866 are 
removed to reduce its contact with H207 ECL2 , resulting in 
increased Gi bias. Furthermore, we substituted D 7 with a hydrox-
ybenzo or other benzo derivative to improve the bioutilization of 
the agonist and replaced the C-terminal F 8 with different groups 
to obtain a series of small-molecule derivatives (Figures 3F, 3J, 
and S4O). Interestingly, one of these small-molecule compounds, 
z-44, completely lost the ability to activate Gq and Gs but retained 
a similar potency to Lyz-866 in the activation of Gi signaling down-
stream of CCKBR. Therefore, z-44 is a Gi-biased agonist of the 
CCKBR (Figures 3K, S4P, and S4Q; Table S2).
Next, we determined the cryo-EM structure of the z-44-
CCKBR-Gi1 complex (Figures 3F and S4R–S4U; Tables S1–
S3; Data S5). Notably, z-44 assumed a linear configuration 
similar to that of D 7 -F-(Br) 8 of Lyz-866, which is located at the 
bottom of the ligand pocket (Figures 3L, S4V, and S4W). Impor-
tantly, substitution of D 7 with a hydroxyl-benzo by z-44 enables 
stronger engagement with both H376 7.35 and Y380 7.43 , reorient-
ing these two residues to face outward from the ligand pocket, 
compared with the two residues in both Lyz-866- and CCK8s-
bound CCKBR structures (Figure S4W). The specific interaction 
of z-44 with CCKBR was supported by mutagenesis character-
ization (Figures S4X and S4Z).

We isolated primary cortical neurons from C57BL/6J mice to 
examine the protective effects of the Gi-biased agonist z-44 
and the Gq-biased agonist 3r1 against Aβ42 aggregation 
in vitro, with CCK8s used as a positive control. Whereas 3r1 
effectively increased the viability of primary cortical neurons in 
a concentration-dependent manner (EC 50 = 0.037 ± 0.001 nM), 
z-44 had no significant effect (Figures S6A and S6B). Consis-
tently, the administration of 3r1 (0.01 or 0.05 mg/kg) through 
intraperitoneal injection every day for 3 months, but not z-44 
(0.1 or 0.5 mg/kg), significantly rescued neuronal apoptosis in 
5×FAD mice (Figure S6C). Furthermore, the number of second-
ary branches and the axon length of neurons were significantly 
increased by the administration of 3r1 but not z-44 
(Figures S6D–S6F).

were used to evaluate the effects of z-44 and 3r1 on 5×FAD mice 
at 4 months of age in vivo (Figure 5A). In mouse pharmacokinetic 
studies, 3r1 showed a reasonable half-life and excellent brain 
penetration properties (Figures S4E and S4F). Notably, the 3r1-
treated 5×FAD group spent substantially more time in the plat-
form quadrant than the control group did, but the CCK8s- and 
z-44-treated group showed no significant change (Figures 5B,

NOR test, the 3r1-treated 5×FAD mice exhibited significantly 
improved cognitive function, as assessed by the discrimination 
rate. In contrast, CCK8s and z-44 treatment were ineffective 
(Figures 5D, 5E, and S6L). Considering that a reduction in 
learning and memory abilities is associated with impaired synap-
tic plasticity, we used a field excitatory postsynaptic potential 
(fEPSP) approach to record synaptic activity in 5×FAD mice 
(Figure 5F). Importantly, the impairment of hippocampal LTP in 
5×FAD mice was rescued by both CCK8s and 3r1 (Figure 5F).

3r1 exerts its beneficial effects through the 
upregulation of ADAM10 and PLCB4
We set out to explore the molecular mechanism underlying the 
improvement in cognitive performance in 5×FAD mice in 
response to 3r1 treatment. Amyloid plaques, as one of the patho-
logical hallmarks of AD, are known as the aggregation products of 
soluble Aβ42. 47 Notably, 3r1 treatment (0.01 or 0.05 mg/kg) signif-
icantly reduced the number of plaques in 5×FAD mice, whereas 
the administration of z-44 (0.1 or 0.5 mg/kg) did not significantly 
change the number of plaques (Figures 5H and 5I). We next per-
formed unbiased transcriptome analysis of the hippocampi of 
5×FAD mice treated with 3r1 compared with those of control 
vehicle-treated mice. Gene expression profiling revealed 537 
significantly upregulated genes and 422 significantly downregu-
lated genes (Figure 6A). Gene set enrichment analysis via Kyoto 
Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrich-
ment analysis revealed that the ‘‘calcium signaling pathway’’ 
and ‘‘cGMP-PKG (cyclic guanosine monophosphate-protein 
kinase G) signaling pathway’’ were significantly enriched in the up-
regulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in 5×FAD mice 
treated with 3r1 compared with vehicle-treated controls 
(Figure 6B). In contrast, the ‘‘AD pathway’’ was enriched in the 
downregulated DEGs (Figure 6B). Adam10, which is associated 
with ‘‘AD’’ and upregulated in response to 3r1 treatment 
(Figures 6C, S7A, and S7B), encodes α-secretase, a key enzyme 
that modulates the dynamics of Aβ42 aggregation. In general, the 
toxic Aβ peptide is generated by the digestion of amyloid precur-
sor protein (APP) by β- and γ-secretases in the amyloidogenic 
pathway. Parallel to this pathway, soluble amyloid precursor pro-
tein α (sAPP) is produced from APP by α-secretase digestion and 
is regarded as nontoxic. 48,49 We therefore measured the activity 
and expression levels of these three secretases. Compared with 
those in wild-type mice, the expression levels of β- or γ-secretase 
were increased in the hippocampi of 5×FAD mice, whereas the 
expression level of α-secretase (A Disintegrin And Metalloprotei-
nase domain-containing protein 10 [ADAM10]) was decreased
(Figures 6C, S7A, and S7B). 3r1 treatment had no significant effect 
on γ-secretase activity or the expression levels of β- or 
γ-secretase (Figures 6C, 6D, S7A, and S7B). However, the expres-
sion levels of α-secretase in 5×FAD mice, at both the mRNA and 
protein levels, were significantly increased in response to 3r1 
treatment (Figures 6C, S7A, and S7B). ADAM10 immunofluores-
cence in primary neurons also revealed that Aβ treatment signifi-
cantly reduced ADAM10 expression, whereas 3r1 treatment abro-
gated the effects of Aβ (Figures 6O and 6P). Consistently, free 
sAPP was increased by 3r1 treatment (Figure 6E). Moreover, the 
administration of 3r1 significantly increased the number of sec-
ondary branches and the axon length of the neuron, which was in-
hibited by GI254023X, a selective inhibitor of ADAM10 50 
(Figures 6G, 6H, and S7C). In addition, GI254023X decreased 
the protective effects of 3r1 on cell apoptosis (Figures 6I and 
S7J). We therefore speculated that the increased expression level 
of α-secretase, which consumes APP to inhibit amyloid plaque 
formation, in response to 3r1 treatment underlies the improve-
ment in cognitive performance in 5×FAD mice after 3rl treatment.

DISCUSSION
Because ligands of GPCRs have been demonstrated to be effec-
tive in the treatment of neuronal diseases such as schizophrenia 
and Parkinson’s disease, 53–55 the therapeutic potential of GPCR 
modulators in AD has attracted tremendous attention. 56–59 In 
particular, a selective partial agonist of the M1 muscarinic recep-
tor has been developed for AD treatment. In parallel with these 
studies, we reported that interactions between the GPCR mem-
ber CCKBR and its endogenous ligand CCK in the hippocampus 
have beneficial effects on learning and memory. 18,22 However, 
the idea of activating CCKBR to treat AD is difficult to test 
because endogenous peptide agonists such as CCK8s cannot 
pass through the BBB, and CCKBR activates many different G 
protein subtypes, which may have distinct functions. Here, by 
leveraging 7 structures of CCKBR bound by three different G 
protein subtypes, we were able to develop biased compounds 
with not only the ability to cross the BBB but also selectivity for 
specific G protein subtypes. At least one such compound, 3r1, 
has shown beneficial effects for AD treatment in animal models
through the biased activation of Gq signaling and increased 
α-secretase levels downstream of the CCKBR.
Comparison of the structures of the CCKBR in complex with the 
native agonist CCK8s and three G protein subtypes revealed that 
different G protein interaction patterns, including specific residues 
along the cytoplasmic side of the receptor to accommodate the 
binding of the αN and α5 helices, may induce structural rearrange-
ments of the TM2, TM6-7, and ECL regions. In particular, residues 
in TM6 and TM7, such as H207 ECL2 , W346 6.48 , H376 7.39 , and 
H380 7.43 , exhibited distinct conformational states to engage 
with CCK8s. Any mutation of these residues significantly affected 
the landscape of the bias toward different G protein subtypes, 
thus supporting the notion that the conformational changes inside 
the ligand pocket induced by downstream effectors engaging at 
the cytoplasmic side are determinants of preferential signaling 
of a particular GPCR. Peptide-based agonists of the CCKBR 
have been developed in the past. For example, pentagastrin has 
been approved by the FDA (Food and Drug Administration) for 
gastric function testing. The other two FDA-approved selective 
agonists of the CCKBR, BC 197 and BC 264, have been found 
to improve memory and attention but have side effects such as 
anxiety. 60,61 Because the CCKBR can activate multiple G protein 
subtypes, it is interesting to examine whether different G protein 
signaling pathways, such as the Gs, Gq, or Gi signaling pathways, 
are correlated with the functional selectivity of the CCKBR. The 
separation of side effects from therapeutic benefits has recently 
been explored in several agonists that target GPCRs, such as 
serotonin, opioid, adrenergic, prostaglandin, and dopamine re-
ceptors. 46,55,62–65 However, the selectivity of these agonists has 
not been investigated, which has hindered the development of 
precise therapeutics targeting CCKBR. The elucidation of seven 
structures of the CCKBR in complex with different G protein sub-
types in this study provides important guidance for biased 
CCKBR agonist design. Notably, the CCKBR pocket can be 
divided into three regions, which are named the top (T), middle 
(M), and bottom (B) regions, according to their positions. Exploit-
ing the correlation between the specific interactions in a particular 
region (T, M, and B) and the G protein subtype preferences greatly 
facilitates biased agonist design toward the CCKBR. For example, 
the exclusively Gi-biased agonist z-44 was developed by utilizing

Effective clinical interventions for AD are urgently needed. As 
one of the most critical drug targets in the clinic, GPCRs have 
garnered considerable interest for AD treatment, but no drugs tar-
geting GPCRs have yet been approved for such treatment. 
Among these drug candidates, those most closely related to clin-
ical usage are muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (mAChR) ago-
nists. Previous studies have shown that the activation of mAChRs 
enhances neuronal excitability and synaptic transmission, leading 
to improved cognitive function. 66–68 Although our previous studies 
revealed that the activation of the CCKBR improved cognitive 
function, promoted LTP, and enhanced performance in the 
MWM test in mice, direct experimental evidence supporting 
CCKBR as a therapeutic target for AD is lacking. In the present
study, the specific CCKBR-Gq-biased agonist of CCKBR, 3r1, 
but not the CCKBR-Gi agonist z-44, notably ameliorated memory 
impairment, promoted LTP, and improved learning and memory 
abilities in the MWM and NOR tests in animal models. These re-
sults provide direct evidence that CCKBR could be a promising 
therapeutic target for AD treatment. Further mechanistic analysis 
via unbiased transcriptomics revealed that 3r1 effectively dimin-
ishes Aβ deposition and inhibits tau protein phosphorylation via 
the ADAM10-plaque pathway. Moreover, 3r1 promoted synapse 
formation and neuronal survival in AD animal models through 
CCKBR-Gq-PLCB4 signaling pathways. The identification of the 
contributory roles of PLCB4 and ADAM10 in the anti-AD effects 
of 3r1 may broaden our scope for anti-AD treatment. Moreover, 
whether the expression levels of components of this pathway, 
including CCK, the CCKBR, and PLCB4, are altered in brain cells 
during AD development—and whether they can be used for early 
diagnosis—are worthy of future investigation.

links between CCKBR-Gq signaling and cGAS (cyclic GMP-
AMP synthase)-STING (stimulator of interferon genes)/type I 
interferon pathways—both central to AD-related neuroinflam-
mation—remain to be experimentally validated. The translational 
relevance of these findings also requires confirmation in human-
cell-based systems or primate models, particularly regarding the 
role of infectious triggers in CCKBR-driven immunity.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS
Human study
This study’s cohort received approval from the Ethical Committee of the China-Xuanwu Hospital (2024347). All participants provided 
written informed consent. CSF samples were collected from 78 individuals diagnosed with AD for subsequent analysis. Participants 
were categorized into two groups based on CDR global score of AD: mild AD (score=1) and severe AD (score=3).
Animals
All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Experimental Animal Ethics Branch of the Biomed-
ical Ethics Committee of Peking University Health Science Center (DLASBD0617). C57BL/6J mice aged 6-8 weeks were purchased 
from Laboratory Animal Center, Peking University Health Science Center. 5×FAD mice aged 8-10 weeks were purchased from 
Shanghai model organisms. CCKBR -/- mice (129-Cckbr tm1Kpn /J, 129/SvJ background) and 129/SvJ mice were purchased from 
Jackson Lab. Gnαq fl/fl and Gnαs fl/fl mice were obtained from Prof. RJ Lefkowitz (Duke University, Durham, NC). All mice (male) 
used in this study were kept in a specific pathogen free (SPF) environment at temperatures between 21 ◦ C and 23 ◦ C, maintained 
a 12:12 light: dark cycle, and had free access to water and food.
Cell lines
Human embryonic kidney293 (HEK293) cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and 
were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, penicillin (100IU/ml), and 
streptomycin (100mg/ml) in 5% CO 2 at 37 ◦ C. Spodoptera frugiperda (sf9) insect cells were obtained from Expression Systems 
(94-001S) and were cultured in ESF921 serum-free insect cell culture medium (Expression Systems, 96-001-01) at 27 ◦ C and 
110 rpm. Primary neurons were isolated from the cortices of E18 C57BL/6J mouse embryos and cultured in neurobasal medium sup-
plemented with B27, penicillin, and glutamine. The cells were seeded on poly-D-lysine-coated plates and maintained at 37 ◦ C in 5% 
CO2, with half of the medium being replaced every three days. All cell lines were authenticated by the vendor and tested for myco-
plasma contamination prior to each experiment using a PCR Mycoplasma detection kit (Thermo Scientific).
The cohort exclusion criteria consisted of the presence of cancer, pulmonary tuberculosis, AIDS, inflammatory bowel disease, 
hepatitis, cirrhosis, and recent antibiotic use within the past 3 months. All clinical information was collected following standardized 
procedures. The polypeptide protein components of CSF samples were enriched using Oasis PRiME HLB extraction cartridges and 
measured changes in their ability to activate Gi or Gq signalling of CCKBR.
METHOD DETAILS
Oscar ultra-low abundance quantitative proteomics
The CSF proteomics data were analyzed using the Oscar DIA proteomics platform of Shanghai Applied Protein Technology. This 
platform is a proteomics detection system based on the Thermo Fisher Orbitrap™ Astral™ high-resolution mass spectrometer. 
CSF samples, collected post-fasting via lumbar puncture, were uniformly processed and stored at -80 ◦ C. The samples were sent 
together to Applied Protein Technology platform to minimize batch effects and randomly distributed across plates. Proteins were 
enzymatically digested using an in-solution protocol. 73 A reducing agent was added to a final concentration of 20 mM, followed 
by incubation at 30 ◦ C for 2 h. After cooling to room temperature, an appropriate volume of alkylating reagent was added to achieve 
a final concentration of 40 mM. The mixture was oscillated at 600 rpm for 1 min and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 
30 min. The reaction was then terminated by heating at 95 ◦ C. Trypsin was added, and the solution was oscillated at 600 rpm and 
37 ◦ C for 2 h. Samples were then depressurized, lyophilized, and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid (FA). Peptide concentration was 
determined via absorbance at 280 nm (OD280) measurement.
A total of 0.4 μg of peptide segments, mixed with an appropriate amount of iRT standard peptides, was subjected to data-inde-
pendent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry. Chromatographic separation was performed using a nanoflow Vanquish Neo system 
(Thermo Fisher Scientific). Post-separation, samples were analyzed via an Astral high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher 
Scientific) in DIA mode under the following parameters: Ionization mode: Positive ion. Full MS scans (380–980 m/z) were acquired at 
240,000 resolution (at 200 m/z), with a normalized AGC target of 500% and maximum injection time of 5 ms. For DIA-MS/MS, 299 
variable isolation windows (2 m/z width) covering the 380–980 m/z range were fragmented using HCD (25 eV collision energy). MS/ 
MS spectra were acquired at 30,000 resolution, with a normalized AGC target of 500% and maximum injection time of 3 ms. Raw DIA 
files were processed using Spectronaut™ (v18, Biognosys).

Western Blot
For the detection of CCKBR protein levels, the cortical regions were dissected separately from brains of wild-type and CCKBR KO mice. 
Brain tissues were then homogenized in RIPA buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 0.5% NP-40, 4 mM EDTA, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% 
sodium deoxycholate and protease inhibitor cocktail) on ice for 15 min and centrifuged at 16,000 g, at 4 ◦ C for 30 min. The supernatant of 
brain lysates was then used for Western Blot. The concentrations of lysates were quantified by BCA assay. Then, the brain lysates were 
subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with TBS containing 5% non-fat milk at 
room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 ◦ C overnight, and with secondary antibodies at room tem-
perature for 1 h. After washing with TBST, the membrane was developed using the enhanced chemiluminescent detection system.
Immunofluorescence analysis
For immunofluorescence of both in vivo and in vitro samples, mice were deeply anesthetized with an overdose of pentobarbital so-
dium. We transcardially perfused the mice with 30 mL of cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 30 mL of 4% (w/v) paraformal-
dehyde (4% PFA). Brain tissue was removed, post-fixed with 4% PFA, and treated with 30% (w/v) sucrose in 4% PFA at 4 ◦ C for 2 to
3 days. Brain tissue was sectioned on a cryostat to 30 μm slices using a Leica TCS-SP8 DIVE, Germany, and preserved with an anti-
freeze buffer (containing 20% (v/v) glycerol and 30% (v/v) ethylene glycol diluted in PBS) at -20 ◦ C. For immunostaining and quanti-
fication of co-labelled neurons in the hippocampus, serial sections containing the hippocampus were selected for analysis. 
Simultaneously, for the in vitro cultured primary neurons, we directly plated the dissociated primary neurons onto confocal dishes 
for culture and subsequent immunofluorescence staining. Once the neurons reached the desired maturity in vitro, similar staining 
procedures as for the brain sections were followed. Specifically, the neurons were fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature for 
15 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. After blocking with a blocking buffer (10% (v/v) 
goat serum in PBS with 0.2% (v/v) Triton X-100) for 2 h at room temperature, the neurons were incubated with the primary antibody 
at 4 ◦ C for 24 to 36 h. After washing four times in PBS (10 min each), the neurons were incubated with the corresponding fluorophore-
conjugated secondary antibodies for 2.5 to 3 h at 25 ◦ C. The neurons were then rinsed three times with PBS before DAPI staining (at a 
dilution of 1:5000 (v/v) in PBS) or mounting. We mounted all the neurons with 70% (v/v) glycerol in PBS (standard staining) onto slides.

Novel object recognition test
The apparatus used was an open field plastic box measuring 40 × 40 × 50 cm in size. Objects of similar sizes and different shapes 
were used in the experiment. During habituation, each mouse was placed in the box for 10 min on two consecutive days and then 
returned to its home cage. On the third day, two similar objects were placed, and the mice were allowed to interact with the objects for 
10 min, after which they were returned to their home cage. After 1 h of training, one object was replaced with a novel object; mice were 
then reintroduced into the box to interact with the objects for 5 min. The objects and box were wiped with 70% ethanol after each trial 
to remove the odor of previous mice. The positions of the objects were consistent during training and testing. Object exploration was 
defined as sniffing, licking, climbing, or touching the object while facing it. Mice were excluded from the data analysis if they did not 
meet the following criteria during the retention trial: (1) a total exploration time of over 20 s, and (2) a minimum of 2 s exploration time 
each time on each object. Discrimination index was calculated as (T Novel -T Familiar ) / (T Novel + T Familiar ) × 100, where T Novel is time spent 
with novel object and T Familiar is time spent with familiar object.
Morris water maze (MWM)
The MWM was used to measure spatial learning and memory. The test was performed in a circular galvanized steel pool, 90 cm in 
diameter and 40 cm in height, filled with 20 cm of water tainted with non-toxic white paint. The pool was virtually divided into 
four equal quadrants, labeled north, south, east, and west. A camera connected to a video tracking system was mounted above 
the maze.
During training, a platform 10 cm in diameter, made of transparent acrylic plastic, was submerged 1 cm below the water surface 
and placed at a fixed location in one of the quadrants. If a mouse was not able to reach the platform within 60 s, it was guided to the 
platform, where it had to stay for 10 s before being returned to its home cage. All mice were given four trials per day, starting from each 
quadrant once, for 5 consecutive days. The starting position was randomized among the four quadrants of the pool. During training 
trials, the latency to reach the escape platform and the time spent in each quadrant were measured. A probe trial was performed 24 h 
after the last day of training. During the probe trial, mice were allowed to swim in the pool for 60 s without the platform present. Per-
formance during the probe trial was expressed as the percentage of time spent in the target quadrant.
In vitro Electrophysiology
After mice were sacrificed, whole brain was taken and immediately stored in ice-cold ACSF (124 mM NaCl, 26 mM NaHCO 3 , 10 mM 
Glucose, 3 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl 2 , 1.25 mM KH 2 PO 4 and 1.25 mM MgSO 4 , pH 7.4) (bubbled with 95%/5% O 2 /CO 2 ). Hippocampus or 
cortex was dissected from the whole brain. Coronal brain slices of the cortex (300 μm) or hippocampus (350 μm) were prepared using 
Vibrating Blade Tissue Slicer (Campden Instruments). The slices were immediately transferred to an incubator filled with 95%/5% O 2 / 
CO 2 bubbled ACSF at 28 ◦ C. After two hours incubation, the slice was selected and put into Microelectrode Array System (Alpha MED 
Scientific) probes to record extracellular field potentials in response to single stimuli delivered at 10 s-30 s intervals. TBS was used to 
induce long-term potentiation.
Pharmacokinetics
For pharmacokinetic studies, the compound CCK8s, 3r1, LYZ-866, z-44 were dissolved in 2 % DMSO/saline, and administered at 
doses of 1 mg/kg (intravenously) and 1 mg/kg (i.p.), respectively, with nine animals in each group. Blood samples (0.1 ml) were 
collected from mouse orbit at 0, 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 24 h, and were then centrifuged at 5,000 rpm at 4 ◦ C for 10 min 
to collect plasma samples. Brain tissues were collected at 0.5 and 2 h, and were washed with saline, weighed and homogenized 
in 50% cold methanol (brain weight (g)/50% methanol (ml), 1:3) to obtain drug solutions. All samples were stored at -80 ◦ C before 
analysis. Drug concentrations in the samples were determined using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry.
CSF enrichment and G protein dissociation assay
100 μL CSF fluid were collected from each of the 78 AD patients. The polypeptide protein components of CSF were enriched with 
Oasis PRiME HLB extraction cartridges following the manufacturer’s instructions. After eluting and freeze-drying, the polypeptide 
protein components were redissolved with 10 μL of DMSO.
The G protein dissociation assay was performed as previously described. 76 HEK293 cells were transiently co-transfected with 
CCKBR and G protein BRET probes using Lipofectamine 2000 (ThermoFisher Scientific). For the Gαq-Gβγ dissociation assay, G pro-
tein BRET probes used in this assay include Gαq-RLuc8, Gβ, and Gγ-GFP2. For the Gαi-Gβγ dissociation assay, G protein BRET 
probes used in this assay include Gαi-RLuc8, Gβ3, and Gγ9-GFP2. Thirty-six hours after transfection, cells were digested with trypsin 
(Gibco), centrifuged, washed twice with bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) buffer (25 mM HEPES, 1 mM CaCl 2 , 
140 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 0.9 mM MgCl 2 , 0.37 mM NaH 2 PO 4 , 5.5 mM D-glucose, 12 mM NaHCO 3 , pH 7.3) and resuspended in 
500 μL of BRET buffer before the BRET assay. After dividing the cells into 96-well white plates at 1 × 10 5 cells per well, cells 
were stimulated with enriched CSF at indicated concentrations, and luciferase substrate coelenterazine 400a were added at a 
working concentration of 5 μM. BRET signal was measured using a Mithras LB940 microplate reader (Berthold Technologies) 
equipped with 400 nm excitation and 515 nm emission filters. The BRET signal was calculated as the ratio of the light emitted at 
515 nm/400 nm.

cAMP accumulation assay
HEK293 cells were co-transfected with the wild-type or mutant CCKBR or vehicle (pcDNA3.1) and GloSensor plasmids using PEI 
reagent in 60 mm dishes. After incubated at 37 ◦ C for 24 h, cells were seeded into sterile 96-well culture plates at a density of 
40,000 cells/well at 37 ◦ C. Before tests, cells were incubated for 18 h in DMEM containing 1 μg/mL pertussis toxin (PTX) which irre-
versibly inhibits Gi/Go pathway. 77 The cells were washed three times with PBS and then preincubated with serum-free medium con-
taining 5% GloSensor cAMP reagent stock solution (Promega) for 2 h at 37 ◦ C. To detect the Gs pathway, cells were stimulated by 
different concentrations of ligands. The luminescence intensity was examined immediately using a Multimode Plate Reader 
(PerkinElmer).
cAMP inhibition assay
HEK293 cells were co-transfected with the wild-type or mutant CCKBR or vehicle (pcDNA3.1) and GloSensor plasmids using PEI 
reagent in 60 mm dishes. After incubated at 37 ◦ C for 24 h, cells were seeded into sterile 96-well culture plates at a density of 
40,000 cells/well for another twenty-four hours at 37 ◦ C. Cells were washed three times with PBS and then preincubated with 
serum-free medium containing 5% GloSensor cAMP reagent stock solution (Promega) for 2 h at 37 ◦ C. Before tests, cells were incu-

ulated by different concentrations of ligands together with 10 μM Forskolin. The luminescence intensity was examined immediately 
using a Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer).
TGF-α shedding assay
HEK293 cells were co-transfected with 2.5 μg AP-TGF-α, 1 μg wild-type or mutant CCKBR, and 0.5 μg Gq plasmids using PEI reagent 
in 60 mm dishes. After incubated at 37 ◦ C for 24 h, cells were harvested using trypsinization, followed by centrifuging. Cells were 
washed once with PBS and then resuspended in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) buffer. Cells were seeded into sterile 
96-well culture plates 90 μL per well and incubated for 30 min at 37 ◦ C. Then cells were stimulated by 10 μL different concentrations 
of ligands for 1h. After that, conditioned media (80 μL) was transferred to an empty well (conditioned medium). Solution (10 mM p-
NPP, 40 mM Tris–HCl pH 9.5, 40 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl₂) was added into the cell plates and the conditioned medium plates at 80 μL 
per well. A microplate reader was used to measure the absorbance at 405 nm after incubated for 0 h and 1 h at 37 ◦ C. AP-TGF-α 
release induced by ligand was calculated as ΔOD405 CM / (ΔOD405 CM + ΔOD405 Cell ) according to previous report. 78

System setup
We performed MD simulation for three models in a no-fusion/no-mutation background: (A) the CCK8s-CCKBR-Gi complex, (B) the 
CCK8s-CCKBR-Gq complex and (C) the CCK8s-CCKBR-Gs complex. For all models, the initial structures are based on the cryo-EM 
structures reported in this research with the removal of scFv16, the missing residues in different models including unsolved residue in 
CCK8s (D1), CCKBR C-termini residues until C405, N-termini for G alpha subunit and C-termini for G gamma subunit were modelled 
with MODELLER 95 and UCSF Chimera, the unsolved ICL3, N-termini and C-termini for CCKBR were not modelled in this study. The 
titratable residues are remained in dominant protonation state at pH 7, except for D100 2.50 , which was protonated to their neutral 
form, and E151 3.49 , which was protonated in majority of the simulation, as the previous research indicated these residues are 
conserved in protonated status in active class-A GPCRs. The G2 of the G alpha subunit was myristoylated, C3 of the G alpha subunit 
was palmitoylated, C68 of the G gamma subunit was prenylated as previous study of G protein lipid modification. 96 Neutral acetyl and 
methylamide groups were added to cap the N-termini and C-termini for the different protein chains, except for the natural C-termini of 
the G alpha and G beta subunit, which were carboxylated. The well-processed structures were aligned on the receptor in the crystal 
structure of CCK8s-CCKAR-Gq system in Orientation of Proteins in Membrane (OPM). 97 The aligned structures were inserted into 
pre-equilibrated 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) membrane bilayer. The water molecules were added 
to the system and Sodium chloride ions were added to neutralize each system at a concentration of 0.15M. All the systems were 
ensembled in CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder, 98 the final system comprised 237156 atoms including around 382 lipid molecules
and 56987 water molecules (the initial system size:120 A

Simulation protocols
We adopted CHARMM36m force field 99 for all the simulations while the associated TIP3P for water model, the parameters for 
sulfated CCK8 were generated with the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF). 100 After 7000 steps of steepest descent energy 
minimization followed by 5000 steps of gradient energy minimization, all the systems were heated from 0K to 310K over 125 ps in
NVT ensemble using a Langevin thermostate with harmonic restraints of 10.0 Kcal mol-1 A 
˚ -2 on the proteins and ligand. Equilibra-
tions were performed in NVT ensemble then in NPT ensemble in next three steps with harmonic restraints gradually reduced from 5.0
Kcal mol-1 A 
˚ -2 to 0.5 Kcal mol-1 A 
˚ -2 in step-wise manner every 4 ns for 16 ns, and finally performed in 0.1 Kcal mol-1 A 
˚ -2 restraints
for additional 10 ns. All the restraints were removed in production simulations. Production simulations were performed at 1 bar and 
310 K in the NPT ensemble with the Langevin thermostat and the Monte Carlo barostat, using a timestep of 4 fs while employing
hydrogen mass repartitioning methods. 101 The simulations used none-bonded interactions cut-off at 9.0 A 
˚ and particle mesh Ewald
method to calculate long-range of electrostatic interaction. Bond lengths were constrained using SHAKE. 102 For each system, three 
independent 500 ns production simulations were preformed, and trajectories were saved every 1 ns. All the simulations were per-
formed using Compute Unified Device Architecture (CUDA) version of particle-mesh Ewald molecule dynamics (PMEMD) under pe-
riodic boundary condition in Amber 20 software. 103 AmberTools 20 CPPTRAJ 93 package was used to post-process the trajectory, 
while PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.0 Schro¨ dinger, LLC.), Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) and USCF 
Chimera were used for visualization and analysis. 94

ABSTRACT: Gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) represent an emerging type of engineered nanomaterials with intrinsic enzymatic activity
for both chemical and biological applications, but the catalytic activity of most reported AuNCs remains rather limited. Herein, we
report a new, eﬃcient strategy of promoting the peroxidase-mimic activity of AuNCs by tailoring their catalytic interfaces via small
molecule-mediated weak interactions. Inspired by the presence of imidazole structures in many biocatalytic centers, we screened a
series of imidazole-containing small molecules to evaluate their impact on the enzymatic activity of AuNCs. Through monitoring the
absorbance change of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine, 1-methyl-2-imidazolecarboxaldehyde (MCA) was identiﬁed to possess the
most signiﬁcant eﬀect on enhancing the peroxidase-mimic activity of glutathione-stabilized AuNCs (GSH-AuNCs) among all the
examined molecules. Interestingly, the enhancement eﬀect of MCA on the catalytic activity of these AuNCs was found to be highly
reversible and can be switched on/oﬀby simply adding MCA/dialysis treatment. Molecular dynamics simulations and further
experimental analysis conﬁrmed that these MCA molecules were adsorbed on the surface of GSH-AuNCs through weak non-
covalent interactions. The underlying mechanism analysis suggested that the presence of MCA can eﬃciently promote the
production of •OH in the GSH-AuNC system. As a proof of example, we then demonstrated that the presence of MCA can greatly
increase the bioanalytical performance of AuNC-based peroxidase mimics, as evidenced by a 65-fold lower LOD for glucose
detection of AuNCs@MCA than that using AuNCs only. Finally, the present system has been successfully applied for sensing the
blood glucose level of both healthy people and diabetics with promising results.
KEYWORDS: gold nanoclusters, peroxidase mimics, nanozyme, catalytic interface, biosensing
1. INTRODUCTION
Engineered nanomaterials with enzyme-like catalytic activity,
known as nanozymes,1−3 have attracted enormous interest for
their high stability, low cost, and easy preparation in large
quantities. Until now, nanozymes have been widely harnessed
for applications in various ﬁelds such as biosensing,4−8
environmental protection,9,10 cancer therapy,11−13 immuno-
assay,14 antibacterial agents,15,16 and so on. Recently, gold
nanoclusters (AuNCs), composed of few to hundreds of gold
atoms, have emerged as a new type of promising nanozymes
due to their ultrasmall size, low toxicity, good stability, and
multiple enzyme activities.17−19 Therefore, AuNCs with
enzyme-like catalytic activity have been actively pursued for
the disease diagnosis and biosensing of various metal ions,
biomolecules, and cancer cells.20−25 However, compared with
natural enzymes and other reported enzyme mimics, the
potential application of these AuNCs remains largely re-
strained due to their relatively low catalytic activity.26−28

Regarding this, researchers have proposed several approaches
to enhance their catalytic activity, such as metal ion
modiﬁcation,29 inter-cluster assembly,30 and composition
modulation.31 However, these strategies either require
complicated synthesis or lack necessary controllability and
universality. Therefore, it is important to develop new,
alternative strategies capable of eﬃciently enhancing the
activity of these AuNC-based peroxidase mimics, which will
further advance their application for biosensing and disease
therapy.
Recent investigations disclosed the distinct eﬀect of surface
ligands on the biocatalytic activity of nanozymes including
AuNCs, underlying the potential of modulating their catalytic
activity via surface engineering.32−34 Moreover, Yan et al.35
found that histidine (His) can mimic the enzymatic micro-
environment of natural peroxidase to optimize the peroxidase-
like activity of iron oxide NPs, where the side-chain residue of
His, imidazole, was identiﬁed to be the key factor. Inspired by
these prior studies, we speculate that the biocatalytic process of
AuNCs could be modulated via reasonably engineering the
surface microenvironment of AuNCs by using distinct small
molecules, for example, imidazole-containing molecules.
Therefore, in the present study, we ﬁrst screened a variety of
imidazole-containing small molecules to evaluate their eﬀects
on the peroxidase-like catalytic activity of AuNCs. Interest-
ingly, 1-methyl-2-imidazolecarboxaldehyde (MCA) was iden-
tiﬁed to signiﬁcantly enhance the peroxidase-like catalytic
activity of AuNCs in oxidizing 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine
(TMB) in the presence of H2O2. Further experimental
investigation in combination with molecular dynamics (MD)
simulation was performed to reveal the underlying interaction
mechanism between AuNCs and MCA (Scheme 1). Finally, a
new AuNC-based colorimetric biosensing platform was
established, and its potential application for glucose biosensing
in serum samples was successfully demonstrated.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Materials and Apparatus. Chloroauric acid (HAuCl4),
glutathione (GSH), glucose, glucose oxidase (GOx, from Aspergillus
niger), bovine serum albumin (BSA), and cysteine were purchased
from Sigma-Aldrich. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was obtained from
Alfa Aesar. TMB, D-(+) sucrose, and D-(+) mannose were purchased
from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). MCA, terephthalic acid, D-(−)
fructose, lactose, and D-(+) maltose were bought from Adamas.
Na2HPO4 and NaH2PO4 were purchased from Sangon Biotech. All
the chemical reagents were used without further treatment.
A U-3900H spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan) was employed to
record the UV−vis absorption spectra. An FLS 980 spectroﬂuor-
ometer (Edinburgh Instruments, UK) was used to record the
ﬂuorescence spectra. A Talos F200X transmission electron micro-
scope (FEI, USA) was employed to take transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) images.
2.2. Synthesis of Glutathione-Stabilized AuNCs. Glutathione-
stabilized AuNCs (GSH-AuNCs) were prepared according to the
reported literature.36 Brieﬂy, 30 mL of HAuCl4 aqueous solution (4
mM) was mixed with 30 mL of GSH aqueous solution (6 mM) under
vigorous stirring at 90 °C for 6.5 h. Subsequently, the AuNC solution
was puriﬁed by adding 10 mL of methanol to 10 mL of the as-
synthesized AuNC solution. Then, this solution was centrifuged, the
supernatant was discarded, and the precipitates were washed with
methanol before drying under vacuum. The obtained GSH-AuNCs
were stored in the dark at 4 °C until use.
2.3. Synthesis of GSH-Stabilized AuNPs, Cysteine-Stabilized
AuNCs, and BSA-Stabilized AuNCs. The synthesis method of
GSH-stabilized AuNPs (GSH-AuNPs) is as follows:37 1 mL of
HAuCl4 aqueous solution (25 mM) was mixed with 7.8 mL of GSH
aqueous solution (19 mM) under vigorous stirring, and the pH value
of the mixture solution was adjusted to 8 with 1 M NaOH aqueous
solution. Subsequently, 4.73 mL of freshly prepared NaBH4 ice water
solution (2 mg/mL) was added under rapid stirring. The color of the
mixed solution changed to dark red, and GSH-AuNPs were obtained
by continuous stirring at room temperature for 12 h. The AuNP
solution was centrifuged to remove possible precipitation. Then, the
upper solution was puriﬁed through ultraﬁltration centrifugation to
remove small impurities. The obtained GSH-AuNPs were stored in
the dark at 4 °C until use.
The synthesis method of Cysteine-stabilized AuNCs (Cys-AuNCs)
is as follows: 8.7 mL of NaOH aqueous solution (12 mM) was mixed
with 0.3 mL of Cys aqueous solution (100 mM) and stirred for 3 min
at room temperature to make it mix evenly. Then, 1.0 mL of HAuCl4
aqueous solution (20 mM) was added to the mixture and then stirred
vigorously (1200 rpm) at 90 °C for 6.5 h. The synthesized Cys-
AuNCs were puriﬁed through ultraﬁltration centrifugation to remove
free ions and excess ligand molecules and then stored in the dark at 4
°C until use.
The synthesis method of BSA-stabilized AuNCs (BSA-AuNCs) is
as follows:38,39 10 mL of HAuCl4 aqueous solution (10 mM) was
added to the BSA aqueous solution (10 mL, 50 mg/mL) and stirred
vigorously for 2 min before adding the NaOH aqueous solution (1
M). The pH value of the mixture solution was then adjusted to 12 and
further reacted at 37 °C for 12 h, during which the color of the
solution changed from light yellow to dark brown. The synthesized
BSA-AuNCs were puriﬁed through ultraﬁltration centrifugation to
remove impurities and then stored in the dark at 4 °C until use.
2.4. Experiments on the Peroxidase-like Activity of GSH-
AuNCs. Brieﬂy, the experiments were performed by employing a
mixture of GSH-AuNCs (the concentration was calculated to be 4
mM based on Au atoms), H2O2 (30 mM), and TMB (0.5 mM) in
phosphate-buﬀered saline (PBS, 0.1 M, pH 3.0). All the solution was
incubated for 15 min before recording the absorption spectra. A
temperature range of 25−65 °C and a pH range of 2.0−7.0 were
chosen to optimize the experimental conditions. The biocatalytic
activity of both GSH-AuNCs and GSH-AuNPs with/without
imidazole-containing molecules was studied with similar procedures.

2.6. Colorimetric Sensing with GSH-AuNCs and GSH-
AuNCs@MCA. To evaluate the H2O2 sensing performance, 50 μL

3.0) solution. Next, the mixture solution was incubated in a 55 °C

nm using a UV−vis absorption spectrophotometer. For the glucose
standard curve measurement, 25 μL of 1 mg/mL glucose oxidase

100 μL of 10 mM PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min.
Then, 50 μL of GSH-AuNCs, 25 μL of 30 mM TMB, and 1.275 mL

then employed for the subsequent experiments. Similar procedures
were performed for the detection of H2O2 and glucose by using GSH-
AuNCs@MCA.
To evaluate the glucose detection in real biological samples, the
human serum samples (provided by a local hospital) were ﬁrst
ultraﬁltrated to remove large aggregates and particles in the serum
samples. Then, the ﬁltrate was diluted 90 times and measured under
similar conditions reported above.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Eﬀects of MCA on the Peroxidase-like Activity of
GSH-AuNCs. GSH-AuNCs were ﬁrst prepared by the
reported methods and characterized by TEM imaging and
molecular spectroscopy. As shown in Figure S1, the diameter
of GSH-AuNCs is 1.9 ± 0.3 nm. The ﬂuorescence excitation
spectrum of GSH-AuNCs shows a maximum at 425 nm, which
corresponds to the shoulder peak in the absorption spectrum.
As expected, these GSH-AuNCs exhibit strong orange
ﬂuorescence features under UV illumination, and the emission
spectrum shows a maximum at 640 nm upon excitation at 425
nm, which agrees well with the literature.40
Then, we elaborately selected nine types of imidazole-
containing molecules, including imidazole (Im), benzimidazole
(BIm), 2-methylimidazole (2-MI), 2-imidazolecarboxaldehyde
(2-ICA), 4-imidazolecarboxaldehyde (4-ICA), 2-imidazolecar-
boxylic acid (2-ICAC), 2-nitroimidazole (2-NI), MCA, and
histidine (His). Their structures are presented in Figure 1a.
GSH-AuNCs have been reported to possess peroxidase-like
activity that can oxidize colorless TMB to oxTMB in the
presence of H2O2.20,41 Indeed, the formation of a blue-colored
product was observed in the TMB-H2O2 solution in the
presence of GSH-AuNCs. When further adding the above
imidazole-containing molecules, the absorbance of the typical
TMB-based colorimetric system shows diﬀerent extents of
changes (Figure 1b,c). Particularly, the absorbance was
signiﬁcantly increased in the case of MCA and 2-ICA
compared with the control sample, suggesting that both
molecules pose a remarkable eﬀect on the peroxidase-like
activity of GSH-AuNCs. In contrast, there is rather a weak or
even slight decrease in the absorbance of the solution.

In order to elucidate the distinct role of MCA on the
peroxidase-like activity of AuNCs, we ﬁrst studied the enzyme
mimetic activity of MCA alone toward the substrate TMB. As
depicted in Figure 2a, the absorption spectrum of the TMB−

peroxidase-like catalytic activity. However, after introducing
MCA into the reaction solution containing AuNCs (denoted
as GSH-AuNCs@MCA), the peroxidase mimetic activity of
AuNCs was signiﬁcantly increased by ca. 6-fold, as reﬂected by
a signiﬁcantly enhanced absorbance at 652 nm. Simulta-
neously, the color of the reaction solution turned from light
blue to deep blue because of more generated oxTMB in the
solution.
As other nanozymes, the catalytic activity of AuNCs is
strongly inﬂuenced by environmental parameters such as pH
and temperature as well as the concentration of the catalyst. As
shown in Figure 2, GSH-AuNCs@MCA exhibited a higher
catalytic activity than GSH-AuNCs in a wide pH range of 2.0−
6.0, whereas the most signiﬁcant enhancement was observed at
pH 3.0. Indeed, the acidic environment is reported to be
favorable for the peroxidase activity of many nanozymes.
Similar to GSH-AuNCs, GSH-AuNCs@MCA exhibited a
good, stable activity in the temperature range of 25−65 °C,
and the optimal temperature was identiﬁed to be 55 °C
(Figure 2c). Moreover, the concentration of MCA had a strong
inﬂuence on the catalytic activity of GSH-AuNCs@MCA. By
increasing the molar ratio of MCA to Au from 0 to 120, the
absorbance of the solution increased gradually. Further
increasing the amount of MCA will result in a slightly
decreased absorbance. Thus, a molar ratio of 120 was selected
for the subsequent experiments (Figure 2d). Taken together,
the highest enhancement on the catalytic activity of GSH-
AuNCs@MCA can be obtained at pH 3.0, 55 °C, and a molar
ratio of MCA to Au of 120.
3.2. Enhancement Mechanism on the Catalytic
Activity of GSH-AuNCs@MCA. The underlying mechanism
on the MCA-enhanced peroxidase-like activity of AuNCs was
next investigated. The steady-state kinetic parameters are vital
indicators for evaluating the peroxidase-like activity of GSH-
AuNCs. As shown in Figures S2 and S3, typical Michaelis-
Menten curves of GSH-AuNCs and GSH-AuNCs@MCA were
obtained with either TMB or H2O2 as the substrate. The
kinetic parameters (Km and Vm) were calculated from
Lineweaver−Burk plots, and the results are summarized in
Table 1. Typically, a small Michaelis-Menten constant (Km)
indicates a strong aﬃnity between the enzyme and the
substrate, while the maximum reaction velocity (Vm) is
positively correlated with the catalytic activity of the enzyme.
Compared with GSH-AuNCs, GSH-AuNCs@MCA possess
lower Km values toward H2O2, suggesting that GSH-AuNCs@
MCA exhibit higher aﬃnity for H2O2 compared to GSH-
AuNCs. The Vm values of GSH-AuNCs@MCA toward the
H2O2 and TMB substrates are larger than those of GSH-
AuNCs, indicating that GSH-AuNCs@MCA possesses stron-
ger peroxidase-like activity than GSH-AuNCs. The above
results conﬁrm that GSH-AuNCs@MCA exhibits a higher

AuNCs pose a signiﬁcant eﬀect on their interactions with
MCA, which then inﬂuences the catalytic activity of AuNCs.
Then, we wonder whether MCA can pose a similar eﬀect on
the catalytic activity of GSH-stabilized gold particles with a
diﬀerent size. To answer this question, we synthesized GSH-
AuNPs with an average diameter of 3.5 nm (Figure S6),
signiﬁcantly larger than the size of GSH-AuNCs, by the well-
established method.37 As shown in Figure 3b, compared with
GSH-AuNCs, these GSH-AuNPs exhibit a rather weak
peroxidase-like activity, and the addition of MCA did not
result in any observable eﬀect, as reﬂected by negligible
absorbance change (Figure S6d). Large-sized AuNPs are
known to possess weak catalytic activity, which has been
attributed to the strong passivation of their surface atoms by
Au-thiol bonds. In contrast, AuNCs possess a higher
population of low-coordinated gold atoms as well as the
presence of the Au(I)−thiolate complex, which were
considered to be essential for their catalytic activity.45,46
3.3. Weak Interactions between MCA and GSH-
AuNCs. To further elucidate the mechanism of the MCA-
enhanced catalytic activity of GSH-AuNCs, we ﬁrst checked
whether the size of GSH-AuNCs is changed after being
incubated with MCA by using TEM. As shown in Figure S7,
the diameter of GSH-AuNCs after being incubated with MCA
was 1.7 ± 0.4 nm, suggesting that the size of AuNCs is not
changed in the presence of MCA. Previous studies found that
the catalytic activity of gold nanozymes is strongly dependent
on the valence of Au on their surfaces.47,48 Thus, XPS was then
performed to characterize the valence state of Au in GSH-
AuNCs before and after adding MCA. As shown in Figure 4a,
the binding energy of both Au 4f5/2 and Au 4f7/2 is
essentially similar for GSH-AuNCs and GSH-AuNCs@MCA,
which locates at 88.1 and 84.4 eV, respectively. Apparently, the
introduction of MCA did not aﬀect the chemical environment
of Au in GSH-AuNCs. Both results indicate that the
interactions between MCA and GSH-AuNCs are rather weak
despite the remarkable enhancement eﬀect of MCA on the
catalytic activity of AuNCs.
In order to conﬁrm the weak interaction nature of MCA
with GSH-AuNCs, we dialyzed the mixture solution of GSH-
AuNCs@MCA against water to see whether MCA can
dissociate from the surface of AuNCs via the diﬀusion. As
shown in Figure 4b, the absorption spectrum of GSH-
AuNCs@MCA (blue curve) displays a strong absorption
band with the maximum at 289 nm and a weak shoulder peak
at 392 nm, which are attributed to the absorption from MCA
(red curve) and AuNCs (black curve), respectively. After the
dialysis of GSH-AuNCs@MCA by using a membrane with an
MWCO of 1000, the absorption spectrum changed to the

MCA aqueous solution was ﬁrst established. The spatial
distribution of AuNCs, GSH, and MCA was described by
counting the relative concentration along the z-axis direction,
as shown in Figure 5b. It showed that the spatial distribution of
AuNCs, in the central region of simulated MCA aqueous
solution, mainly exists in the range of 2.7−4.7 nm, which is
consistent with the AuNC model with a diameter of 1.9 nm.
The ligand GSH displays a relatively high distribution located
mostly around AuNCs, as shown in Figure 5a. Meanwhile, the
relative concentration of MCA shows a small peak, followed by
a uniform distribution far from the central region, within 2.2−
5.5 nm. This gives the quantitative proof of Figure 5a that
some MCA molecules, which initially present as a homoge-
neous dispersion in aqueous solution, penetrate into the GSH
coatings and even ﬁnally reach the AuNC surface, such as a few
pink MCA molecules adsorb closely on the AuNC surface (in
the right part of Figure 5a).
The radial distribution function (RDF) of GSH and MCA
around the center of mass (COM) of AuNCs was further
calculated to reveal the spatial feature and the interaction
mechanism of MCA enhancing the catalytic activity of AuNCs.
As depicted in Figure 5c, the RDF of GSH molecules around
the COM of AuNCs shows a prominent peak at 1.2 nm.
Considering that the radius of the simulated AuNCs is 0.95
nm, these surface Au atoms are closely integrated with GSH
molecules, as low as 0.25 nm. For MCA, while the RDF
exhibits a main peak at 1.7 nm, it also shows a small shoulder
peak at 1.2 nm, which is at the same position as that of GSH.
This small shoulder peak reﬂects the occurrence of a
population of MCA molecules penetrating into the GSH
layer and locating near the surface of AuNCs, as shown in
Figure 5.

3.4. Biosensing Based on Enhanced Peroxidase-like
Activity of GSH-AuNCs@MCA. Since the peroxidase-like
activity of nanozymes is dependent on H2O2 concentration, we
then established GSH-AuNCs@MCA-TMB reaction systems
for detecting H2O2 under the optimal reaction conditions.
Particularly, with an enhanced peroxidase-like activity, GSH-
AuNCs@MCA is expected to endow the colorimetric sensing
system with higher sensitivity than that using GSH-AuNCs
only. The absorption experiments showed that upon raising the
H2O2 concentration, the absorbance of ox-TMB at 652 nm in
the presence of either GSH-AuNCs@MCA or GSH-AuNCs
gradually increases (Figure S10). However, the absorbance is
signiﬁcantly higher for GSH-AuNCs@MCA than that for
GSH-AuNCs only, which is attributed to the higher
peroxidase-like catalytic activity of the former. As shown in
Figure 6a, when using GSH-AuNCs only as the nanozymes,
the plots show a good linear relationship (R2 = 0.997) in the
range from 0.1 to 30 mM. The limit of detection (LOD) was
calculated to be 74.7 μM for H2O2 based on the formula LOD
= 3 S/K, where S and K are the standard deviation of signals of
a blank sample and the slope of the ﬁtted calibration curve,
respectively. However, when GSH-AuNCs@MCA is used as
the peroxidase mimic, the linear plots display a much larger
slope than that of GSH-AuNCs. Herein, the LOD was
calculated to be 9.8 μM, which is about 7.6-fold lower than
the value of GSH-AuNCs, suggesting a signiﬁcantly enhanced
detection sensitivity for H2O2.
With the promising performance of the GSH-AuNCs@
MCA system for colorimetric H2O2 sensing, we next exploited
their application in glucose sensing. In general, glucose can be
oxidized by glucose oxidase to produce H2O2 in the presence
of oxygen. As shown in Figure 6b, in the case of GSH-AuNCs,
the absorbance at 652 nm versus the glucose concentration in
the range of 100−500 μM shows a good linear response (R2 =
0.990), and the LOD is 86.9 μM. Similar to that in H2O2
sensing, when using GSH-AuNCs@MCA as nanozymes, the
colorimetric response of the sensing system possesses an LOD
below 1.34 μM, which is ca. 65-fold lower than that of GSH-
AuNCs (Figure 6c). Actually, the sensitivity of our GSH-
AuNCs@MCA-based approach is comparable or even better
than those achieved by using many other nanomaterials,51−54
as also listed in Table S1.
To evaluate the selectivity of GSH-AuNCs@MCA for
glucose detection, control experiments were carried out by
comparing the absorbance at 652 nm after the addition of
various sugars, including lactose, sucrose, fructose, maltose,
mannose, and glucose. As shown in Figure 6d, lactose, sucrose,
and fructose all showed almost no optical response, while
maltose and mannose showed a slight absorbance increase,
whose value is 5-fold lower than that of glucose. Meanwhile,
compared to the glucose level, the amount of maltose and
mannose in serum is much lower; thus, possible inﬂuence of
these two sugars for glucose sensing can be neglected.
Furthermore, we evaluated the potential application of the
present system for glucose sensing in real samples. For this
purpose, six serum samples from the local hospital were
chosen, where samples 1−3 were collected from healthy people
and samples 4−6 from diabetics (Table 2). The concentration
of glucose in serum was calculated through a standard addition
method based on the GSH-AuNCs@MCA sensing system as
we established above. As listed in Table 2, the results obtained
by our colorimetric method are in good agreement with the
results that were measured by the Beckman Coulter AU 400
automatic biochemical analyzer. The relative error of our
experimental results compared with the standard glucose assay
kit is less than 5% for all the six tested samples, suggesting the
good reliability and accuracy of our GSH-AuNCs@MCA
system for clinical diagnosis of biological samples.
4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we have reported a new strategy to enhance the
catalytic activity of AuNCs by reasonably engineering their
catalytic interfaces via weak interactions. As demonstrated in
our study, the presence of imidazole-containing MCA on the
surface of GSH-AuNCs can enhance the peroxidase-like
activity by 6-fold, although interactions between AuNCs and
MCA prove to be so weak that simple dialysis can diminish the
enhancement eﬀect. Mechanistic studies revealed that MCA
penetrates through the GSH layer and locates near the surface
of AuNCs, which then promotes their capability in generating
•OH and leads to enhanced oxidation of TMB. Consequently,
these MCA-mediated AuNCs exhibit an enhanced colorimetric
sensing performance, as evidenced by a 65-fold lower LOD for
glucose detection than that using GSH-AuNCs only. These

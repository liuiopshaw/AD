ABSTRACT: Silver is among the most essential antimicrobial
agents. Increasing the efficacy of silver-based antimicrobial
materials will reduce operating costs. Herein, we show that
mechanical abrading causes atomization of Ag nanoparticles
(AgNPs) into atomically dispersed Ag (AgSAs) on the surfaces
of an oxide-mineral support, which eventually boosts the
antibacterial efficacy considerably. This approach is straightfor-
ward, scalable, and applicable to a wide range of oxide-mineral
supports; additionally, it does not require any chemical additives
and operates under ambient conditions. The obtained AgSAs-
loaded γ-Al2O3 inactivated Escherichia coli (E. coli) five times as fast
as the original AgNPs-loaded γ-Al2O3. It can be utilized over 10
runs with minimal efficiency loss. The structural characterizations indicate that AgSAs exhibit a nominal charge of 0 and are
anchored at the doubly bridging OH on the γ-Al2O3 surfaces. Mechanism studies demonstrate that AgSAs, like AgNPs, damage
bacterial cell wall integrity, but they release Ag+ and superoxide substantially faster. This work not only provides a simple method for
manufacturing AgSAs-based materials but also shows that AgSAs have better antibacterial properties than the AgNPs counterpart.
KEYWORDS: single-atom catalyst, atomically dispersed Ag, top-down, ball milling, antimicrobial agent, reactive oxygen species
■INTRODUCTION
Bacterial resistance to conventional antibiotics is a serious
worldwide public health issue in the 21st century.1−3 Silver
products, particularly those containing metallic Ag nano-
particles (AgNPs), have been widely used in bandages,
catheters, fabrics, and air/water purification systems due to
their broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties. Perhaps be-
cause AgNPs outperform bulk Ag, AgNPs have been
extensively researched and widely used as a key antibacterial
agent for a long time, and hundreds of tons of AgNPs are used
annually around the world.4−6 While conventional AgNPs-
based antimicrobial materials release the primary active
species, namely, Ag+ and the accompanying reactive oxygen
species (ROS), considerably faster than bulk Ag,7−13 their
efficacy is nevertheless restricted by the thermodynamic
stability of AgNPs’ crystal lattice associated with the high
energy of Ag−Ag metallic bonds. As a result, AgNPs may still
be unsatisfactory for many daily tasks, such as bandages and
sanitizers, which require the antibacterial agent to be highly
fast-acting,8,14 so there is still research interest in further
improving the performance of Ag-based antibacterial materials.
In this regard, improving Ag’s antibacterial efficacy in releasing
active species is highly desirable since it helps to significantly
reduce operating costs by decreasing the inactivation time
and/or the dosages of antibacterial agents.
Several strategies for improving AgNP’s antibacterial efficacy
by enhancing active species release have been explored and
demonstrated to be effective.8 One of these is the preparation
of AgNPs/support composites using unique supports,
particularly graphene and graphene oxide.15−27 AgNPs and
graphene-based supports were found to have synergistically
increased, robust antibacterial effects due to the very fast
release of Ag+ and ROS even at low doses.28 Another is the use
of semiconductors such as ZnO with light irradiation, which
could significantly boost the antibacterial efficacy of AgNPs
due to the light sensitivity of both semiconductors and
plasmonic AgNPs.29,30 It has also been found that Ag-
containing alloy NPs, such as Ag−Fe,31 Ag−Pt,32 and Ag−
Au−Pd,33 exhibit substantially stronger O2 activation and
consequently antibacterial properties than pure AgNPs. These
approaches, however, are not general due to complex material
production protocols or the requirement for specific supports.

Recently, single-atom-based materials (SAMs) have emerged
as intriguing alternatives to nanoparticles-based materials
because of their well-defined and uniform structure, 100%
atomic efficiency, and most enticingly, high (catalytic)
activity.34−37 SAMs have shown superior performances than
the nanoparticle counterparts in a variety of applications such
as oxidation, hydrogenation, dehydrogenation, reform, electro-
catalysis, and photocatalysis. We anticipate that because
breakdown of Ag−Ag bonds is no longer necessary, atomically
dispersed Ag (AgSAs)-based antimicrobial agents may have
significantly stronger antibacterial activity than the AgNPs-
based counterparts. Thus, converting an AgNPs-based
antibacterial agent into its AgSAs-based counterpart could
significantly improve the agent’s antibacterial performance,
which is ideal from an application standpoint. Indeed, there
have been a few AgSAs-based SAMs previously. However, their
AgSAs are exclusively Ag+, which is strongly coordinated as a
structural component at the surfaces of certain supports like
carbon nitride,38 α-MnO2,39,40 and γ-Al2O3.41 These SAMs are
likely unable to generate the antimicrobial species Ag+ and
ROS. In contrast, there is still a lack of methods for loading
antimicrobial active AgSAs onto the inexpensive and nontoxic
oxide-mineral-based supports for antibacterial applications;
additionally, it is unclear whether such an Ag-SAM could
exhibit multiplying increased antibacterial activity and what
mechanism it employs compared to AgNPs.
In this study, we present a novel approach for loading AgSAs
onto oxide-mineral supports. Namely, by mechanical abrading,
the AgNPs of a readily available AgNPs-loaded oxide-mineral
sample, such as AgNPs-loaded γ-Al2O3, could be mechano-
chemically broken down into AgSAs. This approach signifi-
cantly enhances the antibacterial rate and efficacy of Ag
because the resultant AgSAs/oxide agent could very effectively
activate molecular O2 to generate Ag+ and superoxide. The
antimicrobial performances, the scope of the method, the
chemical environment of AgSAs on γ-Al2O3 surfaces, the
antibacterial mechanism, and the stability in Escherichia coli
inactivation were systematically investigated. To our knowl-
edge, this is the first report on the top-down atomization of
noble-metal nanoparticles into atomically dispersed metal
under mild conditions, as well as the first report on using
AgSAs for releasing Ag+ and superoxide toward antimicrobial
applications. Our findings suggest that certain Ag-SAMs can be
used as fast-acting and durable antibacterial agents.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Materials. AgNO3 (≥99.8%) and NaBH4 (98%) were
purchased from Sinopharm Group Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd.,
China. Ethylene glycol and poly(vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP, MW
= 58,000) were purchased from Tianjin Fuyu Fine Chemical
Co. Ltd. 2,2,6,6-Tetramethyl-1-piperidinyloxy (TEMPO),
cysteine, γ-Al2O3 (300−400 mesh), α-Al2O3 (80 nm), SiO2
(500 nm), TiO2 (anatase, 100−300 nm), α-Fe2O3 (30 nm),
Fe3O4 (200 nm), SnO2 (50−70 nm), hydroxyapatite (HAP,
200 nm), nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT), parachlor-
obenzoic acid (pCBA), and furfuryl alcohol (FFA) were
purchased from Macklin Organics Co. Ltd., China. Superoxide
dismutase (SOD) and E. coli were purchased from Shanghai
Shifeng biology Co. Ltd. All of the above chemicals were used
without further purification. All aqueous solutions were
prepared with ultrapure water (18.25 MΩ·cm−1).
Instruments. Aberration-corrected high-angle annular
dark-field scanning TEM was carried out on a JEM ARM
200F instrument (HAADF-STEM; JEOL, Japan) or an FEI
TalosF200x instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating
at 200 kV. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra
were recorded on a JOEL X320 instrument at room
temperature. A Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB 250
instrument was used for X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS). Before analysis, all samples were kept at 473 K and
below 10−3 Pa for 6 h. 27Al and 1H solid-state magic-angle-
spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (MAS NMR) spectra
were recorded at 11.7 T on a Bruker Advance III 500 MHz
spectrometer equipped with a 4 mm double-resonance probe.
Prior to the 1H NMR measurements, the samples were pre-
dehydrated under vacuum at 473 K for 12 h. Ag K-edge X-ray
absorption spectroscopic (XAS) spectra were recorded in
fluorescence mode at room temperature, using the BL14W1
XAFS beamline at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation
Facility. Each sample was scanned 20 times. The signal drifted
slightly, but it was corrected. The X-ray absorption near-edge
structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine
structure (EXAFS) data reduction and analysis were carried
out using the Athena program, which is included in the
IFFEFIT software package.
Preparation of Ag/Al2O3. An AgNPs colloidal solution
was prepared first following the literature method with some
modification.42 In detail, 0.76 g of AgNO3 and 2.0 g of PVP
were dissolved in 100 mL of ethylene glycol while stirring.
Then, 20 mL of deionized water containing 0.2 g of NaBH4
was added dropwise with vigorous stirring. The resultant
solution was ultracentrifuged, washed multiple times with
water, and dried overnight under vacuum at 60 °C. Finally, the
AgNPs solid was redispersed into a 1 g L−1 aqueous solution
and stored at 4 °C in the dark before use. The average particle
diameter (Figure S1) and ζ-potential of the fresh solution were
15.3 nm and −24.2 mV. After 1 week of storage, both values
barely changed, and meanwhile the fraction of dissolved
AgNPs determined by inductively coupled plasma-mass
spectrometry (ICP-MS, PerkinElmer NexION 350X) was ca.
1/106. Hence, the stability of AgNPs could ensure subsequent
studies. To remove any soluble contaminants and increase the
coverage of surface hydroxyl groups, γ-Al2O3 was pretreated in
water at 180 °C in a Teflon-lined autoclave. XPS data show
that the fraction of surface OH groups in total oxygen varied
from 31.9 to 40.0% before and after the pretreatment (Figure
S2). Finally, 2 g of γ-Al2O3 was dispersed in 26 mL of
deionized water in a 50 mL centrifuge tube, and 4 mL of the
above AgNPs solution (containing 4 mg of AgNPs) was added.
The suspension was sonicated for 5 min to promote AgNPs
adsorption by γ-Al2O3. The solid material was then isolated by
centrifugation, washed several times with water, and finally
dried overnight under vacuum at 60 °C. This sample is labeled
Ag/Al2O3−0. It was fully dissolved in 1.0 M HNO3, and the Ag
loading was determined to be 0.2 wt % using ICP-MS.
Grinding Method. 1 g of Ag/Al2O3−0 was manually
ground in an agate mortar with a 10 cm diameter at a speed of
about five cycles per second under ambient conditions. For
convenience, the obtained sample was named Ag/Al2O3-time;
for example, Ag/Al2O3−5 denotes the sample obtained after 5
min of grinding. Note that time here refers to the total actual
work time.
To prepare samples in large quantities, a mortar grinding
machine (model F-RM200; FOCUCY Experimental Instru-
ment Co., Ltd) was employed. For this, 6 g of Ag/Al2O3−0
was added to the agate mortar each time, and the tungsten

Assay of ROS. NBT, pCBA, and FFA were used as

(model JEM-1011, JEOL).
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Enhancement of Antimicrobial Activity by Grinding.
Initially, a representative antimicrobial nanomaterial,8 i.e.,
AgNPs-loaded γ-Al2O3 sample (denoted as Ag/Al2O3−0),
was chosen as a model to investigate the effects of grinding on
the antimicrobial performance (Figure 1A). γ-Al2O3 was
investigated first because it is nonreducible, inert in E. coli
inactivation, and rich in surface hydroxyl groups. The color of
the Ag/Al2O3−0 sample changed considerably during grinding,
going from yellowish to gray gradually. When the grinded
samples were used for inactivation of E. coli, an enhancement
of performance by grinding was seen immediately. Specifically,
while Ag/Al2O3−0 left a large amount of E. coli cells after 6 h,
the grinded samples showed both faster inactivation and higher
inactivation efficiency (Figure 1B). The 20 min-grinded
sample, i.e., Ag/Al2O3−20, could inactivate all E. coli cells
within 3 h. A longer grinding time did not improve the
antibacterial performance anymore (Figure S3). The anti-
bacterial rate constant, kapp, was then calculated using the
Geeraerd model (eq 1).43,44 As shown in Figure 1C, the kapp
value for Ag/Al2O3−0 is 1.06 h−1, increases for the grinded
samples, and reaches a plateau of 4.98 h−1 for Ag/Al2O3−20.
Following the success in increasing the antimicrobial activity
of AgNPs-loaded γ-Al2O3 by grinding, we set out to see if this
method could be scaled up. Thus, we used a mortar grinding
machine to mill the Ag/Al2O3−0 sample (Figure 2A,B). This
proof-of-concept test yielded over 5 g of the resultant material,
which showed a 4-fold acceleration of E. coli inactivation. We
also examined the generality of this method and found that for
the AgNPs-based materials of the common oxide minerals,
including α-Al2O3, TiO2, Fe2O3, Fe3O4, SiO2, SnO2, and
hydroxyapatite (HAP), grinding generally greatly increased
their antimicrobial activities (Figure 2C).
Characterization of the Grinded Samples. Next, Ag/
Al2O3−0 was chosen as a model to study the roles of grinding
on the structures of the AgNPs-loaded nanocomposites. It was
found that grinding did not affect the loading of Ag, BET area,
Al/O ratio, or phase of γ-Al2O3 (Tables S1 and S2 and Figures
S5 and S6). In contrast, the high-resolution transmission
electron microscopy (HR-TEM) images show that the average
diameter of AgNPs gradually decreased with grinding time (see
Figures 3A and S7A−C). However, after 20 min of grinding
(Ag/Al2O3−20; Figure S7D), AgNPs became barely detect-
able, indicating that the grinding process can break down
AgNPs into smaller Ag species. The diffuse reflectance UV−vis

based on atomically dispersed Ag(I).38−41,52 The low oxidation
state of AgSAs could also be verified by a NaBH4 soaking test.
For this, a Ag/Al2O3−20 sample was soaked in a large amount
of fresh NaBH4 aqueous solution (50 mM) at room
temperature for 30 min. However, the EPR signal intensity
was maintained, and meanwhile, no nanoparticle or cluster
formed according to a series of electron microscopic images
(Figure S10), which indicates that the Ag species in the soaked
samples are still highly dispersed. Notably, NaBH4 is a strong
reducing agent that has been widely applied for the preparation
of AgNPs by reducing Ag+ ions. Thus, the soaking test firmly
indicate that AgSAs in Ag/Al2O3−20 are irreducible.
Furthermore, as will be demonstrated later, Ag/Al2O3−20
could reduce molecular O2 into superoxide in water via a
single-electron transfer (SET) process, suggesting that the
nominal valence state of AgSAs on Al2O3 is 0.
Having the above information in hand, we then conducted a
detailed characterization of the chemical structure of Ag
species in the most active sample Ag/Al2O3−20 by using AC
HAADF-STEM, high-energy-resolution XAS, and MAS NMR.
The energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) mapping analysis revealed
homogeneous distributions of Ag, O, and Al elements on the
Ag/Al2O3−20 surfaces (Figure 4A), whereas large aggregations
of Ag were absent. According to the AC HAADF-STEM
images which are atomic resolution, numerous small,
individual bright dots uniformly and randomly distributed on
the Ag/Al2O3−20 surfaces (Figure 4B). The remarkable
difference in Z-contrast between Ag and Al/O indicates the
small bright dots are the atomically dispersed Ag species. The
high-energy-resolution XAS with a synchrotron radiation light
source is a powerful, element-selective, and highly sensitive
technique that has been widely used in characterizing SAMs. It
provides statistical information on the coordination environ-
ment and chemical state of the absorbing species, particularly
those with a large Z-contrast to the background elements.
Herein, it was used to characterize the valence state and
coordination configuration of the Ag species in Ag/Al2O3−20
in detail. In the Ag K-edge XANES spectra, the absorption
threshold of Ag/Al2O3−20 is close to that of Ag foil but far
from that of Ag2O (Figure 4C, indicated by the arrow),
suggesting that the Ag atoms of Ag/Al2O3−20 have a low
oxidation state, which is consistent with the XPS and EPR
results (recall Figure 3). Figure 4D shows the Fourier
transform k3-weighted EXAFS spectra of Ag/Al2O3−20, Ag
foil, and Ag2O at the Ag K-edge. In the case of Ag foil, the
intense Ag−Ag coordination peak was observed at 2.7 Å. For
Ag2O, the most intense peak, centered at 1.7 Å, is attributed to

To summarize, the above evidences indicate that AgNPs on
the γ-Al2O3 surfaces were broken down into near-neutral
AgSAs during the grinding process, which is a mechanochem-
ical process.62 It can also be inferred that OH-μ2 moieties are
important for the atomization of AgNPs because they serve as
anchoring sites.
Han et al. recently reported the first-time use of ball milling
to convert metallic Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu into their SAMs.62
However, the metals are limited to those that in the reactivity
series rank higher than or close to hydrogen, and meanwhile,
high-purity N2 was used as protecting gas. To the best of our
knowledge, the current study is the first to show that noble-
metal nanoparticles can be atomized into atomically dispersed
metal under mild conditions. The DFT analyses indicate that
the migration of an AgSA from a AgNP surface to the
hydroxylated γ-Al2O3 surfaces is thermodynamically favorable
(see the Supporting Information for discussion). Hence,
grinding plays the role of overcoming the kinetic barrier for
this transformation since this mechanochemical process62 can
induce high local temperature and pressure.
Mechanism of E. Coli Inactivation. To understand the
antibacterial mechanism of the AgSAs-containing materials,
temporal changes of morphology and membrane integrity of E.
coli cells due to the interaction with Ag/Al2O3−20 were
evaluated by TEM. The untreated E. coli cells were typically
rod-shaped with smooth and intact cell walls. When treated
with Ag/Al2O3−20, the cells adsorb many Ag/Al2O3−20
particles on their outer surfaces. Then, the cell walls gradually
became blurred and damaged, leading to leakage of intra-
cellular contents and accretion of the cells, and meanwhile, the
shape and size of cells also changed dramatically (Figure 6).
Hence, AgSAs on the Al2O3 surfaces function as bactericides
against E. coli through disrupting the bacterial cell wall
integrity. This is similar to the roles of AgNP-based
antimicrobial agents in E. coli cell damage in the previous
reports.12,26,27
It has been known that the release of Ag+ ions, accompanied
with the production of ROS, determines the efficacy of AgNPs
in antibacterial applications.10−12 Hence, to understand why
Ag/Al2O3−20 exhibited considerably higher antimicrobial
activity than Ag/Al2O3−0, we examined their activities in the
formation of both Ag+ and ROS. The formation of Ag+ was
studied by dispersing Ag/Al2O3−20 or Ag/Al2O3−0 in water
and incubating it at 37 °C while stirring. The amount of
released Ag+ during this process was determined by ICP-MS
after removing the nanoparticles. As shown in Figure 7A, Ag/
Al2O3−20 released nearly 7-fold more Ag+ ions than Ag/
Al2O3−0 at 6 h. On the other hand, ROS chemical probes like
NBT, which is a superoxide capture, were added to the above
suspension during incubation so that the formed ROS could be
captured in situ for quantification. Superoxide was detected as
the only ROS in both Ag/Al2O3−0 and Ag/Al2O3−20 systems,
while other ROSs like OH, 1O2, and H2O2 were not detected
(Figures 7B and S15). Importantly, Ag/Al2O3−20 generated
much faster superoxide than Ag/Al2O3−0 (Figure 7B). Note
that although the conditions in the aforementioned tests
differed slightly from those used to inactivate E. coli, it could
still be inferred that Ag/Al2O3−20 produced Ag+ and
superoxide faster than Ag/Al2O3−0 during the antimicrobial
process.

if Ag+ ions and superoxide were the genuine active species

Following the mechanism of superoxide generation by AgNPs
which is an SET process,13 the formation of superoxide by Ag/
Al2O3−20 can also be attributed to the SET reduction of
molecular O2 by AgSAs. This confirms that the atomically
dispersed Ag species in Ag/Al2O3−20 have a nominal valence
state of 0. Furthermore, the faster release of Ag+ and
superoxide of Ag/Al2O3−20 than Ag/Al2O3−0 implies that
when an AgNP is broken down into AgSAs, it is more active in
the SET reduction of O2. This is attributed to the absence of
Ag−Ag metallic bonds in Ag/Al2O3−0, suggesting that using
AgSAs could be a viable strategy for overcoming the kinetic
barrier associated with the breakdown of the crystal lattice
structure of AgNPs during O2 activation. On the other hand,
since AgSAs release the same active species as AgNPs, they
should be of low cytotoxicity toward mammalian cells.
Recycling Tests. The recovery and reusability of
antimicrobial nanomaterials are essential for their use in real-
world applications. In this regard, the recycling experiments
showed that after 10 successive cycles, Ag/Al2O3−20 still
exhibited excellent activity in the inactivation of E. coli (Figure
8A). We examined the loss of AgSAs by using EPR signal
intensity and found that ca. 35% of AgSAs was consumed
(Figure 8B). We also characterized the recycled sample after
the 10th run. A series of electron microscopic analyses (Figure
S17) indicated that no AgNPs or nanoclusters formed in the
recycled sample.
The results presented thus far have demonstrated that
AgSAs should exhibit the following advantages over AgNPs:
(1) significantly higher activity, which drastically decreases
inactivation time and allows for lower doses, and (2) rapid
action, which implies AgSAs do not require a long induction
period. Therefore, our findings suggest that AgSAs may be
used as a quick acting but durable enough antimicrobial agent
alternative to AgNPs.

DNA Programmable Peroxidase Activity of Oxidized
Polypyrrole Quantum Dots
Wei Yang, Tianchi Leng, Hao Sun, Weicheng Miao, Xinyi Wang, Dou Xu, Can Zhang,
and Yimin Fang*
Signiﬁcant progress has recently been made in the application of oxidized
graphene (GO) quantum dots as enzyme mimics in various biomedical ﬁelds
due to their bio-compatibility and excellent solubility in physiological media.
However, their catalytic performance and controllability are barely satisfactory.
Here, this study constructs oxygen-functionalized polypyrrole quantum dots
(o-ppy QDs) with excellent peroxidase activity in a mild condition. Compared
with oxidized graphene QDs, o-ppy QDs exhibit superior catalytic eﬃciency
(120 times higher than HRP). More importantly, it is found that guanine (G)
and adenine (A) bases possess higher binding aﬃnities to o-ppy QDs. G base
is able to signiﬁcantly increase the peroxidase activity while A base decreases
the activity, providing a fascinating method to precisely regulate the catalytic
activity of o-ppy QDs in a programmable manner by the design of DNA
sequences. The enhancement on the peroxidase by G base regulation is
attributed to the existence of carbonyl group that promotes its catalytic
activity, while A base tends to block the original carbonyl group on o-ppy QDs.
Based on this feature, a colorimetric and ﬂuorescent dual-mode biosensor for
detecting DNA methylation is developed. This study holds signiﬁcant
theoretical and practical implications for the development of nanozymes and
precise regulation of their catalytic activity.

In this study, we proposed a new method for the synthesis of
o-ppy QDs by top-down and bottom-up approaches. Compared
with other carbonyl-functionalized graphene QDs, o-ppy QDs
can be prepared in a mild condition and exhibit superior cat-
alytic activity as evidenced by their Michaelis-Menten kinetic con-
stant, which is even 120 times higher than HRP. The binding
aﬃnity of o-ppy QDs to various DNA strands was investigated.

that this work will provide valuable insights into the further de-
velopment and regulation of a variety of novel high-performance
enzyme mimics through simple and eﬃcient synthesis strategies
and surface DNA regulation methods.
2. Results and Discussion
Previous researches have demonstrated that the carbonyl group
present in carbon nanomaterials can serve as an active site for ox-
idative dehydrogenation, which can be commonly found in GO,
carbon nanotubes, and graphene QDs.[13,14] Polypyrrole exhibits
a sp2 hybrid arrangement similar to carbon atoms; however, the
preparation of graphene oxygen-functionalized quantum dots re-
quires the use of concentrated acid at high temperature,[14b,19]
while o-ppy QDs can be prepared at a milder condition as ppy
is much more active than graphene-like materials. In this work,
o-ppy QDs were prepared using a simple, safe, and rapid top-
down and bottom-up approach (Figure S1, Supporting Infor-
mation). Firstly, ppy NPs were synthesized via chemical oxi-
dation polymerization of pyrrole monomer in the presence of
(NH4)2S2O8.[20] Subsequently, the as-prepared ppy NPs under-
went peroxide reaction in the presence of high concentrations of
H2O2 to produce oxidized polypyrrole segments that were rich in
carbonyl, hydroxyl, and carboxyl groups.[21] Finally, the carbonyl
group rich o-ppy QDs were obtained by carbonization through
a hydrothermal reaction process, in which a signiﬁcant number
of hydroxyl and carboxyl groups were removed from the oxidized
polypyrrole segments.
The o-ppy QDs were synthesized with an average diameter
of 8 nm, which were conﬁrmed by dynamic light scattering
(DLS) result (Figure S2, Supporting Information) and transmis-
sion electron microscope (TEM) image in Figure 2a. The inset of
Figure 2a showed the adjacent lattice fringe with an inner-plane
spacing at 0.38 nm, corresponding to an interlayer spacing of ppy
NPs.[21] In the oxidation process, oxygen functional groups such
as carbonyl, hydroxyl, or carboxyl groups were introduced to car-
bon nanomaterials. To conﬁrm the oxygen functionalization of
o-ppy QDs, Fourier infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) analysis was
initially conducted to verify the introduction of various oxygen
groups on their surfaces. As depicted in Figure S3 (Supporting
Information), the peaks observed at 1715 and 3357 cm−1 corre-
spond to the characteristic vibrations of carbonyl and hydroxyl
groups, respectively. Notably, the oxygen absorption peak in our
o-ppy QDs exhibited signiﬁcantly higher intensity than that of
its precursor ppy NPs, providing a strong evidence for the suc-
cessful introduction of oxygen functional groups into quantum
dots. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were utilized to fur-
ther investigate the surface distribution of various oxygenation
groups on both quantum dots and their precursors. As illustrated
in Figure 2b, after semiquantitative characterization via XPS, the

The
classic
peroxidase
substrate
3,3′,5,5′-
tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) was chosen as the model catalytic
reaction in the presence of H2O2 to investigate the peroxidase-
like activity of o-ppy QDs. As depicted in Figure 3a, o-ppy QDs
exhibited remarkably high peroxidase-like activity. In contrast,
ppy NPs showed negligible enzymatic activity under identi-
cal experimental conditions, indicating that carbonyl groups
played a crucial role during biocatalysis (Figure 3b). Similar
to other nanozymes, o-ppy QDs exhibited concentration, pH,
and temperature-dependent behavior as peroxidase mimics
(Figure 3c,d). In particular, o-ppy QDs maintained good enzyme
activity under both low and high temperature, highlighting their
potential wider applications.
To further verify the high biocatalytic performance result-
ing from the abundance of carbonyl groups, we established the
Michaelis–Menten model and analyzed the kinetic parameters
of our o-ppy QDs and classical graphene QDs in Figure S4 and
Table S2 (Supporting Information). The Lineweaver–Burk plot
yielded values for maximum initial velocity (Vmax) and Michaelis–
Menten constant (Km). As it is well known, the Km value rep-
resents a constant that reﬂects the binding aﬃnity between en-
zymes and substrates, while the Vmax value indicates the turnover
number of enzymes which reﬂects their biocatalytic activity. By
utilizing H2O2 and TMB as substrates, we determined that o-
ppy QDs have a superior aﬃnity for H2O2 substrate and greater
catalytic ability for substrate TMB with Km values of 0.107 and
0.133 × 10−3 m, respectively, along with corresponding Vmax val-
ues of 16.0 × 10−8 M s−1 and 34.5 × 10−8 M s−1 (Figure S4, Sup-
porting Information). More notably, the Km and Vmax values of
o-ppy QDs were signiﬁcantly lower and higher than those of clas-
sical graphene-like oxidized quantum dots, respectively. The re-
duced Km value and increased Vmax value can be attributed to the
abundant carbonyl groups on the surface of o-ppy QDs, which
served as binding sites and active sites for substrates during
biocatalysis.

O-ppy QDs possessed a plethora of carbonyl structures, which
might exhibit a strong binding aﬃnity to DNA strands through
hydrogen bonds. The binding aﬃnity between o-ppy QDs and
polyA, polyT, polyC, as well as G-rich DNA strands was inves-
tigated via agarose gel electrophoresis (Figure 4a). Our results
demonstrated that o-ppy QDs exhibited robust interaction with
polyA and G-rich DNA strands, leading to the formation of
high molecular weight bands. To further validate this speciﬁcity,
changes in 𝜁potential before and after binding were analyzed
(Figure 4b) using G-rich strands as an example. The 𝜁potential
value of the conjugation was signiﬁcantly lower than both G-rich
strands and o-ppy QDs, conﬁrming successful binding of G-rich
strands to o-ppy QDs.[14a] In addition, the G-rich chain’s speciﬁc
recognition by o-ppy QDs was conﬁrmed using DNA dye 4s red
plus. Figure S6 (Supporting Information) shows that the UV–Vis
absorption peak of 4s red plus overlapped with most of the ﬂu-
orescence emission peak of o-ppy QDs, indicating the possible
ﬂuorescence energy resonance transfer (FRET). The binding of
G-rich and o-ppy QDs resulted in a signiﬁcant reduction of their
distance, leading to an increased FRET eﬀect. Therefore, the ﬂu-
orescence attenuation of o-ppy QDs and ﬂuorescence enhance-
ment of 4s red plus can be observed in Figure 4c, indicating their
speciﬁc interaction between o-ppy QDs and G-rich sequences.
It is well known that G-rich DNA strands are able to form G-
quadruplex (G4) structures in the presence of K+.[22] As depicted
in Figure 4d, thioﬂavine T (ThT) can insert to G4 structure to gen-
erate strong ﬂuorescence signals, making it as an eﬃcient way to
indicate the DNA structure. Based on this principle, we can ex-
plore the structure of G-rich chains while they interact with o-ppy
QDs. The ﬂuorescence intensity of ThT was investigated in var-
ious systems (Figure 4e). Compared with the absence of G-rich
strands, a strong ﬂuorescence intensity can be observed while
ThT was inserted into G4 structure. However, in the presence of
o-ppy QDs, the ﬂuorescence intensity of the system drastically
decreased, suggesting that o-ppy QDs competitively bound to G-
rich strands, and overwhelmed the G4 structure even in the pres-
ence of K+. To further conﬁrm the high binding aﬃnity of o-ppy
QDs with G-rich strands, we ﬁrstly added K+ to G-rich chains to
form G4 structure before introducing o-ppy QDs. It was found
that a signiﬁcant decrease of ﬂuorescence intensity was observed
after the addition of o-ppy QDs, indicating its high binding aﬃn-
ity to G-rich strands that unfolded G4 structure even if it had al-
ready been formed. This was also further supported by agarose
electrophoresis in Figure 4f, in which o-ppy QDs could open pre-
formed G4 structures (lane 4). Thus, it was believed that o-ppy
QDs ﬁrmly bound to the G-rich strands, which can unfold the
conventional G4 structure.

The regulation mechanism may involve an increase in car-
bonyl content on the surface of o-ppy QDs. As illustrated in
Figure S14 (Supporting Information), the binding of o-ppy QDs
to G-rich strands increased the surface carbonyl group on the
QDs, which signiﬁcantly enhanced the catalytic activity. Con-
versely, when combined with a poly A strands without carbonyl
groups, the original carbonyl group on o-ppy QDs was blocked,
thus o-ppy QD activity was inhibited, conﬁrming our hypothesis
in the introduction section. Although the adsorption through G
base consumes the carbonyl group, it generates the new active
site (carbonyl group) at the same time, thus it does not compro-
mise the activity. Moreover, as shown in the supporting informa-
tion Table S1 (Supporting Information), each G-rich strand con-
tains many G bases. As the G bases in the DNA strands can not
be comempletely employed to block the active sites, the excessive
G bases can increase the overall carbonyl groups, thus the activity
is increased. Therefore, the peroxidase activity of o-ppy QDs can
be positively and negatively regulated by programming the base
sequences and DNA lengths.
Various factors, such as exposure to toxic chemicals, oxida-
tive damage, and ionizing radiation,[23] might result in the struc-
ture change of DNA bases, which serve as signiﬁcant indicators
of diseases. These factors have a profound impact on the con-
formation of non-canonical DNA structures; for instance, gua-
nine is susceptible to alkylation while G-rich sequences tend
to adopt non-canonical conformations.[24] A thorough exami-
nation of structural alterations caused by nucleotide modiﬁca-
tion can be conducted through X-ray crystallography or multidi-
mensional Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy.[25]
However, these methods are costly, time-consuming, and de-
structive. Enzyme sensing exhibits an inherent exponential am-
pliﬁcation process and is a powerful tool for detecting even sub-
tle structural alterations in various biological targets,[26] mak-
ing it as a potential rapid method for probing such a subtle
structural change.
It has been revealed that G-rich chain enhanced the catalytic
ability of o-ppy QDs by increasing the content of carbonyl groups
on their surface, then 6-site oxygen methylation on base G (6-mG)
in the G-rich chain is likely to aﬀect the catalytic ability of o-ppy
QDs (Figure 6a). 6-mG not only destroyed the carbonyl group
in G base, but also attenuated the binding aﬃnity between G
base (without protonation) and o-ppy QDs, thereby compromis-
ing the enhancement eﬀect of G-rich chain on peroxidase activ-
ity of o-ppy QDs. As shown in Figure S15 (Supporting Informa-
tion), methylation in the G-rich DNA strand inhibited peroxidase
activity of o-ppy QDs, which further conﬁrmed our hypothesis.
Thus, the detection of methyl-G methylation in G-rich chains can
be achieved based on this principle. The binding pH and tem-
perature between o-ppy QDs and G-rich chains were optimized
(Figure S16, Supporting Information). It was found that the high-
est binding eﬃciency occurred under pH 5 at a temperature of
45 °C. The concentrations of o-ppy QDs and G-rich DNA were
optimized to achieve optimal colorimetric and ﬂuorescence sig-
nal enhancement eﬀects. Speciﬁcally, a concentration ratio of 1:1
or 0.5:1 between the two components was found to be most ef-
fective (Figure 6a,b). The presence of G-rich chains was observed
to enhance both enzymatic catalysis and ﬂuorescence properties
of o-ppy QDs. Ultimately, under these optimized conditions, de-
tection of colorimetric and ﬂuorescence signals for G-rich DNA
as well as methylated G-rich DNA at varying concentrations was
achieved using o-ppy QDs. We observed that the diﬀerentiation
between methylated and unmethylated G-rich chains was eﬀec-
tively achieved through colorimetric and ﬂuorescent signals, with
a detection limit of 2 × 10−9 m. Therefore, o-ppy QDs can serve
as a dual-signal probe for detecting G-rich methylation. Interest-
ingly, the colorimetric signal exhibited more pronounced diﬀer-
ences in response to subtle changes in DNA methylation due to
the inherent exponential ampliﬁcation reaction by nanozymes.
Additionally, we were able to discern G-rich methylation as low
as 2 × 10−3 m in the diluted serum (5%, Figure S17, Support-
ing Information) by colorimetric method using o-ppy QDs as
nanozymes.
3. Conclusion
In summary, we have successfully synthesized carbonyl-rich
polypyrrole quantum dots (o-ppy QDs) with high peroxidase ac-
tivity through a simple, fast and safe method. Notably, it was
found that G and A bases exhibited higher binding aﬃnities to o-
ppy QDs. The presence of G base signiﬁcantly enhanced the per-
oxidase activity while A base inhibited, providing an interesting
approach for programmatically regulating the catalytic activity of
o-ppy QDs. The reason was attributed to the increase or blockage
of carbonyl groups on o-ppy QDs after the modiﬁcation of G-rich
or poly-A DNA strands respectively. The o-ppy QDs were utilized
as highly eﬃcient peroxidase mimics and dual-signal probes for
the speciﬁc recognition and methylation detection of G-rich DNA
strands. Our design provides more evidence that the increase of
surface carbonyl group on nanozymes was an eﬃcient way to fur-
ther promote their peroxidase activity, but also held signiﬁcant
theoretical and practical implications for precisely regulating cat-
alytic activity on nanozymes.
4. Experimental Section
Synthesis of o-ppy QDs:
O-ppy QDs were synthesized using a combi-
nation of top-down and bottom-up approach (Figure S1, Supporting Infor-
mation). In brief, polypyrrole nanoparticles were prepared via chemical oxi-
dation polymerization. An amount of 30 μL of pyrrole monomer (99%) was
dissolved in 12 mL of pure water with stirring. Then, 3 mL of 0.14 mol L−1
ammonium persulfate was added and placed in an ice water bath for mag-
netic stirring reaction for 3 h. The black products obtained by centrifuga-
tion were washed thrice with ethanol and water, respectively. Subsequently,
they were placed in dialysis bags with a molecular weight cut-oﬀof 3000 Da
for 24 h before being dried at 60 °C under vacuum conditions. The prod-
uct was stored at room temperature for future use. To prepare the oxi-
dized polypyrrole segments solution, 1 mL of hydrogen peroxide solution
(1 mol L−1) was added to a mixture containing 5 mL of the nanoparti-
cle solution (1 mg mL−1), which was then reacted in a water bath main-
tained at 45 °C for 2 h. As the black products dissolved, the solution transi-
tioned from dark to a pale yellow. The resulting solution was subsequently

A Gaussian 09 program was used to optimize the geometry of A, T, C,
and G bases and o-ppy QDs. For the ab initio method, the required sim-
ulation time grows exponentially with the number of the atoms involved.
In order to simplify the simulation, this work just considered the major
contributing atoms from QDs here. The major functional group, carbonyl
group containing segment instead of the whole o-ppy was simulated. The
N atom adjacent to the carbonyl group was saturated by CH3 group to
avoid its further interaction with the bases. The binding aﬃnity between
four DNA bases and o-ppy QDs structure was explored by calculating the
energy changes after their combination. The binding eﬀects of o-ppy QDs
and poly A, poly T, poly C, and G-rich DNA strands were further veriﬁed by
agarose electrophoresis.

DNA Methylation Detection:
The recognition of o-ppy QDs by G-rich
sequences through hydrogen bonding led to ampliﬁcation of both enzyme
activity and ﬂuorescence. The colorimetric signals generated by the cataly-
sis of substrate TMB by o-ppy QDs were analyzed. However, if the methy-
lation damage aﬀects the binding aﬃnity of o-ppy QDs with G bases in
the G-rich sequence, it can hinder electron transfer ability of o-ppy QDs,
resulting in a decline in enzyme activity and ﬂuorescence as well as a de-
crease in colorimetric signal and ﬂuorescence value (Figure 1c).

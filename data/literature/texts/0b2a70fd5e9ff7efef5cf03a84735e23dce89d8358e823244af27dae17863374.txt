Assembling two different types of NPs into

With the realization of programmable 
assembly of NPs, it would be highly desir-
able to achieve similarly exquisite control 
over the NP itself, such as the structure 
and functionality. For gold, the atoms 
typically adopt the fcc packing in both the 
bulk and nanoparticle forms.[31] In the past 
decade, significant efforts have been made 
in achieving atomically precise, ultras-
mall gold NPs (often called nanoclusters, NCs, with core sizes 
of 1–3 nm). More importantly, their total structures have been 
determined by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD). The 
current atomically precise nanochemistry is indeed moving 
toward programmable synthesis of NCs with control over the 
structure, such as the bcc, fcc, hcp, decahedra (Dh), icosahedra 
(Ih), multi-tetrahedral (Td) network, etc., (Scheme 1, right). Such 
successes provide an excellent opportunity to correlate the 
structure of NCs with their properties such as optical absorp-
tion. Besides, recent progress in controlling NCs with atomic 
precision has opened new horizons, as the quantum-sized 
NCs exhibit fundamentally different properties from their plas-
monic counterparts (5–100  nm).[32,33] Specifically, the attained 
atomic level insights reveal that the kernel structure plays deci-
sive roles in the optical properties,[34] while the organic–inor-
ganic interface is decisive to chirality and catalysis,[35,36] and the 
surface ligand shell is critical to assembly and photolumines-
cence.[37,38] We foresee that the advances in nanochemistry will 
be capable of producing atomically precise NC materials that 
rival, and to some extent exceed, what conventional molecular 
chemistry has achieved at the molecular scale.
Among the appealing physicochemical properties of NPs, 
the optical properties have long been one of the major topics 
in nanoscience.[1–6,39–41] Gold NPs account for the red color of 
stained glass in church windows, whereas silver NPs are typi-
cally yellow. Scientific studies on the optical properties of Au 
NPs date back to Faraday’s time. Later, Mie solved the Max-
well’s equation for spherical particles and successfully simu-
lated the extinction (i.e., scattering + absorption) spectra.[42–44] 
With the recent establishment of atomically precise nanochemistry, capa-
bilities toward programmable control over the nanoparticle size and struc-
ture are being developed. Advances in the synthesis of atomically precise 
nanoclusters (NCs, 1–3 nm) have been made in recent years, and more 
importantly, their total structures (core plus ligands) have been mapped out 
by X-ray crystallography. These ultrasmall Au nanoparticles exhibit strong 
quantum-confinement effect, manifested in their optical absorption prop-
erties. With the advantage of atomic precision, gold-thiolate nanoclusters 
(Aun(SR)m) are revealed to contain an inner kernel, Au–S interface (motifs), 
and surface ligand (-R) shell. Programming the atomic packing into various 
crystallographic structures of the metal kernel can be achieved, which plays a 
significant role in determining the optical properties and the energy gap (Eg) 
of NCs. When the size increases, a general trend is observed for NCs with fcc 
or decahedral kernels, whereas those NCs with icosahedral kernels deviate 
from the general trend by showing comparably smaller Eg. Comparisons 
are also made to further demonstrate the more decisive role of the kernel 
structure over surface motifs based on isomeric Au NCs and NC series with 
evolving kernel or motif structures. Finally, future perspectives are discussed.
1. Introduction
Controllable synthesis to obtain precise materials through 
design is one of the major scientific goals but it is highly chal-
lenging. With breakthroughs in solution phase nanochemistry, 
the synthesis of nanoparticle (NPs) has witnessed significant 
progress over the past 20 years, from excellent size control to 
shape control.[1–5] Such exquisite control endows nanomate-
rials many new properties for applications in optics, catalysis, 
sensing, biology, and biomedicine.[6–11]
Programmable nanomaterials were first achieved in control-
ling the assembly of NP building blocks.[12–15] The nature of 
DNA base pairing and programmability allowed the creation of 
programmable nanomaterials,[16–19] which was achieved when 
applying DNA oligonucleotides to colloidal Au.[20–22] Gold NPs 
stabilized by other ligands, e.g., thiols (H-SR), amines, silanes, 
etc., can also form face-centered-cubic (fcc) superlattices,[23–25] 
whereas hexagonal assembly can be achieved by dithiols on the 
surface of Au NPs.[26] Besides, hexagonal close packing (hcp)

The optical spectra of metal NPs with size larger than ≈3 nm 
are now well understood. The optical extinction is dominated 
by surface plasmon resonance (SPR), of which the SPR posi-
tion depends on the size, shape, and the solvent.[1–6,44,45] For 
NPs with large size (e.g., ≈100 nm)—non-negligible compared 
to the optical wavelength, scattering dominates the spectrum, 
and radiative depolarization effects lead to redshift of SPR 
when the diameter of nanospheres increases.[46] In the case 
of smaller NPs, absorption dominates the extinction and scat-
tering is minor. The size-dependent SPR band is broadened 
and becomes essentially unidentifiable for Au NPs of diam-
eter below ≈2  nm,[47,48] and the quantum-sized NCs exhibit 
molecular behavior, which is fundamentally different from 
the plasmon behavior of conventional NPs.[49] Besides, non-
linear optical measurements indicate that NCs smaller than 
the critical size for electronic energy quantization have non-
saturated absorption compared to plasmonic NPs.[50,51] Note 
that the nonlinear optical properties may have a stronger 
influence of ligand-metal interactions in molecular NCs than 
in conventional plasmonic NPs.[52,53] Theoretically, ab initio 
methods illustrate SPR for NCs with 2  nm metal core (i.e., 
Au314(SH)96).[54]
Herein, we summarize the atomically precise Au-SR NCs 
with programmable kernel structures and their electronic/
optical absorption properties. Generally, the energy gap (Eg) 
decreases with increasing size of NCs. Compared to the trend 
delineated by the Au-SR NCs with fcc and Dh or Marks Dh 
(M-Dh) kernels, those NCs with Ih kernels, on the other hand, 
show smaller Eg for comparable sizes. At small sizes (gold atom 
number n  <  ≈50), various atomic packing modes for the ker-
nels of NCs can be achieved, resulting in very sensitive changes 
to the optical absorption spectra; and the programmable bcc, 
fcc, and hcp kernels can even bring about a drastic variation 
in photoexcited carrier lifetime by more than three orders of 
magnitude. By contrast, at large sizes (n > 100), the structural 
effect gradually fades out. As different R groups on thiolates 
(protecting the same Au–S core) has been proved to have only 
minor influences on the optical properties, further comparisons 
are made on isomeric Au NCs and size/structure evolving NC 
series to stress the major role of the kernel structure over the 
effect of surface motifs. Future perspectives are also provided.
2. Programmable Syntheses and Structures  
of Au-SR NCs
The color of metallic gold NPs involves both interband (d to sp) 
and intraband (within the sp band) transitions.[44] However, with 
fewer metal atoms in NCs, i.e., when the size of NC is com-
parable to the de Broglie wavelength of conduction electrons 
(λ = 0.5 nm), significant quantum confinement occurs, resulting 
in discrete electronic structure and, accordingly, multiple exci-
tonic absorption peaks observed in the optical spectrum.[55–57] In 
early time (before the structures of NCs were solved by SCXRD), 
the Whetten group studied the thiolate-passivated gold NCs 
(but not atomically precise) and the optical absorption spectra 
of these NCs show size-dependent step-like profiles.[58,59] The 
structurally characterized Au102(SR)44 (SR = SPh-p-COOH) pos-
sesses a M-Dh kernel,[60] however, shows a featureless decay-like 
spectrum except a 269 nm peak from the thiolate ligand.[61]
By contrast, [Au25(SR)18]− (SR = SC2H4Ph) shows intense, mul-
tiple optical absorption peaks (1.8, 2.8, and 3.1 eV),[62] providing 
evidence that quantum size effects dominate the absorption 
behavior of Au-SR NCs with a few dozens of metal atoms. The 
intraband and interband transitions of molecular Au-SR NCs 
are both prominent and can be elucidated by time-dependent 
density functional theory (TDDFT) calculations based on the 
crystal structures. Of note, Aun(SR)m NCs are very different 
from the phosphine-protected ones,[63–67] as the Aun(SR)m struc-
tures possess an Au kernel and a shell of staple-like motifs,[68] 
whereas no such motifs exists in phosphine-protected NCs. As 
to Au25(SR)18, the Ih Au13 kernel is protected by six -SR-Au-SR-
Au-SR- dimeric staple motifs (Figure 1a),[62] and the orbitals near 
the HOMO–LUMO gap are primarily composed of 6sp atomic 
orbitals of Au atoms of the kernel (Ih Au13). Specifically, the 
optical absorption peaks at the low-energy end arise from excita-
tions between kernel Au based orbitals, whereas higher-energy 
peaks are related to excitations from motif-based orbitals to 
kernel Au based orbitals.[62,69] Below the nearly triply degenerate 
HOMO set, the states are primarily contributed by Au 5d and S 
3p atomic orbitals from the staple motifs (Figure 1b,c).[62]

2.1. Programmable Au-SR NCs by Kinetic/Thermodynamic 
Control
The most exciting part of atomically precise Au-SR NCs is that 
their sizes/structures can be kinetically/thermodynamically 
controlled in the synthesis, and subsequently, the optical prop-
erties can also be tailored. Based on the optimized reaction con-
ditions, including using a large excess of thiol (e.g., thiol-to-Au 
molar ratio of 2:1 or higher) to convert Au(III) into Au(I)−SR 
complexes, and then using a great excess of reducing agent 
(e.g., 10 equiv of NaBH4 per mole of Au) to reduce Au(I) to 
Au(0), Au-SR NCs of various sizes were obtained.[72] However, 
the obtained product was a mixture of Au-SR NCs of different 
sizes. The Tsukuda group used gel electrophoresis to separate 
glutathione (SG)-protected Au NCs prepared from Au(I)SG 
polymers, and distinct optical spectra were observed for NCs 
of different sizes, including Au10(SG)10, Au15(SG)13, Au18(SG)14, 
Au22(SG)16, Au22(SG)17, Au25(SG)18, Au29(SG)20, Au33(SG)22, and 
Au39(SG)24.[73,74]
Indeed, careful control over the reaction kinetics would 
create a specific chemical environment which directs the exclu-
sive formation of a single-sized Au-SR NC while suppressing 
other sizes, that is, “one pot, one size” as proposed and executed 
by the Jin’s group since 2006. The use of HSC2H4Ph resulted 
in successive reports of [Au25(SR)18]−, Au38(SR)24, Au144(SR)60, 
and Au333(SR)79.[75–79] Kinetic control in the initial stage, i.e., 
controlling the initial mass range of Au(I)-SR polymers, effec-
tively eliminates the formation of larger NCs in the synthesis 
of [Au25(SR)18]−,[75] and a size-focusing process was observed by 
tracing the optical spectra over the aging process (Figure 2a).[76] 
Reaction in acetone resulted in polydisperse Aux(SG)y predom-
inantly in the range of 8 to 18  kDa, and based on this crude 
product, size-focusing with excess HSC2H4Ph at 80 °C (thermo-
dynamic selection) gave rise to Au38(SR)24 of molecular purity 
(Figure 2b).[77] In the preparation of Au144(SR)60, thiol etching at 
80 °C for 24 h caused the decomposition of the 7.4 kDa (Au25) 
and other species, leaving the 34 kDa NC as the only product 
(Figure  2c).[78] The essence of the size-focusing method is to 
control the size range of the Aux(SR)y crude product (prior 
to the size-focusing step) by kinetic control, which can be 
achieved by controlling the thiol:Au ratio, temperature, and 
reducing rate.[80] Otherwise, a too broad size range of Aux(SR)y 
would result in more than one stable size in the product after 
size focusing, and the isolation would be difficult. Reducing the 
thiol:Au ratio from 3:1 (to prepare Au144(SR)60) to 2:1 allowed 
one to make even larger sizes, such as Au333(SR)79 and Au≈520.[79]
Interestingly, the three NCs (Au25, Au38, and Au144) were 
later found to have Ih kernel structures by SCXRD.[62,82,83] 
[Au25(SR)18]− contains an Ih Au13 kernel protected by six 
Au2(SR)3 motifs (Figure  2d).[62] Based on the experimental 
optical spectrum of Au:SC18 NC with core size of 8  kDa 
(without counting the mass of organic part in thiolates),[84,85] 
the structure of Au38(SR)24 was theoretically predicted to have a 
face-fused bi-Ih Au23 kernel and six Au2(SR)3 motifs distributed 
on the two Au13 units bridged by three Au(SR)2 motifs.[86,87] 
Experimentally, the Au38 structure was solved by Qian et  al. 
(Figure  2e).[82] The crystal structure of Au144(SR)60 was solved 
by replacing SC2H4Ph with SCH2Ph.[83] The hollow Au12 inner 
kernel is enclosed by an Ih Au42 shell, then a rhombicosido-
decahedral Au54 shell, and finally protected by thirty Au(SR)2 
motifs (Figure 2f).[83] As to the largest Au333(SR)79 NC of atomic 
precision, nascent SPR was observed (see Section  3.1). No 
structure of fcc Au333 has been attained, but recent high-angle 
annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy 
(HAADF-STEM) shows an oblate structure (Figure 2g).[81]

In other words, not only can the size/structure of the starting 
Au-SR NCs be programmed by kinetic and thermodynamic

further programmed via transformation as well, and in some 
cases, LESIT can be repeatedly performed.
The ligand exchange on [Au25(SR)18]− with HSPh-tBu at 
80 °C resulted in a new stable size of Au28(SR’)20 (Figure 3a) 
with high yield (>90%, Au atom basis), indicating the high effi-
ciency of LEIST.[89] Moreover, by carefully tuning the transfor-
mation kinetics, i.e., 40 °C (as opposed to 80 °C), Au20(SR’)16 
with a bi-Td Au7 kernel was obtained.[93] Time-dependent UV–
vis and ESI-MS analyses revealed the details of how Au38(SR)24 
was transformed into Au36(SR’)24 (yield >90%).[88]  As shown 
in Figure  3b, the original characteristic peaks in the absorp-
tion spectrum were at 490, 620, 750, and 1050 nm (for Au38); 
at 10 min, a new absorption band at 550  nm appeared, and 
after 30  min, two new sets of mass peaks (indicating Au36 
and an intermediate Au40) with comparable intensities were 
observable, suggesting the formation of Au36 due to LEIST; the 
spectrum showing characteristic absorption peaks at 380 and 
570  nm for Au36 became clean when the reaction reached 
120  min. The Au28(SR’)20 and Au36(SR’)24 NCs obtained by 
LEIST have an fcc Au20 and an fcc Au28 kernel, respectively, 
and both kernels are protected by four Au2(SR’)3 motifs 
(Figure 3a,b, lower panels).
Zeng et al. continued to report the achievement in transfor-
mation from Au144(SR)60 to Au133(SR’)52, with the LEIST con-
ditions to be SPh-tBu:SC2H4Ph ≈ 370:1 and 80 °C for ≈4 days. 
Interestingly, the conversion does not change the kernel struc-
ture dramatically—not like the above cases of Au25 (Ih) to Au28 
(fcc) and Au38 (bi-Ih) to Au36 (fcc), the Au133(SR’)52 structure 
from inside-out contains an Ih Au13 inner kernel, an Ih Au42 
shell, an open shell of Au52, and 26 Au(SR)2 surface motifs 
(Figure 3c).[91] Note that Au144(SR)60 has a hollow Ih Au12 kernel, 
thus, it is intriguing to see when the total Au number reduces 
from 144 to 133 upon LEIST, a single Au atom is inserted into 
the center of the smaller Au133 NC. The formation of Au133 is 
more likely through a gradual outside-in rearrangement, but it 
still requires further study.[94]
Au279(SPh-tBu)84 was synthesized by a combination of size 
focusing and LEIST. Specifically, the mixture of Au144, Au333 
and Au≈520 was subjected to a size focusing process with 
excess HSCH2Ph-tBu to achieve single product of Au333(SR)79, 
and then LEIST was conducted in excess HSPh-tBu to result 
in Au279(SR’)84.[92] Au279(SR’)84 was solved to have an fcc Au13 
kernel, an fcc Au42 shell, then an fcc Au92 shell (all cubocta-
hedral), an outmost Au102 part, multiple surface motifs and 
bridging thiolates (Figure 3d).
Additionally, LEIST is also widely applied to obtain other 
sizes. Das et al. obtained [Au23(SR)16]− with an fcc Au13 kernel,[95] 
which can be further transformed into Au24(SR’)20 with a bi-Td 
Au8 kernel (see Section 3.1).[96] Other cases include Au30(SR)18 
to Au36(SR’)24;[97] Au44(SR)26 to Au43(SR’)25;[98] Au99(SR)42 to 
Au103S2(SR’)41,[99] etc. (SR and SR’ represent different thiolates). 
A key condition for LEIST is that the thermal condition is 
required to overcome the energy barrier between stable sizes.

2.3. Programmable Au-SR NCs by Isomeric Methylbenzenethiols
In 2015, Chen et  al. [100] reported that the sizes of Aun(SR)m 
NCs can be controlled by employing isomeric benzenethiols 
with –CH3 group at different positions on the benzene ring, 
i.e., ortho-, meta-, para-methylbenzenethiolates (o-MBT, m-MBT 
and p-MBT). Interestingly, when the –CH3 group changes from 
ortho- to meta- to para-position, a clear trend of increasing size 
from Au40 to Au104 to Au130 was observed (Figure 4), indicating 
the hindrance of –CH3 to the interfacial Au-SR bond can con-
trol the size of atomically precise Au-SR NCs.[100]
In an earlier study, a less stable Au40(SC2H4Ph)24 was detected 
prior to the final product of highly stable Au38(SC2H4Ph)24.[80] 
However, when o-MBT was applied in Chen’s work,[100] 
Au40(o-MBT)24 NCs was obtained after size focusing. Note that 
Au38(o-MBT)24 can also be obtained by ligand exchange from 
Au38(SC2H4Ph)24 (the structure with a bi-Ih Au23 kernel is main-
tained, as indicated by the optical spectra, see Section 3.5).[101] 
The Au40(o-MBT)24 obtained by the size-focusing process high-
lights the importance of synthesis methods, i.e., size focusing 
results in Au40(o-MBT)24  (the most robust one survives) 
whereas  ligand exchange  from Au38(SC2H4Ph)24  results in 
Au38(o-MBT)24 as opposed to Au40(o-MBT)24. Note that during 
the  formation of  Au38(SC2H4Ph)24,[77]  an Au40(SC2H4Ph)24 
NC (structure unknown yet) was  formed but eventually dis-
appeared due to less stability, which could be a quasi-isomer 
of  Au40(o-MBT)24. The absorption spectrum of Au40(o-MBT)24 
shows two distinct peaks at 365 and 430  nm as well as a 
flat band from 550 to 657  nm (Figure  4a). The fcc kernel of 
Au40(o-MBT)24 is composed of a vertex sharing bi-Td Au7 sur-
rounded by a hex-Td Au18 ring (six Td Au4 units fused by vertex 
sharing), and the kernel is then protected by six Au(SR)2 and 
three Au2(SR)3 motifs.[102]
The formula of m-MBT-protected Au-SR NC was tentatively 
assigned as Au104(m-MBT)41 according to the electrospray ioni-
zation (ESI) MS analysis, in which CsOAc was added to pro-
duce positively charged adducts (the structure not known yet). 
Higaki et  al. recently reported an Au103S2(2SNap)41 structure 
(2SNap = 2-naphthalenethiolate),[99] which is only one atom 
smaller and illustrated here (Figure 4b inset and right panels).

successively by an Ino-Dh Au32 shell, an Au40 cage which covers

3. Electronic and Optical Properties of Au-SR NCs
The general structure of Au-SR NCs is composed of a kernel, 
multiple motifs of Au–S, and an outmost shell of R groups. 
Compared to Au-PPh3 NCs,[106] the Au-SR NCs have more 
complicated electronic and optical properties[34] due to the 
emergence of gold-containing staple motifs. While the kernel

3.1. General Size/Structure Dependency
The frontier orbitals (HOMO, LUMO, and adjacent ones) are 
critical in determining the energy gap of atomically precise 
NCs. Despite the clear compositions of frontier orbitals, the 
optical properties are more complicated than thought initially. A 
recent landmark study covered Au-SR NCs from 23 to ≈940 Au 
atoms[34] and provided a grand view to relate the size/structure 
of Au-SR NCs to their optical properties. A three-stage evolution 
is concluded — small-sized NCs (n < 50) show optical properties 
highly dependent on the structure (i.e., nonscalable); medium-
sized NCs (50 < n < 100) exhibit both size and structural depend-
ency so as to be in the intermediate regime; and large-sized NCs 
(n > 100) demonstrate size-dominant optical properties whereas 
the structural effect fades out (i.e., scalable optical properties).[34] 
Ligands are not specified in this section as the R groups of thi-
olates are less critical to the optical properties (see Section 3.5).
The strong quantum confinement in small gold NCs 
(n  <  ≈50) makes their optical properties very sensitive to 
a small change in the structure, even at the single-atom 
level. For example, for [Au23(SR)16]−,[95] Au24(SR)20,[96] and 
[Au25(SR)18]−[62] in which the Au atom number increases one-by-
one, their optical absorption peaks show dramatic differences 
due to their different kernel structures (i.e., fcc-Au13, bi-Td-Au8, 
and Ih-Au13, respectively, Figure 5, top three). As the kernel Au 
atomic orbitals contribute greatly to the HOMO set and LUMO 
set,[62] the programmable kernel structure is responsible for the 
much different positions of the lowest energy absorption peaks 
of the three NCs (Figure  5, top three spectra). Moreover, the 
spectral profiles are also different among the Au23/24/25 NCs.
The fcc Au36 NC demonstrates an exceptionally large 
HOMO–LUMO energy gap of 1.7  eV, which greatly exceeds 
that of Au38 (0.9 eV) although the sizes of the two NCs differ 
only by two Au atoms.[90] The kernel structures, i.e., fcc Au28 
for Au36 versus bi-Ih Au23 for Au38 (see Section 2.2), are critical 
in determining the onset of optical absorption for small-sized 
Au-SR NCs. As to the quantum box series of Au NCs with fcc 
kernels, i.e., Au28(SR)20,[89] Au36(SR)24,[90] Au44(SR)28,[107] and 
Au52(SR)32,[102] in which the Td Au4 units coil up into double 
helixes (Figure  5, insets, the two strands are marked in cyan 
and yellow, respectively),[107] the optical absorption exhibits 
a uniform evolution and the Eg decreases as size increases 
(Figure 5, yellow spectra). For another series, i.e., Au28(SR)20,[108] 
Au34(SR)22 and Au42(SR)26,[109] the Td Au4 units coil up via an 
alternative way in the kernels, and evolutions in optical absorp-
tion and Eg are similar as those of the fcc Au28/36/44/52 series. 
As the size increases, quantum confinement becomes weaker, 
resulting in the shift of absorption onset to lower energy (i.e., 
smaller Eg) and less intense absorption peaks as the electronic 
states become more crowded.

3.2. Crystal Phase Control
Carrier recombination follows the generation of excitons, and the 
dynamics is strongly related to the structure of the nanomate-
rials. As to small-sized Au-SR NCs (n < 50), the Jin group carried 
out extensive studies for an in-depth understanding of the effects 
of atomic packing inside the kernels of Au-SR NCs, including Ih, 
Dh and fcc structures and Au38S2(SR)20 with a bcc kernel,[126] as 
well as Au18(SR)14 and Au30(SR)18 NCs with hcp kernels.[127,128]
Correlation between the programmable atomic packing 
structures and the carrier lifetimes reveals the importance of 
the kernel structures. Recently, Zhou et al.[129] reported a drastic 
variation in photoexcited state lifetime of bcc Au38S2(SR)20 com-
pared to hcp Au30(SR)18 with comparable Eg (≈1.5 eV, Figure 10). 
Surprisingly, it was found that the bcc-Au38 has an exceptionally 
long carrier lifetime (4700 ns), whereas the hcp-Au30 shows a 
very short lifetime (1  ns).[129] The dramatic three-orders-of-
magnitude variation in excited state lifetime is dictated by core 
atomic packing since the ligands of both hcp and bcc NCs are 
the same. Considering that the kernels of bcc Au38 and hcp 
Au30 are composed of multiple tetrahedral Au4 units, the carrier 
lifetime is strongly related to the connectivity of Au4 units. DFT 
calculations demonstrate that when the distance between Au4 
units is shorter, a larger overlap between HOMO and LUMO 
would lead to shorter lifetime, and vice versa.[129]
3.3. Isomerism
Structural isomerism is common in organic molecules due to 
the diversity of carbon bonding. Such behavior is also observed 
in Au-SR NCs thanks to the advantage of SCXRD to reveal the 
total structures. The structural isomerism in atomically precise 
NCs refers to the same number and type of metal atoms and 
ligands (i.e., the same formula). When the organic tails of the 
ligands are different, it is quasi-isomerism only. But here we 
loosely combine both types as isomerization.
The first isomer pair pertains to the Au38(SR)24 system 
(SR = SCH2CH2Ph)—Au38(SR)24Q reported by Qian et al. has 
a bi-Ih Au23 kernel,[82] whereas the Au23 kernel of Au38(SR)24T 
reported by Tian et  al.[130] is a mixed phase of an Ih part and 
an fcc part fused together (Figure 11a), and consequently, the 
surface motifs are different as well. Although both Au38 NCs 
show multiple peaks in their optical spectra, the peak wave-
lengths are quite different (Figure 11a)—480, 520, 570, 527, 740, 
and 1035 nm for Au38(SR)24Q, but 505, 540, 610, 700, 880, and 
1090  nm for Au38(SR)24T.[82,130] We relate the relatively small 
energy gaps of both Au38 NCs to the Ih packing mode in both 
kernels, but the difference in the spectral profiles is decided by 
the electronic structures which are greatly affected by the struc-
tures from both inner kernels and outer motifs.

both have fcc Au20 kernels but with different patterns (1D vs 
2D) of the assembly of Td-Au4 building blocks (Figure 11b).[131] 
The Au36(SR)24-1D shows the same two optical features like 
other Au36 NCs,[90] as they share the same Au–S structure (see 
Section 3.5). By contrast, the new Au36(SR)24-2D exhibits more

3.4. Structure Evolution
The previous sections have demonstrated the major effects of 
size and structure on the optical absorption of Au-SR NCs. The 
analysis on the isomerism effects also indicates that the kernel 
is more decisive than the surface motifs. To further illustrate 
the relationship between the programmable structures and the 
optical properties of metal-SR NCs, several correlated series in 
which the kernel and motif structures can be delicately tailored 
are demonstrated in this subsection.

3.4.3. Cu/Cd Doping Induced Motif Structure Evolution
In a series of NCs stabilized by thiolate and phosphine ligands 
bearing pyridyl groups, the Ih Au13 kernel is symmetrically face-
capped by two, four and eight Cu atoms, respectively, resulting 
in bimetallic NCs with 8 electron superatomic electronic struc-
tures that do not have surface motifs (i.e., bare metal cores). The 
optical gaps of Au13Cu2, Au13Cu4, and Au13Cu8 are estimated to 
be 1.88, 1.87, and 1.80 eV, respectively (Figure 17a).[164] Although 
the absorption edges are similar, the absorption profiles are 
quite different among the three, which we ascribe to the fol-
lowing factors: 1) the three NCs are protected by different ligands 
with pyridyl groups which participate more in frontier orbitals 
than phenyl group; 2) the capping Cu atoms bond directly to 
the Ih Au13 kernel, hence, part of kernel atoms rather than motif 
atoms. In the follow-up work on Au13Cu2, when only replacing 
the six PPh3 ligands with three di-phosphine, the absorption 
spectra do not change much at wavelengths >450 nm.[165] As to 
Au13Cu4, single ligand exchange results in another Au13Cu4 with 
one more selenide ligand and one less phosphine ligand.[166] As 
the coordination is not disturbed strongly and the thiolate to 
selenolate change does not affect the absorption much, similar 
spectra are seen. Both cases indicate the domination of the elec-
tronic structure by the Au13Cun kernel.
The Bigioni group reported [Ag44(SR)30]4− in 2013.[167] The 
structure of Ag44 is composed of an Ih Ag12 kernel, then a 
dodecahedral Ag20 shell, and finally six Ag2(SR)5 mount motifs 
in an octahedral configuration. In the same year, Zheng et al. 
revealed a structural evolution on the surface in bimetallic 
[Au12+nCu32(SR)30+n]4− (n = 0, 2, 4, 6), and the Keplerate two-shell 
Au12@Cu20 kernel is mounted with (6-n) units of Cu2(SR)5 and 
n units of Cu2Au(SR)6 motifs.[168] We note that upon insertion 
of Au(SR) into the motif, the distance between two Cu atoms 
in the same motif increases from ≈2.76 to ≈4.01 Å; and the 
distance between motif Cu to the beneath Cu20 shell increases 
from ≈2.75 to ≈3.10 Å. Despite the structure evolution in the 
motifs, the Cu20 shell which directly bonds to the innermost 
Au12 kernel is intact, and correspondingly, the optical absorp-
tion properties show no change for the four NCs of different n 
values (Figure 17b).[168] By contrast, the Cu part connects to the 
Au13 kernel in the Au13Cux alloy series evolves (Figure 17a) and 
an obvious change in optical spectra is accompanied.
The change in surface structure is also observed from 
[Au23(SR)16]− to Au19Cd2(SR)16]−, as the Cd doping induced 
reconstruction of the staple motifs. Due to the structural trans-
formation only occurs on the surface without affecting the 
kernel structure much, the absorption peak at ≈580  nm for 
[Au23(SR)16]− slightly redshifts due to the involvement of Cd in 
frontier orbitals (Figure 17c).[169]

3.5. R-Group Effect
The absorption spectrum serves as the fingerprint of specific 
sized NCs of atomic precision. Note that the formation of 
Au-SR NCs is ligand-driven, i.e., LEIST usually results in size 
and/or structure transformation (see Section  2.2).[88,96,98,124] 
Beside the LEIST process, there is also ligand exchange from 
thiolate to thiolate, which retains the size and structure as well 
as the optical absorption features.[170] DFT calculations were 
used to simulate the ligand effects on the structure and optical 
properties,[171] and found that the R group of thiolate with low 
polarity does not disturb the Au25S18 core significantly; but for 
p-SPhX ligands, a major distortion was found for electron-with-
drawing groups, e.g., NO2 and COOH. Theoretical comparison 
between SR and SeR protected Au NCs with an identical core 
structure indicates similar profiles of optical absorption spectra 
and electronic structure.[172] Experimentally, Au25 stabilized 
by various thiolates, including SC2H4Ph,[62] 2SNap,[36] 1SNap 
(1-naphthanelenthiolate),[36] SPh,[169] and selenides, including 
SeC2H4Ph and SePh,[174] were reported (Figure  18a). The 
primary absorption peak (at ≈680 nm) shows a slight redshift 
when replacing non-aromatic thiolates with aromatic ones. 
When SPh is changed to SePh, the redshift becomes more 
obvious, and the smaller HOMO–LUMO energy gap is prob-
ably due to the higher bonding energy of Au–Se than Au–S.[175]
Au36(SR’)24 can be readily obtained by ligand exchange from 
Au38(SC2H4Ph)24 at 80  °C (Section  2.2).[88] It has been dem-
onstrated that when the incoming ligand has a substituent at 
the para position of SPh, including 2SNap, p-MBT, 3,4DMBT 
(3,4-dimethylbenzenthiolate), p-EBT (para-ethylbenzenthiolate), 
SPh-tBu, SPh-p-F (para-fluorobenzenethiolate), SPh-p-OCH3 
(para-methoxybenzenethiolate), or is a cyclic thiol, such as SPh 
and S-c-C5H9, Au36 is favored over Au38 as long as the thermody-
namic barrier can be overcome by heating at high enough temper-
atures.[101] The absorption centered at 555 nm for Au36(S-c-C5H9)24 
(non-aromatic ligand) shifts to lower energy for all other Au36 
NCs when aromatic ligands are applied (Figure 18b) due to the 
electronic conjugation effect of aromatic ligands that extends over 
to the core, resulting in stabilization of electronic orbitals.[176]
Au38(SR)24 protected by various thiolates other than SC2H4Ph 
can be obtained by ligand exchange as well. In the cryogenic (80 K) 
optical absorption spectra (Figure 18c,d), peaks II/III of the resulted 
Au38 with aromatic ligands all shift to lower energy, and a unani-
mous splitting in peak II is observed (Figure  18c,d, highlighted 
by shadow). Interestingly, for Au38 protected by ortho-substituted 
SPh-X (including 1SNap, o-MBT, 2,4DMBT (2,4-dimethylbenzen-
thiolate), o-EBT (ortho-ethylbenzenthiolate), 2,5DMBT (2,5-dimeth-
ylbenzenthiolate), 2,6DMBT (2,6-dimethylbenzenthiolate), SPh-o-F 
(ortho-fluorobenzenethiolate), SPh-o-OCH3 (ortho-methoxyben-
zenethiolate), the absorption peak at the lowest energy (Figure 18c, 
peak I) is generally blueshifted compared to the Au38(SC2H4Ph)24 
reference, while for other Au38 NCs protected by ligands without 
ortho-substitution, the corresponding peak I (Figure 18d) is clearly 
redshift for all the cases, stressing the substituent-site effects on 
the frontier orbital levels near the HOMO–LUMO gap.
[Ag44(SR)30]4− NCs display multiple peaks in the absorption 
spectra (Figure 19a), which do not vary much with the type of 
surface ligand.[177] As to the alloy [Au12Ag32(SR)30]4−, only two 
peaks are prominent, although some weak shoulder peaks are 
still observable, and the peaks redshift by ≈18 nm for SPh-p-F 
protected Au12Ag32 NC compared to SPhF2 (3,4-difluoroben-
zenethiolate) or SPhCF3 (4-(trifluoromethyl)thiophenolate) sta-
bilized ones (Figure 19b).[177]
In 
short, 
as 
indicated 
by 
[Au25(SR)18]−,[36,38,76,173]

ally, the energy gaps of NCs with Ih kernels are smaller than

properties are extremely sensitive for small-sized Au-SR NCs, 
of which the atomic packing structure can be “programmed” 
into diverse structures of hcp, bcc, fcc, Td Au4 and Oh Au6 net-

and surprising, e.g., a three-orders-of-magnitude variation in 
carrier lifetime is observed for Au-SR NCs with bcc versus hcp 
kernel.[129]
Isomerism (i.e., different total structures with the same for-
mula) and quasi-isomerism (i.e., different core structures of 
the same size induced by different ligands) in Au-SR NCs also 
bring prominent changes in optical absorption as the structures 
of the kernel and/or surface become different. Several series of 
correlated NCs are illustrated for the different functions of the 
kernel and surface motifs. The variation in the kernel structure 
causes significant changes to both the absorption spectrum and 
energy gap, whereas tailoring the surface structure only leads to 
minor effects on the optical absorption. Thus, the kernel gener-
ally plays a more decisive role in affecting the electronic/optical 
properties than does the motif structure.
With respect to the future perspectives, although the non-
metallic to metallic/plasmonic transition is fairly clear for 
Au-SR NCs, i.e., from Au246(SR)80 to Au279(SR)84,[92] whether the 
transition depends on the detailed structure (or the size only) 
deserves more studies. The M-Dh kernel of Au246(SR)80 and the 
fcc kernel of Au279(SR)84 are apparently different, which may or 
may not have influences on their optical properties. Based on a 
recent study,[34] the structural influence on electron dynamics 
in the large size regime (n > 100 atoms) diminishes, thus we 
expect that the detailed structure type would have minor effects 
on the critical size of transition, but more evidence is certainly 
helpful and desired. Therefore, it would still be meaningful to 
test whether an Au-SR NC with fcc kernel being smaller than 
Au279 exhibit any authentic plasmon behavior.

complicated. Regarding the critical transition size of Ag-SR 
NCs, we have compiled the optical absorption spectra 
(Figure  20a) of large-sized (n  > 100)  Ag-SR NCs with crystal 
structures solved, including [Ag136(SR)64Cl3]− with a Dh 
kernel,[178] [Ag141(SR)40X12]3+ (X = Cl, Br, or I) with an inter-
penetrating bi-Ih kernel,[179] Ag146(SR)80Br2 with a distorted Dh 
kernel,[180] and Ag206(SR)68F2Cl2 and Ag374(SR)113Br2Cl2 with 
M-Dh kernels.[178,181] Ag206 and Ag347 are plasmonic, with a dis-
tinct SPR band at 465 nm for both.[178,181] The smaller Ag141 also 
seems to be plasmonic by showing a single broad peak at the 
same position in the spectrum, and DFT analysis indicated that 
the band is plasmonic in nature, which was assigned to tran-
sitions involving a large number of single-electron/single-hole 
states.[179] Interestingly, the Ag146 NC, which lies between Ag206 
and Ag141, was confirmed to be non-metallic as indicated by i) 
multiple excitonic absorption peaks and ii) electron dynamics 
independence on the pump fluence.[180] Coming down to the 
Ag136 size, it displays a broad major peak at 450 nm and a weak 
peak at 772 nm, indicating its non-metallic nature.[178] Although 
the different kernel structures (i.e., the interpenetrating bi-Ih 
kernel in Ag141 and the distorted Dh kernel in Ag146) may be 
responsible for their metallic and non-metallic behavior, respec-
tively, the detailed nonmetallic to metallic transition in Ag-SR 
NCs remains to be understood in future work. There is an indi-
cation that the metallic state emerges earlier (i.e., smaller sizes) 
in Ag-SR NCs than Au-SR NCs.[180]
Second, compared to Au-SR and Ag-NCs, the transi-
tion behavior of the alloy system is much less studied. Silver 
doping at various controllable levels gradually changes the 
absorption spectra as reported in Au25(SR)18,[146] Au38(SR)24,[158] 
Au144(SR)60,[159] etc. Generally speaking, more Ag substitutions 
gradually increase the energy gap of the doped NCs. Doping 
Pt/Pd into the 8e [Au25(SR)18]− changes its closed electronic 
shell to an open one (6e), resulting in redshifted optical absorp-
tion onset.[149,184–186] The doping effect on the optical properties 
without changing the structure of the template has been sum-
marized in some excellent reviews published recently.[155–157] 
In future work, it deserves a careful investigation on whether 
doping Ag or other heteroatoms into Au-SR NCs shifts the 
nonmetal-to-metal transition size to a smaller or larger size 
than the homometal system. Recently, several bimetallic 
M-SR NCs of large sizes have been reported. Au267−xAgx(SR)80

the transition size for bimetallic M-SR NCs is suspected to be 
between M144 to M267, which might also depend on the doping 
extent of heteroatoms. Future work should carefully study such 
issues.
Third, the recently discovered localization of electronic struc-
ture on specific atom(s) is critical to optical properties and is 
made possible by pursuing atomically precise NCs.[162] The 
work is still at its early stage and worth further studies in the 
future. The fact that certain atom(s) in the kernel are more 
decisive to the frontier orbitals seems shape-driven, not fol-
lowing the superatom rule which has been commonly used to 
rationalize the stability of small- to medium-sized metal NCs. 
Such localization in electronic structure is clearly related to the 
stability of NCs with no electronic shell closing, and indicates 
the active site(s) that can be easily doped.
Fourth, although changing the R group on the thiolate 
without altering the structure of NCs does not bring much 
difference to the optical absorption, different thiolates (i.e., 
different R groups) may sometimes cause structural transfor-
mation in both the kernel and surface motifs, i.e., the sizes/
structures of NCs are ligand-directed, but what features of 
ligands (steric and/or electronic factors) cause the transforma-
tion still remain unclear, so are the detailed transformation 
mechanisms in many cases. Future research on the optical 
properties of a series of NCs stabilized by the same ligand and 
with minimum structural alteration, e.g., an atom-by-atom 
evolution with high programmability, will be particularly desir-
able as this will permits one to further understand the roles of 
the kernel and surface structures on various properties at the 
unprecedented level.
Fifth, the assembly of multiple icosahedral 13-atom units 
into a linear or cyclic chain via vertex- or face-sharing is quite 
intriguing.[188–190] Beside the series of Au13, Au24, Au25, and 
Au37 NCs with gradual elongation (Figure 17), the assembly of 
Ih Pt@Ag12 building blocks into linear or cyclic NCs and their 
corresponding optical absorption or electronic structures have 
been reported.[191,192] However, the face-fused structure of Ih 
units has only been found in Au38(SR)24 via a dimerization pro-
cess so far.[193] Thus, for the future programmable control over 
the atomic packing and the understanding of the optical prop-
erties, it is highly desirable to create more of face-fused 1D NCs 
beyond TDDFT calculations.

erties is vital, for example, matching the nanocluster absorp-
tion band with the excitation wavelength in photocatalysis[194] 
and photoacoustic imaging in biomedicine,[198] tuning the

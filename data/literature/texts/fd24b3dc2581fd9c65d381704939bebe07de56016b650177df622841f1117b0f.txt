ABSTRACT: Pd-based nanozymes have emerged as promising
alternatives to natural enzymes, but their application is still
constrained due to suboptimal activity and poor specificity. As
efficient hydrogen storage nanomaterials, the specific implications of
implanted hydrogen on the enzyme-mimicking activity of Pd-based
nanomaterials remain largely uninvestigated. In this study, we
discovered that hydrogenation process significantly enhances the
enzyme-like activity of Pd-based nanomaterials, although reaction
specificity varies in dependence on the synthetic route of Pd
hydrides. Pd/H2 nanocubes (NCs), which are synthesized by
directly injecting hydrogen gas into a solution containing Pd NCs,
exhibit a selective enhancement in antioxidative activity against
cytotoxic hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide anion (O2
•−), and
hydroxyl radical (•OH) due to the sustained release of bioreductive hydrogen. In contrast, stable Pd hydride NCs, which are
prepared through the in situ catalytic decomposition of alternative sources of hydrogen atoms, exhibit a remarkable enhancement in
exclusive H2O2 activation pathways, specifically exhibiting peroxidase (POD)-like and catalase (CAT)-like activities. Multiple
spectroscopic characterizations and density functional theory (DFT) calculations confirmed that this high catalytic activity and
specificity of PdH NCs arise from lattice tensile strain and electronic structure change. Based on these findings, a PdH/glucose
oxidase (GOx) nanocomplex was developed for cascade catalysis in tumor therapy. This work not only reveals that hydride
formation can influence both the activity and selectivity of Pd nanozymes but also provides a viable strategy for the precise regulation
of specific enzyme-like activity in hydrogen-loading nanozymes.
■INTRODUCTION
The broad applicability of natural enzymes beyond biological
systems has driven the development of cost-effective
alternatives that offer enhanced activity and stability. Over
the past few decades, advances in nanoscience have positioned
nanozymes as versatile tools in fields such as biomedicine,
bioanalysis, environmental science, and the food industry,
owing to their enzyme-mimetic properties.1,2 Various engi-
neered nanomaterials, including metal-based, metal-oxide or
sulfide-based, carbon-based, and single-atom nanostructures,
have been developed to mimic the activities of multiple
enzymes.3−5 Enhancing the catalytic efficiency of nanozymes
remains a priority for biomedical applications, with research
showing that their performance can be fine-tuned through
modifications in size, morphology, composition, and surface
structure.6−8 Among these factors, the arrangement of surface
atoms plays a significant role in catalytic performance.9−11 For
instance, palladium (Pd) nanocrystals with {100} facets show
superior oxidase (OXD)- and peroxidase (POD)-like activities
compared to {111} facets,12 while concave Pd nanostructures
nanocrystals with high-index {730} facets outperform the
planar nanostructures with low-index facets in catalyzing
ascorbate autoxidation.13,14 Additionally, engineered platinum
(Pt) hollow nanodendrites demonstrate exceptional POD-like
activity due to optimized Pt atom utilization.14
In addition to catalytic efficiency, achieving high specificity
in nanozyme activity has become an area of growing
interest.15−17 For instance, CeO2 nanomaterials exhibit near
100% specificity for POD- and catalase (CAT)-like activity on
{100} surfaces and haloperoxidase (HPO)-like activity on
{111} surfaces.18 Strained Pd icosahedra demonstrate higher
POD-like activity than unstrained Pd octahedra despite having
the same enclosed {111} facets.19 Ru-based nanozymes with

Hydrogen gas (H2) has garnered attention as a therapeutic
agent due to its selective anti-inflammatory and anticancer
properties.21−25 However, its poor solubility and indiscrimi-
nate diffusivity often result in suboptimal systemic concen-
trations and short reaction times. To address these limitations,
innovative H2 delivery nanoplatforms have been developed.
Solid hydrogen donors, such as MgH2 and CaH2, have been
incorporated into oral formulations to improve H2 loading
capacity.26,27 Pd-based nanomaterials, known for their
excellent H2 storage properties, stand out for their ability to
selectively deliver H2, control its release, and facilitate catalytic
hydrogenation.28−31 Furthermore, despite the effects of
hydrogen incorporation on the catalytic performance of Pd-
based materials have been reported, these research all focus on
industrially catalytic properties such as electrochemical formic
acid oxidation, electrochemical methanol oxidation reaction,
and electrocatalytic N2 reduction.10,32−34 The impact of
hydrogen incorporation on the enzyme-like activity of Pd-
based nanomaterials has not been previously explored. It
should be noticed that biomedical enzyme-like catalysis is
much different from industrial catalysis in terms of reaction
substrate, reaction condition, catalytic process, and application
field. In addition, in the biomedical field, researches have
predominantly focused on the biological functions of
molecular hydrogen, with hydrogen-storing Pd-based nanoma-
terials primarily serving as a vector for hydrogen delivery.23
The influence of hydrogen introduction on the biological
functions of hydrogen-storing Pd nanocarriers, as well as the
biological effects it triggers, have long been considerably
underestimated.
This study investigates the impact of incorporated H on the
catalytic efficiency and specificity of H2-storage nanozymes,
using Pd-based nanomaterials with H2 storage properties as a
model (Scheme 1). Two types of Pd hydrides were
engineered: Pd/H2 NCs, synthesized by directly injecting
hydrogen gas into a solution containing Pd NCs, demonstrated
a selective enhancement in antioxidative activity against
cytotoxic hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide anion
(O2
•−), and hydroxyl radical (•OH). This enhancement is
attributed to the catalytic hydrogenation effect of Pd NCs and
sustained bioreductive H2 release. In contrast, stable PdH NCs,
produced through the in situ catalytic decomposition of
alternative hydrogen sources, exhibited remarkable enhance-
ment in the exclusive H2O2 activation pathway. Specifically,
they displayed POD-like and CAT-like activities while their
OXD-like, SOD-like and •OH scavenging capabilities were
even lower than those of the Pd NCs counterparts.
Spectroscopic characterizations and density functional theory
(DFT) calculations revealed that these improvements arise
from lattice tensile strain triggered by H incorporation, thereby
tuning both the structural and electronic properties of Pd
nanocrystals. Building on these findings, a PdH/glucose

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Specifically Enhanced Antioxidative Enzyme-like
Activities in Pd NCs via H2 Gas Injection. H2 gas was
employed to introduce H2 into Pd NCs, forming Pd/H2 NCs.
Initially, cubic Pd NCs were synthesized, followed by H2
incorporation through bubbling H2 gas into the Pd NCs
solution leveraging the strong coordination between Pd and H2
(Figure 1a). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images
(Figure 1b) confirmed that the synthesized Pd NCs exhibited a
uniform cubic morphology with an average size of 12.1 ± 1.9
nm. Upon exposure to H2 gas, the solution transitioned from
orange to dark brown within 5 min and remained stable for an
extended duration (Figure 1c). The morphology or size of the
Pd NCs remained unchanged following H2 incorporation, with
excellent dispersion observed (Figure 1d).23 UV−vis spectros-
copy revealed a distinct absorbance peak at approximately 280
nm post-H2 loading, with an enhanced absorption in the visible
to near-infrared regions compared to the original Pd NCs,
indicating successful H2 loading (Figure 1e).
Our previous research has demonstrated that Pd-based
nanomaterials exhibit strong multienzyme-like performance in
biomedical applications.12,13 In this study, the enzyme-like
activities of Pd NCs were evaluated to determine the impact of
H2 injection. The H2O2-scavenging capacity, assessed using a
CAT detection kit, was significantly enhanced in Pd/H2 NCs,
which scavenged 56.6% of H2O2 compared to 33.8% by Pd
NCs (Figures 1f and S1). Control experiments with hydrogen-
rich water (HRW) showed minimal effects on H2O2 content,
suggesting that the enhanced CAT-like activity originated from
Pd/H2 NCs rather than dissolved hydrogen. The OXD- and
POD-like activities of Pd NCs were evaluated using 3,3′,5,5′-
tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) as a substrate. Pd NCs oxidized
TMB effectively in sodium acetate (NaAc/HAc) buffer, with
additional enhancement observed in the presence of H2O2,
demonstrating OXD- and POD-like activities. However,
hydrogen loading did not further enhance these activities, as
Pd/H2 NCs exhibited oxidation efficiencies comparable to
those of Pd NCs (Figures S2 and S3). Superoxide dismutase
(SOD)-mimicking activity of Pd/H2 NCs toward O2
•−was
assessed by monitoring the reduction of O2
•−radicals using an
enzymatic generating system and electron spin resonance
(ESR).35,36 Pd/H2 NCs reduced the ESR signal of BMPO/·
OOH by 46.5%, surpassing Pd NCs (27.1%) and HRW
(2.8%), demonstrating enhanced SOD-mimicking activity

Antioxidative nanozymes are widely recognized for their
crucial role in combating oxidative stress, a key factor
underlying numerous diseases and aging processes. The
development of Pd/H2 NCs with selectively enhanced
antioxidative enzyme-like activities represents a significant
advancement in nanozyme-based therapeutics. Their ability to
combat oxidative stress, combined with the targeted delivery
capabilities of nanomaterials and sustained release of
bioreductive hydrogen, makes them highly promising
candidates for therapeutic applications in neurodegenerative
diseases, cardiovascular disorders, and other oxidative damage-
related pathological conditions.
Previous reports indicate that the H2 incorporated into Pd/
H2 NCs through normobaric H2 gas bubbling are inherently
unstable and can be released as bioreductive H2.23 To elucidate
the mechanism underlying the enhanced antioxidative enzyme-
like activities of Pd/H2 NCs, methylene blue (MB) was
employed as an oxidative probe to evaluate their reductivity
and H2 release. As shown in Figures 1j and S6, Pd/H2 NCs
exhibited sustained bioreductive H2 release, possibly due to the
catalytic properties of Pd NCs that facilitate the hydrogenation
reactions. Notably, the observed H2 release was significantly
higher than that of HRW, confirming the superior H2 storage
and release capacity of Pd NCs. To further investigate their
stability, Pd/H2 NCs were stored for varying durations, and
their •OH scavenging ratio was measured. As shown in Figure
S7, Pd/H2 NCs maintained their enhanced •OH scavenging
capability for over a week. However, centrifugal washing led to
a significant decline in their
•OH-scavenging capability,
rendering it comparable to that of Pd NCs (Figure 1k). This
reduction is attributed to H2 loss from the Pd NCs during
washing and disruption of the adsorption−desorption
equilibrium between dissolved H2 and the Pd NCs in the
solution (Figure S8). Consequently, the destabilization of the
aqueous Pd/H2 NC system during washing resulted in
decreased antioxidative enzyme-like activities. These findings
confirm that Pd NCs serve as efficient H2 carriers, activating
H2 into a bioreductive form and exhibiting excellent
antioxidative enzyme-like properties. However, the instability
of the Pd/H2 NCs system highlights the need for developing
stable solution-phase PdH nanostructures. Such advancements
are essential to fully understanding and optimizing the impact
of the hydrogenation process on the biomimetic activity of Pd
nanocrystals.

(N2H4).32,37−42 These methods enable significant H incorpo-
ration into Pd NCs under controlled temperature and pressure,

In a typical synthesis, presynthesized Pd NCs were efficiently
converted into PdH NCs by heating them in DMF (Figure
2a). The resulting products were collected through centrifu-
gation and subjected to sequential ethanol and water washes to
eliminate residual DMF and adsorbed H2 from the solvent and
the surface of the Pd NCs. The stable incorporation of H
atoms within the lattice of Pd NCs was evidenced by a distinct
color change in the solution, transitioning from orange to dark
gray. This chromatic shift was more pronounced compared to
that observed in presynthesized Pd NCs and Pd/H2 NCs
(Figure 2b). UV−vis absorption spectra revealed further
attenuation of UV absorption in PdH NCs, confirming
successful H incorporation. Similar to Pd/H2 NCs, the cubic
shape and size of the original Pd NCs were preserved in PdH
NCs (Figure S9).
Stable H incorporation was hypothesized to induce
deviations in Pd atom positions within the lattice, leading to
lattice mismatch and strain. High-resolution transmission
electron microscopy (HRTEM) confirmed this hypothesis,
revealing a lattice spacing of 0.197 nm for Pd NCs, consistent
with a face-centered cubic structure with {100} facets. After
DMF treatment, PdH NCs displayed a lattice spacing of 0.228
nm, representing approximately 6.3% lattice expansion
compared to Pd NCs (Figure 2c−e). While TEM provides
insights into local structure changes, synchrotron-based X-ray
diffraction (XRD) was employed to characterize the overall
crystal structure (Figure 2f). The XRD pattern of Pd NCs
displayed distinct diffraction peaks characteristic of a face-
centered cubic (fcc) structure. In PdH NCs, these peaks
shifted to lower 2θ values, indicating lattice parameter
expansion. Furthermore, the emergence of a PdH phase was
confirmed, with diffraction peaks aligning closely with standard
values for PdH0.64. The expanded Pd lattice and formation of
PdH NCs were further corroborated by the reduction in unit
cell parameters.
Previous theoretical and experimental studies suggest that
lattice expansion is associated with a reduction in Pd 3d
binding energy.39 To further investigate this alteration in
electronic structure, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)
was performed. A negative shift of approximately 0.2 eV in the
Pd 3d5/2 peak was observed following surface reconstruction,
signifying modifications in electronic structure and a down-
ward shift of the d-band center (Figure 2g). X-ray absorption
near-edge structure (XANES) spectroscopy further confirmed

Selectively Promoted CAT- and POD-like Activities of
PdH Nanocrystals. The incorporation of H atoms alters the
lattice constant of Pd nanostructures and induces surface
strain, which will significantly influence the surface character-
istics and biocatalytic properties of Pd NCs. Accordingly, the
enzyme-like activities of PdH NCs were subsequently
evaluated. Unlike Pd/H2 NCs, PdH NCs did not exhibit
enhanced scavenging abilities for •OH and O2
•−compared to
pristine Pd NCs (Figures S10 and S11). Moreover, the total
antioxidative capacity of PdH NCs against DPPH was slightly
lower than that of pristine Pd NCs (Figure S12), possibly due
to a minor decrease in dispersity following multiple washes.
The H2O2 scavenging capability of PdH NCs was then
assessed by examining their ability to catalyze the decom-
position of H2O2 into oxygen (O2) under neutral conditions.
Unexpectedly, the introduction of PdH NCs into an H2O2
solution led to the rapid generation of macroscopic O2
bubbles, which were absent in the Pd NCs-H2O2 solution
within the first 5 min (Figure 3a). Over time, the quantity and
size of O2 bubbles in the PdH NCs solution surpassed those
observed in the Pd NCs solution. Quantitative analysis using
ESR oximetry revealed a significant broadening of the ESR
signal line width and reduced peak intensity, indicative of a
faster rate of O2 generation (Figure 3b).36 The dissolved O2
concentration increased from 0.33 mM to 0.47 mM for Pd
NCs and to 0.72 mM for PdH NCs after 20 min (Figure 3c).
These findings indicate that PdH NCs catalyze the conversion
of H2O2 into O2 in a concentration- and time-dependent
manner, with a significantly higher rate of dissolved O2
production compared to Pd NCs (Figures S13 and S14).
The consumption of H2O2 was monitored by measuring the
intensity of its characteristic absorption at 240 nm. As shown
in Figure 3d, H2O2 content decreased gradually in the presence
of Pd NCs, as evidenced by reduced absorption at this
wavelength. However, PdH NCs induced a pronounced and
time-dependent reduction in H2O2, with a consumption rate at
least 5 times higher than that of Pd NCs. Validation using a
H2O2 assay kit confirmed that H2O2 elimination increased with
PdH NCs concentration, achieving complete H2O2 removal at
50 μg/mL within 5 min, while Pd NCs at the same
concentration scavenged only about 22.7% of H2O2 (Figures
S15 and S16). Kinetic analysis showed that both Pd NCs and
PdH NCs followed Michaelis−Menten kinetics across H2O2
concentrations ranging from 5 to 60 mM (Figure S17). The
specific activity of PdH NCs was determined to be 20 units/
mg, nearly 5 times greater than that of Pd NCs, further
demonstrating the enhanced CAT-like activity of PdH NCs.
Moreover, PdH NCs showed a minimal decline in catalytic
activity after seven cycles, indicating sustained catalytic
capacity under physiological conditions (Figure S18). Unlike
natural CAT, the catalytic activity of PdH NCs increased
linearly with pH, reaching optimal activity in neutral to basic
environments (Figure S19). These findings suggest that the
stable incorporation of H atoms into the Pd crystal lattice
selectively enhances the CAT-like activity of Pd nanocrystals.
The POD- and OXD-like activities of PdH NCs were next
evaluated under mildly acid conditions using TMB as the
chromogenic substrate. As shown in Figure S20, the
absorbance of oxidized TMB at 652 nm in the presence of
PdH NCs without H2O2 was slightly lower than that observed
for Pd NCs, suggesting that lattice expansion does not
influence the OXD-like activity of Pd NCs. However, PdH
NCs rapidly catalyzed the oxidation of TMB by H2O2,
producing a pronounced blue oxidation product within 5 min,
whereas a comparable concentration of Pd NCs induced only a
weak color change (Figure 3e). Extending the duration to 30
min intensified the color of Pd NCs, although it remained
significantly less intense than that of the PdH NCs. The UV−
vis spectra showed a consistent increase in TMB absorbance
for PdH NCs over time, plateauing at 50 min, a significantly
faster rate compared to Pd NCs (Figures 3f and S21). These
results indicate that while both Pd NCs and PdH NCs catalyze
TMB oxidation, PdH NCs exhibit much higher POD-like
activity. To quantify the POD-mimicking efficiency of PdH
NCs, the influence of H2O2 concentrations on the TMB
oxidation rate was examined. Typical Michaelis−Menten
curves were derived for both Pd NCs and PdH NCs (Figure
S22). The apparent specific activity for the POD-like activity of
PdH NCs at pH 4.6 was approximately 1,500 units/mg, four
times greater than that of Pd NCs, indicating that lattice
expansion significantly enhances the POD-like activity of Pd
NCs. In addition, we conducted a preliminary comparison of
the POD-like activities of PdH nanomaterials with other
typical metals and metal oxide nanomaterials reported in the
literature.6,43 As shown in Figure S23, the specific activity of
PdH NCs is not only significantly higher than that of Pd NCs
but also exceeds those of other metals nanozymes (such as
monometallic Ru NPs or alloyed AuPtCo NPs) and metal
oxides (e.g., Fe3O4 NPs). The potential involvement of •OH in
the enhanced POD-like activity was further explored using
terephthalic acid (TA) as a fluorescent sensor, which
specifically reacts with •OH to produce a fluorescence signal
at 435 nm. Figure 3g illustrates a strong fluorescence signal at
435 nm upon adding H2O2 and PdH NCs, confirming the
selective enhancement of POD-like activity by Pd NCs under
mildly acid conditions. To further verify this result, we
employed ESR spectroscopy to detect the generated
•OH
radicals. As shown in Figure 3h, samples containing the spin
trap DMPO and H2O2 exhibited no ESR signal. However, the
addition of either PdH NCs or Pd NCs produced a distinct
1:2:2:1 ESR signal corresponding to the standard
DMPO/•OH adduct, indicating that both Pd-based nano-
zymes can catalyze the generation of
•OH from H2O2.
Notably, the signal intensity was significantly enhanced in
the presence of PdH NCs compared to Pd NCs, correlating
with the enhanced POD-like activity observed for PdH NCs.
Moreover, the introduction of the •OH scavenger epigalloca-
techin gallate (EGCG) completely quenched the DMPO/•OH
signal, further confirming that PdH NCs exert their POD-like
activity by catalyzing the generation of •OH from H2O2. To
further investigate the surface changes of PdH NCs during the
catalytic process, we conducted in situ attenuated total
reflection Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectra.
Following the addition of H2O2, a distinct vibrational signal
appeared at 1625 cm−1, which is attributed to the formation of
adsorbed OH* intermediates during the activation of H2O2
(Figure S24). The significantly higher intensity observed in
PdH NCs relative to Pd NCs reflects the generation of more

Finally, the stability of PdH NCs was systematically
evaluated to assess their robustness in biomedical applications.
As shown in Figures S25 and S26, PdH NCs maintained
effective catalytic activity over a wider pH range and at higher
temperatures, indicating their potential for applications under
various conditions. In addition, the POD-like enzymatic
activity of PdH NCs remained stable over extended periods
in both water and PBS (Figure S27). Furthermore, we
performed TEM characterization of PdH NCs dispersed in
water over different time periods. As shown in Figure S28, the
lattice spacing of PdH NCs aged for 60 and 180 days remained
consistent with that of newly synthesized PdH NCs, and was
significantly wider than that of Pd NCs. These results
collectively demonstrate the robust stability of the PdH NCs
after long-term storage.
The aforementioned results highlight that stable PdH NCs,
produced through in situ hydrogenation of Pd NCs, exhibit
specific enhancements in POD- and CAT-like activities
through selective activation of H2O2 (Figure 3i,j). In contrast
to the Pd/H2 NCs, the OXD-like, SOD-like, and
•OH
scavenging capabilities of PdH NCs remained unchanged or
were even lower than those of the Pd NCs (Figure S29). Thus,
the stable incorporation of H atoms into the Pd crystal lattice
selectively enhances POD- and CAT-like activities, likely due
to modifications in the lattice constants and surface strain.
Universality Testing of Different PdH Nanostruc-
tures. To verify the universality of selectively enhanced
nanozyme activities of PdH nanocrystals, diverse models of
PdH nanostructures, including varying H content, alternative
H sources, and different morphologies of Pd nanocrystals, were
designed to determine the impact of stable H incorporation on

of the resulting PdH NCs improved from 27.6% to 42.7%,
further increasing to 82.8% after 4 h of reaction. The CAT-like
activity plateaued once the H:Pd ratio reached its maximum
value, corresponding closely with the degree of hydride
formation. A similar trend was observed for POD-like activity,

Selectively Enhanced Catalytic Mechanism of PdH
NCs toward H2O2. To elucidate the role of stably
incorporated H in selectively enhancing the catalytic activity,
the reaction mechanisms and energy profiles for the POD- and
CAT-like activities of PdH NCs were systematically inves-
tigated. Due to its hydrogen-rich lattice structure, PdH NCs
are hypothesized to function as an in situ H reservoir,
dynamically supplying H during catalytic processes. To validate
this hypothesis, a hydrogen-supplying mechanism for both
POD- and CAT-like activities of PdH was proposed and
compared with conventional catalytic pathways (Figure 5a,b).
The corresponding reaction energy profiles are presented in
Figure 5c,d.
For the POD-like activity of PdH, the catalytic mechanism
(Figure 5a) can be delineated into three stages: (1)
H2O2adsorption and isomerization. H2O2 molecules adsorb
onto the PdH(100) surface, forming a stable adsorption
complex. This step is thermodynamically more favorable on
PdH (−0.53 eV) than on Pd (−0.22 eV) (Figure 5c). The
adsorbed H2O2 subsequently undergoes isomerization and
decomposes into H2O and O* adsorbates, consistent with the
conventional mechanism of POD.48 (2) Lattice H involvement.
The O* adsorbates capture two H atoms from the PdH lattice,
forming OH* and H2O* intermediates and creating H
vacancies within the lattice. This step is unique to the H-
supplying mechanism and is thermodynamically more
favorable (reaction energy, 0.36 eV) compared to the
traditional pathway (0.67 eV). (3) Substrate oxidation and
lattice regeneration. The PdH facilitates the oxidation of a
substrate, such as TMB, yielding oxTMB while restoring the
lattice to its original state by refilling the H vacancies with
substrate-derived H atoms. This H-supplying mechanism
enhances the catalytic efficiency of PdH NCs, particularly in
the rate-determining step of H2O2 activation, which exhibits a
lower reaction energy (0.64 eV) for H2O desorption compared
to Pd (0.72 eV). These findings validate the hypothesis that H-
rich lattice of PdH actively participates in the catalytic process,
contributing to its superior POD-like activity compared to Pd.
Based on these findings, the CAT-like activity of PdH NCs
was further explored leveraging its unique H-supplying
capability. Unlike the POD-like mechanism, which primarily
involves H atoms in oxidizing substrates, the CAT-like
mechanism highlights PdH’s ability to facilitate the dispro-
portionation of H2O2 (Figure 5b). In the proposed CAT
mechanism, PdH undergoes the following steps: (1) Adsorbed
H2O2 molecules homolytically cleave into two OH*
adsorbates. (2) Lattice H atoms are directly utilized to
generate two H2O molecules, leaving H vacancies in the PdH
lattice. This step differs from the conventional mechanism,
which relies on a second H2O2 molecule as the hydrogen
source. (3) The H vacancies are subsequently filled by H
atoms from an H2O2 molecule, resulting in the formation of O2
and the restoration of PdH to its initial state, completing the
catalytic cycle. The reaction energy profile for this mechanism
(Figure 5d) demonstrates that PdH exhibits significantly
enhanced CAT-like activity under the H-supplying pathway.
These results further highlight the critical role of lattice H in
modulating the catalytic efficiency and reactivity of PdH.
In addition, we also analyzed the OXD-like activity, •OH
scavenging activity and the SOD-like activity of Pd and PdH
from the perspective of theoretical calculation. It has been
reported that the adsorption and activation of O2 on the
surface of nanomaterials can describe their OXD-like activity.
The adsorption energy can serve as a physical descriptor of
OXD-like activity and is numerically consistent with the
Brønsted−Evans−Polanyi (BEP) relationship with the activa-
tion energy of O2. To verify phenomenon observed in our
experiments, we analyzed the adsorption energies of O2 on
both Pd and PdH surfaces.12,49 As shown in Figure S33, Pd can
activate oxygen, stretching its O−O bond length from 1.23 Å
in vacuum to 1.33 Å. The adsorption energy is −0.88 eV.
When H is incorporated into the Pd lattice to form stable PdH,
its ability to activate oxygen becomes weaker. The O−O bond
length of adsorbed O2 is shorter than that on Pd, at 1.31 Å, and
the adsorption energy is −0.49 eV, which is 44% lower than
that on Pd. This indicates that after hydrogen incorporation,
the affinity for O2 is significantly decreased, and the activation
ability is significantly weakened. Consequently, the OXD-like
activity of PdH is weaker compared to Pd. The
•OH
adsorption energies on Pd and PdH, which are calculated to
be −4.83 and −4.87 eV, respectively, are quite close (Figures
4c and S34). This could explain why hydrogen incorporation
fails to significantly enhance the •OH scavenging capacity.

Structural and Electronic Modifications Induced by H
Incorporation in the Pd Lattice. H atoms can be
intercalated into the Pd crystal lattice leading lattice expansion,
companying with structural and electronic transformations. To
confirm these effects, structure optimization was performed on
the primitive cells of Pd and PdH via DFT calculations. The
optimized structures revealed significant lattice expansion by
4.9%, with the lattice parameters increasing from a = b = c =
3.89 Å to a = b = c = 4.08 Å, which is well consistent with the
lattice expansion observed in the experiment. The correspond-
ing interatomic Pd−Pd distance increased from 2.75 to 2.89 Å,
highlighting the significant structural changes induced by H
intercalation (Figure 5e). This H-enriched lattice structure
effectively serves as a dynamic reservoir for H, thereby
facilitating H-supplying chemical reactions.
The impact of stable H incorporation on the electronic
structure was further explored using the projected density of
states (PDOS) analysis for both Pd and PdH (Figure 5f). The
results indicated a narrowing of the d-bandwidth, along with an
upward shift of the d-band center (from −1.66 to −1.54 eV)
and the Fermi levels (from −2.63 to −2.36 eV) (Figure S36).
These electronic modifications suggest the enhanced inter-
actions between PdH and potential substrates, implying an
increased adsorption capacity to H2O2 (−0.53 eV/PdH vs
−0.22 eV/Pd).
Collectively, these findings demonstrate that H-intercala-
tion-induced lattice expansion is an effective strategy for tuning
both the structural and electronic properties of Pd nanocryst-
als, paving the way for their novel applications in biocatalysis
and materials science.

Their Therapeutic Efficacy in Tumor Treatment. Nano-

highly toxic •OH, which are crucial for catalytic tumor therapy,

elevated H2O2 levels and acidic conditions.50,51 The
introduction of H can selectively enhance the catalytic activity
of Pd NCs toward H2O2. However, the limited intracellular
levels of H2O2 and the mild acidity in tumor tissues could
hinder the persistence and efficiency of the PdH NCs-catalyzed
reaction, potentially reducing their anticancer efficacy. To
address these challenges, GOx was combined with Pd NCs to
construct a cascade catalytic system. GOx oxidizes glucose into
gluconic acid and H2O2, simultaneously increasing substrate
availability for POD-like reactions and enhancing the acidic

The PdH@GOx NCs were synthesized by first coating
negatively charged PdH NCs with positively charged poly-
(allylamine hydrochloride) (PAH). Subsequently, the GOx
molecules were covalently attached to the amino groups of
PAHs via carbodiimide-catalyzed amide bond formation

successful assembly of the nanocomposites, with the surface
charge shifting from −16 to +45 mV after PAH onto the PdH
NCs and subsequently to −3 mV upon GOx attachment
(Figure S37). The UV−vis absorption peak of GOx at 280 nm
was observed in PdH@GOx NCs, further verifying successful
loading (Figures S38 and S39). Quantitative analysis indicated
a maximum GOx loading capacity of approximately 9.8 wt %.
At this concentration, the presence of GOx did not noticeably
affect the enzyme-like activity of PdH NCs (Figure S40). In
the PdH@GOx NCs system, GOx catalyzed the decom-

of •OH. Concurrently, gluconic acid lowered the pH of the
reaction system, further promoting the POD-like activity of
PdH NCs and enhancing their anticancer efficacy (Figure 6a).
To verify the activity of PdH NCs@GOx in converting
glucose to gluconic acid, PdH@GOx NCs were added to a
glucose solution, and the pH changes during the reaction were
monitored. The pH of a glucose solution initially remained
near 7.0. However, upon the addition of PdH@GOx NCs, the
pH rapidly decreased to below 4.8 within 2 h (Figure S41),
indicating sustained catalytic production of gluconic acid and a
corresponding decrease in pH over time. Notably, after 1 h of
reaction, the rate of pH reduction in the PdH@GOx NCs
group was significantly faster than that observed in the Pd@
GOx NCs group. These findings suggest that the enhanced
POD-like activity of PdH@GOx NCs accelerates the catalytic
oxidation of glucose by promoting the decomposition of H2O2,
thereby improving the efficiency of gluconic acid production.

NCs, were subsequently analyzed. As shown in Figure S42,
Pd@GOx NCs or PdH@GOx NCs elevated H2O2 concen-

its enzymatic activity. However, the rate of increase in H2O2
concentrations was slower in the PdH@GOx NCs solution,
primarily due to the higher POD-like activity of PdH NCs,
which rapidly catalyzed and decomposed some of the
generated H2O2 into O2 or
•OH. Furthermore, the
concentrations of H2O2 were positively correlated with the
concentrations of PdH@GOx NCs (Figure S43). To analyze
the dynamic changes of H2O2 and pH in the tumor
microenvironment, we used homogenates of mouse tumor
tissues to simulate a realistic tumor environment (Figure S44).
The result further demonstrate that PdH@GOx NCs can
catalyze the oxidation of glucose in the tumor microenviron-
ment. Based on these findings, the ability of PdH@GOx NCs
to produce •OH in the cascade catalytic reaction involving
glucose was assessed. After the addition of PdH@GOx NCs,
the absorbance of TMB at 652 nm increased, indicating the
production •OH (Figure 6b). Compared to Pd@GOx NCs,
PdH@GOx NCs demonstrated higher absorbance, reflecting
their superior efficiency in generating

Encouraged by the selectively enhanced enzyme-like
activities of PdH NCs and the reliable H2O2 supply provided
by GOx, the potential therapeutic efficacy of the cascade-
catalyzed PdH@GOx NCs against 4T1 cells was investigated
in vitro. Incubation with various concentrations of GOx or Pd
NCs for 24 h did not significantly affect cell viability (Figure
6c), indicating their good biocompatibility. In contrast, PdH
NCs induced cell death in 4T1 cells in a concentration-
dependent manner. Notably, PdH NCs exhibited negligible
cytotoxicity to normal vascular endothelial cells (HUVECs)
and intestinal epithelial cell line 6 (IEC-6), even at
concentrations as high as 200 μg/mL (Figure S46). This
selectivity is likely due to the elevated oxidative stress state of
tumor cells, which facilitates the POD-like activity of the PdH
NCs and leads to the generation of more toxic •OH radicals. In
addition, PdH@GOx NCs exhibited even greater cytotoxicity
against 4T1 cells compared to PdH NCs, highlighting the
additional contribution of GOx in elevating cell death through
cascade catalytic reactions. To further assess the cytotoxic
effects on tumor cells, live/dead cell staining was conducted
using calcein acetoxymethyl ester (calcein-AM, green) and
propidium iodide (PI, red). As depicted in Figure S47, cells
treated with Pd NCs exhibited strong green fluorescence,
indicating most cells remained viable. In contrast, a significant
number of red-stained dead cells were observed in 4T1 cells
treated with PdH NCs, and nearly all cells were killed
following treatment with PdH@GOx NCs, consistent with cell
viability results. To compare the levels of cytosolic ROS in 4T1
cells, laser scanning confocal microscopy was employed using
the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate
(DCFH-DA) as a ROS indicator. The green fluorescence
intensity in 4T1 cells treated with PdH@GOx NCs was much
stronger than that observed in cells treated with PdH NCs or
Pd@GOx at the same concentration, indicating a higher level
of oxidative stress induced by PdH@GOx NCs (Figures S48
and S49). The apoptosis-inducing capacity of PdH@GOx NCs
was investigated by analyzing the apoptosis rates of 4T1 cells
using flow cytometry with Annexin V-FITC and PI staining.
The percentages of cells in the early and late apoptotic stages
were significantly increased following treatment with PdH@
GOx NCs (Figure 6d,e). This apoptotic pathway is likely due
to ROS levels exceeding the critical threshold, disrupting the
ROS scavenging system and inducing cellular apoptosis.

contrast, PdH NCs and Pd@GOx NCs demonstrated
moderate therapeutic effects, with tumor inhibition rates of

■CONCLUSIONS
This study provides a novel insight into the selective regulation
of enzyme-like activities in H-loaded Pd nanomaterials through
molecular H. The different synthetic routes for creating Pd
hydrides greatly affect the enzyme-like activity and specificity
of Pd-based nanozymes. Initially, Pd/H2 NCs, synthesized by
directly injecting hydrogen gas into an aqueous solution
containing Pd NCs, demonstrated a selective enhancement in
antioxidative enzyme-like activities against H2O2, O2
•−, and
•OH due to the sustained release of bioreductive H2.
To address the intrinsic instability of the Pd/H2 NCs
system, stable PdH nanocrystals were synthesized via the in situ
catalytic decomposition of alternative H sources, such as DMF.
These stable PdH NCs exhibited specific enhancements in
POD- and CAT-like activities through selective activation of
H2O2, while their OXD-like, SOD-like, and •OH scavenging
activities showed no significant changes or were lower than
those of the Pd NCs. The selective enhancement in H2O2
activation of PdH NCs was positively correlated with the
extent of H incorporation into the PdH NCs. Furthermore,
this phenomenon was also observed when N2H4 was used as a
H source and with spherical morphologies of Pd nanomateri-
als.
Multiple spectroscopic analyses and DFT calculations
revealed that lattice tensile strain and changes in electronic
structures were critical factors driving the high catalytic activity
and specificity of PdH NCs. Building upon the observed
enhanced selective H2O2 activation, the PdH/GOx nano-
complex was developed as a cascade catalytic system in tumor
therapy. The introduction of GOx facilitated the generation of
abundant H2O2 and intensified tumor acidosis, thereby
enhancing the specificity of POD-like activity of PdH NCs
within tumor microenvironments. As a result, the PdH/GOx
nanocomplex demonstrated remarkable anticancer efficacy
through a cascade catalytic reaction, with minimal damage to
normal tissues.
This study not only uncovers the influence and mechanism
of hydride formation on the activity and selectivity of Pd
nanozymes but also introduces a promising strategy for the
precise regulation of specific enzyme-like activities in H-loaded
nanozymes. These findings open new opportunities for
breakthroughs in enzymatic applications and highlight the
potential of PdH-based systems in advanced catalytic and
therapeutic platforms.

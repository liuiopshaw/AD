Abstract
Nanozymes have progressed from simple enzyme mimics to a versatile 
class of artificial catalysts that expand functionality beyond the reach 
of natural enzymes. In this Perspective, we examine how the materials 
design of nanozymes enables catalytic behaviours that are inaccessible  
to biological systems, including activity in non-biological environments,  
promotion of non-natural reactions and the integration of multiple 
catalytic functions within a single nanostructure. Through nanoscale 
control over physicochemical parameters and emulation of key enzyme 
architectures, nanozymes can achieve finely tunable activity and 
selectivity. Facile surface functionalization and inherent electrical 
connectivity further enhance their performance in complex systems. 
Collectively, these attributes extend catalysis beyond the constraints 
of natural enzymes, driving innovations in an array of different fields, 
including biomedicine, agriculture, environmental remediation and 
energy conversion.

Versatility in catalysis
Natural enzymes have evolved to catalyse reactions in highly selective 
and efficient ways within biological systems. Nanozymes expand this 
catalytic ability through the use of chemistries and materials not readily 
available within biological systems.
Catalysis in non-biological environments
Natural enzyme activity is typically restricted to biological or 
biological-like environments. Although some enzymes have adapted 
to specialized niches, such as acidic organelles21, alkaline secretions22–25 
or psychrophilic and thermophilic ecosystems26–30, they generally func-
tion within narrow physicochemical windows shaped by evolutionary 
pressures. Most enzymes lose activity or denature irreversibly under 
non-physiological conditions because their active sites depend on 
the proper folding of a polypeptide chain, which creates a specific 
coordination and solution environment to facilitate the reaction. This 
structural sensitivity usually limits the applicability of natural enzymes 
to environments closely resembling their native physiological ones. 
By contrast, nanozymes are composed of inorganic or hybrid nano-
materials whose active sites operate via surface-mediated adsorption 
of substrates and intermediates without molecular folding involved. 
This feature enables nanozymes to perform biological reactions under 
temperature or pH conditions that are beyond the physiological limits 
of natural enzymes, providing more effective solutions for catalysis and 
sensing (Fig. 1). For instance, bare Fe3O4 nanoparticles exhibit stable 
peroxidase-like activity in buffer-free environments at temperatures 
approaching the melting point of common plastics (130–260 °C)31.
A key asset of nanozymes is that they can be designed to be intrinsi-
cally compatible with non-biological solutions, such as those with reac-
tive inhibitors, high ionic strength or organic solvents32. For instance, 
Cu2O nanozymes maintain stable laccase-like activity in high saline 
conditions, making them effective for phenolic pollutant detection and 
removal in wastewater33. In another case, the introduction of an organic 
solvent, which would denature an enzyme, into a nanozyme-based 
fluorescence assay can enhance product solubility and reduce quench-
ing, thereby improving assay sensitivity without compromising 
performance34. Notably, native enzymes can also be engineered to 
tolerate high-salt or organic solvent conditions, but such adaptations 
are generally restricted to narrow solvent windows and often require 
extensive modification to preserve stability35,36. However, a nanozyme 
material can be chosen and designed from the outset to have both 
intrinsic activity and stability in a range of solution conditions.
It is worth noting that nanocatalysts of natural origin are hypoth-
esized to have acted as proto-enzymes, catalysing life-related reac-
tions on prebiotic Earth and in extraterrestrial environments37,38. For 
example, nano-minerals composed of iron, nickel or metal sulfides may 
have driven carbon and nitrogen fixation, generating carbon skeletons 
and ammonia as fundamental building blocks of life39–41. Clay minerals 
such as montmorillonite and kaolinite, with their high surface area and 
nanoconfinement effects, could have concentrated these intermedi-
ates and promoted the polymerization of amino acids and nucleotides 
into peptides and RNA strands42,43. In addition, nano-minerals may have

Introduction
Natural enzymes are biological catalysts responsible for the vast 
majority of catalytic processes required to sustain life. Their catalytic 
activity and specificity arise from evolutionarily optimized active sites 
within folded protein structures. These properties give enzymes major 
industrial importance, and they are mass-produced for applications as 
diverse as biosensors, detergents and the production of fine chemicals1. 
Nevertheless, the complex protein structures of these systems can 
render them fragile and prone to both chemical and conformational 
degradation, and in some cases, their production can be costly. The 
latter is particularly true if complicated expression, extraction and 
purification processes are required.
As such, the concept of artificial enzymes that could perform simi-
lar roles to natural enzymes, but with greater stability, has been pur-
sued since the last century. Early research in this area largely focused 
on mimicking the molecular arrangement of enzyme active sites2, but 
attention later shifted towards nanomaterials as artificial enzymes. 
These nanomaterials with enzyme-like activities, termed ‘nanozymes’3, 
have now fully emerged as promising alternatives to their native 
counterparts. Since their conceptual introduction in the early 2000s4,5, 
a wide variety of nanozymes composed of inorganic (in most cases) 
and/or organic materials have been actively developed6–11.
Most nanozyme reviews have focused on efforts to engineer 
these materials to replicate the functions of natural enzymes, but with 
improved catalytic efficiency or stability12–15. Although such biomim-
icry has yielded nanozymes with remarkable catalytic performance, 
it implicitly treats natural enzymes as the upper benchmark for func-
tion. An alternative and increasingly important question asks: in what 
ways might nanozymes do more than natural enzymes? From a mate-
rials perspective, nanozymes are not constrained by the structural, 
chemical or evolutionary limitations of proteins — instead, their syn-
thetic and often inorganic nature extends the accessible functional 
space of catalysts. This shift in viewpoint places conceptual and mecha-
nistic understanding at the centre of nanozyme research, as reflected 
in previous reviews addressing this topic16–20.
Viewed through this lens, nanozymes are not merely improved 
substitutes for enzymes but represent a broader class of catalytic 
materials with distinct design principles. Their composition, archi-
tecture and modes of operation need not follow the rules governing 
protein-based catalysis, suggesting the possibility of functions that 
extend beyond traditional enzymatic paradigms. Recognizing and 
articulating these distinctions is essential for moving the field beyond 
performance comparisons and towards a deeper understanding of 
what nanozymes can uniquely offer.
In this Perspective, we adopt a materials-centric view to examine 
how synthetic design principles enable nanozymes to expand cata-
lytic function beyond what nature offers. Drawing on concepts from 
materials science, nanofabrication and surface engineering, we outline 
how nanozymes can close the existing gaps with natural enzymes and 
ultimately surpass them. First, we discuss the versatility of nanozyme 
catalysis, focusing on how access to non-biological chemistries and 
architectures enables catalytic activity across diverse environments 
and supports functions inaccessible to natural enzymes. Then, 
we elaborate on the tailorability of nanozymes, emphasizing how mod-
ern nanofabrication and surface engineering strategies provide precise 
control over activity, selectivity and modes of regulation. Next, we high-
light how these attributes position nanozymes to tackle challenges that 
remain beyond the reach of enzymes, particularly in contexts in which 
biological catalysts face practical or fundamental limitations. Finally,

Catalysis beyond biological reactions
Nanozymes offer exceptional versatility in catalysing non-native 
chemical reactions that are inaccessible to natural enzymes. One such 
class of reactions is bioorthogonal catalysis, which refers to chemical 
transformations that proceed inside living organisms without inter-
fering with endogenous biochemical pathways50 (Fig. 2a). Natural 
enzymes are incapable of facilitating these reactions, as they have 
been evolutionarily refined to react with strict substrate specificity and 
structural constraints. To expand nanozymes beyond what enzymes 
can do, researchers have developed bioorthogonal nanozymes by 
incorporating transition metal catalysts into nanomaterials through 
encapsulation or surface conjugation51.
Depending on their catalytic design, bioorthogonal nanozymes 
can mediate various metal-catalysed transformations within biologi-
cal environments, including uncaging or cleavage reactions, coupling 
reactions, intramolecular cyclization and transfer hydrogenation52,53. 
These reactions enable the selective conversion of inert prodrugs 
or imaging agents into active forms at disease sites, such as tumour 
tissues or bacterial biofilms, allowing localized therapeutic or

Multicatalytic activities
Natural enzymes, in general, are evolutionarily optimized for high 
specificity, often catalysing a single primary reaction with remarkable 
efficiency. Notable examples include peroxidases that preferentially 
reduce hydrogen peroxide, glucose oxidases that react specifically with 
β-d-glucose, and superoxide dismutases that catalyse the dismutation 
of superoxide radicals to mitigate oxidative stress. Although some 
native enzymes, such as alcohol dehydrogenases and some matrix 
metalloproteases56,57, can act on more than one type of substrate or 
reaction, multi-enzymatic behaviour — in which a single structure 
exhibits different catalytic activities depending on its materials com-
ponents and surrounding environment — can be incorporated into 
nanozymes relatively readily (Fig. 2b). One such nanozyme is a gold 
nanorod coated with a carbon shell containing nanoscale substrate 
channels that penetrate to the gold surface58. This nanozyme used an 
applied electrochemical potential to alter the pH of the local solution 
environment inside the channels. A physiological pH inside the chan-
nels allowed the selective oxidation of dopamine, whereas a change in 
potential to split water increased the pH to oxidize glucose under alka-
line conditions. The nanozyme thus achieved both catechol-oxidation 
and glucose-oxidation activities in different reaction environments 
without bulk pH adjustment. This multi-enzymatic behaviour was 
performed within an unadulterated whole-blood sample.
Through rational control of composition and architecture, dis-
tinct activities can also be colocalized within one nanozyme to build 
cascade systems59,60. One example is a nanoparticle system consist-
ing of an Ag core surrounded by a porous Cu shell (Fig. 2b). CO2 was 
first reduced to CO on the Ag core, followed by the diffusion of CO 
intermediates through the confined nanochannels within the porous 
Cu shell, where they were further converted into C2–C3 multicarbon 
products. These C2 and C3 hydrocarbon products cannot be produced 
directly from carbon dioxide by enzymes60. Moreover, nanozymes can 
be designed to integrate both enzyme-mimicking and bioorthogonal 
catalytic functions. As an example, a Ce-metal organic framework 
(MOF) containing isolated single Cu sites within the lattice exhibited 
both oxidase-like and peroxidase-like activities to generate ROS and 
kill tumour cells (Fig. 2b). To enhance selectivity, the same nanozyme 
was further cloaked with cancer cell membranes, which provided 
homologous targeting and immune evasion for preferential uptake 
by tumour cells. Once internalized, the elevated glutathione levels 
triggered a Cu(II)/Cu(I) redox cycle, sustaining ROS generation while 
simultaneously activating azide–alkyne click chemistry for in situ drug 
synthesis within a tumour61.
Tailorability
Natural enzymes can achieve extraordinary catalytic activity and selec-
tivity. Their protein scaffolds precisely position the active site, control 
substrate access and create microenvironments that lower energy bar-
riers. These evolutionary strategies have inspired the synthetic design 
of nanozymes to mimic enzymatic structures, activity and selectivity.
Optimizing catalytic activity
In natural enzymes, the choice of metals at catalytic centres is limited 
by geochemical availability and by the need for stable coordination 
with amino acid ligands within the protein structure. Thus, the majority 
of enzyme-active sites are built from a limited group of metals, most 
frequently Fe, Cu, Zn and Mg (refs. 62,63). Nanozymes are not bound 
by these constraints and can incorporate elements rare or absent in 
biological systems to optimize performance (Fig. 3a). For example, 
nanozymes based on platinum-group metals often display outstanding 
peroxidase-like activity64,65.
Catalytic activity can also be enhanced by increasing the density 
and reactivity of active sites. In proteins, catalytic centres are typi-
cally well defined and limited in number, whereas nanozymes can be 
designed to host far more sites within the same volume. Even when the 
turnover frequency of individual sites is modest, the collective activ-
ity of nanozyme catalytic centres can be substantial. This efficiency 
is often achieved by tailoring the morphology of nanozymes, includ-
ing size, shape and surface roughness, to maximize the exposure of 
active sites66 (Fig. 3b). Tuning the crystallographic facets provides 
another strategy for enhancing catalytic activity. Surfaces enriched 
in low-coordination atoms are generally more reactive than flat fac-
ets dominated by highly coordinated atoms (Fig. 3c). For instance, 
Pd concave nanocrystals with high-index (730) facets show much 
higher oxidase-like catalytic activity than Pd nanocubes with low-index 
(100) facets67. The (730) facets expose unsaturated six-coordinated Pd 
atoms that donate electrons to and orient absorbed O2 into a geom-
etry favourable for hydrogen bonding and efficient H-transfer with 
ascorbate, whereas the (100) facets are dominated by more saturated 
eight-coordinated atoms with lower reactivity.

sic strain, which perturbs bond lengths and orbital overlap to alter 
the energetics of defect formation and charge transfer. For example, 
in ultrathin ceria nanoplates, tensile strain elongates Ce−O bonds 
and increases their covalency, which modifies the overlap between 
Ce 4f and O 2p orbitals68. This change lowers the energy barrier 
for oxygen vacancy formation and stabilizes the resulting defects, 
thereby facilitating Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycling and boosting superoxide 
dismutase-mimetic activity. A second approach for optimizing activity 
is surface alloying, in which the incorporation of a secondary element 
redistributes electronic density at the catalytic interface. For example, 
in Ni–Pt nanoparticles, the presence of Ni downshifts the d-band centre 
of Pt, weakening the adsorption of HO*/O* intermediates relative to 
pure Pt (ref. 69). This structural motif optimizes the balance between 
binding and release, enabling more efficient oxidant transfer to sub-
strates and resulting in markedly enhanced peroxidase-like activity. 
A third strategy is to engineer the coordination environment of metal 
centres in single-atom nanozymes, in which the number, geometry and 
type of coordinating ligands together define the local electronic state. 
In carbon-supported, planar Fe single-atom nanozymes, introducing 
an extra axial N ligand to generate FeN5 sites substantially enhances 
oxidase-like activity compared with FeN4 (ref. 70). This increased activ-
ity is because the additional ligand exerts an electron push-effect into 
the O2 antibonding orbital, weakening the O−O bond and facilitating 
its cleavage. In another design, inspired by the presence of phospho-
rus in natural enzymes, replacing one of the N ligands in FeN4 with a P 
atom created FeN3P sites71. The softer donor character and lower elec-
tronegativity of P relative to N reduced electron withdrawal from the 
Fe centre, promoting H2O2 activation and intermediate formation. 
As a result, FeN3P nanozymes displayed peroxidase-like activity almost 
10 times higher than FeN4 analogues, approaching the catalytic effi-
ciency of natural horseradish peroxidase. Notably, in single-atom 
nanozymes, the support ligands (or neighbouring sites) serve as 
more than electron donors or acceptors and can directly participate 
in catalysis by binding co-reactant species, functional groups or inter-
mediates to enhance the catalytic performance of the active centre72. 
For example, Ru functions as a co-catalytic support that anchors and 
electronically couples with single-atom Pt, providing oxophilic sites for 
OH adsorption, which subsequently react with CO bound to Pt during 
the methanol oxidation reaction73. This metal–support interaction 
enhances the resistance to CO poisoning and promotes the efficiency 
of the reaction.
Despite growing efforts to tune the electronic structure of 
nanozymes, the optimization of catalytic activity has, in many 
cases, remained empirical and system-specific. In this context, stud-
ies have sought to identify quantitative descriptors that correlate 
electronic configuration with catalytic performance in nanozymes, 
most notably in crystalline nanozymes with well-defined coordina-
tion environments74–78. For example, in spinel-type AB2O4 systems, 
the occupancy of antibonding d orbitals at octahedrally coordinated 
B sites (eg) provides a volcano-type relationship with peroxidase-like 
activity, indicating an optimal balance between adsorption and release 
of oxygen-containing intermediates77. At low eg occupancy, these inter-
mediates interact too weakly with the surface to sustain efficient cata-
lytic turnover, whereas at high eg occupancy excessively strong binding 
hinders their removal, leading to a maximum activity at intermediate 
values. In these materials, eg occupancy can be systematically tuned by 
modulating the composition or valence state of B-site cations, thereby 
adjusting metal−oxygen bond covalency. Extending this concept, 
antibonding orbital occupancy at tetrahedrally coordinated A sites 
(t2) has been proposed as an analogous descriptor, in which cation 
substitution at the A site allows systematic tuning of t2 filling and reveals 
a clear peroxidase-like activity maximum at intermediate occupancy78. 
These examples point to the need to identify other descriptors to 
guide predictive optimization of nanozyme activity through targeted 
electronic-structure tuning.
In addition to elemental, structural and electronic features of 
active sites, the catalytic activity of nanozymes can also be tailored by 
controlling the reaction environment around the active site. Enzymes 
achieve this control through substrate channels that regulate the 
flow of reactants, local solution pH and concentration of reactants 
near the active site. Inspired by these substrate channels, nanozymes 
can be constructed with artificial nanochannels to replicate confined 
environments and control mass transport and the local solution 
environment (Fig. 3e). An early example of this strategy was in PtNi 
nanozymes prepared by selectively etching Ni domains to generate 
isolated nanoscale channels, whereas the outer surface remained 
passivated by surfactants to restrict catalysis to the recessed sites79. 
In this architecture, oxygen reduction proceeds mainly within the 
channels, in which nanoconfinement enriches the concentration of 
protons used in the reaction, thus increasing the specific activity of 
oxygen reduction80. Compared with unetched particles, in which 
reactions occur on the exterior surfaces exposed to the bulk solu-
tion, these isolated channels sustain a distinct microenvironment 
reminiscent of enzyme substrate channels. Aside from changes to the 
physical architecture, nanozymes can be chemically engineered with 
microenvironments that mirror the role of substrate channels (Fig. 3f). 
For example, porous MOFs were functionalized with acidic or basic 
polymers confined within their internal pores, in which poly(acrylic 
acid) locally enriched protons to sustain peroxidase-like activity at 
physiological pH, whereas poly(ethylene imine) accumulated hydrox-
ide ions to promote hydrolase-like activity81. Rather than relying on 
geometric confinement, this approach modulates catalytic activity 
through ionizable functional groups positioned near the active sites, 
allowing local acid–base conditions to be regulated independently 
of the bulk solution. Importantly, although enzyme microenviron-
ments are fixed by amino acid structure, protein sequence and fold-
ing, nanozyme microenvironments can be modularly designed and 
post-synthetically tuned.
Tuning selectivity
Natural enzymes are renowned for their high specificity, whereas 
nanozymes usually show broader reactivity with less discrimination. 
For many applications, replicating enzyme-like selectivity remains a 
central challenge. By drawing inspiration from enzyme features such 
as metal coordination centres and substrate pockets, nanozymes 
are increasingly being engineered to achieve pathway (Fig. 4a–c) or 
substrate selectivity (Fig. 4d–f) in complex environments82–84.
For metal-based or metal compound-based nanozymes, selectivity 
can be tuned by altering elemental composition or the facets exposed 
on the surface as described earlier, as different atomic configura-
tions present distinct adsorption strengths towards substrates and 
intermediates85,86. Substrate recognition can be further enhanced 
by introducing recognition structures onto the surface using strate-
gies such as molecular imprinting, physical adsorption or surface 
modification87–89. For example, molecularly imprinted polymers 
were grown around Fe3O4 nanozymes using 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylb-
enzidine (TMB, a typical substrate for peroxidase) as the template.

Substrate confinement and control of local microenvironment 
provide another route to reaction specificity, mimicking the functions 
of substrate channels in natural enzymes. Such channels not only guide 
substrates to buried catalytic sites but also establish a local chemical 
environment that governs reaction pathways while restricting access 
of inhibitors and interferents. A representative example is provided 
by gold nanorods coated with a conductive carbon shell containing 
isolated nanochannels93. In this design, the catalytic gold surface is 
accessible only through nanoscale channels that sterically and/or 
electrostatically exclude chloride ions, proteins and other electroactive 
metabolites present in blood. Under electrochemical pulses, hydroxide 
ions are generated and retained within the confined channels, estab-
lishing a locally alkaline environment that selectively activates the 
glucose-oxidation pathway on the gold surface. The combination of 
pathway control through local pH modulation and substrate discrimi-
nation through nanoconfined access enables selective glucose oxida-
tion directly in unadulterated whole blood, a level of specificity that is 
rarely achievable with catalysts operating on fully exposed surfaces.
A further dimension of catalytic selectivity is chirality, one of the 
defining hallmarks of natural enzymes94 (Fig. 4g–i). To emulate pathway 
enantioselectivity, nanozymes can be engineered with intrinsically 
chiral surface topographies that preorganize nonchiral molecules 
and direct their assembly along specific orientations, thereby produc-
ing enantioenriched products. For example, Ag and Cu nanohelices 
act as chiral nanozymes that preorient adsorbed prochiral 2-anthra-
cenecarboxylic acid molecules into opposite Si−Si or Re−Re facial 
stackings95. The stacking mode is determined by the handedness of the 
nanohelices, with left-handed ones promoting Si−Si stacking to form 
the (+)-cyclodimer and right-handed ones inducing Re−Re stacking to 
yield the (−)-cyclodimer upon photoinduced [4 + 4] cyclodimerization.
Substrate enantioselectivity can be introduced by surrounding 
the active centres with chiral ligands, which impose steric constraints 
and directional interactions that bias binding towards one enantiomer 
over the other. For example, dicopper sites embedded in a porous MOF 
scaffold were coordinated with chiral amino acid ligands, creating 
complementary environments that preferentially catalysed the oxida-
tion of l-DOPA or d-DOPA depending on the ligand handedness96. The 
matching enantiomer can effectively access the active sites, whereas the 
unmatched pair experiences steric repulsion between the amine group 
of DOPA and the bridging oxygen in the N(O)−Cu2O2−(O)N core at the 
active site, leading to pronounced enantioselectivity that mimicked how 
natural enzymes use chiral residues in their binding pockets. Extending 
this concept to complex macromolecular systems, chiral function-
alization of active centres enables site-selective catalysis by aligning 
targeted bonds for activation. For example, photoactive chiral Cu1.96S 
nanoparticles functionalized with d-penicillamine selectively cleaved 
the Asn101–Pro102 bond within the tobacco mosaic virus capsid under 
right-handed circularly polarized light97. The local peptide motif sur-
rounding the cleavage site formed a tight hydrophobic pocket and polar-
ized interface that positioned the targeted peptide linkage near surface 
Cu sites, where a localized exciton induced the oxidation of Cu+ to Cu2+, 
thereby enabling electron transfer and hydrolysis. In comparison, the 
l-enantiomer showed weak affinity and much lower cleavage efficiency.
Providing connectivity
Establishing efficient electronic communication with the active sites of 
natural enzymes remains a major challenge in bioelectrocatalysis and 
biosensing — two important applications of enzymes. Owing to their 
inherently non-conducting polypeptide composition, natural enzymes 
often exhibit poor direct electron transfer to/from their active sites 
when immobilized on electrodes98. This issue is compounded by the 
fact that the redox active centres of many enzymes are located deep 
within the protein, many ångstroms from the electrode surface, such 
that electron transfer is slow99.
By contrast, if nanozymes are made from conducting materi-
als, as they often are, then electrical connectivity can be simple and 
direct. For nanozymes with poorly conducting materials, electrical 
connectivity can be improved by engineering conductive shells or 
matrices (such as transition metals, carbon-based materials or conduc-
tive oxides) to establish efficient electron transport pathways. Strate-
gies have been developed to ensure effective nanozyme–electrode 
interfaces. The most straightforward approach is to drop-cast 
nanozyme dispersions directly onto working electrodes such as 
glassy carbon electrodes. Although this method is simple and widely 
used in laboratory research, the resulting coatings often suffer from

Ease of conjugation and functionalization
Conjugating natural enzymes with ligands (such as functional groups 
or biomolecules) requires caution, because such modifications can 
disrupt the geometry of the active site or destabilize the overall protein 
structure, resulting in loss of function. By contrast, nanozymes have 
a greater tolerance for functionalization owing to their favourable 
surface chemistry and large accessible surface area, enabling efficient 
and versatile conjugation with a wide range of ligands. Furthermore, 
their generally greater density and larger size relative to enzymes make 
nanozyme–ligand conjugates easier to separate from reaction mixtures 
and purify by centrifugation.
Depending on their surface characteristics, nanozymes can be 
functionalized through either covalent or noncovalent methods105. 
Covalent approaches involve forming stable chemical bonds between 
the nanozyme surface and ligands bearing reactive groups such as 
carboxyl, amine or thiol moieties. These stable linkages are particularly 
useful for attaching ligands such as bioreceptors, facilitating con-
trolled delivery and targeted biodistribution. Noncovalent methods, 
by contrast, rely on weaker interactions such as electrostatic interac-
tions, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions or π−π stacking106. 
Although this approach is generally simpler and can better preserve the 
intrinsic catalytic structure of a nanozyme, it may result in less stable 
conjugates under variable environmental conditions.
Importantly, the nanoscale dimensions of a single nanozyme 
provide sufficient surface area to allow the attachment of multiple 
ligands (either identical or diverse) and to enable precise control over 
the ligand-to-nanozyme ratio. This tunability is essential for optimiz-
ing nanozyme performance in bioapplications such as therapy and 
biosensing. For instance, in therapeutics, nanozymes can be simul-
taneously functionalized with stealth polymers to extend blood 
circulation107, targeting ligands to enhance tumour accumulation108, 
and therapeutic agents such as photosensitizers or chemotherapeutic 
agents to improve treatment efficacy109. In biosensing, nanozymes are 
commonly conjugated with bioreceptors that selectively recognize and 
bind target analytes. In general, increasing the number of bioreceptors 
per nanozyme enhances the binding activity of the conjugate. However, 
excessive loading can increase nonspecific interactions with biological 
matrices, leading to elevated background signals110. Therefore, opti-
mizing the bioreceptor-to-nanozyme ratio is essential for achieving 
high-performance biosensing.
Stimuli-responsive regulation
Natural enzymes can respond to changes in their surrounding envi-
ronment, and such responses are typically mediated through bio-
logical adaptation. These regulatory processes include altered gene 
expression and post-translational processes that adjust enzyme abun-
dance, stability or localization111,112. This regulation is typically medi-
ated through cellular signalling pathways rather than direct control of 
individual catalytic centres113. Such evolutionarily optimized responses 
often operate on relatively long timescales and are difficult to reverse 
once stress-induced deactivation or degradation occurs.
In contrast to enzymes, regulation of nanozyme activity can 
be deliberately engineered at the material level by exploiting the 
physicochemical properties of the nanomaterials themselves. Exter-
nal stimuli, such as light97,114, photothermal115,116, sound117, electrical 
potential58, radiation118 and magnetic fields119, can be incorporated 
as regulatory inputs to influence electron transfer, local reaction 
environments or energy flow at catalytic interfaces. These stimuli 
can be applied remotely and, in many cases, reversibly, enabling 
rapid and programmable control over catalytic activity through 
modes of regulation that are largely inaccessible to natural enzyme 
systems. For example, light-responsive nanozymes use photoin-
duced charge generation and transfer processes to reversibly modu-
late catalytic activity, enabling switchable and tunable behaviour 
in photoactive nanozyme-based systems120,121. In a representative 
example, semiconductive g-C3N4 nanosheets function as a photoac-
tive nanozyme to catalyse the oxidation of glucose under illumina-
tion, with light-generating protons that establish a transmembrane 
gradient to drive ATP synthesis by reconstituted ATP synthase. In 
this system, illumination serves as an external regulatory input that 
controls the rate of proton generation and, consequently, modulates 
downstream bioenergetic processes114.
Tackling challenges beyond the reach of enzymes
Although many natural enzymes are already manufactured at scale 
and successfully deployed in industrial applications, such as food pro-
cessing and detergents, their use is not universally optimal across all 
settings. In practice, constraints associated with enzyme purification 
and formulation, dependence on controlled storage and transport 
conditions, finite operational lifetimes and vulnerability to harsh phys-
icochemical environments can compromise enzyme cost-effectiveness 
in certain applications122. In other contexts, some reaction types and 
modes of operation cannot be realized with enzymes at all. By contrast, 
nanozymes can overcome limitations that have long hindered progress 
in biomedicine and other technologically important fields (Fig. 5).
Applications in biomedicine
In vivo, the therapeutic utility of enzymes is often curtailed by their 
rapid proteolytic degradation, particularly in tumours or inflamed 
tissues, where proteases are overexpressed123. Nanozymes, however, 
are resistant to proteolysis, allowing prolonged function in these 
environments124. In addition, pathological features such as hypoxia, 
acidosis and redox stress further compromise enzyme activity, whereas 
nanozymes maintain robust catalysis under these conditions and can 
modulate local redox balance through controlled generation or scav-
enging of ROS125,126. These properties enable them to promote can-
cer cell apoptosis or protect neurons from oxidative damage in the 
context of stroke and neurodegeneration32,127. Beyond their intrinsic 
biomimetic activities, nanozymes can be rationally engineered to inte-
grate selected functionalities, such as prodrug activation128, photother-
mal effects129, gas generation130, magnetothermal responsiveness119 and 
ultrasound-triggered activity131, each typically introduced through 
surface functionalization or regulated via external stimuli-responsive 
mechanisms within a single platform. These multifunctional designs

In vitro, nanozymes have also demonstrated broad utility across 
various biomedical applications. Among these, their role in in vitro 
diagnostics is particularly impactful, as they address the persistent 
limitations of enzyme-based bioassays in detecting low-abundance 
biomarkers during early stages of specific diseases105,138. For example, 
in cancers or infectious diseases, key biomarkers such as carcinoem-
bryonic antigen or viral antibodies often circulate at concentrations 
below the detection threshold of horseradish peroxidase-based 
bioassays139–142. By replacing horseradish peroxidase with highly active 
nanozymes, the detection limit can be improved by orders of mag-
nitude, enabling earlier and more reliable diagnosis69,110,143. Beyond 
sensitivity, nanozymes are more compatible with portable diagnostic 
formats owing to their robustness, electrical connectivity and ease 
of integration. They enable lateral flow devices, continuous electro-
chemical sensors and microfluidic devices that are cost-effective, 
refrigeration-free and operable at the point of care. For instance, Au–Ir 
core–shell nanoparticles have been used as labels in lateral flow devices, 
in which their exceptional peroxidase-like activity produced strong col-
orimetric signals143. The Au–Ir nanoparticle-based lateral flow devices 
enabled the detection of two cancer biomarkers with limits of detec-
tion in the picogram-per-millilitre range, hundreds of times lower than 
those achieved with conventional gold nanoparticle-based devices.
Applications beyond biomedicine
Nanozymes operate efficiently in harsh or non-biological environments 
that typically limit protein-based enzymes. In agriculture, nanozymes 
allow reliable detection of trace contaminants such as toxins, pesti-
cides, heavy metals, additives and pathogens in complex environments, 
many of which act as inhibitors that compromise protein activity144,145. 
Other than detection, nanozymes directly contribute to crop productiv-
ity through multiple interconnected mechanisms146. First, by mimick-
ing antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and catalase, 
nanozymes protect chloroplasts from ROS damage, thereby sustaining 
photosynthetic energy conversion and maintaining cellular homeo-
stasis under stress147. This antioxidant function also helps to shield 
plants from oxidative injury caused by drought, salinity or heavy-metal 
exposure148. Second, nanozymes influence plant hormone signalling. 
For example, γ-Fe2O3 nanozymes can alleviate oxidative stress in nod-
ules of soybean, reducing ethylene and jasmonic acid while enhancing 
cytokinin biosynthesis, changes that promote nodule development 
and delay senescence149. Third, nanozymes extend their impact to the 
rhizosphere, where they alter redox balance and supply micronutrients 
that enrich beneficial microbial communities. For example, Fe-based 
nanomaterials release soluble Fe that enhances flavonoid biosynthesis 
and secretion in soybean, which in turn act as signalling molecules to 
attract rhizobia such as Allorhizobium and Mesorhizobium, thereby 
promoting nodule formation and nitrogen fixation150. Extending from 
field to table, nanozyme-based antimicrobial and anti-biofilm coatings 
have also been applied in food packaging, where their peroxidase-like 
activity catalyses ROS generation to oxidize microbial metabolites, 
suppress spoilage and prolong shelf life151.
In environmental remediation, nanozymes have emerged as robust 
catalysts for wastewater treatment, in which they efficiently degrade 
persistent organic pollutants such as phenols, chlorinated aromatics, 
synthetic dyes, antibiotics, pesticides and microplastics152. Particu-
larly, magnetically active nanozymes (such as Co3O4/CoFe2O4 hollow 
nanocubes153, Ag/Fe3O4@hexagonal boron nitride nanozymes154 and 
ferromagnetic chitosan nanozymes155) can oxidatively degrade these 
compounds through peroxidase-like or oxidase-like pathways, convert-
ing them into less toxic products such as carbon dioxide, water and 
short-chain organic acids, while allowing the nanozymes to be recycled 
for repeated use. In addition to water treatment, nanozymes have been 
integrated into air purification systems to remove volatile organic com-
pounds. For example, an atomic potassium-modified carbon nitride 
nanozyme embedded in face masks exhibited both flavin oxidase-like 
and peroxidase-like activities in analogy to natural aerobic metabolism, 
leading to the degradation of several representative volatile organic 
compounds, as well as persistent organic pollutants and bacteria, 
thereby achieving broad-spectrum protection with recyclability156.
In the energy sector, nanozymes can enhance biofuel cells, hydro-
gen production, alcohol production and biogas recovery by mimick-
ing enzymatic redox processes. These versatile catalytic functions 
enable them to serve as catalysts for the large-scale manufacture of 
fine chemical products. In addition, nanozyme design is increasingly 
directed towards using biomass waste as raw material in harsh envi-
ronments, disaster relief or space stations, where they could produce 
life-supporting oxygen and water while also meeting energy demands 
through biofuel cells. A representative example is a metal–ligand 
dual-site single-atom nanozyme that mimics urate oxidase with high 
substrate specificity157. By separating the uric acid binding site (Ni) from 
the oxygen reduction site (β-C in the ligand), the nanozymes achieve 
enzyme-like selectivity for uric acid and enable its efficient oxidation 
to power a biofuel cell directly from human urine. Notably, their mild 
reaction conditions, biocompatibility and ease of integration have ena-
bled the miniaturization of nanozyme and nanozyme–enzyme hybrid 
biofuel cells, which are now being incorporated into wearable and 
implantable self-powered biosensing and therapeutic systems158–160. 
For example, nanozyme-based self-powered sensors operate as bio-
fuel cells, in which Au-reduced graphene oxide hydrogel nanozymes at 
the anode catalyse the oxidation of ascorbic acid to generate electrons, 
whereas PtCu bimetallic hydrogel nanozymes at the cathode drive

Outlook
The most exciting outlook for nanozymes lies not in reproducing 
what enzymes can do but in redefining catalysis from a perspective 
freed from biological constraints. To date, many established exam-
ples rely on the enhanced stability of nanozymes to operate under 
harsh conditions or over extended timescales that challenge natural 
enzymes162,163. Beyond stability, nanozymes grant access to reaction 
spaces that are inaccessible to natural enzymes. This capability stems 
from the materials-based nature of nanozymes and emerges from the 
integration of multiple design dimensions discussed throughout this 
Perspective. These include the ability to catalyse non-native reactions, 
as exemplified by bioorthogonal nanozymes164. In addition, the intrinsic 
physicochemical properties of nanozymes further expand accessible 
modes of operation. In this context, electrical conductivity enables 
catalytic turnover to be directly coupled to signal readout165,166 and, 
conversely, allows external electrical inputs to modulate the local 
reaction microenvironment and access multiple reaction pathways58. 
Optical responsiveness allows spatiotemporal control over catalytic 
activity167, whereas magnetic properties facilitate efficient separa-
tion and seamless workflow integration110. In parallel, versatile sur-
face functionalization provides an additional and largely independent 
level of control. Surface chemistry offers tunable handles to modulate 
catalytic activity and selectivity through electrostatic, steric or chi-
ral interactions94,106, while enabling the modular, building-block-like 
integration of targeting ligands, biorecognition elements, therapeutic 
cargos or circulation-enhancing coatings108. Collectively, these attrib-
utes signify a broader conceptual shift: nanozymes can be regarded as 
synthetic catalytic systems whose functions are rationally designed 
and assembled from material-enabled properties to address specific 
application needs. The true value of nanozymes lies in enabling catalytic 
capabilities that cannot be achieved with enzymes alone.
Realizing the full potential of nanozymes will require a strategic 
balance between their materials-enabled strengths and their practical 
limitations, with careful identification of the contexts in which they 
offer the greatest value. In living systems, natural enzymes operate 
within sophisticated regulatory networks that dynamically adjust 
catalytic activity through gene expression and feedback control. 
Nanozymes are not designed to replicate such endogenous regula-
tion. Instead, they are designed to operate alongside it, using com-
plementary, materials-encoded modes of control that sustain activity 
under conditions in which biological regulation is slow, irreversible 
or vulnerable to stress. Within this framework, a key design priority 
is the development of nanozymes that combine biocompatibility 
with predictable and programmable clearance pathways. Strategies 
such as degradable or stimuli-responsive architectures can introduce 
defined ‘exit’ pathways or temporal control over activity53,164, mitigating 
long-term accumulation risks and ensuring biosafety in both human 
and ecological systems. Notably, these considerations underscore an 
important principle: nanozymes are deliberately engineered for com-
plementary, material-based modes of control rather than biological 
self-regulation. In many diagnostic, therapeutic and environmental 
applications, it is precisely this capacity for sustained or externally 
regulated activity that defines their unique utility.
Multistep reaction systems present a different set of design con-
siderations. Cascade catalysis based on natural enzymes is already 
highly effective, benefiting from evolutionarily optimized kinetics and 
pathway-level coordination. Nanozyme-based cascades are therefore 
not intended to replicate these biological systems but are best viewed 
as programmable alternatives for multistep reactions that are less 
constrained by evolutionary selection or biological regulation. This 
design freedom enables the use of non-natural substrates, alternative 
reaction sequences and integration with non-biological components.
Achieving fine structural precision comparable to that of protein 
enzymes remains challenging. This challenge is especially pronounced 
for complex or multifunctional architectures, in which multiple func-
tionalities, catalytic roles or additional physicochemical modalities 
are integrated within a single nanozyme. In such systems, even small 
variations in morphology, composition or surface modification could 
lead to disproportionate changes in physicochemical behaviour and 
catalytic performance. Progress in this direction will likely depend on 
greater standardization across synthesis, functionalization and evalu-
ation workflows, as well as the adoption of automated and data-driven 
approaches to improve reproducibility and control during scale-up.
Expanding nanozyme chemistry beyond the current emphasis on 
oxidoreductases and hydrolases acting on small inorganic substrates 
such as hydrogen peroxide, superoxide and molecular oxygen repre-
sents an important opportunity. To date, these enzyme classes have 
dominated the field largely because their catalytic mechanisms rely 
on relatively simple redox or bond cleavage processes that are read-
ily accessible on inorganic or hybrid material surfaces. Developing 
nanozymes that emulate other biologically important enzyme classes, 
including isomerases, ligases, lyases, translocases, transferases and 
synthetases, could open access to more complex catalytic transforma-
tions under programmable and non-biological control168. Such capabili-
ties would broaden the functional scope of nanozymes in biological 
signalling, regulation, metabolism and macromolecular processing.
Looking ahead, the accessible chemical and materials space 
makes the design possibilities for nanozymes extraordinarily large, 
and a trial-and-error approach may be inefficient for acquiring desired 
properties. Data-driven workflows that use artificial intelligence to 
rapidly capture complex structure–activity relationships169 will be key 
to directing nanozyme design, along with the integration of experi-
mental data with theoretical simulations, such as density functional 
theory and molecular dynamics, to provide mechanistic insight at 
both the electronic and reaction–environment levels170,171. Achieving 
artificial intelligence-directed design, however, will also depend on 
standardized assays, curated databases and open data platforms that 
ensure reproducibility across the field, as recommended in other areas 
of science172.
The ability of nanozymes to function in settings and roles uncon-
strained by biological evolution opens many possibilities spanning 
scales from intracellular microenvironments to large-scale extrater-
restrial systems. For example, nanozymes enable the construction 
of artificial organelles that act as long-lasting ‘factories’ within liv-
ing cells168,173. These organelles are built by encapsulating catalytic 
modules inside microcompartment materials, mimicking the com-
partmentalization of natural organelles to achieve localized and pro-
tected reactions. Such nanozyme organelles could be engineered 
to provide sustained therapeutic effects, for example, compensat-
ing for genetic enzyme deficiencies such as superoxide dismutase 
1 mutations in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis or uricase deficiency in 
gout. Incorporating bioorthogonal nanozymes could further extend 
the organelles’ capabilities to non-native reactions, transforming 
them into in situ drug factories that generate therapeutics or imaging

Perspective
agents directly at disease sites. Integration with CRISPR technologies 
could then upgrade these systems into programmable gene-editing 
factories, offering precise release of editing components, protection 
from premature degradation and stepwise activation under defined 
cues. In this vision, nanozyme-based artificial organelles could evolve 
into cellular factories that unify diagnosis, regulation and therapy 
within a single programmable entity. Nanozymes may also contribute 
to space exploration, where natural enzymes cannot endure radiation, 
vacuum or extreme temperatures. Their catalytic resilience could 
sustain closed-loop life-support systems by recycling biomass and 
converting waste into usable products under extraterrestrial condi-
tions. Beyond internal circulation, nanozymes could also tap into local 
planetary resources, with potential applications such as oxygen extrac-
tion from Martian regolith and water release from lunar ice. In this way, 
nanozyme catalysis bridges internal recycling and on-site resource 
utilization, providing a catalytic basis for self-sustaining habitats and 
long-term human presence beyond Earth.

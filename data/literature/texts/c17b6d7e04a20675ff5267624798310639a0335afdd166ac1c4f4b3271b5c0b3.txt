ABSTRACT
Copper-loaded mesoporous nitrogen-rich carbon nitride (xCu-mC3N6) was synthesized using a mesoporous silica SBA-15 hard
template. While low-angle XRD data and transmission electron microscopic images confirm the periodic hexagonal porous texture
of the synthesized material, the nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms revealed high specific surface areas with an average pore
diameter of ∼4.9 nm. CHN analysis established the formation of C3N6, and near-edge X-ray absorption Fine Structure (NEXAFS)
measurements confirmed N─C═N in the C3N6 framework. The copper-based nanoparticles are anchored onto the CN framework
within the mesochannels of C3N6. The inhibition zones revealed that Cu-mC3N6 exhibited a stronger antibacterial effect compared
to mC3N6, and interestingly. Gram-positive Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis) performs better than gram-negative Escherichia coli (E. coli)
bacteria. Further, the colony-counting method quantitatively confirmed the superior antibacterial performance of xCu-mC3N6,
compared to mC3N6. Cu-mC3N6 also achieved an 87% increase in intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in B.
subtilis and 71% for E. coli-treated cells, implying ROS-mediated cell death in Cu-mC3N6, while mC3N6 exhibited a lower level of
ROS owing to weak electrostatic interactions with bacteria, resulting in lower antibacterial effects. The present study provides
insight into the synthesis of nitrogen-rich nanocomposites for disinfection.

Introduction
Bacterial infections are a cause of concern as they severely
impact human/animal health [1, 2]. Staphylococcus aureus, E.
coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa bacteria are present on sur-
faces and even in the air, causing the rapid spread of diseases
infecting low-immune individuals in large numbers [3]. Tra-
ditional disinfection strategies for reducing surface-borne risk
include bleach-based products, which are unhealthy for the
skin. To overcome this issue, antimicrobial coatings, including
heat/electric fields and light-responsive materials, are commonly
implemented [4]. Pathogen anchoring capability owing to the
electrostatic/chemical interactions their surfaces offer, their
localized electron density, and dimension-tunable unique set of
physical and chemical properties render nanostructured mate-
rials appropriate to be employed for anti-bacterial performance,
including cell physical damage to the cell membrane. Moreover,
reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated in the presence of metals
have been reported to interfere with cellular processes [5–8].
Metallic nanoparticles, including Ag, Cu, Zn, etc, metal oxide
nanoparticles such as Ag2O, TiO2, ZnO, CuO, MgO, etc., and
several other compounds, such as metal nitrides, sulfides, phos-
phides, borides, and others with or without a cationic surfactant,

have already been employed as antibacterial surface-coating
materials. [9–11] However, the toxicity of these nanostructured
materials has been one of the long-awaited concerns, preventing
their commercialization as antibacterial coatings. This needs to
be adequately addressed. [12, 13] Carbon allotropes, including
graphene, graphene oxide, fullerene, and carbon nanotubes
(CNTs), exhibit antibacterial performances that depend on shape,
size, surface chemistry, bacterial types, and their interfacial
interactions and environments [14, 15]. Compared to other
existing carbon-based nanomaterials, non-metallic graphitic car-
bon nitride (g-C3N4) exhibits better anti-bacterial activity [16].
While interfacial interactions are being explored to establish
the mechanism of antibacterial activity, tweaking the nitrogen
ratio in carbon nitride can result in a modulated band structure
in materials (making them light-responsive) along with defects
suitable for antibacterial performances [17]. Moreover, nitrogen-
rich non-stoichiometric carbon nitrides (C3N5/C3N6, etc.) with
more defects (such as vacancies) in their lattices containing edge
nitrogen atoms are better equipped for anchoring metal nanopar-
ticles, rendering them potential materials for antibacterial
coatings [18–26].
Herein, we report on the synthesis of mesoporous C3N6 (mC3N6)
and Cu-functionalised mesoporous C3N6 (Cu-mC3N6) samples
through the incorporation of aminoguanidine and copper nitrate
precursors inside the nanochannels of SBA-15 template, followed
by carbonisation at 400◦C and their characterizations through
advanced microscopic and spectroscopic techniques. Further-
more, their anti-bacterial properties were investigated using
Gram-positive Bacillus subtilis and Gram-negative E. coli. Agar
well plate method (qualitative method), was carried out for these
two bacteria at 5, 10, 20, and 40 mg/mL concentrations. Their
colony counting (quantitative method) was done at a concen-
tration of 40 mg/mL. The morphology of the treated bacteria
was then analysed through SEM imaging, and ROS production
was evaluated by ROS assay with 40 mg/mL concentration. All
the antibacterial measurements were carried out under ambient
conditions.

Results and Discussion
2.1
Physical Characterization of mC3N6 and
Cu-mC3N6
The mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6 were synthesized by pore filling
of C/N/Cu precursors at low temperature (up to 160◦C) into
hard template SBA-15 and followed by calcination at 400◦C
under a continuous nitrogen flow. Upon synthesis of mC3N6
and xCu-mC3N6, the template was removed using a dilute (5%)
HF solution (Figure 1a). The synthesized mC3N6 appeared light
yellow, whereas xCu-mC3N6 appeared brown, and the shades
deepened upon the increase of copper concentration in the
synthesized xCu-mC3N6 materials (camera picture in Figure 1b).
Dispersion of mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6 in phosphate buffer saline
solution exhibited gradual darkening of shades upon increase of
copper loading (camera picture in Figure 1c). The SEM images of
mesoporous C3N6, 0.25 Cu-C3N6, 0.75 Cu-C3N6, and 1.50 Cu-C3N6
are depicted in Figure 1b–e. SEM image of the mC3N6 sample
exhibited an ordered elongated cylindrical porous morphology
mimicking the mesoporous SBA-15 template (Figure 1d). Gradual
increase in copper loading (from 0.25 to 1.50 g copper nitrate)
along with CN precursors resulted in more and more disorder
in porous texture (Figure 1e–g). High copper loading in 1.5
Cu-mC3N6 resulted in the complete collapse of porous texture
(Figure 1g). TEM image reveals the formation of an ordered
hexagonal porous texture in mC3N6 mimicking the ordered
mesoporous structure of the SBA-15 template (Figure 1h). The
atomically resolved HRTEM image in Figure 1i exhibits a locally
ordered atomic structure with apparent hexagons comprising
of carbon and nitrogen atoms. Crystallographic features in the
HRTEM image witnessed a compromised crystalline order on
the verge of amorphization. 1.5Cu-mC3N6 sample exhibited a
mesoporous texture relatively less ordered, as apparent from the
TEM image shown in Figure 1j. Elemental mapping shown in
Figure 1k demonstrates evenly distributed C, N, O, and Cu atoms
on the surface of 1.50 Cu-mC3N6.
The N2 adsorption-desorption isotherm of all samples demon-
strated type-IV isotherms. Interestingly, mC3N6, 0.25 Cu-mC3N6,
0.75 Cu-mC3N6 and 1.25 Cu-mC3N6 samples exhibited H2-type
hysteresis, confirming the presence of ink-bottle-shaped pores
(Figure 2a). However, higher copper loading in 1.5Cu-mC3N6 gave
H3-type hysteresis, indicating slit-like pores likely resulting from
a collapsed structure. The BET surface area of mC3N6 was 190.14
m2/g, which systematically decreased to 159.78, 142.09, 136.32,
and 113.69 m2/g upon gradual copper loading with 0.25, 0.75,
1.25, and 1.50 g, respectively Figure 2a. Figure 2b shows the pore
size distribution of mC3N6 and various copper-loaded Cu-mC3N6
samples, highlighting that as the Cu content increases, there is
a gradual increase in the pore sizes up to ∼6.6 nm, in contrast
to the narrow size distribution centred around 4.9 nm observed
for mC3N6. This is due to the partial collapse of the mesopores
of the mC3N6. The comprehensive structural parameters are
provided in Table S1. The UV–vis absorption spectra for mC3N6
and xCu-mC3N6 are shown in (Figure 2c). The data indicated a
gradual increase of the absorption with Cu loading in xCu-mC3N6
samples, which established that Cu is integrated into the mC3N6
framework. [27] We could interestingly observe the plasmonic
peak of copper at ∼700 nm wavelength, which became dominant
at higher copper loading.
Low-angle XRD was recorded for the SBA-15 template and mC3N6
[28]. As illustrated in Figure 2d, three distinct peaks were wit-
nessed in low-angle XRD corresponding to (100), (110), and (210)
reflection planes of the SBA-15 template, which is assigned to the
p6mm space group of the hexagonal porous textures, revealing
that the ordered mesoporous structure of the SBA-15 template
had been replicated in C3N6. This finding further validates the
observation of periodic pores in TEM (Figure 1h). However, XRD
peaks broaden due to a slight disorder caused during replication.
Compromised porous textures were observed in xCu-mC3N6,
where x = 0.25-1.25 g due to copper loading (Figure S1), as
apparent from broadening of low-angle XRD peaks. However,
1.5 Cu-mC3N6 exhibited a lack of any observable low-angle XRD
peak, indicating a complete collapse of porous texture. (Figure
S1) The high-angle (2θ ∼27◦) XRD peak for the mC3N6 sample is
attributed to the (002) crystallographic plane of the graphitic CN
structure (Figure 2e), which is corroborating with locally ordered
crystallographic structure observed in HRTEM (Figure 1i). Based
on the above microscopic, spectroscopic, and crystallographic
measurements as well as N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms,

Solid-state NMR spectroscopy was used to determine the chem-
ical structure of mesoporous Cu-doped carbon nitride (C3N6)
synthesised from aminoguanidine. The 13C NMR spectrum of
mC3N6 showed three main signals at 162, 158, and 150 ppm,
corresponding to C═N/C═N of the heptazine (tri-s-triazine) and
CN3 carbons, respectively (Figure S3a). The signal for the N2C–
NHNH2 carbon of the aminoguanidine precursor at 160 ppm
was absent in the C3N6 spectrum, indicating the removal of
–NHNH2 protons and the formation of azo linkages during
polymerisation. The 13C NMR results suggest that heptazine units
are linked by symmetrical azo bridges, with the central and
external carbons displaying a slight downfield shift from 158 ppm
in the precursor to 159 ppm in C3N6, due to deshielding caused
by N 2p–π conjugation, thereby extending the π-conjugated
network. The 13C NMR spectrum of Cu-C3N6 showed two noisy
weak signals at 164 and 155 ppm due to the paramagnetism;
these peaks are slightly shifted compared to the pristine C3N6
spectrum, indicating the effect of copper in the C3N6 frame-
work (Figure S3b). Copper is a paramagnetic material. Currents
generated while spinning the sample in the magnetic field
possibly disrupts the NMR signals. Therefore, we got very noisy
data.

To understand the origin of the enhanced activity of Cu doped g-
C3N6, in Figure 3, we plot the atom-projected density of states of
pristine and Cu doped g-C3N6. Pristine g-C3N6 and Cu-doped g-
C3N6 reveal significant differences in their electronic structures,
corroborating their antibacterial performance. Pristine g-C3N6
(Figure 3a) exhibits a band gap, in which the valence and
conduction bands are dominated by nitrogen 2p states, however
C-2p has a significant contribution at the bottom of the valence
band, showing typical characteristics of sp2 hybridised π states.
The well-defined bandgap, although PBE underestimates the
band gap, limits electron transfer to adsorbed oxygen species.
In contrast, Cu doping (Figure 3b) introduces additional elec-
tronic states near the Fermi level, primarily from the Cu 3d
orbitals, which hybridize with the nitrogen 2p orbitals and
the lone pair states of g-C3N6. This hybridization effectively
narrows the bandgap and enhances the electron density at the
Fermi level, facilitating electron transfer to adsorbed oxygen
molecules and promoting the formation of superoxide radicals.
The increased density of states near the Fermi level in Cu-g-C3N6,
therefore supports enhanced ROS generation, which is a key
factor in its enhanced antibacterial activity compared to pristine
g-C3N6.
2.3
Antibacterial Activity Performance of C3N6
and xCu-C3N6
mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6 samples were tested for quantitative
determination with the agar well diffusion technique. The
mesoporous C3N6 exhibited low activity, demonstrating 12 mm
zone inhibition against B. subtilis for 4 mg of samples. In fact,
100 µL of 40 mg/mL stock solution was added to the well. xCu-
mC3N6 samples, however, with x = 0.25, 0.75, 1.25, and 1.50 g;
exhibited a zone of inhibition ranging from 13 to 27 mm over
a concentration range between 5 mg to 40 mg/mL (Figures 4a
and 5a). Earlier, Ag-loaded graphene oxide was demonstrated to
have a zone of inhibition ∼10.4 mm at 1 mg/ml and ∼11.7 mm at
2 mg/ml concentration [34]. These results suggested that mC3N6
has a weak antibacterial activity, possibly due to its limited
permeability or penetration property into agar gel. Similarly, xCu-
mC3N6 samples exhibited higher antibacterial activity with a
subsequent increase in Cu content as compared to mC3N6, which
confirms the vital role of incorporation of copper into mC3N6 to
boost up its antibacterial performance. Further, the antibacterial
activity of 1.50 Cu-mC3N6 sample was tested against B. subtilis and
compared the performances with tetracycline antibiotics (Figure
S4a and Figure 5a) and found the performance comparable. On
the other hand, mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6 were tested against gram-
negative E. coli bacteria at the concentration of 40 mg/mL The
result revealed that there is no zone of inhibition except for the
1.50 g Cu-C3N6 sample, which exhibited a 9 mm of inhibition
zone as illustrated in Figures 4a and 5a. This lower antibacterial
activity of mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6 against E. coli is attributed to
the complex cell membrane in E. coli arresting the penetration of
antibacterial agents [35].
Based on the zone of inhibition results (Figures 4a and 5a),
40 mg/mL was considered the optimum concentration for mC3N6
and xCu-mC3N6, and these samples were further evaluated by the
colony counting method against Bacillus subtilis. The results indi-
cated that mC3N6 exhibited more than 50% of colonies reduction,
which was significantly higher than the control. The antibacterial
performances of mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6 are shown in (Figures 4b
and 5b). On the other hand, xCu-mC3N6 samples bestowed
even further enhanced antibacterial performance with gradually
increased copper concentrations in the mC3N6 material. While
bacterial colony count was registered to be 78 x 105 CFU/mL for
0.25 Cu-C3N6, a complete eradication of bacteria (0 CFU/ mL)
was observed for the 1.50 Cu-C3N6 sample, which establishes that

The antibacterial activity against gram-negative E. coli bacteria
was also evaluated for mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6 materials through
colony counting analysis. The trend observed in colony counting
for E. coli was found to be similar to that for Bacillus subtilis.
While the number of E. coli colony counts 152 x 105 CFU/mL
for mC3N6, it was registered to be 19 x 105 CFU/mL for 1.50 Cu-
mC3N6 (Figures 4c and 5c). The prepared xCu-mC3N6 samples
showed a concentration-dependent antibacterial effect. However,
1.5 Cu-mC3N6 sample could not kill all bacterial colonies against
E. coli. These results demonstrate that E. coli colonies grow faster
than Bacillus subtilis, which is apparent from high initial cell
counts. The colony counting method of antibacterial performance
measured for mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6 materials thus hint at these
materials being excellent antibacterial agents for Bacillus subtilis
as compared to E. coli. Hence, our materials can be employed
for direct anti-bacterial coating or can be used as filler along
with different polymers, which can be easily coated on targeted
surfaces. Our observations are fully consistent with the literature,
which suggests that an extra membrane in E. coli, which is
made up of lipopolysaccharides, results in poor permeability of
nanomaterials through its cell wall [36].
The antibacterial mechanism of synthesized mC3N6 and xCu-
mC3N6 was investigated by H2-DCFDA assay against gram-
positive Bacillus subtilis and gram-negative E. Coli. This assay
was used to determine the intracellular ROS production. The
results indicated that xCu-mC3N6 materials generate more ROS
due to the Cu content in the mesoporous material, which leads
to ROS-mediated cell death. In comparison, mC3N6 generates
minimal ROS, which is consistent with its antibacterial activities
experimentally measured by other methods. Furthermore, when
1.50Cu-C3N6 was used, ROS production levels for the case of
B. subtilis was 87%, and that for E. coli was 72%. 1 mM H2O2
was used as a positive control. These results indicate that other
possible factors, such as physical cell membrane damage, might
also be involved in the antibacterial performance of mC3N6 and
xCu-mC3N6, as shown in (Figure 5d).
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of B. subtilis and E. coli
control cells established robust and consistent shape surfaces.
Upon treatment of these bacterial cells with xCu-mC3N6 sam-
ples, we could detect considerable damage to the cell mem-
brane, some of which were heavily damaged. Meanwhile, lower
(0.25-0.75 g) copper loading in xCu-mC3N6 samples gives rise
to distortion of shapes caused by strain developed due to
the interactions between bacteria and xCu-mC3N6 samples.
When copper content is gradually increased (1.25–1.50 g), then
cell wall thinning and severe damage are vividly witnessed.
Interestingly, in 1.50 Cu-mC3N6, cell rupture is so obvious.
(Figure 6)

(because of the high strain developed in non-stoichiometric
carbon nitride systems). These features of mC3N6 will give it
a stronger chemical interaction with bacteria. Moreover, sharp
edges in mC3N6 will help in the physical scission of cell walls,

C3N6 is typically synthesized with a well-organized, mesoporous
structure composed of a polymeric network of carbon and
nitrogen atoms. The electronegativity difference between Cu and
N can lead to polarization effects, which can further disrupt
the ordered bonding patterns in the C3N6 matrix. Bonding
and molecular structures of copper-doped C3N6 can lead to a
suitable electronic character, with adequate carrier (electrons)
concentration resulting in improved chemical reactivity. When
we attempted to dope copper atoms into C3N6, it was observed
that it got dispersed into the C3N6 lattice by assuming the vacant
sites (pores). This creates lattice strain within the carbon nitride
network due to differences in atomic sizes and bonding energies.
This strain, if beyond a certain limit, may lead to defects and
irregularities in the structure. Excessive copper loading results in
agglomeration and may block the mesopores of C3N6, reducing
the uniformity of the porosity and introducing irregularities.
However, under a suitable set of synthesis conditions and at
lower copper loading, copper doping in the lattice is achieved,
which can be utilized as a pathway for material manipulation. Cu-
doped C3N6 has been demonstrated to have excellent antibacterial
behaviour [38, 39], better than several non-metallic structured
materials [40, 41] It should be noted that the present report on
the use of C3N6 as an anti-bacterial material is the first of its
kind.
3
Conclusion
In this investigation, the nitrogen-rich mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6
samples were successfully synthesized by a hard-templating
technique using SBA-15 as a template. High nitrogen content,
resulting in higher defect concentrations in the mC3N6 sample
helps in better incorporation of copper in the system. More-
over, hard templating generated mC3N6 having a high (∼181.33
m2/g) specific surface area can help in better copper loading.
Elemental mapping acquired in SEM revealed a uniform distri-
bution of copper in the mC3N6 framework. Zone of inhibition
(qualitative method) results indicated that xCu-mC3N6 exhibited
a significantly enhanced antibacterial activity as compared to
mC3N6. Moreover, these antibacterial materials developed in
the present research were considerably more effective for gram-
positive Bacillus subtilis than gram-negative E. coli bacteria.
Quantitative colony counting results revealed that the antibac-
terial performance of the synthesized mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6

Methods
4.1
Materials
All chemical reagents, including P-123 surfactant, hydrochlo-
ric acid, tetraethyl orthosilicate, aminoguanidine hydrochloride,
copper(II) nitrate hemipentahydrate, hydrofluoric acid (HF), and
phosphate-buffered saline solution, were obtained from Sigma-
Aldrich and used for experimental purposes without further
purification.
4.2
Synthesis of mC3N6 by the Hard-Templating
Method
The silica template was synthesized using the described protocol
[42, 43]. To prepare the mesoporous C3N6, [30, 44] one gram
of the SBA-15 template and four grams of aminoguanidine
hydrochloride, along with 3 g of DI water. The mixture was
thoroughly blended, and SBA-15 was filled with aminoguanidine
hydrochloride precursor. After that, the obtained material was
dried in the hot air oven at 100◦C for 6 h, followed by heating it at
160◦C for another 6 h. Finally, the resultant product was ground
to a very fine powder, which was carbonized using a tubular
furnace at 400◦C for 5 h under a continuous flow of nitrogen.
After the carbonization process, the sample was allowed to cool
at room temperature, followed by hydrofluoric acid (HF) washing
to remove the silica template. The sample was then washed 3 to 4
times using ethanol to remove any excess HF and dried overnight
at 100◦C. (Figure 1a).
4.2.1
Synthesis of xCu-mC3N6
The synthesis of xCu-mC3N6, where x denotes the amount of
copper source added during the synthesis in gram was achieved
by a similar process as the mC3N6, with the addition of copper
precursor (copper (II) nitrate hemipentahydrate) in step 1. A
certain amount (0.25, 0.75, 1.25, and 1.50 g) of copper was included
in the synthesis. The synthesis and washing protocols were
exactly the same thereafter. (Figure 1a).
4.3
Characterization of mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6
The synthesized mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6 were characterized
using different techniques. The textural characterization of
surface area and pore size was determined under the LN2
environment in the surface area analyzer (Micromeritics ASAP
2420). The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller method was utilized to
analyze the surface area, and the pore size was evaluated using
Barrett–Joyner–Halenda analysis. The powder X-ray diffraction
was collected from an Empyrean diffractometer operating at
1.6 kW with Cu Kα (λ = 0.154 nm) radiation to examine the
crystallographic structure in synthesized samples. Scans were
obtained for 2θ ranging from 0.8-10◦in steps of 0.001 s were
recorded for evaluating the ordered mesoporous structure and
in the 2θ range of 10-80◦to elucidate the crystal structure
after copper incorporation into mesoporous C3N6. FT-IR analysis
was carried out with an FTIR/NIR Frontier spectrophotometer
(Perkin Elmer) to confirm the silica samples within the 4000–
400 cm−1 range. Particle morphology and Cu distribution were
imaged using a JEOL 7900F FE-SEM equipped with an EDX
detector. Chemical composition of synthesized products was
assessed using a Perkin–Elmer EA 2400 elemental analyser.
The contents of copper in the xCu-mC3N6 samples were mea-
sured in a PerkinElmer Nexion 2000 ICP. TEM analysis was
conducted on a JEOL (JEM-2100 TEM). NEXAFS analysis was
performed at the Australian Synchrotron’s Soft X-ray Beamline
Spectroscopy.
4.4
DFT Calculations
All spin-polarised density functional theory (DFT) calcula-
tions were performed using the Quantum ESPRESSO package
[45, 46] with plane-wave basis sets and projector augmented-
wave (PAW) [47] pseudopotentials. The electronic structure
was described using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) [48]
exchange-correlation functional within the generalised gradient
approximation (GGA). Grimme’s DFT-D3 [49] dispersion cor-
rection was used to account for the van der Waals interactions.
A plane-wave energy cutoff of 60 Ry was chosen for the wave-
functions and 480 Ry for the charge density, ensuring adequate
convergence of total energies and forces. A Gaussian smearing
method with a degauss parameter of 0.001 Ry was applied to
accelerate the convergence. Brillouin zone integrations were
performed using a Gamma-centred 6 × 6 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack
k-point mesh [50]. Atomic relaxations were performed until the
forces on all atoms were less than 103 Ry/Bohr. The mesoporous g-
C3N6 was modelled as a 2x2 hexagonal cell with lattice parameters
of 21.152 Å each. To avoid the interaction between the periodic
images 15 Å vacuum was ensured perpendicular to the surface.
The loading of Cu into g-C3N6 was modelled by adsorbing a single
Cu atom close to the edge. The adsorption energies are calculated
as follows

where Eslab + adsorbate, Eslab, and Eadsorbate are the DFT energies of the
adsorbate on the slab, the slab alone and the adsorbate alone in
the gas phase (for Cu, bulk fcc).

mC3N6 materials against Bacillus subtilis and E. coli was eval-
uated using both qualitative (agar well plate) [35, 51] and

overnight. The concentration of bacterial cells reached OD600

4.5.2
Agar Well Diffusion Method
First, 75 µL of each bacterial inoculum was evenly spread
to the LB agar plates using a sterile swab or spreader and
punched to create a well with a diameter of 7 mm on the
plates. The positive control was tetracycline (100 µg/mL), and DI
water was used as the negative control. Different concentrations
(ranging from 5 - 40 mg/mL) of both mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6
were loaded into the wells. Then the plates were incubated
for 24 h at 37◦C, and the zone of inhibition was measured
using a Vernier caliper. The experiment was conducted in
triplicate.
4.5.3
Colony Counting Method
The gram-positive and gram-negative bacterial cells were pre-
pared at 1.0 × 107 CFU/mL concentrations in Phosphate Buffer
Saline solution (pH = 4.5). mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6 (0.25, 0.75,
1.25, and 1.50 g) samples were prepared at different concen-
trations (5–40 mg/mL) in phosphate buffer saline solution and
sonicated for 30 min. The 200 µL mixtures containing bacterial
cells and sample concentrations were then incubated together
for 2 h. After incubation, the treatment solutions from each
group were serially diluted with PBS buffer. Subsequently, the
bacterial solution (1 mL) was loaded on Petri film for enumerating
E. coli and B. subtilis, respectively. and incubated at 37◦C for
overnight.
4.5.4
Determination of ROS Production
Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were quantified by
staining with 1 mM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate
(H2-DCFDA), following the previously established procedure
[54]. The mC3N6 and xCu-mC3N6 were incubated with B. subtilis
or E. coli cells at a final concentration of 107 CFU/mL for
2 h. The cells were then incubated at 37◦C with 200 µM of
H2DCFH-DA dissolved in DMSO and incubated for 30 min in
darkness. Fluorescence was measured at an emission wavelength
of 523 nm and excitation at 503 nm on a Varioskan lux spec-
trofluorometer. To evaluate ROS generation, the treatment cell
emission intensity was compared to that of the positive control
(1 mM H2O2 treatment) and negative control (untreated cells). Net
fluorescence was calculated by measuring the autofluorescence
levels of bacterial cells incubated without a probe and background
fluorescence originating from buffer solutions. This included
triplicate analyses of each condition.
Statistical analysis of the differences in zones of inhibition,
bacterial colonies, and ROS production was carried out using
One-Way ANOVA in SPSS 28.
4.5.5
Post-Treatment Imaging by SEM
Morphological changes of bacteria were examined using Scan-
ning Electron Microscopy (SEM: JEOL-7900F) [53]. The bacteria-
xCu-mC3N6 solution was incubated for 2 h and then centrifuged
at a speed of 6000 rpm at room temperature for 5 min. 5 µL
of the incubated suspension was deposited onto silicon wafers.
The samples were then fixed by adding 3 µL of a fixing solution
(4% paraformaldehyde) and kept for 30 min at room temperature.
The bacteria were dehydrated through different concentrations
of ethanol (30 - 100%). Following dehydration through different
concentrations, the bacteria were kept for 5 min to undergo
fixation. Finally, the samples were coated with platinum to
undergo SEM observation.

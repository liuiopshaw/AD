A B S T R A C T
Oxidative stress and its associated complications pose serious threats to human health, demonstrating the 
pressing need for effective redox-active materials. This study presents the biogenic synthesis of a redox-active 
Copper oxide/Magnesium oxide (CuO/MgO) nanocomposite using Citrus limonium extract and evaluates its 
antibacterial, antioxidant, and antidiabetic activities, along with CuO nanoparticles for comparison. X-ray 
diffraction (XRD) analysis divulges the interwoven CuO and MgO phase in a single matrix with a crystallite size 
of 44 nm. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra expose the vibrational bands at 864, 678, and 570 cm−1, 
corresponding to Cu–O, Mg–O, and Cu–O–Mg. Optical analysis reveals a bandgap energy of 1.47 eV for the 
CuO/MgO composite, higher than 1.29 eV observed for CuO nanoparticles, indicating the improved visible-light 
absorption. Morphological examination reveals irregular aggregates with porous surface texture of the CuO/MgO 
composite. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis substantiates the Cu2+, Mg2+, and O2−oxidation 
states of rudiments in the nanocomposite. Biologically, the CuO/MgO nanocomposite exhibits potential anti­
bacterial activity against S. epidermidis and E. coli, with inhibition zones of 27.4 ± 0.15 mm and 25.2 ± 0.1 mm, 
respectively, and shows enhanced antioxidant activity with IC50 values of 140.72 ± 0.6 μg/mL. Furthermore, it 
demonstrates stronger inhibition of α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzymes, 79.8% and 86.1%, respectively, 
compared to CuO nanoparticles. Overall, this study confirms that the CuO/MgO nanocomposite, synthesized via 
a green approach, exhibits enhanced structural, optical, and biological properties, making it a promising 
candidate for biomedical applications targeting oxidative stress-related disorders.
1. Introduction
Over the years, rapid population and economic growth, along with 
numerous anthropogenic activities, have posed countless environmental 
threats to ecosystems worldwide. [1]. Harmful pollutants such as carbon 
dioxide (CO2), sulfur dioxide (SO2), methane gases (CH4), and nitrogen 
oxides (NOx) contain noxious ultrafine particulate matter (PM2.5), 
contaminating the atmosphere via air, water, and soil resources [2]. 
Upon inhalation, these pollutants enter the human body through the 
respiratory system and trigger adverse effects by initiating oxidative 
stress.
“Oxidative stress” refers to a detrimental condition arising from the 
excessive generation of toxic free radicals. These unstable molecules 
contain reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as superoxide anion (O₂•-), 
hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and hydroxyl radical (•OH). These species 
damage vital cellular components and disturb the redox homeostasis 
process, resulting in an imbalanced psychological state of the body [3]. 
Consequently, the body needs an alternative supplementary agent to 
restore the redox balance, such as an antibiotic and an antioxidant, to 
counteract this stress.

In recent decades, considerable research efforts have focused on 
developing new eco-friendly and biocompatible materials for biomed­
ical applications. By adhering to the fundamentals of the traditional 
medical system for preparing medicines using metal ash (Bhasma) to 
treat numerous ailments, it closely aligns with green chemistry princi­
ples [4]. Using this method, synthesized nanoparticles, such as copper 
oxide (CuO), zinc oxide (ZnO), nickel oxide (NiO), magnesium oxide 
(MgO), and titanium dioxide (TiO₂), via various natural extracts [5,6]. 
Apart from single metal oxide nanomaterials, their composite forms, 
such as binary, ternary, and mixed metal oxide forms, are enticing 
attention owing to their multifunctional properties [7,8].
Among the metal oxides, CuO is a prominent multifunctional p-type 
semiconductor material. CuO nanoparticles have a two-variable oxida­
tion state, such as Cu1+ (cuprous) and Cu2+ (cupric) states, and this 
formation is primarily due to their partially filled d orbitals in copper 
atoms [9]. This dual oxidation state enhances the redox behaviour of the 
material and makes it biocompatible for therapeutic purposes [10]. On 
the other hand, MgO is another widely studied metal oxide with a 
distinct crystalline structure [11]. Magnesium has been used as a min­
eral supplement for many years. In biomedicine, magnesium oxide has 
been utilized to treat cardiac and intestinal disorders [12].
Although studies on CuO and MgO individually are abundant. Yet, 
research on the CuO/MgO composite system for biomedical applications 
is still rare. For instance, Jeetendra Kulkarni et al. synthesized a CuO/ 
MgO nanocomposite using urea as fuel through the simple solution 
combustion technique to evaluate its structural, optical, and photo­
catalytic properties [13]. Sehrish Abbas et al. synthesized a CuO/MgO 
nanocomposite using Opunita monacantha plant extract via a green 
approach and investigated its enhanced antimicrobial and photo­
catalytic properties [14].
In view of the biocompatible and redox behaviour of individual CuO 
and MgO nanoparticles. Our work is designed to synthesize a CuO/MgO 
nanocomposite using lemon juice (Citrus limonium) as a natural aging 
and reducing agent. The rationale for selecting Citrus limonium over 
other citrus extracts is due to the presence of high content of bioactive 
organic acid content and essential oils [15,16]. Moreover, it predomi­
nantly contains natural antioxidants and stabilizing properties.
Based on the above-mentioned points, our current work focuses on 
the synthesis of CuO nanoparticles and CuO/MgO nanocomposite using 
Citrus limonium extract via a cost-effective green synthesis process with 
slight modifications to the bhasma method. The as-prepared materials 
were characterized and explored their redox behaviour by conducting in 
vitro biomedical application studies such as antibacterial, antioxidant, 
and antidiabetic assays.

2.2. Preparation of the lemon extract
The Fresh lemon fruit was used for the preparation process. The 
Citrus limonium (L.) belongs to the Rutaceae family, native to Asia 
[17,18]. The mature lemon fruits accessible in this work were obtained 
from the Thanjavur region, Tamil Nadu, India. The selected fruits were 
measured approximately from 5 to 7 cm in diameter, with individual 
weights ranging from 50 to 100 g. The fruits exhibited both oval and 
round shapes. Initially, the lemon fruit was cleaned, cut into halves, and 
squeezed. Subsequently, the extracted juice was filtered through 
Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The resulting extract with a pH of 2, free 
from pulp and solid particles, is utilized for the synthesis process.
2.3. Synthesis of CuO nanoparticles and CuO/MgO nanocomposite
The present synthesis method conceptually follows selected princi­
ples of the traditional Bhasma protocol with slight modifications. In this 
context, the aging step, analogous to Bhavana and the thermal treatment 
puta corresponding to Marana, was explicitly incorporated as an essen­
tial step [4].
For CuO synthesis, 4.9 g of copper (II) acetate was added to 50 mL of 
lemon extract (pH 2) under magnetic stirring at 80 ◦C. The stirring was 
continual for 75 min with the pH of ⁓4. Subsequently, the homoge­
neous solution was aged for two days at room temperature, and the pH 
increased to ~5, indicating a decrease in acidity. Further, the aged so­
lution was poured onto Petri plates and desiccated at 100 ◦C for 3 h. The 
dried samples were calcinated at 450 ◦C for three h in a muffle furnace, 
yielding brown-coloured CuO nanoparticles.
For CuO/MgO nanocomposite synthesis, 4.9 g of copper (II) acetate 
and 6.4 g of magnesium nitrate were dissolved separately in 50 mL of 
lemon extract (initial pH ~3) under stirring at 80 ◦C for 30 min. After 
that, each mixture was combined, and the stirring process was allowed 
to continue for another 1 h. The pH increased slightly to ~4 due to the 
presence of both salts. Consequently, the resulting solution was aged for 
two days at room temperature, and the pH remained approximately 4. 
The aged solution was poured onto Petri plates and desiccated at 100 ◦C 
for 3 h. The product was calcined at 450 ◦C for 3 h in a furnace, yielding 
a fine powder composed of a smoky-black CuO/MgO nanocomposite.
2.4. Characterizations
The XRD analysis was done using a PANanalytical-PW 340/60 
X'pertPro diffractometer with a Cu-Kα source. The measurements were 
recorded in continuous PSD fast-scan mode with a step size of 0.02◦and 
a time per step of 0.25 s over the 2θ range of 10–80◦. The FTIR spectra 
were obtained using a Perkin-Elmer Spectrum II instrument. Diffuse 
reflectance spectra (DRS) were documented using a SHIMADZU/ 
UV2600 spectrophotometer. The XPS investigations of the composite 
sample were done with a Thermo Fisher KAN9954133 spectrometer. 
The morphological analysis was investigated with a scanning electron 
microscope (SEM), VEGA3, TESCAN. The elemental composition was 
investigated using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), BRUKER 
Nano, GmbH, D-12489.

samples. It is observed that the CuO nanoparticles and CuO/MgO 
nanocomposite samples show lower reflectance (stronger absorbance) in

3.4. SEM and EDX studies
Fig. 4(a) and (b) illustrate the SEM micrographs of the CuO nano­
particles and CuO/MgO nanocomposite, respectively. Fig. 4(a) displays 
a rough, dense morphology with void spaces and smaller particles 
agglomerated on their surface. Fig. 4(b) exhibits uneven particles with 
dense agglomerated morphology having pores on their surfaces. The 
agglomeration observed in the nanocomposite sample is owed to the 
formation of clusters within the matrix due to the amalgamation of CuO 
and MgO in a single compound. The aggregation of smaller sticky par­
ticles on the surface of both samples may be due to the leaching property 
of lemon juice's tendency to adhere to the surface, together with the 
effect of controlled calcination at 450 ◦C, which could promote the 
particle–particle interactions [33,34]. This indicates that the bioactive 
agents present in Citrus limonium involve the reduction during the syn­
thesis process [35]. Consequently, the SEM analysis reveals that the 
mixed phase of CuO and MgO, along with pores, indicates the large 
surface area of CuO/MgO nanocomposite, which makes it a suitable 
surface-active material. The pores observed in the SEM images are likely 
a result of both synthesis and calcination [36]. Loosely packed nano­
particles formed during synthesis create initial voids, while the 
decomposition of residual organics and partial sintering during calci­
nation further contribute to pore formation. These pores enhance the 
surface area, providing more active sites for interaction with biological 
entities and free radicals.
Fig. SF2 (see Supplementary Information) shows the particle size 
distribution of (a) CuO and (b) CuO/MgO, derived from SEM micro­
graphs using ImageJ software. The CuO exhibits a broad particle size 
distribution in the micrometer range, from a few micrometers up to 40 
μm. In contrast, the CuO/MgO composite shows a significantly narrower 
size distribution, with particle sizes mainly ranging from a few micro­
meters to 12 μm. A notable change observed in the composite is the 
increased population of smaller-sized particles. This size reduction can 
be attributed to the presence of MgO, where fine MgO nanoparticles 
uniformly decorate and partially cover the CuO structures.
Further, Fig. 5(a) and (b) confirm the occurrence of key elements 
such as Cu and O in the CuO nanoparticles and Cu, Mg, and O in the 
CuO/MgO nanocomposite samples, respectively. Fig. 5(a) shows the 
EDX spectrum of the CuO sample, confirming the presence of Cu and O 
elements. Copper is the dominant element with a weight percentage of 
96.79 wt% and an atomic percentage of 88.37 at.%, while oxygen 
contributes 3.21 wt% and 11.63 at.%. Moreover, the EDX spectrum of 
CuO/MgO nanocomposite shown in Fig. 5(b) exposes the presence of Cu, 
Mg, and O, confirming the successful formation of the nanocomposite. 
Copper shows a weight percentage of 54.85 wt% and an atomic per­
centage of 25.90 at. %, magnesium exhibits 16.51 wt% and 20.39 at.%, 
and oxygen accounts for 28.64 wt% and 53.71 at.%. The increased ox­
ygen content and the presence of Mg verify the incorporation of MgO 
into the CuO matrix.

3.6. Antibacterial studies
Effective bio-antibacterial drugs are vital despite the growing threat 
of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, which pose a serious threat to world 
health [43]. These antibacterial agents are essential for combating 
clinically related diseases and addressing infections related to environ­
mental exposures and immunodeficiency diseases. The antibacterial 
action of the CuO and CuO/MgO samples was tested against two clini­
cally relevant Staphylococcus epidermidis and Escherichia coli using the 
disc diffusion approach. Fig. 7 shows the inhibition zone formed around 
each disc loaded with the specimens, along with the control and the 
standard antibiotic, i.e., amoxicillin, for comparison. The CuO nano­
particles and CuO/MgO nanocomposite exhibit better inhibitory activity 
against both selected microorganisms. Graphical representation of the 
zone of inhibition around the discs in diameter (mm) and its triplicate 
values are depicted in Fig. 8. Antibacterial activities are observed based 
on a concentration-dependent manner.
For CuO nanoparticles, at the lowest concentration of 50 μg/mL, the 
sample exhibits a lower zone of inhibition against E. coli (9.3 ± 0.1 mm) 
and S. epidermidis (6.4 ± 0.25 mm). At the higher concentrations of 100 
and 150 μg/mL, the sample shows potent repressive activity on 
S. epidermidis (16.3 ± 0.2 and 22.2 ± 0.15 mm) and E. coli (18.3 ±
0.1and 19.3 ± 0.1 mm). On the other hand, the CuO/MgO nano­
composite shows a better zone of inhibition for all the concentrations. At 
the highest concentration (150 μg/mL), the CuO/MgO formed an inhi­
bition zone around the disc, which is 27.4 ± 0.15 and 25.2 ± 0.1 mm for 
S. epidermidis and E. coli, respectively. And for 100 μg/mL, the sample

Fig. 9 illustrates the antibacterial activity of the CuO/MgO nano­

contains Cu2+ and Mg2+ ions along with O2−functional groups. When a 
composite sample is dissolved in ethanol during tests, the nano­

gram-negative bacteria like E. coli, and the nanoparticles interact with 
the bacterial outer membrane [45]. The outer membrane of the bacteria 
contains a lipopolysaccharide (LPS) layer, which carries a negative 
charge. Then, the positively charged metal ions interact with the nega­
tive bacterial surface. The electrostatic interaction happens between 
them it disrupting the outer membrane layer, impairing lipoproteins, 
and suppressing the cell membrane permeability [46]. Then the metal 
ions penetrate the bacterial cell and affect internal components, making 
the bacteria more susceptible to ROS such as hydroxyl radicals (•OH) 
and superoxide anions (O•-) inside the bacteria [47]. This ROS genera­
tion causes damage to crucial biomolecules in pathogens like DNA, 
proteins, and lipids through oxidative stress, which disrupts normal 
bacterial function [33].

nanocomposite behaves contrarily because the cell wall contains a thick

3.7. Antioxidant studies
Several processes, including the inhibition of chain start, the binding 
of transition metal ion catalysts, the breakdown of peroxide, radical 
scavenging activity, and reduction capacity, have been linked to anti­
oxidant action [50]. Free radicals have one or more unpaired electrons 
and belong to a separate chemical entity. Because of their extreme 
instability, these free radicals destroy other molecules by taking their 
electrons to become stable. These free radicals are developed inside the 
intracellular system of the human body and are highly reactive to the 
chemical species in the body system, leading to cell damage. The human 
body constantly produces these radicals because it needs them for im­
mune system function, chemical signaling, detoxification, and energy 
provision [51]. The radical scavenging ability of the synthesized CuO 
nanoparticles and CuO/MgO nanocomposite was appraised by super­
oxide anion and DPPH radical scavenging analyses. Fig. 10(a) and (b) 
represent the percentage inhibition activity of CuO nanoparticles and 
CuO/MgO nanocomposite at various concentrations and compared with 
a standard antioxidant, BHT. Table 3 reveals the IC50 values of the CuO 
nanoparticles and CuO/MgO nanocomposite compared with the stan­
dard antioxidant drugs. The lesser the IC50 value, the higher the 
hydrogen-donating ability.
The DPPH radical scavenging action of the synthesized samples is 
shown in Fig. 10(a), which reveals that at the 950 μg/mL concentration, 
the CuO nanoparticles and CuO/MgO nanocomposite exhibit 79.5% and 
83.9% free radical scavenging activity, respectively. Similarly, Fig. 10
(b) shows the superoxide anion radical scavenging activity of CuO 
nanoparticles and CuO/MgO nanocomposite. At a 950 μg/mL concen­
tration, the CuO nanoparticles inhibit 78.7% of free radicals, and the 
CuO/MgO nanocomposite inhibits 84.7% of free radicals. Based on the 
obtained results of the IC50 value, the CuO/MgO nanocomposite has a 
noteworthy radical scavenging action compared to CuO nanoparticles 
and the standard BHT. Reports on the green synthesis of CuO/MgO are 
scarce. A few studies have explored the antioxidant activity of CuO and 
MgO-based nanocomposite. For example, Kuldeep Kumar et al. reported 
that CuO/NiO nanocomposite prepared via a green approach with Ipo­
moea carnea leaf extract showed 96.40% inhibition at 800 μg/mL [52]. 
Muhammad Yaseen et al. prepared a CuO-SiO2 nanocomposite via the 
sol-gel method and assessed its antioxidant activity through a DPPH 
assay. The CuO-SiO2 composite exhibited 59.18% radical scavenging 
activity at 1000 μg/mL with an IC50 of 78.66 μg/mL [53]. Based on the 
result of the current study, the lemon extract-based CuO/MgO nano­
composite exhibited better DPPH and superoxide radical quenching 
activity, even at lower concentrations. Notably, the lower IC50 of 140.72 
± 0.6 μg/mL suggests that a minimal amount of nanocomposite is

3.8. Antidiabetic studies
Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a multifaceted metabolic disease marked 
by insufficient or impaired insulin secretion, which raises blood sugar 
levels excessively over time (hyperglycemia). In hyperglycemic disor­
der, digestive enzymes play a vital role in the digestion of carbohydrates 
[54]. Acarbose is used as the standard reference drug for comparison. As 
shown in Fig. 12, CuO nanoparticles and CuO/MgO nanocomposite 
samples exhibit significant enzymatic action in concentration- 
dependent reduction for both enzymes. The IC50 concentration values 
of the synthesized materials are shown in Table 4.
In the case of the α-amylase inhibitory assay, the percentage of in­
hibition of CuO nanoparticles is found to increase based on concentra­
tion. But at the highest concentration of 950 μg/mL, the CuO 
nanoparticles and CuO/MgO nanocomposite inhibit 77.2% and 79.8% 
of α-amylase enzymes, respectively. For the α-glucosidase enzymatic 
assay, it is found to be 78.8% and 86.1%, corresponding to the CuO 
nanoparticles and CuO/MgO nanocomposite, respectively. These results 
exposed that the existence of mixed-phase CuO and MgO in the nano­
composite exhibited stronger inhibition against the α-amylase and 
α-glucosidase enzymes compared to CuO nanoparticles. Because of the 
improved structural, morphological, and optical features of the com­
posite, it may facilitate better interaction with target enzymes compared 
to bare CuO nanoparticles. Moreover, this study contributes novel 
insight into the field of biomedicine, as the synthesis and application of 
CuO/MgO nanocomposite for antidiabetic purposes have not been 
widely explored. However, there is an existing study on the synthesis of 
CuO-based nanocomposites for diabetes management. For example, 
Arumai Selvan et al. synthesized Ag/CuO nanocomposite using Murraya 
Koenigii and Zingiber officinale extracts and demonstrated their notable 
antidiabetic activity [55]. The synergetic effect of the CuO/MgO nano­
composite may be attributed to the occurrence of some traces of 
bioactive compounds in the sample detected in FTIR analysis.
The probable mechanism behind the antidiabetic effect of the CuO/ 
MgO nanocomposite against alpha-amylase and alpha-glucosidase en­
zymes is shown in Fig. 13. This figure explains what happens when the 
CuO/MgO nanocomposite is taken orally. After the oral administration 
of the CuO/MgO nanocomposite, it travels through the esophagus and 
enters the digestive system. In the gut, particularly near the pancreas in 
our digestive system, it secretes an enzyme such as alpha-amylase and 
alpha-glucosidase [56]. These enzymes normally work on breaking 
down carbohydrates into simple sugars (glucose) that can be absorbed 
into the bloodstream [57]. In case the level of secretion of this enzyme is 
excessive or it becomes overactive, it can lead to rapid breakdown of 
carbohydrates and a sudden increase in blood glucose levels, resulting in 
hyperglycemia [56]. Over time, this can put stress on pancreatic beta 
cells and increase the risk of developing type 2 diabetes and related 
complications. However, the entering CuO/MgO composite particles 
interact with the active sites of beta cells and slow down the breakdown 
of complex carbohydrates into glucose [55]. This process may delay the 
digestion of carbohydrates and help to prevent a sharp rise in blood 
glucose levels after meals, improving postprandial glycemic control.
After absorption into the bloodstream, the active components in the 
composite, including Cu2+ ions, Mg2+ ions, oxygen species, and trace 
bioactive compounds, reach the pancreatic β-cells [58]. The β-cells play 
a major role in our body; they stimulate the insulin production and 
secretion process. They also sustenance the activity of intracellular en­
zymes involved in glucose sensing ability and enhance the storage of 
glucose as glycogen, and improve the cellular glucose uptake [59]. By 
enhancing the function of β-cells, the nanocomposite helps the pancreas 
to respond efficiently to changes in blood glucose levels. Overall, the 
analysis of the CuO/MgO nanocomposite is a more effective agent in 
controlling postprandial glucose levels for treating diabetic-related 
diseases.

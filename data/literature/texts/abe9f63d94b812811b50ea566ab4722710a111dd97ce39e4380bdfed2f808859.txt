Synthesis of highly stable luminescent silver nanoclusters 
in metal–organic framework for heightened antibacterial activity
Arushi Verma1   · Anirudh Singh1 · Neetu Shukla2 · Sintu Kumar Samanta1   · Amaresh Kumar Sahoo1 
Received: 4 December 2021 / Accepted: 22 February 2022 / Published online: 13 March 2022 
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag GmbH, DE part of Springer Nature 2022
Abstract
Size of the nanoparticles plays a very crucial role in their bactericidal activity as smaller size render better perforation via 
bacterial cell wall and are thus found to be more effective against resistant bacterial infections also. In this regard, fluorescent 
silver nanoclusters (Ag NCs) having a size less than 2 nm ought to be a potent bactericidal agent against both Gram positive 
and Gram negative bacterial strains. However, stabilization of such small sized NPs in aqueous medium that is amenable for 
biological applications is very challenging. Herein, a simple and fast method of synthesis of Ag NCs was proposed using 
metal–organic framework (MOFs) as scaffold, which was found to be very stable in the ambient conditions. As-synthesized 
Ag NCs-in-MOF nanocomposite was characterized by UV–Vis spectroscopy, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), 
and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The nanocomposite was highly bactericidal against both Gram positive and 
Gram negative bacterial strains and caused cell wall perforation that was evident from flow cytometry analysis. Results also 
showed that DNA damage had occurred due to treatment of Ag NCs-in-MOF, ultimately leading to bacterial cell death with 
considerably lower dose of silver.
Keywords  Nanocluster · Metal organic framework · Antibacterial
1  Introduction
Globally, occurrence of increasing number of cases of resist-
ant bacterial infection demands development of novel thera-
peutic agents as effective replacement of conventional antibi-
otics to overcome the challenges of this looming threat. This 
is more alarming in case of third world countries where use 
of antibiotics is not monitored and managed properly which 
more often results in prevalence of multi-drug resistant 
(MDR) bacterial strains. Thus, development of apposite 
alternatives of antibiotics against which bacteria would offer 
less chance of resistance is very essential. Various research 
groups have been working profoundly to address this global 
issue. In this regard, nanoscale materials offer significant 
prospects owing to their dimensionality and unique physico-
chemical properties. In addition, only a very few reports are 
available for development of antibiotics resistance in bacte-
ria against nanomaterials [1]. Several nano-scaled materials 
are being explored for their antibacterial properties which 
include metal nanoparticles and nanoclusters, primarily 
made up of Au, Ag, Cu; metal oxide NPs specially ­Fe3O4, 
ZnO, CuO, MgO, ­TiO2 NPs etc. [2]; carbon quantum dots, 
and several nanocomposites associated with them. Bayrami 
et al. biogenically synthesized ZnO nanoparticles using 
Vaccinium arctostaphylos L. leaf extract and tested its bio-
medical applications including antimicrobial activity [3]. 
Similarly, Rad et al. reported synthesis of chromium and 
cerium co-doped magnetite/reduced graphene oxide nano-
composite which showed potent antibacterial activity against 
S. aureus [2]. In their recently reported work, the group has 
synthesized graphene-based ZnCr layered double hydroxide 
nanocomposites as bactericidal agents [4]. Another class of 
nanomaterial, i.e., titanium-based nanotubes with drug car-
rying capability were reported by Mojtaba et al. in 2019. [5]
Recently explored nanoscale material is metal nanoclut-
ers (NCs) which are ultra-small-scale nanoparticles having

size less than 2 nm with a very strong quantum confinement 
effect. Their optical, electronic, chemical properties differ 
considerably from their comparatively larger nanoscale 
counterparts. Metal nanoclusters typically possess discrete 
energy levels of electrons, larger Stokes shift, quantized 
charges, and chirality. Due to the presence of discrete energy 
levels, nanoclusters are not known to possess surface plas-
mon resonance (SPR), such as the other nanoparticles, and 
are, therefore, sometimes referred to as missing link between 
atoms and nanoparticles. The synthesis of NCs is considered 
a bit tricky and complex due to stability issues. They usually 
have a core–shell structure in which the organic functional 
groups or protecting ligands on the surface, protect the metal 
core inside. Several metal NCs with core of Ag, Au, Cu, Pd, 
Pt have been reported. The most distinguished protecting 
ligand that can be attached to its surface being the thiols 
(HS-R). In addition, polymers, proteins, DNA can also serve 
as protecting ligands in metal nanoclusters. Synthesis of pure 
NCs is a challenge due to its ultrasmall size which causes 
agglomeration of metal ions if synthesized without proper 
protection. Typical synthesis routes include photoreduction, 
sonochemical/electrochemical synthesis, chemical etching, 
microwave assisted synthesis etc. [6]. Synthesis of Au25 
cluster with high yield and molecular purity using kinetically 
controlled method by Zhu et al. in 2008 was considered as a 
major breakthrough in this field [7]. Ever since then similar 
methods have progressed considerably to synthesize atomi-
cally precise metal NCs as well. Owing to its huge applica-
tions in nanotechnology, including nano-medicine, several 
research groups are extensively working upon synthesis of 
stable metal nanoclusters. Numerous studies are being con-
ducted as nanomaterials are being explored for their bio-
medical properties; however, the most widely researched 
and used precursors for nanomaterial synthesis associated 
with antibacterial applications are Gold and Silver. The gold 
nanoparticles (Au NPs) and gold nanoclusters (Au NCs) are 
also extensively being tested for their antimicrobial proper-
ties; however, gold is comparably inert in nature and having 
less potent bactericidal property as compared to silver [8, 
9]. Moreover, Au being very expensive, thus scientists have 
been focusing more on silver-based nanomaterials.
The most commonly used nanoscale materials are silver 
nanoparticles (Ag NPs) which provides significant possi-
bilities and hopes due to their potent bactericidal effects 
against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial 
strains. The bactericidal mechanisms proposed by various 
recent studies demonstrated that cellular internalization of 
Ag NPs causes release of Ag + ions which elicits several 
downstream signaling including generation of high level of 
reactive oxygen species (ROS) [10]. Besides, recent reports 
established that Ag NPs might affect the functions of several 
important bio-macromolecules, such as DNA and enzymes 
which are essential for the growth and proliferation of the 
bacteria [11]. Moreover, several studies proposed that Ag 
NPs causes irreparable damage (i.e., perforation) to bacte-
rial cell wall that ultimately leads to bacterial cell death. 
However, despite its potent bactericidal property, cytotox-
icity caused due to higher doses of Ag NPs limits its direct 
use as antibacterial agents [12]. Thus, researchers have 
been focusing to develop various new strategies to reduce 
the dose of silver. Nanocomposites of Ag NPs have been 
found to be one of the effective alternatives as they could 
exhibit bactericidal activity with lower doses of silver. Sev-
eral nanocomposites have been developed using agents, such 
as amphiphilic molecules, antibacterial peptides, dendrimers 
and self-assembled supramolecules, which exhibited strong 
bactericidal outcomes with a minimum concentration of sil-
ver [13, 14]. However, these were found to be very costly, 
and raised the issues of stability of Ag NPs for long term 
storage that are crucial for translational research. Another 
viable option to get a better bactericidal effect is to reduce 
the size of the Ag NPs. Previous reports established that 
smaller the size of Ag NPs better the bactericidal property of 
the NPs [15, 16]. In this regard, luminescent silver nanoclus-
ters (Ag NCs) having size less than 2 nm got edge over the 
conventional Ag NPs. The ultra-small size of Ag NCs offers 
better penetration through biological barriers as well as high 
reactivity within the cellular micro environment which lead 
to serve it as a better bactericidal agent as compared to Ag 
NPs. It is worth mentioning that Ag NCs are a new class of 
luminescent nanoscale material that possess several unique 
physicochemical properties which are vividly different from 
its counterpart, i.e., Ag NPs. However, synthesis of Ag NCs 
offers the foremost challenge of its stabilization in aqueous 
environment due to the issues of chemical stability of Ag 
­(E0, 0.80 V) as compared to Au ­(E0, 1.50 V) [17–19]. Vari-
ous methods have been developed; however, these are found 
to be time consuming along with the use of multistep harsh 
reaction conditions and poor yield of luminescent Ag NCs. 
Moreover, very often it has been noticed that Ag NCs are 
oxidized in the ambient condition which limits their long-
term applications and storage [20–22].
Synthesis of the fluorescent NCs possess huge difficulties 
due to their ultra-small size, which makes them unstable in 
aqueous medium. Thus, it requires a controlled method of 
synthesis. Moreover, routine methods of Ag NP synthesis 
may not be applicable here. For synthesis of stable Ag NCs, 
proper scaffold as well as stabilizing agent are very much 
essential. In present case, we have synthesized Ag NCs using 
metal organic framework (MOF) by considering terephthalic 
acid and ­ZnSO4. This MOF served as scaffold as it provides 
much greater internal surface areas to host the ultra-small 
size Ag NCs, which was further stabilized by thiol contain-
ing ligand glutathione [17]. MOFs are promising organic/
inorganic hybrid porous substances that possess characteris-
tics of miscellaneous structures, high degree of crystallinity,

high thermal/chemical stability, have multiple active sites, 
several adaptable channels as per application, and also 
deliver a stable skeleton to provide stability and hold nano-
structures. Their applications have been potentially proved in 
drug delivery, fluorescent sensing, catalysis, gas absorption, 
separation and storage etc. [23, 24] These have the ability to 
form hybrids or encapsulate varied materials such as metal 
ions, dyes, nanomaterials such as quantum dots, nanoclus-
ters etc. The most crucial challenge that researchers face in 
synthesizing NCs is that due to high surface to volume ratio 
and thereby high surface energy, they are typically thermo-
dynamically unstable. As a tendency to attain stability, they 
usually self-agglomerate, which increases the size and dif-
fers the shape of synthesized nanocluster. To prevent this 
from happening, a 3D porous support structure is required, 
which encapsulates the metal NC, anchoring it from multi-
ple sides. MOFs are being looked upon as these porous 3D 
structures that would play a promising role in supporting and 
stabilizing the synthesized nanocluster [25]. It has also been 
observed that strong metal–matrix interaction facilitates the 
formation of nanoclusters in a very controlled manner within 
the well-defined cavity of MOFs. Several stabilized NCs in 
MOFs have been reported by numerous research groups so 
far, including Ag NCs, Au NCs, Cu NCs etc. In the present 
case, to increase the bactericidal potency, a cationic biode-
gradable polymer such as chitosan was also added during the 
synthesis of Ag NCs. Thus, we have developed a fast, and 
one step synthesis of luminescent Ag NCs-in-MOF which 
was found to be very stable in the ambient condition. The 
Ag NCs-in-MOF nanocomposite showed strong bactericidal 
activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bac-
terial strains with much reduced concentration of silver. As 
a model system, ampicillin resistant green fluorescent pro-
tein (GFP) expressing recombinant E. coli were taken. The 
results demonstrated that perforation of bacterial cell-wall 
and degradation of cellular DNA are the underlying cause 
for synergistic bactericidal activity of the nanocomposite.
2  Experimental section
2.1  Chemicals and growth media
Luria–bertani (LB), luria–bertani agar (LB agar), and 
chitosan (Medium MW) extra-pure were procured from 
SRL, silver nitrate ­(AgNO3—99.0%) was purchased from 
Sigma–Aldrich, zinc sulphate heptahydrate ­(ZnSO4.7H2O) 
was purchased from Himedia laboratories Pvt. Ltd., tere-
phthalic acid (Benzene—1,4–Dicarboxylic acid) and glu-
tathione-reduced (GSH) extra pure, nitroblue tetrazolium 
(NBT) were purchased from SRL. Milli Q water was used 
in all the experiments. High-purity molecular biology grade 
chemicals and reagents used for agarose gel electrophoresis 
were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich.
2.2  Bacterial strains and culture method
The green fluorescent protein (GFP) expressing recombi-
nant E. coli (Gram-negative) bacteria were grown in LB 
medium at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. Bacillus sub-
tilis MTCC121 (Gram-positive), Enterobactor aerogenes 
MTCC2822 (Gram-negative), Escherichia coli MTCC739 
were procured from Microbial Type Culture Collection and 
Gene Bank (MTCC), Chandigarh. B. subtilis and E. aero-
genes were grown in LB media containing beef extract at 
37 °C and 30 °C with shaking at 200 rpm, respectively.
2.3  Synthesis protocol
For making the MOF, 0.4 g of ­ZnSO4 and 0.2 g of tereph-
thalic acid were taken. Synthesis of Ag NCs was done by 
taking 2.5 mL of 50 mM glutathione (GSH), 5 mL of 0.5% 
(w/v) chitosan that was prepared by dissolving in 0.1% acetic 
acid and 2.5 mL of freshly prepared 10 mM silver nitrate 
­(AgNO3). These were added into the MOF and kept on stir-
ring for 45 min at room temperature. For the adjustment 
of pH at 7.2 ± 0.2, NaOH (10 N) was added dropwise to 
the reaction vessel. The milky colloidal suspension showed 
strong blue emission when viewed under UV trans-illumi-
nator [10].
2.4  Characterization
The formed nanocomposite (Ag NCs–MOF) was character-
ized using UV–Vis Spectroscopy (Systronics 2202), Fluo-
rescence Spectroscopy (Analytical-FS2060), XPS spectros-
copy, FTIR spectroscopy and TEM. TEM images for the 
synthesized framework was obtained from JEOL–JEM 2100 
TEM (Camera–AMT) operating at a maximum accelerating 
voltage of 200 kV. The XPS analysis of sample was done 
using VersaProbe II XPS. For FTIR analysis, samples were 
mixed with KBr for preparation of pallet and analysed using 
a Perkin Elmer IR spectrometer.
2.5  Measurement of oxidative stress by NBT assay
In presence of reactive oxygen species (ROS), nitro blue 
tetrazolium salt gets reduced to blue colored formazan which 
is then quantified by measuring its absorbance to study the 
oxidative stress. For ROS measurement, overnight grown 
bacterial culture was incubated in presence of minimum 
inhibitory concentration (MIC) dose of nanocomposite in 
0.2 mL Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) at 37 °C for 
30 min along with its individual controls. Then, 0.1 mL of 
10 mg/mL NBT solution was added and the reaction mixture

2.6  Gel electrophoresis
Agarose gel electrophoresis was carried out to find out the 
effect of synthesized nanocomposite on DNA integrity. For 
this, plasmid DNA (pDNA) was isolated from GFP express-
ing recombinant E. coli bacteria following standard proto-
col. 2.53 μg/uL of pDNA was then incubated with vary-
ing amount of nanocomposite at 37 °C for 30 min. Both 
untreated and nanocomposite-treated DNA were then sepa-
rated by gel electrophoresis (0.8% agarose, 70 V) and the 
integrity of the DNA was observed under UV-transillumi-
nator after staining with ethidium bromide (1 mg/mL) [27].
2.7  Flow cytometry analysis
Fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) study was con-
ducted on GFP expressing recombinant E. coli as a model 
system using propidium iodide (PI) staining. The PI dye 
cannot enter the viable cells; however, if the cell membrane 
is damaged or perforated as in case of compromised or dead 
cells, the dye enters inside to interact with the DNA and 
shows red fluorescence. The bacteria was treated with half 
MIC dose of synthesized Ag NCs for 3 h along with its indi-
vidual controls, such as only chitosan and MOF (i.e., ­ZnSO4 
and terephthalic acid). The bacterial cells were then col-
lected by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 5 min. The superna-
tant was discarded and the pellet obtained was re-dispersed 
in 0.9% NaCl solution. Furthermore, 2.5 µL of 0.2 mM PI 
was added in each sample tube. Analysis of sample was 
done in flow cytometer (BD FACS Calibur system, BD Bio-
sciences). Fluorescence was detected at FITC-A channel and 
PI-A channel which corresponded to 525 ± 10 nm (green) 
and 620 ± 10 nm (red) emissions, respectively. About 10, 
000 events were recorded for each sample and plotted in the 
logarithmic scale [28, 29].
3  Results and discussion
The Ag NCs were synthesized within a MOF using zinc 
sulphate ­(ZnSO4) and terephthalic acid [17]. For the syn-
thesis of luminescent Ag NCs, glutathione (a tripeptide 
made up with L-glutamine, L-cysteine, and glycine) and 
biodegradable cationic polymer chitosan were considered 
which served as stabilizing and reducing agents [30–37]. As 
mentioned above, synthesis of the fluorescent NCs possess 
huge difficulties due to its ultra-small size, which makes it 
unstable in aqueous medium, and requires controlled method 
of synthesis. Synthesis of stable Ag NC demands appro-
priate scaffold and suitable stabilizing agents. The current 
research demonstrates synthesis of MOF made up of tereph-
thalic acid and ­ZnSO4 to support the Ag NC synthesis. The 
produced MOF acts as a scaffold to hold and facilitate the 
Ag NC synthesis. Further thiol containing ligand glutathione 
was used to stabilize it. To increase the bactericidal potency, 
chitosan—a cationic biodegradable polymer was also added 
during synthesis Ag NCs. The as-synthesized Ag NCs-in-
MOF was characterized by UV–Vis spectroscopy which 
showed a peak at 245 nm due to presence of glutathione 
in the system. It did not reveal any peak at around 400 nm 
which corresponds to absorbance due to SPR of Ag NPs as 
reported by earlier studies (Fig. 1a) [18]. For the genera-
tion of SPR, free conduction bands electrons resonate with 
electromagnetic wave (i.e., incident photons). Thus, the SPR 
depends on the size and shape of the metal nanoparticles as 
well as refractive index of the dispersion medium. Metal 
nanoclusters typically possess discrete energy levels of elec-
trons due to its ultra-small size (< 2 nm) instead of con-
tinuous band structure. As mentioned earlier it also shows 
larger Stokes shift, quantized charges, and chirality. Due to 
the presence of discrete energy levels, nanoclusters are not 
known to possess SPR like the other nanoparticles. How-
ever, the as-synthesized Ag NCs-in-MOF showed strong 
blue luminescence at 430 nm (Fig. 1b while excited with 
UV light (310 nm).
Time-dependent luminescence study was also carried 
out which revealed that the reaction was virtually com-
pleted within 45 min, since no further increase in the emis-
sion intensity was observed after 45 min (Fig. S1). Overall 
results showed that there was no absorption peak of SPR 
for Ag NPs; rather it showed strong luminescent with UV 
excitation which confirmed the formation of stabilized Ag 
NCs-in-MOF as corroborated with the previous reports [20].
Furthermore, TEM was performed which showed spheri-
cal particles having the average diameter of 1.16 ± 0.3 nm 
(Fig. 2a). The corresponding particle size distribution was 
calculated based on the TEM image (Fig. 2b). It was also 
seen that there were polymeric layer-like structures. These 
were possibly due to the presence of the MOF and chitosan. 
Moreover, it was found that Ag NCs were arranged spe-
cifically on the matrix of MOF and there were hardly any 
particles present outside it. Further to find the change in 
functional groups of the precursors during cluster synthesis, 
FT–IR study was performed. It was found that peak cor-
responds to thiol (-SH) of glutathione at 2550 ­cm−1 was 
disappeared after synthesis of Ag NC, that is possibly due 
to oxidation of glutathione led to formation of metal–thiol 
interaction (Fig. S2) [38].

Furthermore, to quantify the bacterial cell death caused 
by nanocomposite, propidium iodide (PI)-based flow 
cytometry assay was conducted. It is well known that PI is 
impermeable through viable bacterial cell wall; however, 
it permeates through damaged bacterial cell wall via pas-
sive diffusion and interacts with the pDNA. Therefore, the 
amount of PI positive cells corresponds to the number of 
dead/compromised cells. The signal for green emission 
produced by viable cells due to expression of green fluores-
cence protein (GFP) was collected in the FITC-A channel 
in FACS. Likewise, the PI emission signal was collected in 
PI-A channel which corresponds to the red emission. The 
results showed that considerable number of cells were in 
dead (PI positive) or compromised state (double positive, 
i.e., PI and GPF positive) by Ag NCs-in-MOF treatment. 
Only 5.9% cells were found to be in compromised state 
in control (untreated) cells, whereas in case of treatment 
with chitosan, scaffold for Ag NC (i.e., chitosan, GSH, and 
MOF), and Ag NCs-in-MOF nanocomposite, 29.6%, 47.7%, 
and 61.4% cell population were found in compromised state, 
respectively (Fig. 5).
Moreover, there was significantly higher number of cells 
(11%) in dead state in case of Ag NC-treated cells as com-
pared to the individual controls. Thus, it was evident that 
significant cell wall perforation and/or damage was caused 
possibly due to penetration of the ultra-small size Ag NCs 
through bacterial cell wall. This ultimately led to substantial 
amount of cell death upon treatment with the Ag NCs-in-
MOF as compared to its individual controls [28, 29].
Finding out the molecular mechanism of bacterial cell 
death is the prerequisite for development of any novel bac-
tericidal agent. Previous reports demonstrated that silver-
based nanocomposite led to elevation of the basal level of 
the reactive oxygen species (ROS) which caused bacterial 
redox imbalance inside the cells. Therefore, to find out the 
amount of ROS produced by nanocluster treatment, we 
performed standard NBT assay. Interestingly, the results 
illustrated that there was noticeable decrease in the ROS 
level in case of nanocluster-treated cell as compared to its 
controls, that was probably due to glutathione (GSH) being 
one of its precursor compounds (Fig. 6a). Glutathione is a 
well-known antioxidant molecule responsible for decrease 
in the ROS level inside the cells. Thus, in the present case, 
it is anticipated that ROS generation was not significant in 
the Ag NCs-in-MOF-treated cells due to presence of GSH.
To understand the underlying mechanism for bactericidal 
property, we studied the effects of nanocluster on the integ-
rity of the DNA. The agarose gel electrophoresis of plasmid 
DNA (pDNA) purified from nanocluster-treated E. coli bac-
terial cells showed the significant degradation of the DNA as 
indicated by its smearing on the gel. Moreover, level of DNA 
degradation was increased with increasing concentration 
of Ag NCs, whereas pDNA purified from the control cells 
(untreated) showed a prominent intact DNA band (Fig. 6b). 
However, it is important to find out the component of the 
nanocluster that was responsible for the DNA damage [46].
Literature suggests that Ag NCs without specific surface 
passivating agents which interacts with DNA does not have 
any direct effect on the DNA integrity [47]. However, in the 
present case, the Ag NCs were stabilized using chitosan and 
MOF, while cationic chitosan forms polyplex with negatively 
charged DNA [48]. Moreover, Ag NCs in presence of MOF 
­(Zn2+ ions–terapthelic acid complex) could interact with the 
DNA and lead to destabilization of the DNA conformation 
[49–51]. Therefore, it is proposed that high bactericidal 
activity of the nanocomposite was possibly due to ultra-
small size of Ag NCs that caused the cell wall perforation 
and then both Ag NCs and MOF triggered DNA damage.

4  Conclusions
Synthesis of a fluorescent silver nanocluster using 
metal–organic framework was done using a rapid synthesis 
protocol. This method implies in-situ encapsulation of Ag 
NCs into MOFs forming a composite. The metal–organic 
framework encapsulated Ag NCs was found to be highly sta-
ble. Synthesized Ag NCs-in-MOF has heightened antibacte-
rial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative 
bacteria, as well as on antibiotic resistant strain, at a con-
siderably lower dosage of silver. The cell wall perforation 
followed by DNA damage was found to be the underlying 
cause of bacterial cell death by the nanocomposite.

A B S T R A C T
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaque 
deposition and neurofibrillary tangles, which collectively drive neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and 
cognitive decline. Here, we investigated whether a peptide epitope vaccine targeting the Aβ1–10 sequence could 
mitigate Aβ-induced pathology in AD mouse model. Three Aβ1–10 peptides, i.e. Aβ1–10-N, Aβ1–10-D1H, and 
Aβ1–10-S8R were synthesized, and Aβ1–10-S8R was further conjugated to ovalbumin (OVA) or keyhole limpet 
hemocyanin (KLH) to enhance immunogenicity. Among seven treatment groups, Aβ1–10-D1H and Aβ1–10-S8R, 
particularly when conjugated to OVA or KLH, effectively suppressed Aβ, amyloid-beta precursor protein (APP), 
and beta-secretase 1 (BACE-1) expression, decreased inflammatory cytokine production by astrocytes and 
microglia, and increased the levels of key synaptic markers (synaptophysin, synaptosomal-associated protein 23 
[SNAP-23], postsynaptic density protein 95 [PSD-95]). Carrier protein conjugation also elevated immunoglob­
ulin G (IgG) levels in the spleen, indicative of a robust humoral response. Taken together, these findings 
demonstrate that Aβ1–10-based immunization, especially with OVA or KLH conjugation, reduces Aβ-driven 
neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and memory deficits, suggesting a promising immunotherapeutic 
strategy for AD.
1. Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia, respon­
sible for 60–80 % of all cases. Clinical hallmarks include progressive 
memory impairment, deficits in language and writing, and alterations in 
personality (Alzheimer’s, 2016). For over two decades, it has been 
widely believed that amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides play a key role in initiating 
and propagating AD (Selkoe, 1991; Selkoe, 1994). Although initially 
focused on Aβ plaques, the amyloid cascade hypothesis has evolved to 
highlight soluble oligomers and protofibrils as the most neurotoxic 
forms of Aβ, with a critical involvement in synaptic dysfunction (Haass 
and Selkoe, 2007; Cleary et al., 2005; Klein et al., 2004). Preventive 
approaches targeting Aβ peptides have aimed to limit its production, 
block aggregation, and/or enhance clearance from the brain (Panza 
et al., 2019). An imbalance between production and clearance of Aβ 
appears to drive its pathogenic accumulation, contributing to the 
neurodegenerative process (Mawuenyega et al., 2010; Harrison and 
Owen, 2016; Jack et al., 2018). Multiple studies, employing active and 
passive immunization, have underscored the potential of immunother­
apies targeting Aβ to clear senile plaques and improve cognitive

outcomes (Schenk et al., 1999). Consequently, a significant number of 
therapeutic strategies focus on reducing Aβ levels or preventing patho­
logical aggregation (Haass and Selkoe, 2007; Cleary et al., 2005). These 
include β- and γ-secretase inhibitors/modulators that diminish Aβ pro­
duction, as well as immunotherapeutic approaches that facilitate Aβ 
clearance and reduce tau aggregation (Sigurdsson et al., 2004; Rafii and 
Aisen, 2009; Holtzman et al., 2011).
Passive immunization, by administering exogenous antibodies, is 
among the most developed treatment modalities aimed at pathological 
Aβ species (Plotkin and Cashman, 2020). The rationale for vaccine- 
based strategies targeting the Aβ peptide lies in the premise that anti- 
Aβ antibodies can inhibit aggregation, mitigate toxicity, or promote 
catabolism of the peptide. Ultimately, these mechanisms have the po­
tential to modify disease progression (Kwan et al., 2020). Building on 
seminal findings demonstrating that vaccination with fibrillar Aβ42 
prevents plaque deposition in transgenic mouse models of AD (Schenk 
et al., 1999), numerous preclinical investigations have shown that Aβ- 
targeted immunotherapy can reduce plaque burden and alleviate 
behavioral deficits. These immunotherapies primarily involve Aβ-based 
immunogens or passive administration of Aβ-specific antibodies 
(Morgan, 2011). Subsequent work revealed that the T helper (Th) and B 
cell epitopes within Aβ42 reside in distinct peptide regions (Ghochikyan, 
2009). Specifically, B cell epitopes lie in the N-terminal residues 
(Aβ1–15), whereas Th epitopes are situated in the central and C-terminal 
portions (Aβ15–42) (Axelsen et al., 2011; Gilman et al., 2005). The 
fragment Aβ4–10 has emerged as the dominant B cell epitope recognized 
by high-affinity antibodies, with these antibodies shown to inhibit Aβ 
fibrillogenesis (McLaurin et al., 2002).
In this study, we investigated whether passive immunization with 
Aβ-based peptide epitope vaccines could recognize and clear Aβ aggre­
gates in a murine model of Alzheimer’s disease. Leveraging epitope 
prediction, we synthesized three peptide vaccines (Aβ1–10-N, Aβ1–10- 
D1H, and Aβ1–10-S8R) and subsequently generated two conjugates, 
Aβ1–10-S8R + ovalbumin (OVA) and Aβ1–10-S8R + keyhole limpet 
hemocyanin (KLH), to enhance immunogenicity. These five peptide 
epitope formulations were administered intraperitoneally to assess their 
immunopotency. In addition, we evaluated both biochemical and 
behavioral outcomes, aiming to identify improved vaccine candidates 
for mitigating AD pathology. Notably, Aβ1–10-S8R + OVA and Aβ1–10- 
S8R + KLH elicited robust antibody responses, significantly lowered Aβ 
deposition and senile plaque formation, and ameliorated synaptic 
deficits.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Animals and peptide epitope
Eight-week-old male C57BL/10 N mice (25–30 g) were obtained 
from Samtako Bio (Osan, Republic of Korea). A total of 70 mice were 
randomly divided into seven groups (n = 10 per group; half for western 
blot and the other half for immunofluorescence): 
1. Control group: Received intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) injection of 
sterile saline.
2. Aβ group: Received i.c.v. injection of Aβ.
3. Aβ + N group: Received ICV Aβ + intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 
the N epitope peptide (DAEFRHDSGY).
4. Aβ + D1H group: Received ICV Aβ + i.p. injection of the D1H epitope 
peptide (Ac-HAEFRHDSGY-NH2).
5. Aβ + S8R group: Received ICV Aβ + i.p. injection of the S8R epitope 
peptide (Ac-DAEFRHDRGYC-NH2).
6. Aβ + S8R + OVA group: Received i.c.v. Aβ + i.p. injection of the S8R 
epitope peptide conjugated to OVA (Ac-DAEFRHDRGYC-OVA).
7. Aβ + S8R + KLH group: Received i.c.v. Aβ + i.p. injection of the S8R 
epitope peptide conjugated to KLH (Ac-DAEFRHDRGYC-KLH).
All mice were acclimated for one week under controlled conditions 
(12-hour light/dark cycle, 23 ◦C, 60 ± 10 % humidity) with ad libitum 
access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Animal 
Ethics Committee (IACUC) of the Division of Applied Life Sciences, 
Gyeongsang National University, Republic of Korea (Approval ID: 125, 
Code: GNU-200331-M0020). Peptide-based epitopes were synthesized 
at > 95 % purity by Peptron (Daejeon, Republic of Korea). Peptides were 
derived from the PEP1 sequence (DAEFRHDSGY) and, where noted, 
modified by N-terminal acetylation (Ac) and/or conjugated to carrier 
proteins (OVA or KLH) via a C-terminal thiol group.
2.2. Amyloid beta oligomer Preparation
Amyloid beta (Aβ) oligomers were prepared by dissolving Aβ1–42 
(Sigma-Aldrich) in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to 1 mM, diluting to 100 
µM in PBS, and sonicating for 10 min on ice to promote oligomerization. 
The solution was then incubated at 4 ◦C for 24 h to allow further olig­
omer formation. To confirm oligomer formation, Aβ1-42 was dissolved in 
1 mM HFIP, dried under nitrogen, re-solubilized in 5 mM DMSO, and 
diluted to 100 µM in PBS (pH 7.4). After incubating 24 h at 4 ◦C, 200 µL 
of the solution was injected onto a Superdex 75 Increase 10/300 GL 
column mounted on an ¨AKTA-Pure system (Cytiva). The column was 
equilibrated in PBS at 0.50 mL min−1, and elution was monitored at 280 
nm. Each run produced a single, symmetrical peak at 11.7 ± 0.1 mL that 
corresponds to the 24 kDa hexamer; a minor high-molecular-weight 
shoulder at 8 mL and a small 4 kDa monomer peak at 18 mL accoun­
ted for < 5 % and < 25 % of the total area, respectively. Baseline return 
to zero on either side of the hexamer apex confirmed high resolution and 
purity. Fractions from 11.5–13.0 mL were pooled, and protein concen­
tration determined by micro-BCA. Integrating the hexamer peak relative 
to the total chromatographic area gave a recovery of 65 ± 4 % (n = 3), 
consistent with the literature definition of low-molecular-weight Aβ 
oligomers. Between runs the column was regenerated with 1 M NaCl, 
0.1 M NaOH, and re-equilibrated in PBS.
2.3. Animal Model
Male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks old) were used for in vivo studies. 
Aβ oligomers (2 µg per animal in 3 µL total volume) were injected ste­
reotaxically into the cortex and hippocampus. Stereotaxic injection co­
ordinates were: for the cortex – AP: +1.5 mm, ML: ±2.0 mm, DV: −1.0 
mm; and for the hippocampus – AP: −2.0 mm, ML: ±1.5 mm, DV: −1.5 
mm. Injections were performed under deep isoflurane anesthesia using a 
stereotaxic apparatus and a 2–5 μL Hamilton microsyringe fitted with a 
33-gauge needle. The Aβ solution was infused slowly over approxi­
mately 5 min. Following the injection of Aβ1–42 oligomers into the brain, 
an intraperitoneal injection of the epitope peptide was administered 
immediately.
The procedure was conducted under anesthesia with a mixture of 
0.05 ml/100 g body weight Rompun (Xylazine) and 0.1 ml/100 g body 
weight Zoletil (Ketamine). The stereotaxic surgery was carried out in a 
temperature-controlled, isolated room with a heating system intended to 
regulate body temperature (kept at 36 ◦C to 37 ◦C). Body temperature 
was regularly monitored using a thermometer, as anesthesia-induced 
hypothermia is known to enhance tau phosphorylation (Bretteville 
et al., 2012). Mice were monitored during recovery and maintained for 
7 days post-injection before behavioral or histological analyses.
2.4. Drug treatment
On Day 0, immediately after intracerebroventricular Aβ adminis­
tration, mice received a prime intraperitoneal injection of the carrier- 
conjugated peptide (100  μg in 100  μL PBS; 4  mg/kg), followed by 
identical booster injections on Days 7 and 14. The vaccine stock (2  mg/ 
mL) was thawed once, diluted 1:1 in PBS, and administered via a 29- 
gauge needle into the lower right abdominal quadrant.

2.5. Y-maze test
Behavioral experiments were conducted in the ninth week, two 
weeks after the Aβ injections. The Y-maze test was achieved to evaluate 
the spontaneous alteration. Briefly, each mouse was positioned in the 
middle of the apparatus and allowed to freely navigate the maze for 
three sessions lasting 8 min each. A series of arm entrances were 
recorded. Spontaneous alternation was defined as the mouse consis­
tently hitting three arms in consecutive sets of overlapping triplets. 
Alternating behavior (%) was calculated as [sequential triplets 
(sequential entry into 3 different points)/total number of arms −
2] ×
100.
2.6. Passive avoidance test
The passive avoidance test is a common method for evaluating 
memory and learning in mouse models. In this test, the mice were 
trained to associate a particular environment with an aversive stimulus, 
often a mild foot shock. The chamber is divided into light and dark 
booths, with a gate between them. The mice were allowed to explore 
both booths on the first day. On the following day, the mice were placed 
in a light booth, after 30 s the dark booth was opened. When the mice 
passed through the dark booth, the door was closed and gave a mild foot 
electric shock (0.2 mA/2 s) derived from the grid floor. The mice were 
then put back in the booth without receiving a shock to assess their 
memory and learning skills. The latency to pass through the gate be­
tween the booths was recorded to measure the entrance movement.
2.7. Protein extraction and homogenization of mice brain
Following the behavioral study, all mice were given ketamine/ 
xylazine anesthesia. The brain was instantly extracted and the cortex 
and hippocampus were meticulously dissected. The tissues were then 
frozen on dry ice and stored at a temperature of −
80 ◦C. The tissues 
were homogenized in a PRO-PREPTM extraction solution (iNtRON 
Biotechnology, Dallas Texas MA USA). The samples were subsequently 
centrifuged for 25 min at 4 ◦C at a speed of 13,000 rpm. The supernatant 
was collected and kept at −
80 ◦C for storage.
2.8. Western blot analysis
The Bio-Rad Protein Assay Kit was utilized to quantify the protein 
concentrations (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA, USA). Protein samples 
(15–30 µg) were electrophoresed in equal proportions on a 12–15 % SDS 
PAGE gel before being transferred to a PVDF membrane. The molecular 
weight of the proteins was ascertained by loading a protein marker 
(Gangnam-STAIN, iNtRON Biotechnology, CA USA) in parallel. To 
minimize nonspecific binding, membranes were blocked with 5 % (w/v) 
skim milk and then incubated with primary antibodies diluted 1: 1,000 
for 16 h at 4 ◦C overnight. As directed by the manufacturer’s instructions 
(Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Uppsala, Sweden), protein detection 
was performed using ECL detection reagents after the reaction with 
secondary antibodies coupled with horseradish peroxidase, when 
applicable. The X-ray films were scanned, and the computer-based 
Sigma Gel program version 1.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) was used to 
densitometry the optical densities of the bands.
2.9. Immunofluorescence
The protocol for immunofluorescence staining was as followed. The 
slides underwent 15-minute washes twice in 1 % 1x Phosphate-Buffered 
Saline (PBS), five minutes of proteinase K treatment, and an hour-long 
blocking step in 5 % normal goat serum. After blocking, slides were 
treated with primary antibodies (1:100) for 24 h. After primary antibody 
incubation, the sections were exposed to 1.5 h of fluorescein isothio­
cyanate (FITC) goat-anti-rabbit / bovine anti-mouse TRITC-labelled 
antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA). Following the 
completion of the secondary antibody treatment, the slides were washed 
with PBS and mounted on 4′, 6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 
eight minutes to visualize the nucleus (blue). After that, the slides were 
covered with DAKO fluorescent mounting material. The FluoView 1000 
(FV 1000 MPE, Olympus, Japan) was used to capture the images. By 
summing all the pixels in a region to get an overall value, ImageJ was 
used to estimate the relative integrated density among the various 
experimental groups. The resulting values were then compared between 
the various experimental groups.
2.10. Statistical analysis
To analyze band intensities, X-ray films were scanned and densities 
were evaluated using ImageJ software (version 1.50, NIH, Bethesda, 
MD, USA). Similarly, ImageJ was used to analyze integrated density for 
an immunofluorescence study. The data was shown as the standard error 
of the mean (SEM) ± mean. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and 
the Student’s “t” test were utilized in statistical analysis to compare 
various groups. GraphPad Prism6 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, 
USA) was used to construct the graphs. * denotes a significant difference 
from the Aβ1-42-treated groups, while # indicates a significant difference 
from the vehicle-treated control group.
2.11. ELISA
Aβ1–42 levels were measured using the Invitrogen Mouse Amyloid 
Beta 42 ELISA Kit (Catalog No. KHB3441). Frozen tissue samples (cortex 
and hippocampus) were homogenized in ice-cold PBS containing a 
protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma, P8340) using a tissue homogenizer. 
After homogenization, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 
min at 4 ◦C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. For the 
ELISA, 100 μL of each sample or standard was added to wells pre-coated 
with an anti-Aβ1–42 capture antibody and incubated for 2 h at room 
temperature. Following incubation, wells were washed 4 times with 
wash buffer, and 100 µL of the detection antibody solution was added to 
each well and incubated for an additional 1 h at room temperature. After 
another washing step, the substrate solution was added to each well, and 
the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.
Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (MBS704754, 
MBS2708011, MyBioSource, CA, USA) was used to quantify IL-10 and 
IgG1 concentrations in mouse serum, following the manufacture’s pro­
tocol. Briefly, mouse serum was incubated for 2 h at 37 ◦C per well in a 
96 well plate. Then, the serum was removed and incubated with Biotin- 
antibody for 1 h at 37 ◦C. Next, the plate was washed 3 times, HRP- 
avidin was added, it was covered with adhesive strip, and incubated 
for 1 h at 37 ◦C. Afterwards, TMB Substrate was added, and then Stop 
Solution was added. Lastly, the optical density of each well was 
measured using a microplate reader set at 450 nm.
3. Results
3.1. Identification and design of the Aβ-based peptide epitope vaccine
Using the Aβ sequence as input (Fig. 1A), several candidate peptides 
were predicted as B-cell epitopes through the Immune Epitope Database 
(IEDB) tool (Supplementary Fig. S1A–F). To evaluate the binding af­
finity of peptides, Genetic Optimization for Ligand Docking (GOLD) 
software was used. GOLD utilizes a genetic algorithm to optimize the 
placement of the peptide within the binding site of the target protein, 
taking into account various factors such as binding energy, hydrogen 
bonding, and steric and electrostatic interactions. This approach pro­
vided an in-depth analysis of the interaction between each peptide and 
the target protein, helping to identify the most promising candidates. 
Among these peptides, PEP1 exhibited the highest average scoring 
across ten independent GOLD simulations (Supplementary Fig. S2A).

The GOLD score assigned to PEP1 reflected its favorable binding in­
teractions, indicating strong binding affinity between PEP1 and the 
target protein. This score, which incorporates factors such as the 
strength of the binding energy, hydrogen bonds, and electrostatic 
compatibility, validated PEP1 as a highly stable and potent epitope. 
Toxicity analysis using ToxinPred further indicated that PEP1 is non- 
toxic, thus identifying it as a suitable Aβ-specific epitope (Fig. 1A; 
Supplementary Fig. S2B). To enhance its immunogenicity, PEP1 was 
subsequently modified at the N-terminus with an acetyl group (Ac) and 
conjugated to carrier proteins (KLH or OVA) via a C-terminal thiol 
group. These modifications were designed to increase the peptide’s 
stability, immunoreactivity, and resemblance to native Aβ (Fig. 1A). 
Finally, docking simulations demonstrated that PEP1 residues effec­
tively bind to B cells (Fig. 1B–E; Supplementary Fig. S2A–C), further 
supporting the selection of PEP1 as the optimal Aβ-based epitope 
vaccine.

Y-maze test (n = 10/group). Spontaneous alternation, an indicator of 
spatial working memory, was significantly reduced in Aβ-injected mice 
compared to control mice. Treatment with the non-carrier epitopes 
(D1H and S8R) partially restored spontaneous alternation, whereas 
vaccines conjugated to carrier proteins (S8R + OVA, S8R + KLH) elicited 
a markedly greater improvement (Fig. 2A, B). These findings were 
corroborated by the passive avoidance test, used to assess learning and 
memory. Aβ-injected mice displayed significantly decreased latency to

3.3. Epitope vaccines enhance spleen IgG expression in an Aβ-induced AD 
mouse model
To determine whether epitope vaccination elicits a systemic immune 
response, total IgG levels in the spleen were quantified by immuno­
blotting and immunofluorescence. Our results showed that IgG levels 
were significantly increased in the mice vaccinated with non-carrier 
epitopes (D1H and S8R) compared to the control, Aβ vehicle, and N 
groups (Fig. 3A, B). Notably, the groups vaccinated with carrier- 
conjugated epitopes (S8R + OVA, S8R + KLH) exhibited the highest 
IgG levels, confirming that the addition of carrier proteins enhanced the 
immune response (Fig. 3A, B). This observation was further confirmed 
by immunofluorescence analysis, where IgG expression was markedly 
higher in the spleen of the epitope-immunized groups, particularly those 
conjugated to carrier proteins (Fig. 3C, D). Additionally, plasma IgG 
levels were significantly elevated in the vaccinated groups, with no 
significant differences observed between the vaccine variations 
(Fig. 3F). Plasma IL-10 levels were significantly lower in mice that 
received carrier protein-conjugated vaccines compared to the α-syn 
mice, indicating a potential regulatory role of these vaccines in modu­
lating immune responses (Fig. 3E).
3.4. Epitope vaccine treatment reduces Aβ protein aggregation
Western blot analyses confirmed that Aβ-injected mice exhibited 
markedly elevated Aβ expression in both the cortex and hippocampus 
compared to controls. Epitope vaccination lowered Aβ levels in all 
vaccine-treated groups, although the D1H, S8R, and especially S8R +
OVA, S8R + KLH groups exhibited stronger suppression of Aβ than the N 
epitope group. Correspondingly, the expression levels of amyloid pre­
cursor protein (APP), Aβ deposition, and β-secretase (BACE-1) were 
attenuated in the cortex and hippocampus of vaccinated mice, with the 
most pronounced decreases observed in the S8R + OVA and S8R + KLH 
groups (Fig. 4A–D). Immunofluorescence staining corroborated these 
findings, indicating robust reductions in Aβ accumulation within the 
cortex and hippocampus in all vaccinated groups, with the greatest ef­
fect seen in the carrier-conjugated vaccines (Fig. 4E, F). Lastly, the total 
protein levels of Aβ1-42 was increased in the cortex and hippocampus of 
AD group, but decreased in the vaccinated groups (Fig. 4G, H). Zoomed- 
out images were also acquired using confocal microscopy, focusing on 
the entire cortex and hippocampus. Similar to the results shown in 
Fig. E, a large number of Aβ aggregates were observed in Aβ-treated 
mice. However, these aggregates were reduced in the AB + OVA and AB 
+ KLH treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). Additionally, 
immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of mouse cortex and hippocam­
pus showed a similar pattern of Aβ reduction, with significant decreases 
observed in the vaccinated groups, particularly in the S8R + OVA and 
S8R + KLH groups (Supplementary Fig. 3A).

3.5. Epitope vaccination attenuates Aβ-induced glial activation and 
proinflammatory cytokine production
We next investigated whether epitope immunization modulates Aβ- 
induced astrocyte and microglial activation. Western blotting showed 
that glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and ionized calcium-binding 
adapter molecule 1 (Iba-1) levels were markedly elevated in Aβ-alone 
and Aβ + N groups relative to controls. Conversely, administration of the 
D1H or S8R epitopes partially suppressed the upregulation of GFAP and 
Iba-1, whereas the S8R + OVA and S8R + KLH groups exhibited a more 
substantial reduction. Similarly, the proinflammatory cytokines tumor 
necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β) were 
significantly elevated in Aβ-alone and Aβ + N mice. These increases 
were mitigated by vaccination with D1H and S8R and were most 
effectively reduced by the carrier-conjugated vaccines (Fig. 5A, C-F). 
Immunofluorescence analysis of GFAP and Iba-1 confirmed the immu­
noblot results, revealing a dramatic increase in GFAP and Iba-1 posi­
tivity in Aβ-alone and Aβ + N mice that was partially reversed by D1H 
and S8R and significantly reversed by S8R + OVA and S8R + KLH 
(Fig. 5B, G, H).
3.6. Epitope vaccination mitigates Aβ-induced synaptotoxicity
Finally, to assess synaptic integrity, we examined the levels of post- 
and presynaptic markers including postsynaptic density protein 95 
(PSD-95), synaptosome-associated protein 23 (SNAP-23), and synapto­
physin (SYP). Western blot analysis revealed that Aβ injection reduced 
PSD-95, SNAP-23, and SYP levels relative to controls. Notably, mice 
immunized with D1H or S8R partially restored these synaptic proteins, 
whereas S8R + OVA and S8R + KLH groups demonstrated significantly 
higher expression compared with non-carrier epitope groups (Fig. 6 A, 
C-E). These findings were corroborated by immunofluorescence staining 
of PSD-95 and SYP in both the cortex and hippocampus (Fig. 6 B, F, G). 
In summary, epitope vaccination, particularly with carrier-conjugated 
peptides, effectively protected against Aβ-induced synaptotoxicity.
4. Discussion
In this study, we developed a peptide epitope vaccine approach 
targeting Aβ-induced neuroinflammation and neuropathology. Guided 
by in silico epitope predictions, we synthesized three candidate vaccines 
(Aβ1–10–N, Aβ1–10–D1H, and Aβ1–10–S8R). To further enhance

strong B-cell–dependent immune response. Among the seven treatment

the levels of Aβ, APP, and BACE-1 in the brain, and also diminished 
astrocyte- and microglia-mediated neuroinflammatory responses.

The amyloid cascade hypothesis posits that Aβ plaque accumulation 
triggers a cascade of neurotoxicity, leading to progressive neuronal 
damage and cognitive decline (Serrano-Pozo et al., 2016). Accordingly, 
anti-Aβ immunotherapy has drawn considerable interest as a potential 
disease-modifying strategy, since Aβ-specific antibodies can facilitate 
plaque clearance and improve cognitive function (Chen et al., 2007). 
Achieving a robust, long-lasting humoral immune response generally 
requires that a vaccine antigen contain epitopes recognized by both B 
cells and T-helper cells, because B-cell activation is critically dependent 
on T-cell help (Axelsen et al., 2011). One practical approach to meet this 
requirement is to conjugate a B-cell–targeted epitope to a highly 
immunogenic carrier protein that provides T-cell epitopes. Indeed, 
keyhole limpet hemocyanin (KLH) is widely used to boost T-cell acti­
vation, and ovalbumin (OVA) has also been utilized to enhance the 
immunogenicity of otherwise weak antigens in vaccine development 
(Axelsen et al., 2011; Nakagami et al., 2014; Zhou et al., 2020; Heegaard 
et al., 2015).
A growing body of evidence indicates that Aβ oligomers represent 
the most neurotoxic form of Aβ, contributing to neuronal loss and 
cognitive impairment in AD (Haass and Selkoe, 2007; Cline et al., 2018; 
Mroczko et al., 2018). Both active immunization with Aβ peptides and 
passive administration of Aβ-specific antibodies have been reported to 
reduce cerebral Aβ levels and improve cognitive outcomes in AD mouse 
models (Lemere, 2009). In the present study, we designed and synthe­
sized five peptide-based epitope vaccines i.e. Aβ1–10-N, Aβ1–10-D1H, 
Aβ1–10-S8R, Aβ1–10-S8R + OVA, and Aβ1–10-S8R + KLH to enhance 
immunogenicity. Compared to non-conjugated peptides, both KLH- and 
OVA-conjugated Aβ1–10 peptides elicited higher levels of anti-Aβ anti­
bodies, lowered Aβ deposition, and decreased Aβ oligomers. In partic­
ular, the Aβ1–10-S8R + OVA and Aβ1–10-S8R + KLH groups 
demonstrated the most pronounced reduction in Aβ burden and the 
strongest immune responses.
Aβ levels in the brain are regulated by the proteolytic processing of 
APP, which involves BACE-1 and γ-secretase activities (Vassar et al., 
1999; Roberts et al., 2017). Elevated BACE-1 expression accelerates Aβ 
production, thereby exacerbating cognitive dysfunction (Zhang et al., 
2007; Tamagno et al., 2002; Guglielmotto et al., 2011; Zhao et al., 
2019). Our data show that among the tested vaccines, Aβ1–10-S8R +
OVA and Aβ1–10-S8R + KLH most effectively reduced BACE-1 expres­
sion, aligning with previous reports demonstrating that targeting BACE- 
1 and Aβ can mitigate AD pathology (Liu et al., 2016). Prior work using 
Aβ3–10-KLH vaccines in APP/PS1 mice similarly highlighted the role of 
carrier proteins in enhancing the Th2-biased antibody response and 
lowering levels of both Aβ and hyperphosphorylated tau (Ding et al., 
2017; Zhang et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2017).
In addition to attenuating Aβ deposition, immunotherapy can also 
modulate neuroinflammation. Elevated levels of GFAP and Iba-1 in as­
trocytes and microglia, respectively, reflect reactive gliosis, a key 
pathological feature in AD (Bachstetter et al., 2016; Ahmad et al., 2017).

alleviate neuroinflammatory responses (Ding et al., 2017).
Synaptic degeneration is a key driver of cognitive decline in AD, and 
it is often linked to Aβ-induced synaptotoxicity (Hsieh et al., 2006). The 
loss or dysfunction of critical synaptic proteins—such as synaptophysin, 
PSD-95, and SNAP-23—correlates strongly with synaptic failure and 
memory deficits in AD (Ali et al., 2015; Khan et al., 2021). In our Aβ- 
injected control mice; we observed significant decreases in these syn­
aptic marker proteins. By contrast, mice vaccinated with Aβ1–10-S8R +
OVA or Aβ1–10-S8R + KLH largely preserved synaptic integrity, pre­
sumably because the robust anti-Aβ antibody response prevented olig­
omeric Aβ from disrupting synaptic function. The vaccinated animals 
also showed clear improvements in memory-related behavior, indi­
cating that boosting the immunogenicity of Aβ peptides can confer 
neuroprotection and slow cognitive decline.
We found that IgG levels in the spleen were substantially elevated in 
mice immunized with carrier-conjugated epitopes. Because IgG is a 
principal effector molecule of humoral immunity, this observation un­
derscores the capacity of KLH- and OVA-based vaccines to elicit adaptive 
immune responses against Aβ (Huang et al., 2019; Kashimura, 2020). 
Our epitope was rationally designed by docking the Aβ1-10 peptide onto 
the B-cell-receptor interface so that residues most likely to engage B cells 
were preserved; such a B-cell-centered design is expected to induce a 
Th2-skewed response. In mice a Th2 bias is reflected primarily by 
antibodies of the IgG1 subclass, whereas a Th1 bias is reflected by IgG2a 
and IgG2b. Consistent with this expectation, we observed a strong rise in 
total IgG titers dominated by IgG1 together with marked reductions in 
Aβ pathology, demonstrating that the Th2-oriented response was bio­
logically effective. Other IgG subclasses such as IgG2a, IgG2b and IgG3 
may still be produced at lower levels, and their precise contributions 
remain to be clarified.
As we advance this epitope vaccine strategy, several avenues for 
further research emerge. First, exploring alternative carrier proteins or 
even carrier-free vaccine platforms could improve the safety and flexi­
bility of the approach. It will be important to determine whether other 
carrier systems such as tetanus toxoid or CRM197, can elicit immune 
protection comparable to OVA or KLH. Second, a detailed analysis of the 
antibody response profile is warranted: assessing the distribution of 
antibody isotypes induced by the vaccine will help clarify the Th1/Th2 
polarization of the immune response. Finally, we plan to investigate the 
kinetic aspects of antibody–epitope interactions. By examining antibody 
binding affinity, dissociation rates, and the durability of the immune 
response, we aim to understand how immunity evolves over time and 
whether booster immunizations can further enhance long-term efficacy. 
Insights from these studies will guide the optimization of vaccine design 
and potentially improve its sustained effectiveness against AD.

Eﬀect of ligand structure on the size control of
mono- and bi-thiolate-protected silver
nanoclusters†
Kaiyuan Zheng,a Xun Yuan*b and Jianping Xie
*a
Ligand structure could control the size and structure of thiolate-
protected silver nanoclusters (Ag NCs) in water. Small alkane-thiols,
medium-sized aromatic-thiols, and bulky thiol-containing tri-peptides
direct the formation of Ag25(SR)18, Ag44(SR)30, and Ag9–15(SR)5–10,
respectively. In addition, small alkane-thiol collocates well with
other types of thiolate ligand to generate bi-thiolate-protected
Ag25 NCs with controlled ligand landscapes on their surfaces.
Thiolate-protected metal nanoclusters (NCs) possess a molecular
structure comprising a metal core and thiolate shell. This unique
structure gives metal NCs intriguing physicochemical properties
of both the metal core and ligand shell, such as strong lumines-
cence, quantized charging, molecular magnetism and chirality.1
Recently, the molecular-like properties of metal NCs have been
successfully applied in various fields such as catalysis, sensing,
imaging, energy conversion, electronics, and biomedicine.2 In
the past two decades, tremendous eﬀorts have been devoted to
controlling the metal core of NCs, creating a rich NC family with
diﬀerent sizes, compositions, and structures.3 Besides the metal
core, the thiolate shell on the NC surface is an equally important
component in dictating the structures and properties of thiolate-
protected metal NCs.4 However, several aspects of the eﬀects of
thiolate ligands on the synthesis of metal NCs are not clear at
present. For example, it is still not clear how the ligand structure
aﬀects the size of NCs in water; how two diﬀerent types of
thiolate ligand collocate each other to host the NCs and
what the landscapes of these ligands on the NC surface are;
and how the ligand landscape aﬀects the stability of metal NCs
in solution. Understanding such fundamental issues would
facilitate the design of eﬃcient synthetic strategies for new NC
species. Therefore, there is a pressing need to investigate the
eﬀects of ligand structure on the synthesis of metal NCs.
In this study we have revealed the importance of ligand size
and structure on the synthesis of mono- and bi-thiolate-
protected Ag NCs in water. By using three types of thiolate ligand
with diﬀerent structures and bulkiness (i.e., small alkane-thiols,
medium-sized aromatic-thiols, and bulky thiol-containing tri-
peptides) as model ligands to synthesize Ag NCs (e.g., Ag25(SR)18,
Ag44(SR)30, and Ag9–15(SR)5–10; SR denotes thiolate ligands), we
have demonstrated the key role that ligand structure plays in
regulating the size of Ag NCs in aqueous solution. In addition,
we can use the alkane-thiols to collocate with other types of
thiolate ligand to synthesize bi-thiolate-protected Ag NCs with
controlled size and ligand landscapes (on the NC surface).
To study the eﬀects of ligand structure on the size of the
resulting Ag NCs (Fig. 1a), we chose three types of thiolate
ligand with diﬀerent structures (see the insets of Fig. 1b for their
molecular structures). These three ligands were: a small alkane-thiol,
6-mercaptohexanoic acid (MHA); a medium-sized aromatic-thiol,
p-mercaptobenzoic acid ( p-MBA); and a bulky thiol-containing
tri-peptide, glutathione (GSH). Ag NCs protected by these ligands
were synthesized according to a reported NaOH-mediated

Ag NCs protected by three diﬀerent ligands were synthe-
sized under the same reaction conditions (Fig. S1, ESI†). The
as-synthesized MHA-, p-MBA-, and GSH-protected Ag NCs pre-
sented diﬀerent colors in solution (brown, light brown, and
dark red, respectively; insets of Fig. 1b). We first used UV-vis
absorption spectroscopy to characterize the optical properties
of the as-synthesized Ag NCs. As shown in Fig. 1b (top panel),
the MHA-protected Ag NCs displayed three distinct absorption
peaks at B336, 486, and 665 nm (black curve). These absorp-
tion peaks are characteristic of Ag25(SR)18.6
In comparison, the Ag NCs protected by the bulky GSH showed
an absorption peak at B480 nm (Fig. 1b, middle panel), which
agrees well with the reported Ag14(GSH)8;7 while the Ag NCs
protected by the medium-sized p-MBA showed several absorption
peaks at 422, 496, 548, 648, and 840 nm (Fig. 1b, bottom panel),
which are characteristic absorptions of the reported Ag44(SR)30.8
The distinctive absorption features of the as-synthesized Ag NCs
protected by MHA, p-MBA, and GSH suggest their diﬀerent sizes,
which exclude the formation of large-sized Ag nanoparticles in
solution (with a typical absorption peak at B420 nm).9
We used electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS)
to determine the molecular formula of the as-synthesized Ag
NCs protected by MHA, p-MBA, and GSH. The MHA-protected
Ag NCs (Fig. 1c, top panel) displayed three sets of intense peaks
at m/z B890, 1068, and 1336 in the range of 800–3000, which can
be assigned to [Ag25(MHA)18  5H]6, [Ag25(MHA)18  4H]5,
and [Ag25(MHA)18  3H]4, respectively (see Fig. S2† for detailed
ESI assignments). This assignment is consistent with the UV-vis
absorption spectrum of the MHA-protected Ag NCs (Fig. 1b),
further indicating the successful synthesis of Ag25(MHA)18 in
water. In addition, a native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis
(PAGE) analysis of as-synthesized Ag25(MHA)18 displayed one
single band in the gel (Fig. S3, ESI†), which also indicates the
high purity of the Ag25 NCs obtained in our protocol. The high
purity and fast synthesis (30 min) of Ag25(MHA)18 could be
attributed to the delicate design of the synthetic protocol for
NC formation in water. The time-course UV-vis absorption
spectra of the reaction solution indicated that the Ag25(MHA)18
NCs were formed within 3 min, which was evidenced by the
well-developed characteristic absorption peaks at B336, 486,
and 665 nm (Fig. S4a and b, ESI†) and further confirmed by the
ESI-MS analysis (Fig. S4c, ESI†). To the best of our knowledge,
this was the first successful attempt to synthesize water-soluble
Ag25 NCs. Such clusters would be attractive for applications in
aqueous solutions, such as biomedical applications.
By using bulky GSH as the protecting ligand, smaller Ag9–15
NCs were synthesized (Fig. 1c, middle panel). The ESI mass
spectrum of the GSH-protected Ag NCs shows six sets of peaks at
m/z B847 (#1), B951 (#2), B1065 (#3), B1152 (#4), B1196 (#5)
and B1300 (#6), which can be assigned to [Ag9(GSH)5  5H +
2Na]3, [Ag12(GSH)8  7H + 3Na]4, [Ag15(GSH)8  13H + 9Na]4,
[Ag11(GSH)7  9H + 6Na]3, [Ag15(GSH)10  9H + 5Na]4, and
[Ag12(GSH)8  10H + 7Na]3, respectively (see Fig. S5† for detailed
ESI analyses). In comparison, by using the medium-sized
aromatic-thiol p-MBA as a protecting ligand, a much larger
Ag44 NC species was obtained under the same synthetic condi-
tions. The purity and molecular formula of the as-synthesized
p-MBA-protected Ag NCs were determined by their ESI mass
spectrum (Fig. 1c, bottom panel), where only one set of peaks at
m/z B2335 was seen, which was assigned to [Ag44( p-MBA)30]4
(see Fig. S6† for detailed ESI assignment).
Therefore, Ag NCs protected by small MHA, medium-sized
p-MBA, and bulky GSH possess diﬀerent cluster sizes, suggest-
ing the important role of ligand structure in regulating the size
of Ag NCs in water. We hypothesized that small MHA might
have relatively poor capability for protecting the metal core due
to limited steric hindrance aﬀecting its landscape on the NC
surface (Fig. 1a). Therefore, only Ag NCs with good stability
could survive in such a harsh synthetic environment (e.g., high
pH and elevated temperature). It is well-documented that
Ag25(SR)18 has the same crystal structure as Au25(SR)18, which
has been demonstrated to be one of the most stable NC species
in solution.6,10 Therefore, by using small MHA as a protecting
ligand, only Ag25(MHA)18 NCs, which have superior thermo-
dynamic stability, could be preserved in the reaction solution.
In addition, there is a close correlation between the stability of
Ag NCs in solution and the protecting capability of thiolate
ligands. The weak protecting capability of thiolate ligands
would aﬀect the stability of resultant Ag NCs in solution.
To further verify this, we chose two other small alkane-thiol
ligands (i.e., C4-chain 4-mercaptobutyric acid (MBTA) and
C3-chain 3-mercaptopropionic acid (MPA), see Fig. S7 and S8,
ESI†, for their molecular structures) to synthesize Ag NCs in water.
According to the C-chain length, the protecting capabilities of
MBTA, MPA, and MHA for the Ag core should have the following
trend: MHA > MBTA > MPA. Therefore, the stability of Ag NCs
protected by the above three ligands might also follow this trend.
As expected, by using MBTA and MPA as protecting ligands,
Ag25(MBTA)18 and Ag25(MPA)18 were also synthesized, respectively,
which could be clearly seen from their UV-vis absorption
spectra, showing the characteristic absorption features of Ag25
NCs (Fig. S7a and S8a, ESI†). We also obtained good ESI mass
spectra for Ag25(MBTA)18 (Fig. S7b, ESI†), however we failed
to obtain a good one for Ag25(MPA)18 (Fig. S8b, ESI†). This
suggests a poor protecting capability of MPA for Ag NCs, which
would lead to Ag25(MPA)18 being less stable in solution compared
to Ag25(MBTA)18 and Ag25(MHA)18. The less stable Ag25(MPA)18
could be easily fragmented during the ESI-MS measurement. This
observation provides supporting information to our answers to
why small alkane-thiol ligands led to the formation of Ag25 NCs.
In contrast, the bulky GSH led to the formation of smaller
sized Ag9–15 NCs in aqueous solution. This could be ascribed to
the large steric hindrance of GSH. The larger steric hindrance
of GSH provides better protection to the Ag core. In addition,
the larger steric hindrance of GSH would also prefer a higher
surface curvature on the Ag NCs to allow sufficient space for it to
locate on the NC surface. During the NC synthesis, once small Ag
NCs were formed with the protection of GSH, their growth could
be ceased in solution due to the good protection of the bulky
GSH in aqueous solution. Moreover, the good protection of

Besides the bulkiness (or size) of the ligands, the inter-
ligand interaction and solubility of the ligands could also aﬀect
the size of Ag NCs in solution. As described above, if simply
based on the bulkiness analysis of ligands, p-MBA is of a
medium size between small MHA and bulky GSH, which might
generate Ag NCs larger than Ag9–15 but smaller than Ag25.
However, using p-MBA as a protecting ligand we have synthe-
sized large-sized Ag44 NCs, as shown in Fig. 1 (bottom panel),
which could be ascribed to the inter-ligand interaction as well
as the relatively poor solubility of p-MBA in aqueous solution.12
Although p-MBA is bulkier than MHA, the aromatic benzene
ring enables p-MBA to interact inter-molecularly via p–p inter-
action and assemble on the surface of the Ag NCs. Such inter-
ligand interaction makes close packing of p-MBA on the NC
surface possible, thereby the p-MBA ligands do not require a
high surface curvature of the Ag core to deposit on the NC
surface. Furthermore, the relatively poor solubility of the p-MBA
and p-MBA–Ag(I) complexes in aqueous solution might also
affect the protecting capability of p-MBA for the Ag core, and
the actual protecting capability of p-MBA might be even worse
than small MHA for Ag NCs in aqueous solution. Owing to the
harsh synthetic environment, large-sized stable Ag44(p-MBA)30
NCs were formed in aqueous solution.
Another interesting issue is the competitiveness of the three
diﬀerent types of thiolate ligand as ligand pairs in tailoring the
size of bi-thiolate-protected Ag NCs. For example, if small MHA
was mixed with medium-sized p-MBA for the synthesis of
bi-thiolate-protected Ag NCs in aqueous solution, what size
(Ag25, Ag44 or in between) would the Ag NCs obtained be? To
address this issue, we used small MHA to collocate with small
MPA, bulky GSH, and medium-sized p-MBA as ligand pairs
(with diﬀerent feeding ratios) for the synthesis of bi-thiolate-
protected Ag NCs. As shown in Fig. 2, the ESI-MS data show
that as-synthesized MHA_MPA-, MHA_GSH-, and MHA_p-MBA-
protected Ag NCs were all Ag25 NCs in aqueous solution, and
neither Ag9–15 nor Ag44 NC species were seen. This was further
supported by the UV-vis absorption spectra of the as-synthesized Ag
NCs, where a typical absorption feature of Ag25(SR)18 was observed
(Fig. S9, ESI†). While the formation of Ag25(MHA)x(MPA)18x NCs
(here x is the number of MHAs) was expected, the formation of
Ag25(MHA)x(GSH)18x and Ag25(MHA)x(p-MBA)18x NCs was
totally unexpected. The data suggest that the small MHA ligand
possesses poorer protecting capability compared to bulky GSH
and medium-sized p-MBA, but that it dominates the size growth
of bi-thiolate-protected Ag NCs in aqueous solution. The stability
of the Ag25(MHA)x(GSH)18x and Ag25(MHA)x( p-MBA)18x could
be much better than that of the Ag9–15(MHA_GSH)5–9 and
Ag44(MHA_p-MBA)30 NCs in this reaction system, resulting in
bi-thiolate-protected Ag25 NCs.
We further studied the stability of Ag25 NCs with diﬀerent
bi-thiolate proportions on the NC surface in aqueous solution.12a As
shown in Fig. 2a, the NC products varied from Ag25(MHA)17(MPA)1
to Ag25(MHA)10–11(MPA)8–7, when the feeding ratio of MHA-to-MPA
was changed from 4 : 1 to 1 : 1 (see Fig. S10† for detailed ESI
analysis), which suggests that Ag25(MHA)x(MPA)18x NCs with
different ligand proportions might have comparable stability. On
the other hand, the ligand proportions of Ag25(MHA)x(GSH)18x NCs
only changed from Ag25(MHA)16–18(GSH)2–0 to Ag25(MHA)15–18(GSH)3–0
when adjusting the feeding ratio of MHA-to-GSH from 4 : 1 to
1 : 1 (Fig. 2b and Fig. S11, ESI†). Similarly, the ligand proportions
of Ag25(MHA)x( p-MBA)18x changed from Ag25(MHA)18( p-MBA)0
to Ag25(MHA)11–17( p-MBA)7–1 and Ag25(MHA)14–17( p-MBA)4–1
when the feeding ratio of MHA-to-p-MBA was varied from 4 : 1,
to 2 : 1 and 1 : 1 (Fig. 2c and Fig. S12, ESI†).
These data suggest that both Ag25(MHA)x(GSH)18x and
Ag25(MHA)x(p-MBA)18x with diﬀerent ligand proportions show
diﬀerent stability, where the Ag25 NCs protected predominantly
by MHA ligands possess better stability. This finding is diﬀer-
ent from the common understanding that metal NCs protected
by bulky thiolate ligands could have better stability in aqueous
solution. It could be possible that GSH and p-MBA could not
specially interact with MHA to form an eﬀective protection shell
for Ag NCs in solution, where the introduction of GSH and
p-MBA into the MHA shell of the Ag25 NCs might generate some
defective protection sites in the ligand shell, which impairs the
protection from the MHA steric hindrance. Therefore, introducing
less GSH and p-MBA on the surface of MHA-protected Ag25 NCs
could maximally maintain their good stability in solution.

In summary, we have assessed the role of ligand structure in
regulating the size of Ag NCs in aqueous solution by using
small alkane-thiol, medium-sized aromatic-thiol, and bulky
thiol-containing tri-peptide as model thiolate ligands. The
small alkane-thiol ligand led to the formation of ultrastable
Ag25 NCs as its small steric hindrance would provide poor
protection for Ag NCs in water. In contrast, the bulky thiol-
containing tri-peptide, GSH facilitated the formation of small-
sized Ag9–15 NCs, possibly due to the large steric hindrance of
the bulky GSH, which could provide good protection for the Ag
core, and requires high surface curvature of the Ag core (i.e.,
small core size) for ligand inhabitation. By contrast, using the
medium-sized aromatic thiol, p-MBA, as a protecting ligand,
led to the formation of large-sized Ag44 NCs in aqueous
solution, which could be attributed to the strong inter-ligand
interaction (p–p interaction) of p-MBA ligands, rendering close
ligand packing on the NC surface. In addition, the small
alkane-thiol is a determining factor in controlling the size of
bi-thiolate-protected Ag NCs compared to the medium-sized
aromatic-thiol and bulky thiol-containing tri-peptide. More-
over, bi-thiolate-protected Ag25 NCs with less bulky ligands on
the NC surface could maximally maintain their stability in
solution due to the introduction of less defective protection
sites on the ligand shell. This study is interesting not only
because it has revealed the importance of ligand structure on
the synthesis of metal NCs with well-controlled size and com-
position, but also because it exemplifies the use of ligand
structure to engineer the surface chemistry of metal NCs in
aqueous solution for practical applications.

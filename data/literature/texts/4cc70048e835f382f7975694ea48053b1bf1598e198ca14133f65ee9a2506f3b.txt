ABSTRACT: Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), a drug-resistant
Gram-negative pathogen, is listed among the “critical” group of
pathogens by the World Health Organization urgently needing effica-
cious antibiotics in the clinics. Nanomaterials especially silver nano-
particles (AgNPs) due to the broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity are
tested in antimicrobial therapeutic applications. Pathogens rapidly
develop resistance to AgNPs; however, the health threat from
antibiotic-resistant pathogens remains challenging. Here we present a
strategy to prevent bacterial resistance to silver nanomaterials through
imparting chirality to silver nanoclusters (AgNCs). Nonchiral AgNCs
with high efficacy against P. aeruginosa causes heritable resistance, as
indicated by a 5.4-fold increase in the minimum inhibitory concentration
(MIC) after 9 repeated passages. Whole-genome sequencing identifies a
Rhs mutation related to the wall of Gram-negative bacteria that possibly
causes morphology changes in resistance compared to susceptible P. aeruginosa. Nevertheless, AgNCs with laevorotary
chirality (L-AgNCs) induce negligible resistance even after 40 repeated passages and maintain a superior antibacterial
efficiency at the MIC. L-AgNCs also show high cytocompatibility; negligible cytotoxicity to mammalian cells including JB6,
H460, HEK293, and RAW264.7 is observed even at 30-fold MIC. L-AgNCs thus are examined as an alternative to levofloxacin
in vivo, healing wound infections of P. aeruginosa efficaciously. This work provides a potential opportunity to confront the
rising threat of antimicrobial resistance by developing chiral nanoclusters.
KEYWORDS: Antibacterial activity, chiral silver nanoclusters, antimicrobial resistance, cytocompatibility, Pseudomonas aeruginosa
INTRODUCTION
Antimicrobial resistance has been a worldwide crisis, which can
cause the loss of more than 7 million lives annually. In particular,
people living in developing countries are easily infected with
multidrug-resistant microorganisms due to poor health services
and the abuse of antibiotics. Even in the United States, the
number of infections caused by antibiotic-resistant bacteria
climbed from 2 million to 2.8 million within 6 years (i.e., 2013−
2019).1,2 Moreover, the USA alone spends $25 billion/year to
manage chronic wounds that are associated with high rates of
morbidity and mortality.3−5 Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P.
aeruginosa), a drug-resistant Gram-negative pathogen, can
clinically cause acute or chronic infections, being one of the
top-listed pathogens for hospital-acquired infections. Treatment
of P. aeruginosa infection is extremely difficult because of its
rapid mutations and adaptation to gain resistance to antibiotics.
Thus, P. aeruginosa is listed among the “critical” group of
pathogens by the World Health Organization (WHO) urgently
needing efficacious antibiotics in clinics.
Nanomaterials, most notably silver nanoparticles (AgNPs),
due to their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity have been
tested in a number of antimicrobial therapeutic applications.6,7
For the treatment of infections caused by pathogens such as P.
aeruginosa, topical antimicrobials are normally advocated,
wherein the usage of AgNPs in wounds is growing vigorously.8,9
However, pathogens regardless of Gram-negative or Gram-
positive bacteria can develop resistance to AgNPs;10−13 Panacek

in vivo. This work provided alternative therapeutic strategies to
confront the rising threat of antimicrobial resistance by
developing chiral nanoclusters.
RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Characterization of the Prepared Chiral AgNCs. The
chiral AgNCs were prepared through modifying previously
developed protocols (Supporting Information Scheme S1 and
Text S1); NCs were denoted as L-AgNCs, D-AgNCs, or L-/D-
AgNCs when L-, D-, or a mixture of L- and D-cysteine was used as
the reducing agent and also the surface ligand during synthesis.
An average size of 2.7 ± 0.2 nm was observed for L-AgNCs
(Figure 1a); the measured lattice fringe of 0.237 nm
corresponded to a (111) spacing of Ag0 (Figure S1a). Compared
to L-AgNCs, both D-AgNCs and L-/D-AgNCs had nearly the
same shape, diameter (3.0 ± 0.2 and 2.4 ± 0.2 nm), and size
distribution (Figure 1b,c). The lattice spacings of 0.233 and
0.234 nm observed in the high-resolution transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) images of
D-AgNCs and
L-/D-AgNCs
corresponded to the (111) lattice plane of Ag0 (Figure S1b,c).
No surface plasma peak at ∼420 nm was observed for L-AgNCs,
D-AgNCs, and
L-/D-AgNCs (Figure 1d), indicative of the
ultrasmall size of the prepared particles, which is demonstrated
by TEM images. The prepared AgNCs had a negative surface
charge (Figure 1e); the values of L-AgNCs, D-AgNCs, and L-/D-
AgNCs were −62.7 ± 0.9, −60.9 ± 2.7, and −73.6 ± 3.4 mV,
respectively. The high ζ potential values were achieved, avoiding
NCs aggregation, which is consistent with the observation in
TEM images. The chiroptical properties of the prepared AgNCs
were investigated through circular dichroism (CD) spectra. As
expected,
L-AgNCs and
D-AgNCs exhibited corresponding
mirror image CD scans (Figure 1f), in which peaks at 240,
280, and 327 nm were observed, confirming the formation of
chiral AgNCs. In contrast, L-/D-AgNCs showed a negligible
chirality due to the mixture of L- and D-cysteine used during
AgNCs synthesis (Figure 1f). X-ray photoelectron spectra
(XPS) were used to analyze the prepared AgNCs, showing two
strong Ag 3d peaks assigned to Ag0 (Figure 1g), which supports
the result of lattice spacings. The number of silver atoms present
within the chiral AgNCs was determined by using 2,5-dihydroxy
benzoic acid as a matrix in MALDI-TOF MS analyses (Figure
1h,i); both L-AgNCs and D-AgNCs were composed of 22−29
silver atoms. The number of cysteine molecules of chiral AgNCs
was also calculated on the basis of the atomic ratio from XPS
analysis (Table S1); both
L-AgNCs and
D-AgNCs were
composed of 22−29 cysteine molecules as well. Compared to
chiral AgNCs, L-/D-AgNCs had the same chemical formula
(Figure 1j). The silver contents of L-AgNCs, D-AgNCs, and L-/D-
AgNCs were 46.7%, 46.7% and 45.2%, respectively, as
determined by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry
(ICP-MS).
Antibacterial Activity and Mechanism of Chiral AgNCs
against P. aeruginosa In Vitro. As expected, both L-AgNCs
and D-AgNCs had good antibacterial activities (Figure 2a),
showing comparable antibacterial efficacies of Ag(I) ions (i.e.,
AgNO3), which is an effective antibacterial agent in the medical
field. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of L-AgNCs,
D-AgNCs, and Ag(I) ions against P. aeruginosa were 16.6, 12.8,
and 9.4 μmol Ag/L (Figure S2), respectively. L-/D-AgNCs
relative to chiral AgNCs exhibited a comparable bacterial
efficacy in killing P. aeruginosa (Figure 2a); an MIC at 13.8 μmol
Ag/L was observed for L-/D-AgNCs (Figure S2). Compared to
Ag(I) ions, the prepared AgNCs with or without chirality all
showed superior antibacterial activities.
We thus investigated their antibiotic mechanisms. The
permeability of the bacterial membrane was investigated
through SYTO 9/PI staining and MDA leakage determination.
During co-staining with SYTO 9/PI, SYTO 9 can enter all cells
regardless of live or dead mode, emitting green fluorescence,
whereas PI can only enter cells with compromised membranes,
emitting red fluorescence. After treating P. aeruginosa with
AgNCs or Ag(I) ions at 5.45 μmol Ag/L overnight and co-
staining with SYTO 9/PI, fluorescence images exhibited that the
permeability of treated bacteria increased significantly compared
to the control group, especially in Ag(I) ion-treated cells (Figure
S3). Statistical analysis showed that 35.1−38.0% of PI-positive
cells were visualized upon chiral AgNCs treatment in
comparison to 5.3% observed in the control group (Figure
2b). The proportion of PI-positive cells was about 1.5 times
greater than that in cells treated with L-/D-AgNCs (p < 0.05),
implying that the membrane permeability of bacteria treated
with chiral AgNCs was more serious than that with L-/D-AgNCs.
By comparison, a significant increase in the membrane
permeability of P. aeruginosa was caused by Ag(I) ions, wherein
76.1% of PI-positive cells were visualized (Figure 2b). The
release of MDA was highly associated with the change in
permeability of cell membranes; both chiral AgNCs and L-/D-
AgNCs treatments caused a significant increase in MDA content
compared with the control group, even though the highest level
of MDA was measured in the Ag(I) ion-treated cells (Figure 2c).
The live/dead co-staining imaging together with MDA analysis
proved that an exposure of P. aeruginosa to either AgNCs or
Ag(I) ions could lead to membrane damage and bacteria death,
wherein bacteria treated with Ag(I) ions showed the most
serious membrane injury, followed by chiral AgNCs and L-/D-
AgNCs in sequence.
To further explore the cellular membrane damage, scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) was performed to observe the
morphology of the bacteria after treatments. The control group
of P. aeruginosa exhibited intact morphology and smooth
surfaces, yet certain degrees of collapse and damage in the
bacteria were observed after P. aeruginosa treated with L-AgNCs,
D-AgNCs, or L-/D-AgNCs, especially showing the formation of
“pits” in the cell walls (Figure 2d). There were negligible “pits”
for bacteria treated with Ag(I) ions, although shrinkage and
collapse of the surface were observed (Figure 2d). We also used
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to directly visualize
the change of membrane morphology, confirming the
occurrence of major damage, including the release of intra-
cellular contents and the blurring of the cytoplasm membrane
boundaries (Figure S4). Moreover, the occurrence of outer

Enantiomer-Dependent Bacterial Resistance to Chiral
AgNCs. In order to evaluate possible resistance of bacteria to
the prepared AgNCs, an experimental evolution approach was
adopted to investigate responses of P. aeruginosa to successive
exposure of L-AgNCs, D-AgNCs, L-/D-AgNCs, or Ag(I) ions at
their MICs. The survival populations were further subcultured
onto Mueller Hinton (MH) broth agar and then into 10 mL of
fresh MH broth followed by dosing of fresh AgNCs or Ag(I)
ions at MICs again. This approach can eliminate possible
accumulation of Ag in MH broth irrespective of NCs or Ag(I)
ions from the previous culture to the subsequent culture during
successive exposure. More importantly, this process simulated
the evolution of antimicrobial resistance in bacteria clinically.
Strikingly, the survival of P. aeruginosa gradually increased after 5
passages with L-/D-AgNCs, showing negligible antibacterial
efficacies after 9 passages; the survival percentage reached 90.3%
even at the MIC (Figure 3a), implying that the induced bacterial
resistance to

In Vitro Cytocompatibility of Chiral AgNCs. Given the
antimicrobial resistance, L-/D-AgNCs would be eliminated. We
next examined whether chiral AgNCs are cytotoxic to
mammalian cells. Murine epidermal cells JB6 were first used
as the representatives for mammalian cell lines. Intriguingly, L-
AgNCs failed to impact the cell viability even at 87.2 μmol Ag/L
(Figure 4a), wherein the dose of Ag was 5-fold higher than that
of MIC for P. aeruginosa, indicative of a lack of in vitro
cytotoxicity. Nevertheless, the cell viability of JB6 decreased
with an increasing dose of D-AgNCs, wherein about 65% survival
was observed at 87.2 μmol Ag/L (Figure 4a), suggesting in vitro
cytotoxicity of D-AgNCs. Similar results were observed with
H460, HEK293, and RAW264.7 cells; L-AgNCs against D-
AgNCs were lacking in vitro cytotoxicity (Figure S13).
Moreover, negligible impacts on the viability of JB6 cells were
observed even in L-AgNCs at 501.6 μmol Ag/L (Figure 4b).
These findings exhibited the superior cytocompatibility of L-
AgNCs. The distinct effects of cytotoxicity between L-AgNCs
and D-AgNCs might be attributed to the differences in the
uptake of silver (Figure 4c); D-AgNCs’ (537 ng/106 units)
uptake by JB6 cells was almost 2-fold that of L-AgNCs (293 ng/
106 units).
Considering the same size, morphology, surface charge, and
functional group (Figure S14), and chemical formula between L-
AgNCs and
D-AgNCs, we proposed that the different
cytotoxicities might be related to the interactions between the
chiral AgNCs and cell membranes. To substantiate this view, we
used coarse-grained MD simulations to specifically investigate
differences in the process when
L-AgNCs and
D-AgNCs
penetrated the cell membrane (Figure 4d,e). The Coulombic
(Coul) interaction energy between L-AgNCs and phospholipid
molecules was −693.7 kJ/mol (Figure 4f), being comparable to
that between D-AgNCs and phospholipid molecules (−686.3
kJ/mol), which was reasonable due to negligible differences in
the surface charge between L-AgNCs and D-AgNCs (Figure 1e).
Nevertheless, the van der Waals (VDW) interaction energy from
the former (−597.4 kJ/mol) was far greater than that from the
latter (−454.3 kJ/mol), which resulted in a larger negative value
of the total interaction energy between
L-AgNCs and
phospholipid molecules (Figure 4f), indicative of stronger
binding. Hence, L-AgNCs must overcome more energy to
penetrate the cell membrane.
Treatment of P. aeruginosa-Infected Wounds by
Chiral AgNCs In Vivo. To examine the practical applicability,
we evaluated an in vivo efficacy of L-AgNCs for treatment of P.
aeruginosa-infected wounds using a mouse model, wherein PBS
(control), levofloxacin (the traditional therapeutic agent for P.
aeruginosa), and L-AgNCs were applied to wounds (Figure S15),
respectively. Compared to PBS and levofloxacin, L-AgNCs
conferred appreciable benefits including promoted wound
healing irrespective of wound size or wound tissue, reduced
bacterial burdens, and suppressed immune response (Figure 5).
It was clear that L-AgNCs induced the fastest shrinkage in
wound size among the treatments, showing superior perform-
ance in wound healing even relative to levofloxacin (Figure 5a,b
and Figure S16), corresponding to the greatest reduction in
bacterial counts by L-AgNCs (Figure 5c), indicative of efficacy
for wound healing and bacteria elimination. We investigated the
residual silver in the mice; slight residual silver in the liver,
spleen, and lung was observed, whereas the amount of silver
decreased rapidly once the L-AgNCs treatment was ceased
(Figure 5d), indicating the clearance of L-AgNCs from the mice.
To further understand the process of wound healing,
interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis
factor-α (TNF-α), three pro-inflammatory cytokines vital to
inflammatory disease initiation and progression, were deter-
mined as a function of time (Figure 5e−g). After an infection of
P. aeruginosa (i.e., day 0), the serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and
TNF-α were significantly higher than their healthy levels (i.e.,
day −2) (Figure 5e−g), respectively, suggesting acute
inflammatory responses of the mouse to the bacterial infection.
Without medication, the serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α
continued to increase (e.g., day 4), followed by a slight decline
(e.g., day 7) due to the mouse innate immunity, failing to drop
back to healthy levels (Figure 5e−g). Both levofloxacin and L-
AgNCs exhibited curative effect on the infection, wherein the
serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were far lower than those
of PBS treatment at day 7 (Figure 5e−g). However, only L-
AgNCs successfully lowered the serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and
TNF-α close to healthy levels at day 7 (Figure 5e−g), which
demonstrates effective suppression of the inflammatory
responses by L-AgNCs, implying efficacious antibacterial activity
and lack of off-target toxicity. To further recognize the superior
healing role of L-AgNCs in wound from P. aeruginosa infection,
we evaluated wound histology including hematoxylin and eosin
(H&E) and Masson’s trichrome (Figure 5h and Figure S17).
The lowest degree of inflammatory cell infiltration (indicated by
red arrows) in the wound tissue treated with L-AgNCs was
observed by H&E staining at day 7. Moreover, the wound tissue
treated with PBS and levofloxacin exhibited acanthosis
(indicated by yellow arrows) and hyperkeratosis (indicated by
gray arrows), which were not observed in the wound tissue from
the L-AgNCs treatment (Figure 5h). After L-AgNCs treatment,
accordingly, formation of an extensive collagen was observed in
the wound tissue by Masson’s trichrome staining at day 7
(Figure S17), consistent with the accelerated wound healing.
Obviously, L-AgNCs relative to levofloxacin exhibited superior
performance in wound healing, and particularly negligible
resistance was observed even within 40 passages (Figure S18),
providing an alternative to kill P. aeruginosa in clinical trials.
CONCLUSION
In this study, we validated that it was possible to moderate the
functionalities, such as antimicrobial resistance and cytocompat-
ibility, by imparting chirality to AgNCs. Chiral AgNCs, unlike
their nonchiral counterparts (i.e.,
L-/D-AgNCs) causing
resistance within only 5 passages, exhibited the high efficacy in
killing P. aeruginosa and negligible resistance even within 40
passages. It should also be noted that it was possible that
resistance might evolve over longer time scales, which required
additional investigation to uncover these possibilities. In the
absence of cytotoxicity, moreover, L-AgNCs relative to D-AgNCs
could be a potential alternative antibacterial agent against P.
aeruginosa during chronic wound healing. More importantly,
this work provides a potential opportunity to retard resistance in
clinical trials through developing chiral nanomaterials in which
the chiral nanomaterials could interact with the targeted
pathogens with enantioselectivity efficaciously.

EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Preparation of Chiral and Nonchiral AgNCs. The chiral AgNCs
were prepared through modifying previously developed protocols.37,38
A 30 mL aliquot of L-cysteine or D-cysteine at 55 mM was added into a
glass vessel with 30 mL of silver nitrate at 50 mM, followed by magnetic
stirring for 2 h at 37 °C in a water bath. The chiral AgNCs in the
suspension with white color were collected through centrifugation at
9384g for 10 min and washed three times with ultrapure water to
remove residual cysteine. The pellets were freeze-dried under vacuum
overnight to obtain chiral AgNCs. The nonchiral AgNCs were
synthesized by using a mixture of L-cysteine and D-cysteine with
keeping other conditions constant.
Characterization of AgNCs. The morphology and size of the
prepared AgNCs were observed by a high-resolution transmission
electron microscope (HRTEM, JEOL JEM-2100F, Japan) at 200 kV.
The lattice fringe spacing was determined using the HRTEM
instrument at 200 kV. The ultraviolet−visible (UV−vis) spectrum
from 300 to 800 nm was measured through a Shimadzu UV−vis
spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Japan). The ζ potential was determined
by using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano Series dynamic light scattering
instrument. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra were measured on a Jasco
J-810 spectropolarimeter with a 1 mm path length quartz cuvette at
room temperature, wherein the CD spectra were recorded from 240 to
500 nm, and each spectrum was an average of 5 successive scans.
Infrared spectrometry (IR) was conducted on a Thermo Nicolet iS10
(Japan) with a diamond attenuated total reflectance (ATR) attachment
to qualitative estimation of functional groups on the surface of the
prepared AgNCs. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)
spectrum of the prepared AgNCs was obtained through a Kratos Axis
Ultra DLD spectrometer (Kratos, Japan), in which the binding energy
was calibrated with the C 1s peak at 284.6 eV. Positive ion MALDI TOF
mass spectra were acquired using a Bruker Daltonics FlexAnalysis mass
spectrometer, in which 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB) matrix
substance was used for MALDI-MS. Mass spectra were calibrated using
the standard calibration mix2 in the mass range of 0−8000 Da.
Antibacterial Tests for MIC and Resistance. We purchased the
MH broth from Solarbio Life Sciences (Beijing, China), and we
followed the formula to prepare the bacterial culture medium. Each
tube contained a mixture of MH cultivation broth with bacterial strain
(P. aeruginosa, CGMCC 1.1785) at a final concentration of 4 × 104
CFUs/mL and AgNCs or Ag(I) ions at desired silver concentrations
such as 0, 5.45, 10.90, 16.35, 21.80, 32.70, 43.6, and 87.2 μmol/L. The
microdilution method was used to determine the MIC of AgNCs or
Ag(I) ions to represent their antibacterial activity. The culture medium
was used to prepare stock solutions of AgNCs or Ag(I) ions at 174.4
μmol of Ag/L. The MH medium was added into Biosharp tubes,
wherein 0.5 mL of stock solution and 0.5 mL of culture medium with
bacteria were mixed thoroughly. According to the above method, we
serially diluted silver solutions using a 1:1 dilution every time to obtain
the desired silver levels and bacterial density. The final volume of the
solution was 1.0 mL per tube. All tubes were placed in an incubator
(Stab Max, RADOBIO, Shanghai) with shaking at 200 rpm for
overnight at 37 °C. The survival of bacteria in each tube would be
evaluated through the measurement at 600 nm on a spectrophotometer
(TECAN). Thus, the MIC of AgNCs or Ag(I) ions can be calculated
through SigmaPlot software. For the resistance experiments, 1 μL of
bacterial suspensions from each tube after the exposure at MIC were
inoculated and spread on MH agar plates, in which the survival
populations were further subcultured onto MH broth agar. After
incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, the numbers of bacteria within inoculum
were determined semiquantitatively, and then bacteria were transferred
into 10 mL of fresh MH broth followed by dosing of fresh AgNCs or
Ag(I) ions at MICs again. All tubes were placed in an incubator (Stab
Max, RADOBIO, Shanghai) with shaking at 200 rpm for overnight at 37
°C. The survival of bacteria in each tube would be evaluated through the
measurement at 600 nm on a spectrophotometer (TECAN).
Antibacterial Mechanisms of AgNCs. In this study, commercial
assay kits and fluorescent reagents were used to examine possible
changes in the membrane permeability, membrane potential, and ROS
levels, wherein SYTO 9/PI dye, diSC3(5) dye, and ROS test kit were
applied to bacteria, respectively. Bacteria were cultured to a logarithmic
growth phase, and then each tube contained a mixture of MH
cultivation broth with P. aeruginosa at a final concentration of 4 × 104
CFUs/mL and AgNCs or Ag(I) ions at desired levels. SYTO 9/PI dye
(MX4234, Maokangbio), diSC3(5) dye (MycoLight Ratiometric
Bacterial Membrane Potential Kit, AAT Bioquest), and ROS
(HR9066, Baiaolaibo Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China and
E004-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bio, China) kits were applied to bacteria
after an exposure of AgNC or Ag(I) ions according to the
manufacturer’s instructions.39 During analysis after the co-staining
with SYTO 9/PI, in object-based colocalization analysis by JACoP
(ImageJ), the number of green objects (all cells) was counted, and the
number of these centers that colocalize with the center of a red object
(dead cells) was counted as well. For the analysis of membrane
potential via diSC3(5), Attune NxT acoustic focusing cytometer
(Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher) was used to record the fluorescence (ex,
488 nm; em, 510−530 nm) of diSC3(5) in each tube.
In order to visualize the membrane morphology of bacteria, SEM and
TEM analyses of bacteria were performed in this study. Bacterial
suspensions after exposures were centrifugated at 4000 rpm for 10 min.
The resulting pellet was then dispersed in PBS to wash bacteria,
followed by fixation with glutaraldehyde (2.5%) at 4 °C overnight. For
SEM sampling, dehydration was conducted successively with a series of
graded ethanol solutions (30, 50, 70, 80, 90, 95, and 100%) via
centrifugation. The resulting sample was finally dispersed in 100 μL of
ethanol (100%), and then 10 μL was dropped on a copper tape and
dried overnight. Afterward, the sample was sputtered with platinum for
120 s and imaged under SEM (Hitachi Reguius8100). For TEM
sampling, bacterial samples were incubated in 1% aqueous osmium for
2 h at room temperature, followed by washing with 0.1 M phosphate
buffer solution (0.1 M, pH 7.4) three times. Then, dehydration was
conducted successively with a series of graded acetone solutions (30,
50, 70, 80, 95, and 100%) via centrifugation. Samples were infiltrated
with Spurr’s epoxy resin with increasing ratios, and embedded in Spurr’s
resin at 60 °C prior to sectioning. Ultrathin sections (70−90 nm) were
cut with a diamond knife and retrieved onto 200 mesh thin bar copper
grids. All TEM images were captured on a Hitachi-7800 transmission
electron microscope.
Cytotoxicity of Chiral AgNCs. Cytotoxicity of L-AgNCs and D-
AgNCs was evaluated by using mammalian cells including JB6, H460,
HEK293, and RAW264.7 as models, wherein cells were grown in
medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% (w/v)
penicillin−streptomycin, and 1% (w/v) sodium pyruvate (Sigma-
Aldrich). A CCK-8 assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) was used to
determine the cytotoxicity of the chiral AgNCs. In general, cells were
inoculated into a 96-well plate and incubated in an incubator (Heracell
VIOS 160i, Thermo Scientific) at 37 °C with 5% CO2 overnight. Chiral
AgNCs at the desired concentrations were added into each well,
followed by an incubation of another 24 h in the incubator. Then, the
CCK-8 kit was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The
absorbance was measured at 450 nm through the reader (TECAN) to
show the cell viability under different conditions.
Determination of Silver Concentrations through ICP-MS. In
this study, ICP-MS analysis was performed with an Agilent 8800, in
which each sample was transferred to a clean Teflon vessel for
microwave acid digestion before the ICP-MS determination. It should
be noted that the mixture of acid was dependent on the sample type;
digestion of bacterial samples was carried out with a mixture of
concentrated HNO3 (65%, trace metal grade, Merck) and HCl (35%,
trace metal grade, Merck) in a ratio of 1:3, whereas digestion of organ
samples from mice need concentrated H2O2 (30%, Fisher Scientific)
added to the mixture of acid due to the presence of fat. The microwave
digestion was conducted at 200 °C for 90 min in a microwave digestion
system (Multiwave 5000, Anton Paar). Then all samples were cooled to
room temperature and diluted to a final volume of 20 mL by adding 5%
HNO3 solution, followed by filtration (0.45 μm) and determination.
MD Simulations. All MD simulations were based on a general
AMBER force field with the RESP charges and were carried out by using
the Gromacs-4.6.7 software package. The system was a relaxed liquid

configuration at 298 K. The total run time was 100 ns NPT for the
equilibrium MD simulation. We used the relaxed system as the starting
configuration. Since it was prior to system relaxation MD, energy
minimization was carried out with a composite protocol of steepest
descent using termination gradients of 100 kJ/(mol·nm). The Nose−
Hoover thermostat was used to maintain the equilibrium temperature
at 298 K, and periodic boundary conditions were imposed on all three
dimensions. The particle mesh-Ewald method was applied to compute

value was used for van der Waals interactions. The LINCS algorithm
was applied to constrain the bond lengths of hydrogen atoms. A
leapfrog algorithm was used with a time step of 2 fs.
Antibacterial Efficacy of L-AgNCs In Vivo. All animal procedures
were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and
approved by the Research Center for Eco-Environmental Sciences,
Chinese Academy of Sciences, Institutional Animal Care and Use
Committee (AEWC-RCEES-2023011). In this study, 8 week old male
mice (C57BL/6 from Zhejiang University Center for Veterinary
Sciences) were randomly assigned to 3 groups; wound healing with PBS
buffer was considered as the control group, wound healing with
levofloxacin was considered as the standard clinical treatment protocol,
and wound healing with L-AgNCs was considered as an alternative to
levofloxacin.
An artificial wound about 5−6 mm diameter was made on the
dorsum of each mouse with a scissor, followed by an inoculation with 30
μL of P. aeruginosa (5 × 109 CFUs/mL) two times at the beginning of
the experiment. For wound treatment, 30 μL of PBS buffer or L-AgNCs
(0.2 mg/mL) was applied to the wound and levofloxacin (1 mg/mL)
was taken orally according to the clinical treatment protocol. Each
mouse needed the healing 4 times in total (1 time/1 day). To evaluate
the therapeutic efficacy, each wound was photographed at regular time
intervals, and wound size was measured through ImageJ. Blood samples
(100 μL) were collected from each mouse at the desired intervals (e.g.,
−2, 0, 4, and 7 days) for blood routine test. For analysis of bacterial
CFU, mice were sacrificed and wound tissues were harvested, weighed,
chopped, and homogenized in PBS buffer, followed by centrifugation at
3000g for 6 min at 4 °C to pellet debris. Then, 100 μL of supernatant
was plated onto MH agar for CFU counting. Organs including heart,
liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain were harvested from each mouse at
the desired intervals for ICP-MS measurements. For histomorpho-
logical analysis including H&E staining and Masson’s trichrome
staining, wound tissues were harvested and fixed with 4%
paraformaldehyde solution and washed with 75% ethanol, in which
the images were measured by using ImageJ.
Statistical Analysis. Data were presented as the average value ±
standard deviation. The in vitro experiments were performed in three
independent experiments with at least five repetitions for each
condition. The mouse in vivo experiments were performed with 12
rats for each group. Statistical analysis of the samples was performed
using student’s t-test or ANOVA, and the p value below 0.05 was
considered to be significantly different.

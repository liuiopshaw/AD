Infections caused by pathogenic bacteria such as Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus and Gram-
negative Pseudomonas aeruginosa remain a signiﬁcant healthcare challenge. In this study, we devel-
oped an enzyme-responsive antibacterial nanomaterial that delivers antibacterial agents only in the
presence of bacterial lipase. This was achieved by ﬁrst synthesizing highly potent ultra-small silver
nanoparticles with an average core size of 1.6 ± 0.2 nm and then embedding them into polycaprolactone
nanoparticles (pAgNCs@PCL) via the water in oil in water approach. The pAgNCs@PCL nanoparticles had
an average diameter of 274.1 ± 60.1 nm and a silver nanoparticle encapsulation efﬁciency of 15.7 ± 1.3%.
We demonstrated that silver released from the pAgNCs@PCL was selectively triggered by lipase-
expressing strains of P. aeruginosa and S. aureus. As expected, there was no antibacterial action
observed against E. coli DH5a, which does not express lipase. The pAgNCs@PCL nanoparticles exhibited
minimal cytotoxicity against human ﬁbroblast cells suggesting good biocompatibility. This study resulted
in a highly potent on-demand delivery system that can be potentially incorporated into healthcare
products and devices to prevent and/or treat infections.

1. Introduction
Despite the
availability of
antibiotics,
bacterial
infections
continue to be a major health problem causing signiﬁcant mortal-
ity,
morbidity
and
disability-adjusted
life
worldwide
[1e3].
Wounds and implantable medical devices present ideal environ-
ments for the invasion and proliferation of pathogenic bacteria. The
World Health Organization has stated that healthcare-associated
infections are a global phenomenon responsible for signiﬁcant
morbidity and mortality worldwide [4].
As infection is established, bacteria secrete a range of extracel-
lular enzymes which helps their growth, activity, invasion of the
host immune system and many other functions [5e7]. An example
of such an enzyme is lipase, which hydrolyses long-chain fatty
acids. Lipases are expressed by both Gram-positive and Gram-
negative bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) [8,9]
and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) [10,11] respectively,
which are the two most prevalent pathogenic bacteria species that
cause many serious infections. Examples of such infections are
prosthetic joint infections, skin infections, cystic ﬁbrosis, surgical
site infections, chronic rhinosinusitis, pneumonia, urinary tract
infections and endovascular infections [12e14]. Importantly, some
strains of S. aureus and P. aeruginosa have intrinsic and/or acquired
resistance to antibiotics, making clinical management of infections
caused by these pathogens a major challenge.
Bacterial resistance to antibiotics created the need for new
antibacterial
materials
and
compounds.
Silver
nanoparticles
(AgNPs) have taken a signiﬁcant place in the medical, commercial
and scientiﬁc space owing to their high antibacterial potency and
have been incorporated in many materials and coatings [15e17].
The effectiveness of AgNPs is often attributed to their multiple
antibacterial mechanisms [18]. AgNPs have a large surface area and
can attach to the bacterial cell membrane, resulting in membrane

bind to bacterial DNA, inhibiting replication to enzymes e blocking
metabolism and to cell wall components, disrupting cellular
integrity [19]. This multifaceted mechanism of action ensures the
potent activity of nanosilver against both Gram-positive and Gram-
negative bacteria [20,21]. In addition, targeting multiple sites in the
bacterial cell makes the development of resistance to silver more
difﬁcult [22,23].
A concern that has been often raised when antibacterial silver
products are used in medicine is the danger of toxicity to tissues
and organs [24,25]. One way to address this problem is to carefully
control the rate of release of silver in a manner that ensures anti-
bacterial action while preventing toxicity to mammalian cells [26].
Another way is to embed silver nanomaterials in delivery systems
that respond to external stimuli [27] such as pH [28,29], light
[30,31], temperature [32e34], redox conditions [35,36] and en-
zymes [37,38].
In this work, we aimed to design an enzyme-responsive delivery
system that is triggered by endogenous lipase expressed by path-
ogenic bacteria. Such a system is expected to release its antibac-
terial load only when pathogenic bacteria are present while
remaining passive if a wound is not infected, in this way reducing
the possibility for toxicity. Polycaprolactone (PCL) is a biodegrad-
able and biocompatible polymer that is approved by the Food and
Drug Administration (FDA) for medical use. The polymer has a slow
degradation rate in a physiological environment due to its highly
hydrophobic nature [39]. However, the material is highly sensitive
to degradation by lipase, which occurs via hydrolysis of its ester
bonds [40,41]. This property, and the fact that PCL is considered safe
by regulatory bodies, makes the polymer an ideal candidate for use
in lipase-responsive delivery platforms [42,43]. The advantage of
using enzymes as stimuli is their speciﬁcity and selectivity towards
a substrate of interest [44]. As the antibacterial agent, we chose
polycationic silver nanoclusters (pAgNCs) that we have recently
shown to have very potent antibacterial activity and the capacity to
eliminate established and mature bioﬁlms [22]. These pAgNCs,
referred to as polycationic nanoclusters (NCs), were embedded in
an enzyme-triggered PCL platform (pAgNCs@PCL). The respon-
siveness, antibacterial activity and cytotoxicity of the pAgNCs@PCL
nanocomposites were thoroughly assessed.

2.2. Synthesis of polycationic silver nanoclusters (pAgNCs)
The synthesis of pAgNCs was carried out according to a previ-
ously published protocol [22]. Brieﬂy, chitosan solution (5 mg/mL)
was prepared with acetic acid (1%, v/v) and 10 mL of the solution
was added to 39 mL of MQ water stirring at 500 rpm at room
temperature (RT). This was followed by the addition of 1 mL of
100 mM AgNO3 under continuous stirring. After 3 min, the pH of
the solution was adjusted to 6.5 by the drop-wise addition of 1 M
NaOH and left to stir at 500 rpm for 24 h at RT. The slow progress of
colour change from cloudy colourless to brown conﬁrmed the for-
mation of pAgNCs. The synthesized pAgNCs dispersion was puriﬁed
by dialysis (MWCO 3.5 kDa) against MQ water before lyophilization
using a ModulyoD freeze dryer (ThermoFisher Scientiﬁc, MA, USA)
for 72 h and stored at 4 C for later use.
2.3. Preparation of PCL nanoparticles and pAgNCs@PCL
nanocomposites
Blank PCL nanoparticles (PCL NPs) and pAgNCs-loaded PCL
nanocomposites (pAgNCs@PCL) were prepared using the water in
oil in water (W1/O/W2) method. To synthesize the pAgNCs@PCL
nanocomposites, 200 mg of PCL was dissolved in 30 mL of DCM to
form the oil phase (O). The ﬁrst aqueous phase (W1) was formed by
probe sonicating 105 mg of lyophilized pAgNCs in 15 mL MQ water.
To produce the W1/O phase, O was added dropwise to W1 and
probe sonicated using a Branson 450 Soniﬁer for 1 min on ice. This
was closely followed by the drop-wise addition of the W1/O phase
to 60 mL of 2.0% PVA stirring at 500 rpm to produce the W1/O/W2
phase after probe sonication for a further 1 min on ice. The W1/O/
W2 emulsion was then diluted with 200 mL of 0.3% PVA and
allowed to stir overnight for complete evaporation of the solvent.
PCL NPs were synthesized using a similar protocol without pAgNCs
in the water phase. All nanoparticles were collected by ultra-
centrifugation at 23,200 g for 25 min at 4 C using a Thermo-
Scientiﬁc™Sorvall™RC 6 Plus Centrifuge (ThermoFisher Scientiﬁc,
MA, USA) and washed three times with MQ water. The nano-
particles were then lyophilized using a ModulyoD freeze dryer
(ThermoFisher Scientiﬁc, MA, USA) for 72 h and stored at 4 C until
needed.

2.7. In vitro release study
2.7.1. In vitro release study in the absence and presence of lipase in
PBS
The release proﬁle of Ag ions from pAgNCs@PCL was determined
using the centrifugation method [45]. In the absence of lipase, 2 mg
of the sample was placed in a centrifuge tube and 3 mL of PBS, pH 7.4
was added. The tube was placed on an orbiter shaker (Ratek, Vic.
Australia) at 100 rpm in a temperature-controlled room of 37 C. At
speciﬁc sampling points, the sample was centrifuged at 3760 g for
5 min and 0.5 mL of the supernatant was taken for analysis. An equal
volume of 0.5 mL fresh PBS was replaced for every withdrawal at
each sampling point. Each withdrawn supernatant was digested
overnight with 1 mL of aqua regia. After overnight digestion, 8.5 mL
of MQ water was added to make 10 mL and was centrifuged at 3760 g
for 15 min. The supernatant was analysed using Agilent Technologies
8900 ICP-MS Triple Quad to determine the concentration of Ag ions.
The cumulative percentage of Ag ions released was obtained using
the following equation (3) [46].

2.7.2. In vitro release of pAgNCs@PCL in presence of human
ﬁbroblast (HFFF2) cells
In vitro of Ag release from pAgNCs@PCL was performed in the
presence of human ﬁbroblast cells (HFFF2; CellBank Australia, NSW,
Australia) to determine the effect of released Ag ions on ﬁbroblast
viability. In performing the release study using HFFF2 cells, (Dul-
becco's Modiﬁed Eagle Medium DMEM (Gibco, ThermoFisher Sci-
entiﬁc, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS,
ThermoFisher Scientiﬁc, MA, USA) and 1% (v/v) penicillin/strepto-
mycin (ThermoFisher Scientiﬁc, MA, USA) was used. Fibroblasts
were seeded on 6 well plates at a density of 3  106 cells/well and
3 mL of release medium was added to each well. Brieﬂy, 2 mg of
lyophilized pAgNCs@PCL was added to 3 wells. Untreated cell wells
were used as the control, and the plate was incubated at 37 C. At
different time intervals (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, and 24 h), 0.5 mL of the
supernatant was removed, centrifuged at 3760 g for 5 min and
replaced with 0.5 mL fresh medium.
After 24 h, the cells were incubated with a solution of DAPI
(Invitrogen, Life Technologies, MA, USA) and phalloidin (Invitrogen,
Life Technologies, MA, USA) in PBS for 30 min at RT before washing
off with PBS to remove excess dyes. The HFFF2 cells were imaged
using the confocal microscope (Olympus IX83 Tokyo, Japan) at an
excitation wavelength of 365 nm (emission 461 nm) and 489 nm
(emission 518 nm) respectively. Each sample withdrawn during the
release study was digested overnight with 1 mL of aqua regia. After
overnight digestion, 8.5 mL of MQ water was added to make 10 mL,
and each sample was centrifuged at 3760 g for 15 min. The obtained
supernatant was analysed using Agilent Technologies 8900 ICP-MS
Triple Quad to determine the concentration of Ag ions. The cu-
mulative percentage of silver ions released was obtained using
Equation (3) [46].

2.8.2. Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The MIC and MBC of pAgNCs and pAgNCs@PCL were determined
using Gram-positive S. aureus and Gram-negative P. aeruginosa
because of their clinical relevance and tendency to express lipase
which is capable of degrading PCL [47]. The MIC and MBC of
pAgNCs were also determined against E. coli DH5a as a control due
to its low lipase-secreting characteristic [48,49]. The MIC of each
sample was determined using the broth dilution method [50]. Each
sample was dispersed in MQ water and different concentration was
achieved by serial dilution for all wells. In determining the MIC of
pAgNCs@PCL, the different equivalent concentrations of Ag ion
content as determined by ICP-MS were also obtained by serial
dilution. Brieﬂy, 100 mL of TSB was aliquoted into sterile 96 well
plates for each S. aureus, P. aeruginosa and E. coli. In triplicate. This
was followed by the addition of an equal volume of sample to the
ﬁrst well, followed by serial dilutions (1:2). Each well was then
inoculated with 20 mL of bacteria at a concentration of 1  106 CFU/
mL. The plates were incubated at 37 C for 18 h. Next, the optical
density was measured at 630 nm using a Biotek microplate spec-
trophotometer (BioTek, Synergy HT, VT, USA).
2.8.3. Agar well diffusion assay
The antibacterial activity of pAgNCs and pAgNCs@PCL was
determined using a disk diffusion assay. Bacterial suspensions of
P. aeruginosa and S. aureus with a cell density of 0.5 McFarland units
at a volume of 100 mL were evenly spread on agar plates and left to
dry. This was followed by the addition of each sample equivalent
to 131 mg of Ag to wells created in the agar plates. The PCL NPs were
used as control. Negative controls were also performed on E. coli
(DH5a) agar plates using pAgNCs, pAgNCs@PCL and PCL NPs. Agar
plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 C and the zones of inhibition
diameters were measured.
2.9. In vitro cytotoxicity study
In vitro cytotoxicity was performed using HFFF2 cells, since they
are recognized as mediators of inﬂammation and involved with
wound healing [51,52]. The growth medium used for the study was
DMEM (ThermoFisher, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal
bovine serum (FBS, ThermoFisher, MA, USA) and 1% (v/v) penicillin/
streptomycin (Life Technologies, CA, USA). Fibroblasts were seeded
on 96-well plates at a density of 5  104 cells/well. After 24 h, the
medium was replaced with fresh medium containing either
pAgNCs, PCL NPs or pAgNCs@PCL nanocomposites. The study was
based on the concentrations of MICs obtained during the antibac-
terial study of bacteria that could degrade PCL (P. aeruginosa and
S. aureus). The untreated wells without samples were maintained
as a positive control for viability. The ﬁbroblasts were incubated for
another 24 h followed by the addition of 10% Resazurin in DMEM
media. The ﬂuorescence intensity was measured using excitation at
560 nm and emission at 590 nm after 4 h using a microplate reader
(FLUOstar Optima, BMG Labtech).
2.10. Statistical analysis
Statistical
analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism
version 8.3.0 (538) for windows (San Diego CA, USA). To analyze
ﬁbroblast viability data, an ordinary one-way ANOVA corrected for
multiple comparisons using the Holm-Sidak method was used.
ImageJ software version 1.52a for windows (National Institute of
Health, USA) was used for statistical distribution analysis of particle
size. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and signiﬁcance
was set at p < 0.05.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Synthesis and physicochemical characterization of pAgNCs
Chitosan was utilized as the reducing and capping agent in the
synthesis of the pAgNCs. The colour change of the reaction mixture
from clear to light brown indicated the formation of pAgNCs
(Scheme S1). The UVeVis spectrum of the solution of pAgNCs
showed two distinct bands, at ~384 and ~486 nm (Fig. 1A), which
are consistent with the formation of ultra-small AgNCs. The zeta
potential of the puriﬁed particles, via dialysis in ultrapure water,
was found to be þ37.8 ± 4 mV (Fig.1B). The positive charge is due to
the protonation of the amine groups present in the molecule of
chitosan, which also ensures the high stability of the colloidal so-
lution. The size and morphology of pAgNCs were conﬁrmed via
transmission electron microscopy. The particles had a narrow size
distribution, spherical morphology, and an average core size of
1.6 ± 0.2 nm (Fig. 1C and D).
The synthesis of pAgNCs@PCL was performed using the W1/O/
W2 method, which is suitable for encapsulating hydrophilic drugs
in hydrophobic polymers [53]. The pAgNCs were lyophilized and
then redispersed by probe sonication before use to achieve the
maximum possible loading in the pAgNCs@PCL nanocomposite.
The size distribution and zeta potential analysis of the synthesized
pAgNCs@PCL nanocomposites and control PCL NPs without pAgNCs
are shown in Fig. 2A and B, respectively.

3.4. TGA
TGA was carried out to determine the thermal stability and
fraction of different components in each material. The TGA analysis
of chitosan, pAgNCs, pAgNCs@PCL and PCL NPs showing weight
loss as temperature increases up to 1000 C are presented in Fig. 4B.
The TGA spectrum of chitosan shows a ﬁrst weight loss at 110 C
associated with about mass 9% loss, which can be attributed to the
loss of water absorbed in the polymer [57]. A second weight loss of
up to 42% occurs at 352 C, which can be attributed to the depo-
lymerization of the acetylated and deacetylated units of chitosan,
and the dehydration of the saccharide ring [58]. The ﬁnal degra-
dation event shows that chitosan was degraded completely at
about 700 C.
The thermal degradation and weight loss of 12% at 62 C for the
pAgNCs, can be attributed to the loss of water absorbed by the
sample. The second weight-loss event could be attributed to the
thermal degradation of chitosan used for synthesizing pAgNCs [59].
Further, a steady weight loss occurs until 1000 C which is attrib-
uted to the thermal degradation of other residual compounds
present in chitosan and AgNCs. The weight loss observed at 1000 C
was 64% for pAgNCs [60]. The onset of degradation for PCL NPs was
found to be 203 C. The PCL NPs degraded completely at about
528 C, decomposing the molecular chains to CO, CO2 and H2O [61].
The onset degradation temperature of pAgNCs@PCL occurred at
211 C, a slight increase relative to PCL NPs, which can be attributed
to the presence of pAgNCs. The latter corresponds to a small frac-
tion of the material with thermal stability beyond 1000 C (Fig. S2)
[62].
3.5. In vitro release in the presence and absence of lipase
The in vitro release of Ag ions from pAgNCs@PCL was performed
in the presence and absence of lipase enzyme. The process was
carried out at pH 7.4 in PBS and at 37 C to mimic the physiological
conditions. The results are presented in Fig. 5A. Lipase degrades PCL
by hydrolysing the ester bonds in the polymer chain [63]. In the
presence of the enzymes, pAgNCs@PCL showed a rapid release of
Ag ions (37% after 4 h) and a gradual and continual release of
approximately 65% after 72 h. This process is kick-started by the
adsorption of enzymes to the surface of the nanoparticles, followed
by enzymatic hydrolysis [64]. In comparison, in the absence of
lipase, pAgNCs@PCL showed a signiﬁcantly lower release of only
10% within 72 h. The results conﬁrm our hypothesis and expecta-
tions. The PCL nanoparticles degrade slowly in an aqueous medium
due to the hydrophobicity and crystallinity of the polymer [65]. The
rapid release measured in the presence of lipase can be attributed
to the accelerated degradation of PCL. The morphology of the
pAgNCs@PCL nanocomposites after exposure to PBS, in the absence
and presence of enzymes, is shown in Fig. 5A and B, respectively. In
the absence of enzymes, the morphology of the nanoparticles is not
visibly distorted (Fig. 5A). However, after 72 h in the presence of
enzymes, the nanoparticles are completely degraded (Fig. 5B)
which results in the release of their load, consistent with the results
presented in Fig. 5C [66]. These ﬁndings concur with published
studies reporting that PCL is degraded by lipase secreted by path-
ogenic bacteria such as P. aeruginosa [67]. The results so far conﬁrm
that we have generated an enzyme-responsive nanoparticle system
that can be further examined in bacterial cultures [38].
The release of silver ions from the pAgNCs@PCL in a culture of
human foetal ﬁbroblast cells was also investigated in DMEM
(Fig. 5D). Over 24 h, only 12% of pAgNCs were released with most of
the silver released in the ﬁrst 6 h. The amount of released pAgNCs
and kinetic of release is like that observed in PBS and shown in
Fig. 5C. This is expected as the ﬁbroblasts are not expressing lipase
which could degrade the pAgNCs@PCL nanocomposites.

3.7. Cytotoxicity of pAgNCs@PCL on human dermal ﬁbroblasts
In the context of medical applications, it is important to
demonstrate that the biomaterial is not cytotoxic to mammalian
cells and tissue and is sufﬁciently biocompatible. Cell viability was
also studied using the well-established resazurin assay. Fibroblasts
treated with pAgNCs and pAgNCs@PCL at a concentration equal to
MIC (or 6.8 mg/mL), and for pAgNCs@PCL at 2xMIC, were compared
with cells cultured on a tissue culture plate (TCP) without any
treatment. The results are shown in Fig. 7 B. When cells were
treated with pAgNCs at MIC (6.8 mg/mL), cell viability decreased to
70.8 ± 11.4% (p < 0.01) relative to TCP control (Table S1). There was
no signiﬁcant difference between ﬁbroblast viability treated with
pAgNCs@PCL nanocomposite at MIC and 2xMIC, both being
approximately 90%, which suggests excellent biocompatibility.
Fluorescence microscopy images of the cells cultured for 24h with
and without pAgNCs@PCL are shown in Fig. 7A. The images reveal
that there is no difference in cellular morphology between the
control and cell treated with pAgNCs@PCL, indicating good
biocompatibility. A biomaterial is commonly considered to be
biocompatible if the cell viability is 70% and above [68,69].

4. Conclusion
An enzyme-responsive polymeric material was fabricated using
pAgNCs as antibacterial agents and polycaprolactone as the carrier
sensitive to degradation by lipase for the on-demand release of the
antibacterial agents. We demonstrated the successful synthesis of
PCL nanocomposites loaded with pAgNCs. The release of pAgNCs, in
concentrations sufﬁcient to kill bacteria, was rapidly triggered by the
presence of bacterial lipase. This enzyme-responsive behaviour was
conﬁrmed in the culture of bacteria. The pAgNCs loaded nano-
composites showed high antibacterial activity in the presence of
lipase-secreting pathogens such as S. aureus and P. aeruginosa but no
activity was observed against E. coli DH5a which does not express
these enzymes. The on-demand release characteristics of the nano-
composites also contributed to the reduction of the cytotoxicity
pAgNCs, which is beneﬁcial for medical applications. Considering the
tremendous abundance of lipase at infection sites, the responsive
nanocomposites designed in this study can be used to selectively
deliver the antibacterial agent for the treatment of various infections
caused by lipase-secreting bacteria in the biomedical ﬁeld. The
enzyme-responsive nanocomposites reported in this work could be
an attractive candidate for application on/with medical devices for
treating and preventing bacterial infections.

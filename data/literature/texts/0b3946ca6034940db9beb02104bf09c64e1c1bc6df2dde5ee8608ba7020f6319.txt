Direct Z-Scheme Xylan-Based Carbon Dots@TiO2-x
Nanocomposites for Visible Light Driven Photocatalytic of
Dye Degradation and Antibacterial
Shurui Cong, Jihai Cai, Xiaoyun Li, Junqin You, Lei Wang,* and Xiaoying Wang*
TiO2 photocatalysis has gained attention as a cost-eﬀective way of degrading
contaminants. However, it relies on UV excitation and experiences rapid
carrier recombination. To address these drawbacks, novel xylan-based carbon
dots@hydrogenated TiO2 (CDs@TiO2-x) composited by direct Z-scheme
heterojunction is fabricated as a visible light-driven photocatalytic material,
which broadens the range of light absorption to visible light and suppresses
the recombination of photogenerated carriers. The spindle-shaped
CDs@TiO2-x has C/N/O/S groups, positive charges, and oxygen vacancies on
its surface, and can degrade 89% of methylene blue and 87% of methyl
orange within 90 min, and 99% of rhodamine B within 60 min in visible light.
It also eradicates 99.9% of E. coli in 2 h and 97.1% of S. aureus in 2.5 h. These
performances surpass CDs and TiO2-x, and exhibit advantages in similar
composite materials. The degradation pathways of dyes and the speciﬁc
antibacterial processes induced by ROS are thoroughly investigated through
LC-MS testing and related enzyme activity assays. DFT calculations study the
role of oxygen vacancies, heterojunction mechanism, and catalytic sites. This
study has developed a novel nanocomposite with applications in both dye
degradation and sterilization, oﬀering insights for multifunctional materials
based on photocatalysis and heterojunction.

Amidst rapid industrial and agricultural advancements, the grav-
ity of water pollution has reached alarming heights, particularly
regarding the presence of wastewater containing diverse organic
pollutants (such as methylene blue (MB), methyl orange (MO),
rhodamine B (RhB), phenol, etc.) and even hazardous bacte-
ria, signiﬁcantly endangering human health.[1] Compared with
traditional wastewater puriﬁcation methods, photocatalysis only

Extensive researches have been undertaken to enhance the
photocatalytic performance of TiO2, including element doping,[5]
precious metal deposition,[6] heterojunction structures [7] and hy-
drogenated TiO2,[8] et al. Element doping usually requires com-
plex modiﬁcation of the precursor solution, and the degree of
doping is diﬃcult to control. Precious metal deposition is expen-
sive, and the cumulative toxicity of metals can cause composites
to lose the low toxicity advantage of TiO2 itself. The method of
constructing heterojunctions is highly favored due to its diverse
selection of ligands, which can design diﬀerent composite ma-
terials according to the required situation. As a novel multifunc-
tional carbon nanomaterial, carbon dots (CDs) have full potential
to form composite materials with excellent photocatalytic perfor-
mance with TiO2. The electron-accepting and -transporting prop-
erties of CDs can help TiO2 to form heterojunction and promote
the separation of its photogenerated carriers,[9] and the remark-
able light absorption and conversion abilities of CDs allow for an
expanded absorption range of TiO2.[10] Moreover, by modifying
the surface groups and surface charge of CDs, the composites
can exhibit enhanced binding aﬃnity toward bacteria, leading to
a better antibacterial eﬀect.

fundamentally solve the problem of the diﬃculty in utilizing vis-

pivotal role of surface charge in both degradation and antibacte-
rial processes is often overlooked. Prevailing research on analo-
gous materials primarily focuses on dye degradation, with scant
attention directed toward their antibacterial properties. Given the
intricate challenges posed by real water environments, the imper-
ative to develop materials adept at concurrently addressing these
dual concerns is evident.

2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Characterization of CDs, TiO2-x and CDs@TiO2-x
TEM images of the CDs prepared by solvent-free method are
shown in Figure 1A. Prepared CDs had a distinct graphite lat-
tice structure, characterized by a lattice stripe spacing of 0.21 nm,
corresponding to the (100) diﬀractive plane of sp2 graphitic
carbon.[14] The particle size of CDs (Figure 1B) ranged from 4.3
to 13.2 nm with an average particle size of 8.2 nm. Figure 1C
shows the micromorphology of hydrogenated TiO2 (TiO2-x) and
Figure 1D shows the EDS elemental maps (Ti and O) of TiO2-x.
Due to the existence of a large number of surface defects, the
shape of TiO2-x exhibits a spindle shape with a length of less than
50 nm. The lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.35 nm in the high-
resolution image of TEM were attributed to the (101) plane of
TiO2.[15] Figure 1E shows the micromorphology of CDs@TiO2-x,
smaller CDs are attached to the surface of larger TiO2-x. The el-
ements Ti, O, C, N, and S were evenly overlapped across the se-
lected region (Figure 1F), and the content of S was signiﬁcantly
less. The HRTEM image of CDs@TiO2-x (Figure 1G; Figure S3,
Supporting Information) clearly shows the lattice fringes with a
spacing of 0.21 nm from CDs and 0.35 nm from TiO2-x, which
proved the successful recombination of CDs and TiO2-x.
Figure 2A shows the crystalline structure of CDs, TiO2-x, and
CDs@TiO2-x. The blunt broad peak centered at 23° of CDs corre-
sponded to the (002) plane of graphite.[16] TiO2-x exhibited strong
characteristic diﬀraction peaks of TiO2 (Anatase #21-1272) and
weaker diﬀraction peaks of Ti3O5 (Titanium oxide #11-0217).
This is attributed to the decrease in Ti coordination number
caused by oxygen vacancies (Ov), which resulted in the appear-
ance of Ti3+, demonstrating the successful reduction of TiO2.[4]
The XRD spectrum of CDs@TiO2-x is highly consistent with that
of pure anatase, which is due to the lower crystallinity of CDs
compared to TiO2-x, and the relatively low and dispersed con-
tent of CDs and Ti3O5 in the CDs@TiO2-x. The existence of Ov
was conﬁrmed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analy-
sis (Figure 2B,C). Compared with unreduced TiO2, TiO2−x exhib-
ited a Ti3+ 2p3/2 signal at 458.2 eV in the Ti 2p spectrum and a
more obvious oxygen vacancy signal at 531.9 eV in the O 1s spec-
trum [9b], demonstrating the reduction was due to the generation
of Ti3+ and oxygen vacancies. Meanwhile, the peak of Ti─O─H
formed at 533.5 eV due to surface adsorbed water also showed
a signiﬁcant increase, indicating that after reduction, TiO2-x was
more prone to adsorb water, making it easier to convert H2O
into •OH, which was beneﬁcial for subsequent oxidation reac-
tions. Figure 2D shows the valence band (VB) spectra of TiO2
and TiO2-x, the VB of TiO2 was 2.63 V, after reduction, the VB
of TiO2-x shifted to 2.39 V. As demonstrated in the UV–vis DRS
spectra of TiO2 and TiO2-x (Figure 2E), TiO2 exhibited extremely

2.2. Photocatalytic Degradation of Dyes and Antibacterial
Properties
In
order
to
verify
the
ROS-generating
performance
of
CDs@TiO2-x under visible light, dye degradation experiments
were ﬁrst conducted. Methylene blue (MB), methyl orange
(MO), and rhodamine B (RhB) are the most explored dyes in
photocatalysis, and their degradation enables estimation of
the photocatalytic activity of various materials. Figure 4A–C
shows the kinetics linear simulation curves of the degradation
of MB, MO, and RhB under visible light catalysis of CDs, TiO2-x,
and CDs@TiO2-x. It is clear to see that the dye degradation
ability of CDs@TiO2-x was much stronger than that of its raw
materials CDs and TiO2-x under visible light. CDs@TiO2-x was
able to degrade MB by 89% (k = 0.0251 min−1) and MO by 87%
(k = 0.0236 min−1) within 90 min, and degrade RhB by 99%
(k = 0.1003 min−1) in only 60 min. The eﬃciency of degradation
of CDs@TiO2-x was compared with other similar photocatalysts
reported in the literatures, and the results are listed in Table
S3 (Supporting Information). When it came to visible light
catalysis, CDs@TiO2-x demonstrated distinct advantages, and
CDs@TiO2-x did not have signiﬁcant diﬀerences even compared
to some works that contain ultraviolet lights. Such outcomes
exempliﬁed the exceptional performance of CDs@TiO2-x.
Then, some typical scavengers were used to remove the pho-
togenerated holes (h+) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) that
might be generated in the reaction. There have been several
studies indicating that sodium formate (CHOONa), t-butanol (t-
BuOH), p-benzoquinone (PBQ), and 4-chloro-2-nitrophenol (4-
C-2-NP) are reasonable scavengers for the h+, hydroxyl radi-
cals (•OH), superoxide anion radicals (•O2
−) and singlet oxygen
species (1O2) in the photocatalytic system, respectively.[33] After
they were added into the reaction system, the eﬀects of the corre-
sponding substances were reﬂected by the degree of degradation
reduction. Thus, the role of these substances in the photocatalytic
oxidation of these organic dyes could be elucidated (Figure 4D).
The results indicated that •OH played a major role in the degra-
dation of MB, •O2
−and 1O2 played less role while h+ played no
role, demonstrating MB was mainly oxidized by the •OH, which
was converted from H2O by photogenerated electrons (e−) on the
conduction band (CB) of TiO2. On the contrary, •OH had no role
in the photocatalytic oxidation of MO, •O2
−and 1O2 played al-
most equal primary role in the degradation of MB, and h+ also
played a certain role. This might be attributed to the fact that MO
was mainly oxidized by 1O2 with strong oxidation ability, while
1O2 was mainly produced by •O2
−through h+ oxidation and
other processes.[34] RhB was mainly oxidized by •O2
−, with •OH
and 1O2 playing a smaller role, and h+ played no role. MO and
RB were mainly oxidized by the ROS converted from O2 by pho-
togenerated holes (h+) on the valence band (VB) of CDs. Consid-
ering that •OH and 1O2 both had stronger oxidation ability than
•O2
−, these results reﬂected that RhB was more easily oxidized
than MB and MO, which was consistent with the experimental
results. In addition, the stronger degradation eﬀect on RhB was
also due to the negative charge of this dye, which better inter-
acted with positively charged CDs@TiO2-x (Figure 4F). The above
results also reﬂected CDs@TiO2-x had the ability to produce var-
ious substances with oxidizing ability (h+, •OH, •O2
−and 1O2),
and when facing diﬀerent organic compounds, these oxidizing
substances could play their respective roles, highlighting the po-
tential of CDs@TiO2-x in degrading multiple pollutants.
Considering the signiﬁcant impact of pH on the photocat-
alytic degradation of dyes in practical applications, photocatalytic

To understand the degradation and antibacterial results, a se-
ries of characterizations and electrochemical experiments were
performed to unravel the properties of CDs@TiO2-x. Electro-
chemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to investigate
the charge-transfer rate. Figure 7A shows the EIS spectrum of
TiO2-x, CDs, and CDs@TiO2-x. For Nyquist plots, a larger area
enclosed by the curve and x-axis of the semicircle corresponds
to a greater resistance to electron ﬂow at the electrode-electrolyte
interface. CDs@TiO2-x presented the smallest slope, indicating
the lowest charge transfer resistance and the highest photogen-

(LSV) were measured to investigate the recombination rate of

the ability of CDs@TiO2-x to inhibit the recombination of photo-
generated e−and h+, resulting in more e−and h+ participating
in the generation of free radicals, which promoted the degra-
dation activity of organic dyes and antibacterial performance.

Figure 7E demonstrates how CDs act as photosensitizers (PS)
in antibacterial photodynamic therapy (aPDT). CDs are excited
after absorbing a photon (A), and then transfer from the ground
singlet state (S0) to the excited singlet state (Sn). CDs then release
energy through ﬂuorescence (F) or internal conversion (H), re-
turning to S0, or convert to a longer-living excited triplet state (T1)
via inter-system crossing (ISC). From T1, CDs emit phosphores-
cence (P) or generate ROS through two processes to return to
S0. In the type I route, charges transfer to surrounding molecu-
lar oxygen, oxidize them, and produce superoxide radicals (•O2
−)
and hydroxyl radicals (•OH). In the type II route, energy directly
transfers to the ground state molecular oxygen (3O2), leading to
the emergence of singlet oxygen (1O2).[45] Figure 7F shows the
mechanism through which TiO2-x generates ROS under irradia-
tion, a process commonly known as antibacterial photocatalytic
therapy (aPCT) when employed for antibacterial purposes. When
TiO2-x is exposed to light, electrons in its VB absorb the energy
of photons and undergo a transition to the CB. This transition
leads to the generation of photogenerated electrons (e−) in the
CB and corresponding photogenerated holes (h+) in the VB. Pho-
togenerated e−are easily captured by oxidizing substances such
as dissolved oxygen in water to generate •O2
−. And photogener-
ated h+ can directly oxidize organics adsorbed on TiO2-x surface
or oxidize OH−and H2O molecules adsorbed on TiO2-x surface
to form hydroxyl radicals (OH•). •O2
−can also convert to 1O2 via
hole oxidation or other associated processes.[46]
In order to further study the enhanced photocatalytic ability
of the CDs@TiO2-x, the mechanism of heterojunction formation
was studied. Mott-Schottky (MS) plots were measured to charac-
terize the types of semiconductors and the ﬂat band potentials of
CDs, TiO2-x, and CDs@TiO2-x (Figures S8–S10, Supporting Infor-
mation). The ﬂat band of TiO2-x, CDs, and CDs@TiO2-x were de-
termined to be 0.29, 0.78, and 0.67 V versus the saturated calomel
electrode (SCE), respectively. Therefore, the Fermi levels (Ef) of
TiO2-x, CDs, and CDs@TiO2-x were calculated to be 0.51, 1.00,
and 0.89 V versus NHE, respectively. Figure 7G shows the band
structures of CDs, TiO2-x, and two kinds of heterojunction they
might form based on their band structures: traditional type-II
heterojunction and direct Z-scheme heterojunction, which both
have good charge separation eﬃciency. In the type-II heterojunc-
tion, photogenerated e−gathered in CB of TiO2-x with weak re-
duction potential, and photogenerated h+ gathered in VB of CDs
with weak oxidation potential,[47] these potentials could not reach
−0.33 V (O2/•O2
−) and 2.38 V (H2O/•OH) respectively, demon-
strating that it was diﬃcult for them to produce these active
substances to degrade dyes or kill bacteria. This was not con-
sistent with the above experimental results. While in the direct
Z-scheme heterojunction, after being excited by irradiation, the
photogenerated e−and h+ with strong redox ability retained in
CB of CDs and VB of TiO2-x, while other photogenerated carriers
(photogenerated e−in CB of TiO2-x and photogenerated h+ in VB
of CDs) participate in carrier recombination. Therefore, the di-
rect Z-scheme could solve the problem of carrier recombination
while maintaining strong redox ability, achieving a better pho-
tocatalytic degradation eﬀect and antibacterial eﬀect, consisting
with the experimental results. To further verify the mechanism
of direct Z-scheme heterojunction, the existence of •OH, •O2
−,
and h+ from the CDs@TiO2-x composite was directly ascertained
through analysis of the electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR)
spectra (Figure 7H–J). Subsequent utilization of DMPO for cap-
turing •OH and •O2
−and TEMPO for capturing h+ revealed no-
table signal changes, including the emergence of peaks for •OH
and •O2
−and the attenuation of peaks for h+, further substan-
tiating their generation. In order to provide conclusive evidence
for the mechanism of the direct Z-scheme heterojunction, in situ
XPS testing was conducted on CDs@TiO2-x to detect its electron
transfer pathways under illumination. The high-resolution C 1s,
Ti 2p, and O 1s spectra are shown in the Figure 7K–M. Compar-
ing with the binding energy in the dark, the binding energy of
C 1s shifted toward a lower direction, while the binding energy
of Ti 2p and O 1s shifted toward a higher direction under illu-
mination, indicating that photoelectrons transferred from TiO2-x
to CDs under illumination. Such electron transfer pathway was
consistent with the mechanism of direct Z-scheme heterojunc-
tion. Therefore, the formation of direct Z-scheme heterojunction
was conﬁrmed.

DFT calculations were also employed to conduct an in-depth

of H2O conversion to •OH, after initial adsorption and structural

activity was directly reﬂected through a comparison of reaction-

ter treatment, the activity of 𝛽-galactosidase on the bacterial cell
membranes decreased signiﬁcantly, indicating that the perme-
ability of the cell membranes increased, which was conducive to
the further entry of CDs@TiO2-x and ROS into the bacteria. In or-
der to research the role of ROS in the antibacterial process, super-
oxide dismutase (SOD) activity, catalase (CAT) activity, and mal-
ondialdehyde (MDA) content were measured after 1 h of treat-
ment (Figure 10L–N). SOD and CAT are enzymes for bacteria
to resist oxidative stress, which can scavenge free radicals and in-
hibit the reactive proliferation of free radicals in bacteria.[48] MDA
is one of the products of lipid peroxidation, and its content can
be used to evaluate the degree of oxidative damage and the oxida-
tive damage state of cells.[39b] As shown in Figure 10L–N, SOD
activity, CAT activity, and MDA content in the material groups
increased signiﬁcantly compared with the control groups, indi-
cating that the bacteria produced oxidative stress reaction and
oxidative damage.
Based on the above analysis, the antibacterial mechanism of
CDs@TiO2-x could be summarized as follows (Figure 11). Ini-
tially, the surface of CDs@TiO2-x exhibited a positive potential,
facilitating its electrostatic adsorption onto the negatively charged
bacteria, which altered the potential diﬀerence across the bac-
terial cell membranes, thereby aﬀecting the normal function of
membrane ion channels, disrupting ion homeostasis, and ulti-
mately leading to cell membrane damage. Furthermore, a com-
bination of CDs@TiO2-x and bacteria allowed the Ov present on
the TiO2-x surface to eﬀectively act as electron acceptors, attract-
ing active electrons from the normal respiratory chain of bacteria.
This event triggered extracellular electron transfer (EET) and in-
terfered with the regular functioning of the cell membranes.[50]
CDs could also disrupt the physiological activities of transmem-
brane proteins such as channels, porins, or receptors, thereby in-
terfering with proton pools or electron transport along the res-
piratory chain.[24] Simultaneously, after being excited by visible
light, CDs@TiO2-x generated photogenerated e−and h+, follow-
ing the pattern of direct z-scheme heterojunction, they reacted
with O2 and H2O to produce ROS, which could also attack bacte-
rial cell walls and membranes. These processes led to the destruc-
tion of bacterial cell walls and membranes, resulting in content
leakage and facilitating the inﬂux of CDs@TiO2-x and ROS into
the bacteria. After internalizing within the bacteria, CDs@TiO2-x

3. Conclusion
Direct Z-scheme xylan-based CDs@TiO2-x nanocomposites were
prepared using a facile and simple hydrothermal method for the
ﬁrst time. CDs@TiO2-x exhibited signiﬁcantly enhanced capabil-
ities in dye degradation and antibacterial performance under vis-
ible light irradiation. Results have shown that the introduction of
oxygen vacancies (OV) and Ti3+ directly help TiO2-x absorb visi-
ble light; Ov, the positively charged surface groups, and charges
helped CDs@TiO2-x more eﬃciently attach to the bacterial and
trigger extracellular electron transfer (EET); And the formation of
direct Z-scheme heterojunction resulted in an increase in holes
(h+) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. The photo-
catalytic degradation process of CDs@TiO2-x mainly involves N-
deethylation, chromophores cleavage, and mineralization. And

Preparation of CDs:
CDs were synthesized by a solvent-free method.
Brieﬂy, 1 g of xylan and 1.0 g of thiourea were mixed and grinded for 10 min.
The mixture was transferred into a 20 mL of Teﬂon-lined stainless-steel
autoclave for heating 10 h at 180 °C. After the obtained carbonized powder
is fully dissolved in ultrapure water, it is dialyzed in a cellulose dialysis bag
with a molecular weight cutoﬀof 100 Da in a dark place for 3 days, the
water is changed twice a day to remove the salt and impurities inside.
The puriﬁed solution is evaporated and concentrated at 65 °C, and then
freeze–dried to obtain the CDs powder.
Preparation of CDs@TiO2-x:
For the preparation of CDs@TiO2-x,
15 mg of TiO2-x was dispersed in 15 ml of CDs solution (1 mg/ml). After
being sonicated for 10 min and stirred for 2 h, the mixture was transferred
into a 20 mL of Teﬂon-lined stainless-steel autoclave for heating 4 h at
150 °C. The obtained mixture is centrifuged and the solid part is dried to
obtain CDs@TiO2-x.

Calculation Method:
The spin-polarized density functional theory
(DFT) calculations were carried out in the Vienna ab initio simulation
package (VASP) based on the plane-wave basis sets with the projector
augmented-wave method. The exchange-correlation potential’s treatment
incorporates the Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA) with PBE
parametrization. The calculation was performed with an energy cutoﬀof
500 eV, and the Brillouin-zone integration was accomplished using a Γ-
centered Monkhorst-Pack mesh of 3 × 3 × 1 of TiO2-x and CDs@TiO2-x
surfaces by VASPKIT. For structure relaxation, the maximum force on each
atom was constrained to be below 0.03 eV Å−1 with a converged energy
standard of 10−5 eV. The van der Waals correction of Grimme’s DFT-D3
model was also used in the calculations. To prevent periodic interactions
for interface structures, a large vacuum layer of at least 20 Å was used
along the c-direction perpendicular to the interface.
Antibacterial Mechanisms:
Each strain was treated with CDs@TiO2-x
(1 mg mL−1) or pure water for 2 h, the mixtures were ﬁrst ﬁxed with a
2.5% (volume fraction) glutaraldehyde aqueous solution for 2 h, followed
by centrifugation and two washes with ultrapure water. Then they were
dehydrated sequentially using an aqueous solution of alcohol with a con-
centration gradient ranging from 50% to 100% (in increments of 10%).
Each solvent replacement was accompanied by thorough shaking and a
10 min stand. Finally, the bacteria dispersed in anhydrous ethanol were
dripped onto a silicon wafer at room temperature for drying and observed
using a scanning electron microscope.
For the TEM images of the bacteria, the mixtures were ﬁxed with a 2.5%
(volume fraction) glutaraldehyde aqueous solution for 12 h. Then they
were ﬁxed with a mixture of 1% osmic acid and 2% potassium ferrocyanide
for 2 h. The samples were then rinsed four times with water for 15 min each
time. Sequential dehydration was carried out using acetone solutions with
concentrations of 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, and 95%, followed by two rounds
of dehydration with pure acetone, for 20 min each time. Embedding was
then performed using a mixture of acetone and the embedding agent in a
ratio of 3:1 for 1 h, and then in a ratio of 1:3 for 3 h. Subsequently, the pure
embedding agent was used for overnight embedding at 37 °C. The sam-
ples were then poured into an embedding plate, which was kept at 60 °C
for 48 h to allow for solidiﬁcation into a resin block. The resin block was
cut into 70 mm slices, and the pieces were removed using copper mesh.
After being dried and stained with uranium acetate and citric acid 10 min,
the TEM images could be taken.
Each strain was treated with CDs@TiO2-x (1 mg/mL) or pure water
for certain time (OD260, protein, AKP, MDA, 𝛽-galactosidase: 2 h, SOD,
CAT: 1 h). After treating, BCA protein, alkaline phosphatase (AKP), super-
oxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), malondialdehyde (MDA), and 𝛽-
galactosidase assay kits were used and the changes in absorbance were
used to reﬂect the concentration of related substances.

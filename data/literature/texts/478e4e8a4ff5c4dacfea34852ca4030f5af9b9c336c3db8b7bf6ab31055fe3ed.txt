Sulfonium-ion-containing polymers exhibit significant potential benefits for 
various applications. An efficient strategy to synthesize a type of antibacte-
rial sulfonium-ion-bearing polypeptoids via a combination of ring-opening 
polymerization and a post-polymerization functionalization with various 
functional epoxides is presented. A systematic investigation is further 
performed in order to explore the influence of the overall hydrophobic/hydro-
philic balance on the antimicrobial activity and selectivity of the prepared 
polysulfoniums. Notably, those chlorepoxypropane-modified polysulfoniums 
with an optimized amphiphilic balance show higher selectivity toward both 
Gram-negative Escherichia coli and Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus, 
than to red blood cells. The polymers also show great efficiency in inhibiting 
S. aureus biofilm formations, as well as in further eradicating the mature bio-
films. Remarkably, negligible antibacterial resistance and cross-resistance to 
commercial antibiotics is shown in these polymers. The polysulfoniums fur-
ther show their potent in vivo antimicrobial efficacy in a multidrug-resistant 
S. aureus infection model that is developed on mouse skin. Similar to the 
antimicrobial peptides, the polysulfoniums are demonstrated to kill bacteria 
through membrane disruption. The obtained polypeptoid sulfoniums, with 
high selectivity and potent antibacterial property, are excellent candidates for 
antibacterial treatment and open up new possibilities for the preparation of a 
class of innovative antimicrobials.
1. Introduction
The sulfonium-ion-containing compound with a permanent 
positive charge, is extensively present in both natural plants 
and animals.[1] S-adenosyl-l-methionine (SAM), which is one 
of the most important sulfonium compounds, was first dis-
covered in organisms in 1952.[2] This compound serves as both 
the methyl donor and the free radical precursor in biosynthetic

properties that have a low propensity to the resistance issue.[17] 
In addition, it has been reported that many AMPs show fast bac-
tericidal effect because of the membrane disruption mechanism, 
which is desired particularly for medical emergency.[19] This 
can also reduce the quantity of the bacteria in biofilm, which 
is clinically important.[20] Inspired by these AMPs, the synthetic 
polymeric AMP mimics the bearing of both these motifs, and 
has thus received great interest as a potent antimicrobial. These 
polymeric antimicrobials, such as polyacrylates,[21] polynor-
bornenes,[22] and polypeptides,[23] exhibit significant improve-
ments, including having superior stability, facile synthesis, and 
a low cost, as compared with the natural antimicrobial AMPs. In 
most studies, polyammoniums and polyphosphoniums typically 
act as the cationic motifs. Research on polymeric sulfonium 
salts meanwhile, particularly those possessing a biocompatible 
backbone, have rarely been reported.[7,24]
It is known that having a delicate control over the balance of 
the cationic and hydrophobic moieties is essential to achieve high 
antibacterial activity and low mammalian cell toxicity.[25] Gener-
ally, an incorporation of hydrophobic groups, particularly lipo-
philic alkyl groups, is required in order to obtain high antibacterial 
properties. However, this can also result in increased hemolysis. 
Conversely, higher hydrophilicity reduces toxicity, but also com-
promises on antibacterial activity.[26] Thus, having finely tunable 
antibacterial polymers in both structure and property is highly 
desired. Polypeptoids, also known as nitrogen-substituted poly­
glycines, represent one of the most important of the pseudo-pep-
tides, and show great potential usage for various biomedical appli-
cations.[27] Due to the identical backbone structure, polypeptoids 
exhibit biocompatibility, and a biological activity of proteins.[28] 
The absence of chirality and inter-/intrachain hydrogen bonding 
enables high tunability, which is regulated by side-chain function-
ality. The reduced structural complexity also facilitates structural 
design and engineering.[29] The polypeptoids can be produced 
by a ring-opening polymerization (ROP) of N-substituted N-car-
boxyanhydride (NNCA) with a high molar mass in a large-scale 
yield.[30] A post-polymerization functionalization strategy provides 
a versatile and convenient technique for functionalization.[31]
In this study, through ring-opening polymerization and sub-
sequent post-polymerization functionalization with various epox-
ides, we have prepared a series of polypeptoid sulfoniums. The 
influence of hydrophobic moiety on the antimicrobial activity 
and selectivity was then systematically investigated. By opti-
mizing the hydrophilic/hydrophobic balance, it could be seen 
that the chlorepoxypropane-modified polysulfoniums exhibited 
high antibacterial activity against both Gram-negative Escheri-
chia coli and Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus, with high 
selectivity. The polymers showed a rapid exterminating effect 
on S. aureus but negligible antibacterial resistance after repeated 
usage. In addition, no cross-resistance on commercial antibiotics 
was observed. The obtained polysulfoniums were greatly effi-
cient in their inhibition of the formation of S. aureus biofilm and 
their further eradication of the mature biofilms. A strong in vivo 
antimicrobial efficacy was also observed in these polymers.
2. Results and Discussion
The polypeptoids were first synthesized using a ROP of N-allyl 
N-carboxyanhydride (Allyl-NCA) with benzylamine (BnNH2) 
and 
methoxypolyethylene 
glycol 
amine 
(mPEG2k-NH2, 
Mn  = 2000  g mol−1) as the initiators. This was followed by a 
modi­fication with ethyl thioalcohol in order to yield polypeptoids 
precursors, which are denoted as PNAGn-ET and PEG2k-PNAGn-
ET, respectively. The subscript n represents the average degree 
of polymerizations (DPs) of PNAG (Scheme S1, Supporting 
Information). The polysulfoniums were synthesized by the 
alkylation of (PEG2k-)PNAGn-ET using three different types of 
functional epoxides with different hydrophobicity (Scheme  1a 
and Figure S2, Supporting Information). The first attempt 
involved a quantitative modification of the polypeptoid 
precursors (PEG2k-)PNAGn-ET, while using chlorepoxypro-
pane to yield (PEG2k-)PNAGn-ET-Cl (Figures S5a, S7a–S10a, 
Supporting Information). Similarly, (PEG2k-)PNAGn-ET-Bn and 
(PEG2k-)PNAGn-ET-OEG1–3 were both prepared through a modi-
fication with 1-(benzyloxy)-2,3-epoxypropane and oligo(ethylene 
glycol)1–3 (OEG)1–3, which contained epoxides (Figures S5b, S6, 
S7b–S10b, and S11, Supporting Information). The degree of 
modification was determined to range from 62% to 92.0%, 
possibly due to the steric hindrance effect of bulky substitu-
ents. The chemical structures and functionalization degrees 
of the samples were confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy and 
GPC (Figures S3–S11, Supporting Information), as shown in 
Table S2, Supporting Information, and Table 1. The hydropho-
bicity of (PEG2k-)PNAGn-ET-Bn was higher than that of (PEG2k-)
PNAGn-ET-Cl, while (PEG2k-)PNAGn-ET-OEG1–3 is considered to 
be more hydrophilic. Such a structural design allows for further 
systematic investigation of the hydrophobic/hydrophilic balance 
on antibacterial property and selectivity of polysulfoniums.
All the obtained polypeptoids that contained cationic sulfo-
nium ions showed high water solubility and positive zeta-poten-
tials (ζ) in aqueous solution. The dependence of ζ potential on 
pH values was further examined with the addition of the HCl/
NaOH aqueous solutions. In Figure S12, Supporting Informa-
tion, as pH increases from 2.1 to 13.0, the ζ potential of the 
PNAG25-ET-Bn aqueous solution ranges from +3.2 to +14.3 mV. 
Note that the ζ potential of the polymer solution gradually 
decreases as the pH increases, which is consistent with the pre-
viously reported results on quaternized amine-containing poly-
mers.[32] We then investigated the DP effect on the ζ potentials. 
Typically, the ζ potential value decreased as the DP increased, 
which was perhaps due to the fact that the longer polymer 
chains are likely to bury more charges (Figure S13, Supporting 
Information).[33] In both the PNAGn-ET-Bn and PNAGn-ET-
OEG cases, the potential values were generally independent on 
the modification degree. This suggests that the difference in 
functionalization efficiency can be neglected. Furthermore, the 
potential ζ value of the OEG-modified polypeptoids appears to 
be irrelevant to the OEG's length (Figure S14, Supporting Infor-
mation). However, the incorporation of PEG can reduce the 
potential, which can be ascribed to the screening effect from 
the longer chain length. We further tested the zeta potentials 
of PEG2k-PNAG61-ET-OEG1, PNAG81-ET-Cl, and PNAG81-ET-Bn 
in PBS (Figure S15, Supporting Information). In all cases, in 
spite of having a slightly declined value, the polymers in the 
PBS possessed positive zeta-potentials.

case of Gram-positive S. aureus, higher antibacterial activity 
with increased potential is achieved in PNAGn-ET-Cl, as com-

cationic charges, rather than high hydrophobicity, are the most 
efficient in eliminating the Gram-positive pathogens that typi-
cally possess a thin membrane.[34] In contrast, it is an appro-
priate level of hydrophobicity that is required to carry out 
antibacterial activity against Gram-negative E. coli. We tested 
minimum bactericidal concentrations (MBC) to further study 
the antimicrobial activity. Unlike MIC, that is employed to pin-
point the lowest polymer concentration at which the growth 
of bacteria is inhibited, MBC identifies the lowest polymer 
concentration at which bacteria are completely killed. In gen-
eral, the MBC values of the polysulfoniums are similar to the 
MIC values, or slightly higher in some cases. Furthermore, the 
incorporation of the PEG segment shows a negligible influence 
on both MIC and MBC values.
We further evaluated the hemolysis of all the samples using 
human red blood cells (hRBCs), which were determined by 
HC10 (the concentration of a polymer that causes 10% hemo­
lysis of hRBCs). The selectivity index (SI) was then determined 
from the ratio of HC10 to the MIC values (HC10/MIC). It is 
generally accepted that increased hydrophobicity can enhance 
the ability to lyse red blood cells (RBCs).[26] As expected, the 
PNAGn-ET-Bn samples with the most hydrophobic substituents 
showed the lowest HC10 values, which thus yielded lower 
selectivity in spite of it having high MIC values. In the OEG-
modified polypeptoids cases, HC10 values were observed 
>1000 µg mL−1 due to the existence of hydrophilic OEG groups. 
It is thus shown that PNAGn-ET-OEG displays excellent selec-
tivity toward S. aureus, over mammalian cells. The cell cyto-
toxicity of PNAG25-ET-OEG3, which was tested by live/dead 
staining, exhibited negligible cytotoxicity, even at 1000 µg mL−1 
(Figure S16, Supporting Information). Notably, in spite of the 
presence of the hydrophobic chloromethyl motif, the PNAGn-
ET-Cl also exhibited insignificant hemolysis activity with signif-
icantly high HC10 values >1000 µg mL−1. Combining the good 
antibacterial properties of this type of polymer, this results in 
excellent selectivity up to higher than 128 and 256 toward both 
Gram-negative and positive pathogenic microbes over mamma-
lian cells, respectively (Table 1). We further tested the hemolysis 
of the samples using RBCs obtained from New Zealand rabbits. 
The obtained HC10 (>1000  µg mL−1) were consistent with the 
results achieved when using hRBCs. The SEM was used to 
evaluate the morphology of the RBCs following treatment with 
the polymers. In both cases, being treated with and without 
PNAG25-ET-Cl, the cells remained intact and with smooth sur-
faces (Figure S17, Supporting Information). To further assess 
the cytotoxicity of PNAGn-ET-Cl, live/dead staining and methyl 
thiazolyl tetrazolium (MTT) assay were applied (Figures S18 
and S19, Supporting Information) against HeLa cells. Each 
PNAGn-ET-Cl sample exhibited negligible cytotoxicity at its 
respective MIC values.
To further understand the antibacterial polysulfoniums’ 
action mechanism, we explored the interactions between 
polymer and both types of bacteria. Both the bacteria of S. 
aureus and E. coli, with a cell density of 3 × 108 CFU/mL, were 
incubated with the fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled 
PNAG81-ET-Cl in the presence of Nile Red (NR). As a lipophilic 
fluorescent dye, NR is able to stain the cytoplasmic lipids with 
an emitted red fluorescence.[35] Confocal microscopy of the 
FITC-labeled PNAG81-ET-Cl shows that upon incubation, the 
polymers with green fluorescence preferentially accumulated 
in the red bacteria envelope, which suggests an interaction 
between the polymers and bacteria (Figure  1a). The fluores-
cence intensity profiles confirm that the sample interacts with 
the cell membrane of S. aureus, as indicated by the coincidence 
of the green and red fluorescence (Figure  1b,c). Similarly, we 
incubated E. coli with the FITC-labeled PNAG81-ET-Cl in the 
presence of NR, where the overlaid red and green fluorescence 
can also be observed (Figure S20, Supporting Information).
A membrane-potential-sensitive dye (3,3′-dipropylthiadicar-
bocyanine iodide) (DiSC3(5)) was used to further assess the 
cytoplasmic membrane depolarization ability of PNAG25-ET-Cl. 
The fluorescence intensity of the DiSC3(5) dye increases 
with the dissipation of the transmembrane electrochemical 
gradient, which can be conveniently record by fluorescence 
spectrometry.[36] Figure 2a shows the obvious increase in fluo-
rescence intensity that was seen after S. aureus was incubated 
with the polymer at MIC concentration for a few minutes. The 
fluorescence intensity largely increased as the concentration 
increased to 4 × MIC. This suggests that the samples induce a 
dose-dependent depolarization of the cytoplasmic membrane in 
S. aureus. By contrast, the fluorescence intensity of vancomycin 
as a negative control remained the same. This confirms the 
presence of membrane depolarization in the polysulfoniums 
as vancomycin prevents the targeting of the cytoplasmic mem-
brane in S. aureus. Additionally, studies on the cytoplasmic 
membrane depolarization of E. coli also show a noticeable 
depolarization following incubation with PNAG45-ET-Cl. In 
this case, the fluorescence intensity significantly increases 
after 2-h incubation with the sample. Note that polymyxin B, 
which is known to interact with the cell membrane, exhibits 
a similar variation in fluorescence intensity (Figure S21, Sup-
porting Information). The effect of cytoplasmic membrane 
depolarization on E. coli is very promising as the Gram-negative 
bacteria show intrinsic antibiotic resistance due to their

Gram-negative bacteria is considered more difficult to be killed

Biofilm infections are typically related to chronic infections 
and are rarely curable. Biofilms consist in structured microbial 
communities, which typically contain a diversity of microorgan-
isms embedded in polymeric substances that inhibit the pen-
etration of the antimicrobial reagent. We first used MTT assay 
to assess the ability of sulfonium polypeptoids to inhibit S. 
aureus biofilm formation. In Figure 5a, PNAG81-ET-Cl is shown 
to possess the dose-dependent activity that works against the 
formation of S. aureus biofilm. After being treated with the 
polymer at MIC, the biofilm's bacterial viability is reduced to 
91.50%. As the concentration of the polypeptoid increases to 
2 × MIC meanwhile, the biofilm's viability significantly reduces 
to 0.15%. Similarly, vancomycin at 2 × MIC results in a 0.21% 
bacterial viability. This suggests that the obtained polymers are 
greatly efficient at inhibiting the formation of S. aureus biofilm. 
CLSM was further performed in order to visualize the biofilms 
before/after treatment with the sample. Figure 5c shows a layer 
of the green fluorescent biofilms in the control group, which 
suggests a formation of biofilms that are full of living bacteria. 
Following incubation with the polypeptoids, merely a few dead 
cells were observed in the CLSM image, as indicated by a few 
red fluorescence marks. A similar result was observed after 
treatment with vancomycin. This confirms that PNAG81-ET-
Cl can effectively prevent the formation of S. aureus biofilms. 
We also investigated the ability of PNAG81-ET-Cl to eradicate 
mature S. aureus biofilms, as it has been reported that vanco-
mycin is ineffective in this regard.[39] In this case, the bacterial 
viability of the S. aureus biofilms following treatment with van-
comycin at 32 × MIC remained at 99.18% (Figure 5b). Addition-
ally, the CLSM image confirms that vancomycin is not capable 
of destroying the biofilms (Figure 5d). This is possibly because 
the efficacy of the antibacterial reagents to kill the bacteria 
embedded in the biofilm is significantly lower than that in solu-
tions. Remarkably, after being incubated with PNAG81-ET-Cl at 
32 × MIC, the bacterial viability of the biofilms was reduced to 
14.05%, and the biofilms were observed to have increased red 
fluorescence by CLSM (Figure 5). This suggests that PNAG81-
ET-Cl can efficiently eradicate the mature biofilms. Similarly, 
the PNAG25-ET-Cl analogue can also eradicate the mature bio-
films with bacterial viability of 5.06% after being incubated with 
the polymer at 64 × MIC (Figure S29, Supporting Information). 
In contrast, the activities of both Bn- and OEG-substituent poly-
mers PNAG81-ET-Bn and PNAG25-ET-OEG1 to eradicate mature 
S. aureus biofilms were observed to be insufficient (Figures S30 
and S31, Supporting Information). We attribute this reduced 
penetration through the polymeric matrix of the biofilm to the 
less balanced amphiphilic property.[40]
Antimicrobial resistance, which is closely tied to excessive 
use of antimicrobials or antibiotics, is of great concern. The 
AMPs are known to hardly develop any bacterial resistance.[41] 
Considering their similar antibacterial mechanisms, we also 
evaluated the ability of the polymer to resist bacteria. Initially, 
the MIC and MBC values of PNAGn-ET-Cl toward MRSA 
were determined, which is nearly identical to that of S. aureus  
(Table S3, Supporting Information). Note that MRSA is 
hardly eradicated by conventional antibiotics at >10 × MIC 
concentration against S. aureus (Figure S32, Supporting Infor-
mation). This indicates that polysulfonium performs excellently 
against drug-resistant bacteria. Furthermore, upon multiple 
lethal dose treatments, antibacterial resistance can be induced 
through the use of antibiotic drugs against bacteria.[26,42] We thus 
studied the antibacterial resistance after using PNAG59-ET-Cl 
repeatedly at a 0.5 × MIC concentration. For comparison, a 
conventional antibiotic norfloxacin was also used. After 25 con-
tinuous passages, the MIC values of PNAG59-ET-Cl remained 
constant (Figure 6a). By contrast, a visible increase in the MIC 
values of norfloxacin was observed as S. aureus were grown 
to the 7th passage. With the constant treatment of the 25th 
passage, the MIC of norfloxacin significantly increased to 
1024 times the original MIC. This suggests that the bacteria devel-
oped a resistance to norfloxacin. However, developing resistance 
against the polysulfonium is inherently difficult. Notably, the 
polysulfoniums perform potent antimicrobial activities against 
the norfloxacin resistant S. aureus, with norfloxacin treatment 
for 25th passage (Figure  6b). In addition, both PNAG59-ET-Cl 
and norfloxacin show an excellent antimicrobial performance 
against the S. aureus after treatment with the polymer until the 
25th passage (S. aureus-AG59-Cl-P25). Meanwhile, some commer-
cial antibiotics, such as ampicillin, methicillin, and norfloxacin, 
show a good performance against S. aureus-PNAG59-Cl-P25. 
Their MICs are exactly the same as those against S. aureus  
(Table S4, Supporting Information). All these results indicate 
that upon multiple treatments, the polysulfoniums only show 
a trivial antibacterial resistance. In addition, cross-resistance on 
commercial antibiotics is not observed either.
The antibacterial efficiency of the polysulfonium with regards 
to wound sterilization was assessed using an MRSA infec-
tion model on mouse skin in vivo. During a typical procedure, 
full-thickness wounds with 6  mm biopsy punch were created

treated with a single dose of PNAG25-ET-Cl (15 µL, 1 mg mL−1).

and vancomycin displayed similar potency in in vivo bacteri-

compared to the saline control group with a CFU at 6.7 log/g 
(Figure 7a). Furthermore, the wound healing progress showed

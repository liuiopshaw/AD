ABSTRACT: The accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides is a major hallmark of
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and plays a crucial role in its pathogenesis. Particularly, the
structured oligomeric species rich in β-sheet formations were implicated in neuronal
organelle damage. Addressing this formidable challenge requires identifying
candidates capable of inhibiting peptide aggregation or disaggregating preformed
oligomers for effective antiaggregation-based AD therapy. Here, we present a dual-
functional nanoinhibitor meticulously designed to target the aggregation driving
force and amyloid fibril spatial structure. Leveraging the exceptional structural
stability and facile tailoring capability of endohedral metallofullerene Gd@C82, we
introduce desired hydrogen-binding sites and charged groups, which are abundant
on its surface for specific designs. Impressively, these designs endow the resultant
functionalized-Gd@C82 nanoparticles (f-Gd@C82 NPs) with high capability of
redirecting peptide self-assembly toward disordered, off-pathway species, obstructing
the early growth of protofibrils, and disaggregating the preformed well-ordered protofibrils or even mature Aβ fibrils. This
results in considerable alleviation of Aβ peptide-induced neuronal cytotoxicity, rescuing neuronal death and synaptic loss in
primary neuron models. Notably, these modifications significantly improved the dispersibility of f-Gd@C82 NPs, thus
substantially enhancing its bioavailability. Moreover, f-Gd@C82 NPs demonstrate excellent cytocompatibility with various cell
lines and possess the ability to penetrate the blood−brain barrier in mice. Large-scale molecular dynamics simulations
illuminate the inhibition and disaggregation mechanisms. Our design successfully overcomes the limitations of other
nanocandidates, which often overly rely on hydrophobic interactions or photothermal conversion properties, and offers a
viable direction for developing anti-AD agents through the inhibition and even reversal of Aβ aggregation.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative
disease that accounts for 60−70% of senile dementia cases.1
Despite the first AD case being reported in 1906,2 more than a
century has passed and AD remains an urgent public health
issue that has yet to be resolved.3,4 Currently, most available
drugs only temporarily reduce symptoms, and there are very
few therapies that can actually slow or halt the progression of
AD.5−10 While the molecular mechanism of AD is not fully
understood, mounting accrual of data from many preclinical
and clinical studies has increasingly supported that the
oligomerization and aggregation of amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides
is a crucial initial step in the pathogenesis of the disease.11−13
Aβ peptide aggregation can initiates the pathogenic cascade-
including Tau pathology,14−17 inflammation,16,18,19 synaptic
loss,11,20,21 neuronal dysfuction,11,20,22 and ultimately demen-
tia. The ordered Aβ oligomeric species that form in the
aggregation process (which are rich in β-sheet structure) are
reported to be the most pathogenic, acting through complex
toxic mechanisms.23−27 Extracellular Aβ oligomers can bind to
cell membranes, causing perturbations or damages,28 and can
also activate receptors on membranes (such as eNM-

can also trigger injuries to various organelles38 (such as

recently been proposed by Sciacca et al.,51 introducing an
additional element: free lipids. These lipids form a noncovalent
complex with the protein, thereby facilitating the transfer of
amyloidogenic proteins from the aqueous phase to the
membrane bilayer. Consequently, this process may lead to
severe cell membrane damage, thereby advancing our under-
standing of the mechanisms underlying cell membrane
perturbations or damages in AD. Therefore, a more effective
therapeutic strategy for this devastating disease would be to
synchronously inhibit Aβ peptide aggregation and disaggregate
ordered toxic oligomers/mature fibers, that is, to reverse
peptide aggregation.
With tremendous efforts from both experimental and
theoretical approaches, the mechanism of Aβ peptide
aggregation and the atomic-level structural details of amyloid
fibers have been largely decoded in recent years.50,52−67 These
findings suggest that various interactions, including electro-
static,52,57 van der Waals (vdW),62 and hydrophobic
interactions,54,56,58 collectively drive the initial associations of
peptides. Large conformational fluctuations then lead to the
formation of successive and ordered intermainchain hydrogen
bonds from dangling hydrogen-binding sites on the mainchains
of peptides, ultimately directing the oligomers to convert into
highly ordered, mature amyloid fibers.52 Structurally, mature
Aβ amyloid fibers are built by multiple layers of β-sheets
appearing in a cross-β-spine morphology, with the internal-
facing side chains of adjacent β-sheets fully interdigitated to
create a dry hydrophobic interface.64−66 This highly ordered
and well-packed architecture confers the mature Aβ amyloid
fiber with an extremely high thermodynamic stability. These
fundamental micropictures provide illuminating guidelines for
the rational design of AD-preventive or therapeutic agents, e.g.,
to introduce as much disorder as possible to interrupt the early
Aβ aggregation process or disaggregate the preformed
protofibrils or even mature fibrils. However, despite these
promising guidelines, there are still very few successful cases,
with only two recently approved monoclonal antibodies by the
Food and Drug Administration (FDA), e.g., aducanumab9 and
lecanemab.10 Additionally, another antibody, donanemab, has
shown positive results in a Phase III clinical study.68 It should
be noted that the approval status of monoclonal antibodies by
the FDA, but not by the European Medicines Agency,
underscores significant concerns surrounding their side effects,
as well as the criticisms voiced by the scientific commun-
ity.69−72 This highlights the pressing need for enhanced
strategies for AD management.
Recent great advancements in nanoscience have demon-
strated that nanomaterials (NMs) have the potential to serve
as an ideal platform for reversing peptide aggregation due to
their ability to offer a versatile design space that can effectively
tackle every key link in the aggregation process. For example,
graphene oxide (GO)-based nanosheets, due to their high
hydrophobicity, can disrupt peptide side chains to form a
highly ordered hydrophobic core during the aggregation
process.73,74 Additionally, GO nanosheets can insert into
mature fibers and extract peptides destructively.73 Further-
more, due to their high near-infrared-ray (NIR) photothermal
conversion property, GO nanosheets can disassemble their
surface-attached mature Aβ fibers through thermal disrup-
tion.75 Similarly, NIR irradiation can trigger g-C3N4 and other
NPs to disassemble mature Aβ fibers.76,77 Despite these
desired effects, these nanomaterials suffer from several
disadvantages. The hydrophobic nature of GO nanosheets
results in relatively poor water dispersibility, which can
provoke acute cell membrane toxicity and other biotox-
icities.78,79 In addition, the very limited tissue penetration
depth and the incapability of NIR light beams to reach deep
brain tissues mean that NIR-triggered disassembly of mature
fibers may only have very moderate effects in treating AD.
Furthermore, the consequent increase in brain temperature
from this approach poses significant risks, necessitating
cautious consideration for human application. Very recently,
Yin et al.80 found that ultrasmall C3N nanodots inhibit Aβ
peptide aggregation, alleviate neuron cytotoxicity, prevent
neurite damage, and reduce global cerebral Aβ levels,
particularly in plaques, restoring synaptic loss and ameliorating
behavioral deficits in male APP/PS1 double transgenic AD
mice.
In this study, we chose an endohedral metallofullerene, Gd@
C82, as the framework for our functionalized design for several
reasons. First, its metal embedded structure imparts high
structural stability and tailorability. Second, its high surface-to-
volume ratio makes it possible to implement multiple
functional groups. Moreover, its rigid and spherical structure
enchants a natural steric effect, which can prevent the close
association of peptides.81 Additionally, the biocompatibility of
its analogues has been extensively evaluated in several
biomedicine scenarios such as antitumor treatments.82−85 To
achieve our goal, we have introduced various hydrogen-binding
sites onto the surface of Gd@C82, which can competitively
form hydrogen bonds with the mainchains of peptides. The
charged groups were designed to block specific salt-bridge
interactions between key residues, which play a crucial role in
guiding the longitudinal and lateral growth of protofibrils to
mature fibers. It is worth mentioning that this also significantly
enhanced the dispersibility of Gd@C82 in water. The
remaining sp2 regions on the surface of Gd@C82 can provide
binding affinity to the hydrophobic or aromatic ring containing
residues on the peptides. The resulting functionalized Gd@C82
(f-Gd@C82 NPs) can not only inhibit the aggregation of Aβ
peptides but also disassemble the protofibrils. As a result, the
neuronal cytotoxicity of the Aβ peptides was considerably
reduced. Furthermore, f-Gd@C82 NPs showed excellent
cytocompatibility with various tested cell lines, including
primary neurons, astrocytes, human neuroblastoma cells (sh-
sy5y), and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs).
Strikingly, the successful implementation of hydrophilic f-Gd@
C82 NPs overcomes the disadvantages of many other
nanoinhibitors, in terms of relieving the neuronal cytotoxicity
in AD via the introduction of as many disorders as possible to
inhibit/reverse Aβ aggregation without any external stimulus,
thus offering a feasible route for the rational design of AD
prevention and remedy strategies based on NMs.

respectively. As anticipated, the interaction of f-Gd@C82 NPs
with the oligomer led to a discernible decrease in the level of
formation of ordered β-sheet structures (reduced from 11.5 ±
1.4 to 6.6 ± 0.6, as illustrated in Figure S12d). This
phenomenon predominantly stems from electrostatic inter-
actions, facilitating the specific binding of f-Gd@C82 to the
exposed charged residues of the oligomer (depicted in Figure
S12e,f). Consequently, it is evident that the observed effects
and underlying mechanisms remain consistent, irrespective of
the initial structural configuration.
f-Gd@C82 NPs Relieve the Neurotoxicity of Aβ1−42
Oligomers. Encouraged by the promising findings at the
molecular level, we were motivated to investigate whether f-
Gd@C82 NPs could effectively intervene in Aβ1−42-induced AD
pathology in primary neurons. As soluble ordered Aβ1−42
oligomers (oAβ1−42) formed during peptide aggregation are
known to be the most toxic species to neurons,23,27,89 we
examined the toxicity of oAβ1−42 generated with/without f-
Gd@C82 NPs on neurons. First, soluble oAβ1−42 (25 μM) was
prepared with varying concentrations of f-Gd@C82 NPs (0, 50,
100, 200, and 400 μg/mL) following established protocols.19
We then exposed primary neurons to these oAβ1−42 species for
a duration of 24 h. Subsequently, the viability of the neurons
subjected to the different treatments was assessed by using
multiple methods.
Initially, a comprehensive series of characterizations was
undertaken to validate the formation of oligomers. Illustrated
in Figure S13, the incorporation of acquired AFM images,
silver-stained gel images, TEM images, and dot blot assay
images collectively serves to corroborate the presence of
oligomeric species. Subsequently, using a cell counting kit-8
(CCK8) assay, we found that oAβ1−42 visibly decreased cell
viability (∼55.7%) (Figure 5a). However, the cytotoxicity of
oAβ1−42, when generated with f-Gd@C82 NPs, was greatly
reduced, with 100 μg/mL being the optimal concentration for
detoxification. This was confirmed by lactate dehydrogenase
(LDH) release and LIVE/DEAD staining assays, which
showed significant alleviation of oAβ1−42−induced cytotoxicity
by f-Gd@C82 NPs in the primary neuron model (Figure 5b−
d). We also found that f-Gd@C82 NPs exhibited mild
cytotoxicity in primary neurons and other cells (including
astrocytes, sh-sy5y and HUVECs) (Figure S14). Furthermore,
we analyzed the neuronal morphology using scanning electron
microscopy (SEM). Our results revealed that oAβ1−42
treatment caused significant cellular body shrunk and dendrites
loss, which is strongly associated with cognitive decline in
AD.90−92 In contrast, f-Gd@C82 NPs exhibited a strong
capability in reducing Aβ1−42-induced neuron injuries (Figure
5f). These results strongly suggest that f-Gd@C82 NPs can
effectively mitigate the cytotoxic effects of oAβ1−42 by
inhibiting the aggregation of Aβ1−42 peptides and disassem-
bling these oAβ1−42 into nontoxic or much less toxic species.
Furthermore, f-Gd@C82 NPs themselves possess an excellent
biocompatibility with all tested cell lines.
In light of the potential implications surrounding the
formation of oAβ1−42 in initiating the generation of reactive
oxygen species (ROS), thereby potentially facilitating Aβ1−42-
mediated neuronal cytotoxicity, we sought to investigate
whether f-Gd@C82 NPs could mitigate the cellular oxidative
stress induced by these oligomers (Figure S15). Employing the
DCFH-DA staining method, we discerned a pronounced
increase in the ROS levels within BV2 cells upon exposure to
oAβ1−42. Upon administration of f-Gd@C82, a significant
attenuation in ROS production was observed, underscoring the
efficacy of these NPs in quelling oxidative stress. Importantly, it
is noteworthy that f-Gd@C82 itself did not incite ROS
generation in the cellular milieu. These findings present
compelling evidence for the multifunctional potential of f-Gd@
C82 NPs across diverse pathways.
Furthermore, a plethora of literature has underscored the
capacity of Aβ peptides to induce cell membrane leakage or
damage through various putative mechanisms, including the
proposed lipid-chaperone effect.28,51,93−99 In line with these
insights, we embarked on an examination of sh-sy5y cell
membrane morphology subsequent to exposure to oAβ1−42 (25
μM), oAβ1−42 generated with f-Gd@C82 NPs, and f-Gd@C82
NPs alone, utilizing SEM. Our SEM analysis unveiled that
oAβ1−42 (25 μM) elicited considerable damage to sh-sy5y cell
membranes, as evidenced by the emergence of numerous pores
(Figure S16), consistent with the lipid-chaperone hypothesis in
mediating cell membrane disruption.51 These structural
perturbations in the membranes hold the potential to induce
cell membrane leakage, thereby inducing neuronal cytotoxicity.
Intriguingly, administration of f-Gd@C82 NPs showcased an
attenuation of the detrimental effects on cell membrane
integrity induced by oAβ1−42 (Figure S16). This observation
underscores the promising protective attributes of f-Gd@C82
NPs against Aβ-triggered membrane damage, thereby present-
ing prospective neuroprotective advantages.
Interestingly, we observed a notable accumulation of f-Gd@
C82 NPs within lysosomes in the cellular uptake assays (Figure
S17). Given that neurons are capable of internalizing some
amount of Aβ via endocytosis, predominantly directing it to
lysosomes within the cell,100,101 the presence of f-Gd@C82 NPs
suggests its potential role in facilitating the intracellular
degradation of Aβ fibrils.
Following promising in vitro results, we endeavored to
ascertain the ability of f-Gd@C82 NPs to traverse the blood−
brain barrier (BBB) by utilizing Cy5-modified f-Gd@C82 NPs
for fluorescence imaging. The peak fluorescence intensity was
observed at 8 h postinjection, followed by a subsequent decline
to undetectable levels after 24 h (Figure S18). This temporal
pattern strongly suggests the rapid and efficacious BBB
penetration of f-Gd@C82 NPs, thereby laying a critical
groundwork for their prospective therapeutic utility in AD.
Regrettably, the conduct of further in vivo investigations to
evaluate the anti-AD efficacy of f-Gd@C82 NPs poses a
considerable challenge due to the current insufficient yield of
the drug, rendering it impractical to amass an adequate
quantity of f-Gd@C82 NPs for large scale in vivo
experimentation at the moment.80 Nonetheless, the successful
deployment of this hydrophilic f-Gd@C82 NP circumvents the
drawbacks associated with many other nanoinhibitors, thereby
offering a promising avenue for alleviating neuronal cytotox-

CONCLUSIONS
Inspired by the aggregation mechanism of Aβ peptides and the
architectural features of amyloid fibrils, we have rationally
designed a dual-functional nanoinhibitor based on Gd@C82
NPs, denoted as f-Gd@C82 NPs. Gd@C82 NPs are highly
stable, easy to tailor, and possess a spherical structure, making
them ideal candidates for this application. Our approach
involves introducing abundant randomly distributed hydrogen-
binding sites to the surface of f-Gd@C82 NPs. These groups
can competitively form a highly stable disordered hydrogen-
bond network with the mainchains and side chains of peptides,
inhibiting the formation of ordered intermainchain hydrogen
bonds of peptides. The spherical structure of f-Gd@C82 NPs
provides an inherent steric effect that hinders the close
association of peptides. Furthermore, the introduction of
charged −NH3
+ groups shields the specific salt-bridge
interactions between key residues on the peptides, which
play an important role in guiding the longitudinal and lateral
growth of protofibrils to mature fibrils. The remaining sp2
regions on the surface of f-Gd@C82 NPs provide binding
affinity to the hydrophobic or aromatic ring-containing
residues on the peptides, further impeding the associations of
side chains of peptides to form a hydrophobic core. As a result,
the aggregation of the peptides is significantly suppressed.
These interactions, including vdW, hydrophobic, hydrogen
bonding, and electrostatic interactions, collectively endow f-
Gd@C82 NPs with the capability of disaggregating protofibrils
and mature Aβ fibrils, thereby largely alleviating the neuronal
cytotoxicity of Aβ peptides. These functional modifications
also significantly improved the water dispersibility of f-Gd@
C82 NPs, resulting in ultrasmall diameters that closely resemble
a single molecular size. This further enhances their
bioavailability. Various cytotoxicity assessments suggest that
f-Gd@C82 NPs exhibit decent cytocompatibility with all tested
cell lines. Notably, f-Gd@C82 NPs demonstrate high efficacy in
mitigating Aβ1−42-induced cellular oxidative stress and
shielding cells from Aβ-triggered membrane damage, thereby
offering promising neuroprotective benefits. Moreover, their
rapid and effective penetration of the BBB establishes a critical
foundation for their potential therapeutic application in AD.
The designed f-Gd@C82 NPs overcomes the limitations of
other dual-functional nanoinhibitors, such as GO-based
nanosheets and g-C3N4, which rely on different mecha-
nisms.73,75,76 For instance, the highly hydrophobic nature of
GO nanosheets facilitates their ability to inhibit peptide
aggregations and disassemble mature fibrils but also results in
poor water dispersibility and acute cell membrane toxicity and
other biotoxicities. Moreover, the reported NIR-triggered
disassembly of mature fibrils on the surface of GO nanosheets
and g-C3N4 may encounter obstacles due to the inability of
NIR light beams to reach deep brain tissues. Therefore, the
successful implementation of hydrophilic f-Gd@C82 NPs,
independent of any external stimulus, to relieve neuronal
cytotoxicity in AD through maximal induction of disorder to
counteract Aβ aggregations may provide a promising direction
for the design of AD management therapy based on the
antiaggregation strategy.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Preparation of Functionalized Gd@C82 Nanoparticles
(f-Gd@C82 NPs). Endohedral metallofullerene Gd@C82 NPs
was purchased from Xiamen Funano New Material Technol-
ogy Company and was confirmed to be of 99% purity, and
samples were dried under vacuum at 60 °C for 24 h (h) before
the measurement. Chemicals and biological reagents were
purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and Beijing Chemical Reagent
(Beijing, China) and used as received. Milli-Q ultrapure water
was used for all experiments.
The functional groups onto Gd@C82 NPs were performed
following a reported procedure102 by a one-step reaction under
mild condition. Briefly, Gd@C82 NPs (5 mg) was added to 6
mL of 30 wt % H2O2 and 4 mL of 18 wt % NH4OH. The
mixtures were vigorously stirred at 50−55 °C under ambient
conditions for 10 h. At the end of the reaction, the black
suspension turned to a brown solution with slight insoluble.
After filtration, the filtrate was added with 10 mL of ethanol,
which was used as a poor solvent, to the concentrated solution
(1 mL) to precipitate the solutes. The suspension was
centrifuged at 10 000 rpm for 1 min, and the supernatant
was discarded. The yellowish precipitate was washed twice
with ethanol (10 mL) and dried under a vacuum at 50 °C
overnight. The compound is stored at 4 °C and protected from
light.
f-Gd@C82 NPs Characterization. The morphology and
size of f-Gd@C82 NPs were investigated by transmission
electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Talos F200X). The Fourier
transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of the carbon particles were
obtained with a Bruker Fourier Transform Infrared Spec-
trometer (Hyperion). The X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS)
were measured by a KRATOS Axis ultra DLD X-ray
photoelectron spectrometer. Atomic Force Microscopy
(AFM) was carried out on Bruker (Icon).

Primary Neuron Culture. Mouse primary neuron cultures
were prepared from embryonic day 18 C57BL/6 mice as
previously described.112 All animal procedures were in
compliance with the policies of the Soochow University
Animal Care and Use Committee (SUACUC). In brief,

culture medium was replaced with fresh medium every other
day. This method resulted in cultures highly enriched with
neurons (∼90% purity) with minimal astrocyte and microglial
contamination. We performed morphometric characterization
by SEM.
Primary Astrocyte Culture. Primary astrocyte cultures
were prepared from the cerebral cortex of 1- to 3-day-old
C57BL/6J mice described previously.113 In brief, dissociated
cortical cells were suspended in DMEM (Gibico, Life
Technology) containing 25 mM glucose, 4 mM glutamine, 1
mM sodium pyruvate, and 10% FBS and plated on PDL-coated
75-cm2 flasks at a density of 6 × 105 cells cm−2. Microglial and
monolayers of type 1 astrocytes were obtained 12−14 days
after plating. Monolayers of type 1 astrocytes were obtained
12−14 days after plating. Nonastrocytic cells were detached
from the flasks by shaking and removed by changing the
medium. Astrocytes were dissociated by trypsinization and
then reseeded on an uncoated 96 wells plate. These cells were
allowed to grow to 80−90% confluence before exposure to f-
Gd@C82 NPs treatment.
Preparation of Oligomeric Aβ1−42. oAβ1−42 prepared

Aβ1−42 samples were resuspended in 5 mM DMSO, diluted to
25 μM with neuron medium in the absence or presence of
different concentrations of f-Gd@C82 NPs. After incubated for
24 h at 4 °C, samples were added to neuron culture to
detected cytotoxicity.

Two different sets of simulation systems were prepared for
the computational study: the first one was used to explore the
effect of f-Gd@C82 NPs on amyloid self-aggregation of Aβ1−42
peptides; the second one was for the investigation of whether f-

configurations: one contains two separated peptides named
the Aβ1−42-dimer and served as the control. The other one
named the “Aβ1−42-dimer + f-Gd@C82” system also consists of
two separated peptides but with the presence of two f-Gd@C82
NPs. In the control system, two peptides were separated at
least 1.5 nm from each other were solvated into a cubic water
box (with a box size of 9.0 nm × 9.0 nm × 9.0 nm) containing
23 274 water molecules. The setup protocol of the “Aβ1−42-
dimer + f-Gd@C82” system is very similar to the control one,
except with the random addition of two f-Gd@C82 molecules.
Then, the Aβ-dimer + f-Gd@C82 complex was solvated into a
cubic water box with the size of 9.0 × 9.0 × 9.0 nm, containing
23 153 water molecules. In order to mimic the physiological
conditions of the human body, both the control and “Aβ1−42-
dimer + f-Gd@C82” simulation systems were neutralized, and
0.15 mol/L NaCl was added, which gave the total of 71 214
and 71 166 atoms. The detailed illustration of the initial system
is shown in Figure 3a. The initial isolated Aβ1−42 used in our
molecular dynamics simulation were obtained from the Protein
Data Bank (PDB ID: 1Z0Q).115 This conformation was
observed by NMR and described as an intermediate state
between the α and β conformations of Aβ1−42. In the second
set of simulations, two different protofibril systems were set up
in the absence and presence of a group of 5 f-Gd@C82
molecules (Figure 4a). Each system was named as the
“Aβ1−42 protofibril (pentamer)” system and the “Aβ1−42
protofibril (pentamer) + f-Gd@C82” system. Both protofibril
(pentamer) systems were solvated into 10.0 nm × 10.0 nm ×
10.0 nm water boxes, containing 31 487 and 31 118 water
molecules, respectively. The initial model used for the fibril
simulations was a portion of published protofibril structure
(PDBID: 5OQV) obtained from cryo-EM.64
MD Simulation Methods. All MD simulations were
carried out with the GROMACS-4.6.6 software package,116
together with the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field117 set in
explicit TIP3P water solvent.118 Configurations were visually
presented using the VMD software (v1.9.3).119,120 Following
similar protocols used in previous studies,121,122 each solvated
system was first minimized for 2,000 steps. Then, the systems
were equilibrated for 2 ns at 310 K and 1 atm, by using the V-
rescale thermostat123 and Parrinello−Rahman coupling
methods124 with position restraints on all heavy atoms. During
the production runs, the long-range electrostatic interactions
were treated with the particle-mesh Ewald method,125 The
short-ranged vdW interactions were treated with a cutoff of 1.2
nm. All solute bonds were constrained to their equilibrium
values using the LINCS algorithm,126 and water geometry was
constrained by employing the SETTLE algorithm.127 Periodic
boundary conditions were applied in all directions. For each
type of system, three independent 1000 or 2500 ns trajectories
were collected for analysis.
Analysis. The secondary structure was calculated using the
DSSP program.88 A hydrogen bond is defined when the
separation between a donor and an acceptor is closer than 0.35
nm and the donor−hydrogen−acceptor angle is larger than
150°. The mean intermolecular hydrogen bonds formed
between side chain/mainchain of Aβ1−42 and f-Gd@C82 NPs
were computed by using the g_hbond program. The contact
ratio between f-Gd@C82 NPs and Aβ1−42 fibril was counted in
the same manner as in our previous studies121,122 when the
distance between any heavy atom from a Aβ1−42 target residue
and the f-Gd@C82 NPs is within 0.5 nm.

independent groups were analyzed for statistical significance
using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test. Data sets with more
than two groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA,
followed by Bonferroni or Tukey post hoc test. All p values
below or equal to 0.05 were considered significant. *p < 0.05,
**p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001.

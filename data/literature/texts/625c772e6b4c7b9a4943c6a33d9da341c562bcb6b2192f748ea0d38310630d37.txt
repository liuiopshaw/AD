ABSTRACT: Biomolecule-protected gold nanostructures show good performance in
biomedical applications. However, precise control over gold nanocluster (AuNC)
preparation with biomolecules remains challenging. Here, we develop a simple near-
infrared (NIR)-light-assisted method for in situ reduction of antimicrobial peptide (AMP)-
protected AuNCs. Take advantage of the high photothermal conversion eﬃciency of the
conjugated polymer (CP) upon NIR light irradiation, we promote the rapid reduction of
AuNCs by the AMP on the surface of the CP. The ﬂuorescent properties of the AuNCs
were improved owing to the formation of a unique Au(0)NC@Au(I)AMP core−shell
nanostructure. This nanostructure is attributed to the rapid reduction of Au(0) and collision
and fusion of Au(0) at high temperatures. Integrating antibacterial AMPs, ﬂuorescent
AuNCs, and photothermal CPs, the composites facilitated diﬀerent killing mechanisms for
both bacteria and cancer cells. This material system provides an all-in-one strategy for the
stepwise killing of cancer cells and bacterial infection.
KEYWORDS: conjugated polymer, ﬂuorescence, gold nanocluster, near-infrared light, bacterial killing
■INTRODUCTION
The electronic, photonic, magnetic, and thermal properties of
metal nanostructures can be tailored simply by adjusting their
size and composition.1−5 The tunable properties of metal
nanostructures make them promising for implementation in a
broad range of bioapplications.6−11 Unlike typical larger
nanostructures, ultrasmall metal nanoclusters are composed
of several to a hundred atoms protected by surface thiol
ligands.12−14 This size regime endows the metal nanoclusters
with discrete energy levels.15,16 Electron transfer from ligands
to the metal core display luminescence, which makes such
metal nanoclusters attractive probes for bioimaging.17−19
Ligands play an important role in regulating the performance
of such metal nanoclusters. Biomolecules, such as DNA, RNA,
proteins, and peptides act as both ligands and reducing agents
in metal nanocluster preparation owing to their abundant
functional groups.20−23 Meanwhile, their biocompatibility can
extend applications of metal nanoclusters into the biological
and medical ﬁelds.24 More importantly, the bioactivity of
biomaterials can be inherited to the metal nanoclusters to
directly achieve metal nanocluster functionalization. Antimi-
crobial peptides (AMPs) usually consist of cationic or
hydrophobic blocks, which disrupt the microbial membrane
and lead to a low probability of drug resistance.24,25 Owing to
the reducibility of cysteine in AMPs, metal nanoclusters can be
fabricated that exhibit both ﬂuorescence emission and bacterial
killing.26 Thus, this strategy is a simple way to achieve multiple
functionalization in a single metal nanocluster through the use
of bioactive macromolecule as ligands.
However, the participation of biomolecules allows metal
nanocluster preparation to be performed in a mild aqueous
phase and requires a reaction time of tens of hours.27,28 To
promote reduction and nucleation during the process,
additional energy from microwaves29,30 or ultrasound31 is
input, which considerably reduces the preparation time of the
metal nanoclusters. Light can also be used as a source of
additional energy in the preparation of metal nanoclusters. As a
high-density energy carrier, near-infrared (NIR) light is capable
of eﬃciently transferring heat by photothermal conversion to
create high local temperatures32−34 that might accelerate metal
nanocluster formation. Conjugated polymers (CPs) with π-
conjugated domains possess good light-harvesting, adjustable
band gaps, and eﬃcient photothermal conversion.35−43
Combined with tissue penetration depths of up to several
centimeters of near-infrared light,44 these features promote
CPs to be highlighted in frontiers for tumor diagnosis and
treatment, such as afterglow ﬂuorescence imaging, photo-
acoustic imaging, and two-photon photodynamic and photo-
thermal therapy.45,46 Through band gap engineering, the

light and photothermal CPs represents a system for light-
assisted preparation of metal nanoclusters. Moreover, as
organic optical materials, CPs are free of metal-ion-induced

Thus, the presence of CPs not only harnesses light energy to

multifunctional composite systems by providing a photo-

Recently, microbial infections have been shown to destroy
the distinct boundary between the host and commensal
bacteria, leading to dysfunction and even cancer.48,49 Several
clinical trials have conﬁrmed that certain pathogen infections
cause the emergence and progression of cancers through
immune response mechanisms.50,51 Thus, integrating anti-
bacterial activity into cancer treatments might inhibit the
development of cancer and improve treatment eﬃciency.
Several photothermal platforms based on reduced graphene
oxides, gold nanostructures, and polymers were designed for
the treatment of skin infections.52,53 These wound healing
platforms enable the release of antibiotics and direct thermal
killing of bacteria at the infected sites through the photo-
thermal heating eﬀect. In the development of all-in-one
therapeutic/diagnostic (i.e., theranostic) methods,54,55 multi-
functional nanomaterials are essential for multimode diagnosis
and therapy. Diﬀerent from single sterilization, herein, we
develop a NIR-light-assisted method for in situ reduction of
antimicrobial peptide (AMP)-protected gold nanoclusters
(AuNCs). The integration of ﬂuorescent, antibacterial, and
photothermal properties into a single multifunctional compo-
site enables them to eﬃciently treat cancer cells and bacterial
infections. As illustrated in Scheme 1, nanoparticles of the
conjugated polymer PDPP-DBT (named as CNPs) were ﬁrst
prepared as photothermal conversion agents. The AMP
tachyplesin-I is rich in amine, phenolic, and thiol groups,
which was directly modiﬁed on CNPs. Upon NIR light
irradiation, the Au ions are rapidly reduced and stabilized by
AMP on the surface of the CNPs (Scheme 1b) to form
ﬂuorescent AuNCs. The prepared composites were sequen-
tially used in cell imaging with ﬂuorescent AuNCs, bacterial
killing AMP ligands, and cancer cell killing with the
photothermal CNPs. This stepwise treatment might enable
simultaneous diagnosis and therapy of cancer and bacterial
infections (Scheme 1c). Such multifunctional composites
might be useful in research and treatment of tumors in clinical
applications.

Materials and Methods. The conjugated polymer PDPP-DBT
was synthesized according to previously reported methods.42 The
antimicrobial peptide (AMP) tachyplesin-I (purity 95%) was
purchased from Guoping Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. Poly(styrene-co-
maleic anhydride) (PSMA, average molecular weight (Mn) of ∼1700),
gold chloride trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O), N-hydroxysuccinimide
(NHS), 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochlor-
ide (EDC), 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium
bromide (MTT), and all other chemicals were purchased from Sigma-
Aldrich. The human colon cancer cells HT-29 were obtained from the
Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. All of the culture medium and
buﬀer for the experiments were purchased from GE Healthcare. All
reagents were used directly as received.
The morphology of synthesized samples was examined with a ﬁeld-

cells were acquired on a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSCM,
Olympus FV1200). The ﬂuorescence images of the bacteria and cells
were recorded on an Olympus 1X73 ﬂuorescence microscope. A
Spectra MAX 340PC plate reader was used to perform the

Preparation of the CNP@AMP-AuNCs. The PDPP-DBT
nanoparticles (CNPs) were prepared as previously reported.38 Brieﬂy,
PDPP-DBT and PSMA were dissolved and mixed in tetrahydrofuran
(THF) at concentrations of 50 μg/mL and 0.5 mg/mL, respectively.
The mixture was ﬁltered with 0.22 μm syringe ﬁlters. Then, 1 mL of
the mixture was injected into 9 mL of ultrapure water with 20 W
sonication for 1 min. The suspension was stirred and bubbled with N2
to yield PDPP-DBT nanoparticles.
The peptide-modiﬁed precursor was synthesized by the EDC/NHS
reaction. Brieﬂy, 23 μL of 1.5 mg/mL EDC and 28 μL of 1.5 mg/mL
NHS were injected into 1 mL of CNPs and gently stirred in the dark
for 30 min. Then, 130 μL of 5 mg/mL AMP solution was added into
the mixture. The mixture was further stirred for 12 h to prepare the
CNP@AMP precursor.
In a typical test, 13 μL of 25 mM HAuCl4 aqueous solution was
quickly injected into 1 mL of prepared CNP@AMP solutions with
vigorous stirring for 1 min. Then, 200 μL of 0.1 M phosphate-buﬀered
saline (PBS) (pH = 9.0) buﬀer was added to adjust the solution pH.
The sample was sealed and illuminated for 1 h with an 808 nm NIR
light source at a power density of 5 W/cm2. The as-synthesized
CNP@AMP-AuNCs were concentrated by high-speed centrifugation
(10 000 rpm, 30 min) to remove free molecules. The synthesized
CNP@AMP-AuNCs were stored at 4 °C. For the control group, two
mixtures were prepared by the same method and heated to 37 °C for
1 and 12 h, respectively, without infrared illumination.
To prepare AMP-AuNCs, 130 μL of 5 mg/mL AMP was diluted
into 1 mL of ultrapure water. Then, 1 mL of aqueous HAuCl4
solution was quickly injected into the 1 mL of AMP solutions. The
solution pH was also adjusted by the PBS (pH = 9.0) buﬀer. The
mixture was sealed and heated to 37 °C for 12 h without infrared
illumination to produce the AMP-AuNCs.

Cell and Bacteria Culture. The HT-29 cells were maintained in
RPMI 1640 medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and cultured
at 37 °C in a humidiﬁed atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Escherichia
coli (E. coli) were seeded on a solid Luria−Bertani (LB) agar plate and

For the cells and bacteria mixture, the 100 000 HT-29 cells and 100
μL of E. coli (OD600nm = 1) were mixed in RPMI 1640 medium with

containing 5% CO2.
Antibacterial Assay of the CNP@AMP-AuNCs. The E. coli
solution was diluted to OD600nm = 1 in PBS. The bacterial solution
(100 μL) was mixed with CNP@AMP-AuNCs solutions with ﬁnal
concentrations of 0, 0.05, 0.15, and 0.30 mg/mL, respectively. The
mixture was cultured at 37 °C under gently shaking for 1 h. Then, the
bacterial solutions were further diluted 100 000-fold with PBS. A 100
μL portion of the solutions was spread on an LB solid agar plate and
cultured at 37 °C for 12 h. The number of colony-forming units
(CFUs) was counted, and the CFU reduction rate was calculated.
Cytotoxicity Assay of CNP@AMP-AuNCs and Photothermal
Ablation of Cancer Cells. The cytotoxicity of the CNP@AMP-
AuNCs to HT-29 cells was evaluated through a typical MTT assay.
Brieﬂy, HT-29 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a concentration

concentrations (0−1 mg/mL) of CNP@AMP-AuNCs in the prepared
culture medium for 24 h. The medium was replaced with 100 μL of
0.5 mg/mL MTT and the cells were further cultured for 4 h. Dimethyl
sulfoxide (DMSO, 100 μL) was used to dissolve reduced formazan,
and the absorbance at 570 nm was measured to calculate the cell
viability.

cells were incubated with various concentrations of CNP@AMP-

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
NIR-Light-Assisted in Situ Reduction of AuNCs. The
PDPP-DBT CNPs with carboxyl groups were prepared with a
negative charge of −46 mV by coprecipitation with poly-
(styrene-co-maleic anhydride) (PSMA) (Table S1). The
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image in Figure 1a
shows their good spherical morphologies. The hydration
diameter of the PDPP-DBT CNPs was measured to be 63 nm
(Figure 1d). The donor−acceptor alternating blocks were
designed to give the PDPP-DBT CNPs broad absorption in
the NIR region. As shown in Figure S1, the PDPP-DBT CNPs
had strong absorbance from 700 to 870 nm. After NIR light
irradiation (808 nm) at an intensity of 5 W/cm2 for 20 min,
the temperature of the aqueous solution containing CNP@
AMP nanocomposites increased to 74 °C (Figure S2a). The
photothermal eﬃciency was calculated to be 34.2%, as in
Figure S2b. Thus, the nanocomposites can serve as photo-
thermal conversion agents to eﬃciently transform NIR light
energy into heat and create a local thermal ﬁeld, which
promoted the AuNC preparation. AMP tachyplesin-I consist-
ing of 17 amino acids56 was selected as a reducing agent and
functional ligand to prepare AuNCs. To make full use of the
local heat, the AMP was bioconjugated with CNPs by a typical
carbodiimide reaction between the abundant amino groups of
the AMP and the carboxyl groups of CNPs. As shown in
Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra (Figure S3), the
absorbance peaks at 1028, 1540, 1646, and 1395 cm−1 are,
respectively, assigned to the stretching vibrations of C−N, N−
H, and CO and the bending vibration of N−H. These
features indicate the formation of covalent linkages between
the AMP and CNPs.57 The obtained CNP@AMP maintained
a spherical morphology with a hydration diameter of 66 nm
(Figure 1b−e). The size of the nanocomposites increased (∼3
nm) after the grafting of the AMP. Because arginine in the
tachyplesin-I chain confers a positive charge to the AMP, the ζ-
potential reversed from −46 to 37 mV (Table S1), which
further conﬁrmed the bioconjuation in CNP@AMP. Owing to
the reducibility of thiol, phenolic, and amine groups of the
AMP,58 the Au ions were reduced in situ to AuNCs on the
CNP@AMP under 808 nm NIR light irradiation for 1 h. The
CNP@AMP-AuNCs had a deﬁned morphology (Figure 1c).
The higher contrast in these SEM images might be attributed
to the presence of highly conductive AuNCs. There were no
obvious diﬀerences in the hydration diameter (Figure 1f),
whereas the ζ-potential of the CNP@AMP-AuNCs changed to
be +44 mV. The transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
image (Figure 1g) of the synthesized CNP@AMP-AuNCs
showed that a large number of AuNCs were uniformly
embedded in the outer part of the nanoparticles, where the
peptide was likely distributed. The size distribution of AuNCs
in Figure S4 shows that the average diameter of AuNCs in
CNP@AMP-AuNCs was 2.1 ± 0.2 nm. Next, we conducted X-
ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis. The presence
of Au and S in the CNP@AMP-AuNCs was conﬁrmed, as
shown in Figure 1h,i. High-resolution spectra of the Au 4f
region showed that the binding energies of electrons in the
4f5/2 and 4f7/2 orbitals of Au were 87.55 and 83.85 eV,
respectively (Figure 1h). These peaks were ﬁtted and assigned
to Au 4f5/2 (87.50 eV) and Au 4f7/2 (83.80 eV) of Au(0) and
Au 4f5/2 (88.25 eV) and Au 4f7/2 (84.55 eV) of Au(I).59,60 The

Optical Properties of NIR-Light-Assisted CNP@AMP-
AuNCs. We next examined the properties of CNP@AMP-
AuNCs. As shown in Figure 2a, the CNP@AMP-AuNCs had
ﬂuorescence emission at 670 nm, which is consistent with the
free AMP-AuNCs prepared at 37 °C for 12 h (Figure S5). This
result indicates that the in situ preparation of the AuNCs did
not aﬀect their ﬂuorescent properties. As a control, we
prepared CNP@AMP-AuNCs at 37 °C for 12 h without NIR
light irradiation. The control sample had very weak emission
from the AuNCs. The absolute quantum yields of CNP@
AMP-AuNCs and the control group were 0.67% and 0.05%,
respectively (Figure 2b). However, their absorption spectra
had almost the same shape (Figure 2c). Compared with the
absorption of CNP@AMP in the range of 700−900 nm that
attributed to CNP absorbance, we found no changes. This
result conﬁrms that the AuNC formation did not aﬀect the
absorption and the structure of CNPs. The absorption of
CNP@AMP at 280 nm was attributed to the AMP, whereas
both the CNP@AMP-AuNCs and control group had increased
absorption and scattering, which we attribute to the absorption
of AMP-ligand-protected AuNCs. To gain further insight into
the enhanced emission of the CNP@AMP-AuNCs, we
performed XPS analysis of the control group. When the
reaction time reached 1 h, the binding energies of Au were
located at 88.25 eV (4f5/2) and 84.65 eV (4f7/2) (Figure S6a).
The binding energies of Au for the control group with 12 h
reaction were 87.65 eV (4f5/2) and 83.95 eV (4f7/2). (Figure
S6b). We ﬁtted these peaks to Au(I) and Au(0) and calculated
the ratio of Au(0) to Au(I). As the reaction time increased, the
Au(0)/Au(I) ratio increased from 0.077 to 1.1 (Table S2).
According to the general mechanism,63 Au(III) was ﬁrst
reduced to Au(I) and formed Au(I)−X complexes (X: thiol,
carboxyl, and amine groups). Then, the residues of tryptophan,
phenylalanine, and tyrosine reduced Au(I) to Au(0) and form
Au(0)-on-Au(I)−thiolate intermediates. The collision and
fusion of Au(0) induced the formation of Au(0)NC@
Au(I)AMP core−shell nanostructures with ﬂuorescence
emission.63 Thus, the Au(0)NC@Au(I)AMP core−shell
nanostructure formation was accompanied by an increase of
the Au(0)/Au(I) ratio. For our CNP@AMP-AuNCs, the
Au(0)/Au(I) ratio was 4.9, which was much higher than that
of the control group. Upon irradiation with NIR light, the high

Thus, CNP@AMP-AuNCs with enhanced emission and a
relatively high QY were rapid obtained by the NIR-light-
assisted method. Next, we evaluated the photothermal
properties of the nanocomposites. As shown in Figure 2d, a
sharp rise in the temperature occurred when the sample was
illuminated under 808 nm NIR light irradiation at a low power
density (1 W/cm2). The temperature increased by 20 °C in 5
min and exceeded 50 °C in 15 min. This photothermal eﬀect
can be comparable with common conjugated polymers, such as
polyaniline65 and polypyrrol.66 The photothermal eﬃciency
was 34.5%, which is consistent with the results for CNP@
AMP. After four cycles of NIR light irradiation at 808 nm,
good repeatability was observed (Figure 2e). These results
indicate that CNP@AMP-AuNCs maintained the excellent
photothermal properties of the CNPs that exhibit eﬃcient
photothermal eﬀect and better cycling stability compared with
those of the commercial NIR dye, indocyanine green (ICG),
for clinical phototherapy.42 Moreover, we measured the
ﬂuorescent emission of CNP@AMP-AuNCs after each cycle.
The emission intensities of CNP@AMP-AuNCs were almost
unchanged. Meanwhile, the SEM image (Figure S7a) and
TEM image (Figure S7b) exhibit no discernable morphological
or structural change after laser irradiation, which indicated
their good thermal stability. The above results highlight the
good ﬂuorescence and photothermal performances of CNP@
AMP-AuNCs.
Multiple Biological Applications of CNP@AMP-AuNCs
Composites. Because the CNP@AMP-AuNCs were com-
posed of AMP (tachyplesin-I), we expected the nanocomposite
to retain some antibacterial activity from the AMP. Thus, we
selected Gram-negative bacteria E. coli as a model to detect the
antibacterial activity of the CNP@AMP-AuNCs. As shown in
Figure 3a, the killing eﬃciency improved with the increase of
the CNP@AMP-AuNCs concentration. At a concentration of
0.3 mg/mL, the CFU number decreased by 5 orders of
magnitude and the killing eﬃciency reached nearly 100%
(Figure 3b). Hence, the CNP@AMP-AuNCs maintained the
bioactivity of the AMP. The E. coli strain is associated with
human colorectal cancer.50 Thus, human colon cancer cell line
(HT-29) was selected as a cancer cell model. The composites
with a diameter about 66 nm can be taken up by cells through
endocytosis.67 We evaluated the cytotoxicity of CNP@AMP-
AuNCs against HT-29 cells. More than 89% of the cells
remained when incubated with CNP@AMP-AuNCs for 24 h
in the concentration range from 0 to 0.5 mg/mL (Figure 3c).
The CNP@AMP-AuNCs displayed less toxicity to HT-29 cells
than to E. coli. The toxic eﬀects of CNP@AMP-AuNCs on
bacteria and cells were notably diﬀerent, which we attributed
to the sterilization mechanism of tachyplesin-I. The outer
membranes of the bacteria have a high content of lip-
opolysaccharide (LPS), which is absent from human cells.
Tachyplesin-I strongly binds to LPS and causes ﬂipping of
phospholipid molecules, which impairs membrane integrity
and ultimately inactivates bacteria.63,68 Owing to the strong
emission of CNP@AMP-AuNCs, we used a confocal

Stepwise Killing of Cancer Cells and Bacterial
Infection. Next, we used the CNP@AMP-AuNCs to achieve
stepwise killing of cancer cells and bacterial infection. The HT-
29 cells and E. coli coexist in the intestinal environment.50
Hence, we cultivated them together to evaluate the killing
eﬀect of CNP@AMP-AuNCs. Within 4 h, the CNP@AMP-
AuNCs in the solution ﬁrst interacted with E. coli and red
emission of CNP@AMP-AuNCs was observed from E. coli
(Figure 4). DAPI/PI staining results indicated that the E. coli
were dead, whereas the HT-29 cells remained alive.
Importantly, the CNP@AMP-AuNCs eﬃciently killed E. coli
following 1 h incubation. Then, dim ﬂuorescent signals from
the CNP@AMP-AuNCs can be detected in tumor cells after 2
h incubation. The red ﬂuorescence become strong as culture
time increases to 4 h and stronger after 8 h incubation. During
this period, no orange-red signal of PI was observed from HT-
29 cells. Only after NIR light irradiation for 5 min were the
HT-29 cells killed, as indicated by orange-red ﬂuorescence.
Thus, the cancer cells with bacterial infection were controlled
with a single CNP@AMP-AuNCs. The treatment was
performed according to this time course. First, bacteria in
the cellular environment were killed by the antibacterial
activity of the AMP in CNP@AMP-AuNCs. Then, the strong
emission of AuNCs in CNP@AMP-AuNCs was used to trace
cancer cells. Finally, NIR-light-triggered precise killing of
cancer cells was achieved based on the photothermal
properties of CNPs in CNP@AMP-AuNCs. This composite
provides a prototype single-administration treatment, for
bacterial killing, cell tracking, and cancer cell killing.
■CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we developed a NIR-light-assisted in situ
reduction of CNP@AMP-AuNCs to form multifunctional
composites for stepwise killing of cancer cells and bacterial
infection. The photothermal properties of the CNPs enabled
NIR light irradiation to promote the formation of AuNCs by
increasing the local reaction temperature. This increased
temperature promoted the reduction of Au(0) by AMP and
accelerated Au(0) collisions; thus, a unique Au(0)NC@
Au(I)AMP core−shell nanostructure with a high QY was
obtained in situ. The CNP@AMP-AuNCs successful inte-
grated the antibacterial activity of the AMP, the photothermal
properties of CNP, and the strong emission of AuNCs. The
versatile nature of CNP@AMP-AuNCs gives them diﬀerent
killing mechanisms for both bacteria and cancer cells. This
system provides an all-in-one therapeutic/diagnostic (thera-
nostic) material for stepwise treatment of cancer and bacterial
infection. These ﬁndings may help promote research and
treatment of tumors in clinical applications.

A B S T R A C T
Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) as a pivotal coenzyme plays a central role in the pathogenesis of 
various inflammatory diseases. However, both natural and artificial NADH oxidase (NOX) systems exhibit limited 
efficacy in NAD+ regeneration. Here, we developed a RuCo heterogeneous dual atom metallozymes (DAMs) with 
over-natural NOX activity through spin-mediated and proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET). Under simulated 
inflammatory microenvironment, DAMs exhibited a catalytic efficiency (Kcat/Km) of 0.99 μM−1min−1, surpassing 
reported artificial enzymes by ~9–99 times. Additionally, its quantitative NOX activity reached 2139 U/mg, 
about 6–18 times that of common microbial enzymes. The experimental and computational studies revealed that 
Co not only modulated the spin state of Ru from low-spin to middle-spin, but also its electrophilic microenvi­
ronment facilitated oxygen adsorption to further modulate electronic behavior of Ru with high-spin. Thus, Ru- 
Co-O as high active sites preferred to trap electron and proton from NADH and acidic conditions via a PECT 
mechanism. In a skin injury model, DAMs alleviated inflammation and promoted skin regeneration by restoring 
the NAD+/NADH ratio, enhancing energy metabolism, and driving macrophage anti-inflammatory polarization. 
Notably, no relapse was observed within 20 days post-treatment, highlighting the long-lasting therapeutic impact 
of DAMs.
1. Introduction
Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) serves as an essential 
coenzyme within living organisms, and the lack of NAD+ is closely 
associated with the pathogenesis and progression of various major 
human diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, metabolic 
syndrome, atherosclerosis, and autoimmune diseases [1–4]. Natural 
NADH oxidase (NOX) and hemoglobin demonstrate significant catalytic 
potential in the NAD+ regeneration pathways [5]. Currently, the pri­
mary source of these natural enzymes relies on extraction from specific 
bacteria (e.g., Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, Enterococcus spp.) [6]. How­
ever, the extraction process is not only complex and expensive but also 
the obtained natural enzymes with inherent drawbacks such as poor 
stability and difficulties in long-term storage [7,8]. In contrast, artificial 
enzymes with tunable catalytic activity, functional diversity, high sta­
bility, ease of storage, and lower production costs are expected to 
become a powerful alternative to natural NOX, offering new viable 
pathways for NAD+ regeneration and related disease treatments [7, 
9–13].
Although significant progress has been made in the development of 
artificial nanozymes with NOX-like catalytic activity, their catalytic ef­
ficiency still requires further improvement. In nature, nonheme iron 
metalloenzymes use dioxygen to generate high-spin Fe(IV)=O inter­
mediate for high catalytic performance, such as the ubiquitous

In this work, we developed a RuCo heterogeneous dual-atom met­
allozyme (DAMs) that achieved NOX-like activity surpassing natural 
enzymes via the synergistic interplay of spin manipulation and the PCET 
mechanism (Fig. 1a). Under simulated inflammatory microenvironment 
conditions, DAMs demonstrated a catalytic efficiency (Kcat/Km) of 
0.99 μM−1⋅min−1, with a quantified NOX activity reaching 2139 U/mg, 
substantially exceeding those of reported artificial enzymes and 
commonly used microbial enzymes. Superconducting quantum inter­
ference device (SQUID) results and density functional theory (DFT) 
calculations revealed that the incorporation of Co not only promoted a 
spin-state transition of Ru from low-spin to middle-spin but also opti­
mized the high-spin electronic behavior of Ru by modulating its elec­
trophilic microenvironment to facilitate O2 adsorption. The Ru-Co-O 
served as a highly active site that efficiently captured electrons and 
protons from NADH under acidic conditions through a PCET mecha­
nism, leading to efficient NAD+ regeneration (Fig. 1b). In vivo 
experiments further demonstrated that DAMs promoted tissue repair by 
restoring the NAD+/NADH ratio, improving energy metabolism, and 
driving macrophage anti-inflammatory polarization, with sustained 
therapeutic effects and no observed recurrence. This work pioneers the 
integration of spin-state regulation and the PCET mechanism into the 
design of artificial enzymes, offering a new strategy for developing 
highly efficient bioinspired catalytic systems.

to the increased active sites. Based on the above analysis, the structural 
diagram of DAMs was drawn (Fig. 2d).
The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement spectra 
affirmed the presence of C, N, Ru and Co, matching with the TEM results 
(Figure S5). The deconvolution of the Ru 3d XPS spectrum of DAMs 
exposed characteristic peaks for C-C, C-N, and O-C––O bonds 
(Figure S6a). The binding energies of Ru and Co suggested the concur­
rent presence of Ru3+, Ru4+, Co2+ and Co3+ oxidation states (Figure S6b- 
c) [29–32]. Compared to SAMs, we found that the binding energies of Ru 
were shifted towards lower binding energies in DAMs, indicating elec­
tron transfer from Co to Ru (Figure S6c). The above results demonstrated 
a strong interaction between Ru and Co atoms, which further confirmed 
the formation of Ru-Co mated-atom centers. Additionally, the N 1 s XPS 
spectrum of DAMs displayed four peaks, corresponding to pyridinic N, 
pyrrolic N, graphitic N and M-N (Figure S7) [33–35]. The content of M-N 
implied that DAMs had a high metal atom loading capacity, potentially 
conferring superior catalytic performance (Figure S8). To uncover the 
electronic structure of DAMs, a temperature-dependent magnetization 
(M-T) test was performed over a temperature range of 2 K to 300 K 
under an applied field of 500 Oe (Fig. 2f and Figure S9). The Curie 
constant C was determined by applying the Curie-Weiss law, χ = C/(T - 
θ), to the linear segments of the data above 150 K in Fig. 2g and h, where 
C represented the Curie constant and θ denoted the Curie-Weiss tem­
perature. Utilizing the relationship the effective magnetic moments 
(μeff) for DAMs and SAMs, were calculated to be 4.33 μB and 2.77 μB, 
respectively [15, 36–39]. From these calculations, it can be deduced that 
the optimized DAMs possess 3.44 unpaired d electrons, a number 
notably higher than the 1.94 found in SAMs (Fig. 2i). This difference 
suggested that the introduction of Co atoms had a significant impact on 
controlling the electronic structure of these materials. Furthermore, 
these findings supported the inference the electron configuration of the 
d orbitals in both DAMs and SAMs (Fig. 2g-h and Figure S10). A higher 
count of unpaired electrons implied a greater facility for electron 
transfer between active sites and substrates, which in turn hastens the 
catalytic process. Therefore, DAMs featured atomically paired Ru-Co 
centers in a defect-rich graphite carbon lattice, where the enhanced 
spin-state and electronic interactions synergistically conferred superior 
catalytic performance.
2.2. Evaluation of catalytic activities of DAMs
NOX, a pivotal oxidoreductase, played a crucial role in the metabolic 
processes of organisms. When it comes to the oxidation of NADH, DAMs 
outperformed SAMs in catalytic efficiency (Fig. 3a). This led to a decline 
in the absorption peak at 340 nm (Fig. 3b). The NOX-like activity 
demonstrated a concentration-dependent pattern. Specifically, as the 
concentration of DAMs rose, the amount of NADH oxidized per unit time 
increases (Figure S11). Nonheme iron enzymes in nature use dioxygen to 
generate high-spin iron(IV)=O species for oxygenation reactions [14]. 
Zhang et al. showed that bilateral adsorption of oxygen atoms lowers the 
reaction energy barrier [40]. Thus, dioxygen adsorption promoted 
high-spin species formation and enhanced biocatalysis. The PCET 
mechanism was pivotal in the catalytic activity of NADH oxidase, as it 
concurrently transfers e- and H+, markedly lowering the activation en­
ergy and enhancing both selectivity and catalytic efficiency [41]. 
Consequently, O2, H2O2, and H+, acting as the enhancement factor of 
NOX, can efficiently promote the oxidation of NADH.
In the experiment, when O2, H2O2, and H+ were added sequentially, 
the NOX-like activity of DAMs increased remarkably (Fig. 3c). The 
experimental outcomes clearly demonstrated that as the environmental 
acidity intensifies, the activity of NOX became more robust (Fig. 3d). 
Moreover, under the pH conditions characteristic of both normal and 
inflamed skin, this activity remained relatively high and stable. Pre­
sumably, this was due to the synergistic effect between the high-spin 
characteristics of DAMs and the enhancement factor, which acceler­
ated electron transfer. Kinetic analysis revealed that in the simulated 
acidic environment of eczema-affected skin, the Km value and Kcat/Km of 
DAMs for NADH was 27.74 μM and 0.99 μM−1⋅min−1, respectively 
(Fig. 3e). This value was lower than that of other reported nanozymes 
(Table S1), suggesting that DAMs had a strong affinity for NADH in this 
particular environment [42–51]. DAMs maintained stable activity over 
7 cycles of catalysis (Fig. 3f). Through quantitative analysis, it was 
evident that with the aid of the enhancement factor, DAMs achieved a 
NOX-like activity of up to 2139 U/mg, which was 6–18 times higher 
than that of common organisms (Fig. 3g and Table S2) [6, 52–58]. DAMs 
exerted a significant catalytic effect on the oxidation of NADH, rather 
than displaying NADH dehydrogenase-like (NDH-like) activity (Fig. 3h). 
Meanwhile, it had a negligible impact on the oxidation of NADPH, 
highlighting its selective catalytic properties (Fig. 3i). Although natural 
NOX generate H2O2 or O2
•⁻ as a byproduct during NADH oxidation, the 
products of artificial DAMs here are H2O rather than ROS. This may be 
attributed to the fact that DAMs also possess SOD-like and CAT-like 
activity (Figure S12-13), which can facilitate a cascade reaction to 
convert ROS into H2O and O2. Furthermore, these O2 byproduct assists 
in the enhancement of spin-regulated NOX-like activity of artificial 
DAMs. Thus, through the coupling of spin manipulation with the PCET 
mechanism, the synergy between diatomic sites and enhancement fac­
tors increased unpaired electrons and enhanced the catalytic rate, 
leading to remarkable NOX-like activity.

2.4. Evaluation of tissue repair and metabolic regulation
To assess the antioxidant capabilities and biological safety of DAMs, 
in vitro tests were carried out (Figure S14) [60,61]. The outcomes from 
fluorescent staining revealed that DAMs were capable of efficiently 
eliminating O2
•−and LPS from cellular interiors, thereby evidencing the 
potential of DAMs to mitigate the harm induced by oxidative stress 
within living organisms. In addition, assessments across a concentration 
spectrum from 0 to 25 ng/µL indicated an absence of pronounced 
cytotoxic effected associated with DAMs. To explore the potential 
therapeutic applications of DAMs, which exhibited enhanced NOX-like 
activity, DAMs were administered in a murine model of eczema, using 
the clinical standard tacrolimus (Tac) as a benchmark. Figure S15
illustrated that DAMs significantly hastened the healing of eczema. By 
the tenth day of treatment, symptoms such as swelling and crusting at 
the lesion sites were nearly absent, with recovery outcomes exceeding 
those observed with Tac, RuFe and RuMn. While the exact causes of 
eczema remain unclear, it was widely accepted that inflammation plays 
a pivotal role. To further understand the regulatory impacts of treatment 
groups, CD31 immunohistochemistry (IHC) was conducted. Fig. 5a and 
Fig. 5c showed a notable increase in vascular area within the lesioned 
regions, indicative of substantial immune cell infiltration during 
inflammation. Interestingly, in the DAMs-treated group, this vascular 
expansion was notably reduced, suggesting a potent anti-inflammatory 
effect of DAMs on the lesion areas. In contrast to the control, H&E 
staining of the Eczema group clearly revealed inflammatory cell infil­
tration and epidermal necrosis, reinforcing the link between eczema and 
inflammatory processes (Fig. 5b and Fig. 5d-e). Following DAMs treat­
ment, there was a significant reduction in inflammation, coupled with a 
notable recovery of hair follicles and sweat glands, underscoring the 
efficacy of DAMs in mitigating inflammation. In an effort to understand 
how DAMs modulated eczema at the molecular level, the expression of 
key inflammatory markers in mice was examined. The findings indicated 
that DAMs robustly increased the expression of the anti-inflammatory

To assess the safety of DAMs, extensive toxicity evaluations were 
performed. The findings revealed that the body weight of DAMs-treated 
mice remained comparable to that of both the Con group and the Tac 
group (Figure S18), indicating that DAMs did not exert significant 
adverse effects on the overall health of the mice. Additionally, ICP-MS

complete blood cell counts indicated that all hematological parameters 
in the DAMs-treated group were well within the normal range 
(Figure S20), reinforcing the conclusion that DAMs posed no significant 
toxicity to the blood system (Figure S21). In the in vivo toxicity assess­
ment, DAMs exhibited excellent safety, with no observable toxic re­

DAMs-treated group 20 days after treatment cessation (Figure S22), 
implying that DAMs may offer a fundamental cure for eczema rather 
than merely symptom providing relief.
Overall, this work develops a groundbreaking strategy that syner­
gistically integrated spin-state manipulation and the PCET mechanism 
to dramatically boost the NOX-like activity of DAMs. Incorporating Co 
shifted Ru to a medium-spin state, elevating the unpaired electron 
density from 1.94 to 3.44 and creating a highly active Ru-Co-O center 
under oxygen activation. This site facilitated efficient proton-electron 
capture from NADH under acidic conditions, enabling the DAMs to 
rapidly restore the NAD+/NADH ratio and ameliorate metabolic dysre­
gulation in an eczema model. Spin moderation within artificial metal­
lozymes occurring during electron transfer can dramatically affect the 
PCET pathway to facilitate biocatalytic behavior and activity. By 
emulating natural enzymatic pathways, this study sets a new paradigm 
for advanced biocatalyst design, particularly for the regeneration of 
NAD+, a crucial cofactor in living organisms.
3. Conclusions
In this study, we demonstrated the effective NOX-like activity of 
RuCo heterogeneous DAMs surpassing that of natural enzymes through 
spin-state manipulation and PCET mechanism. During the NOX-like 
process, three co-enhancement factors, identified as O2, H2O2, and H+, 
were discovered for the first time. Notably, DAMs achieved an unprec­
edented catalytic efficiency (Kcat/Km) of 0.99 μM−1 min−1 and a quan­
titative activity of 2139 U/mg, outperforming existing artificial enzymes 
and microbial by 6–99 times. The experimental and DFT studies indi­
cated high-spin and highly active Ru-Co-O center tended to be formed, 
which was ascribed to an electrophilic microenvironment to facilitate 
oxygen adsorption under Co incorporating. In vivo experiments revealed 
that DAMs promoted long-term tissue repair via efficiently converting 
accumulated NADH to NAD+, encouraging macrophage polarization 
from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes, 
inhibiting key pro-inflammatory mediators, and enhancing anti- 
inflammatory cytokine IL-10 production. The therapeutic efficacy of 
DAMs in reducing inflammation and repairing tissue surpassed that of 
the clinical drug Tac, highlighting its potential in inflammatory disease 
intervention.
4. Materials and methods
4.1. Materials preparation
The fabrication of DAMs is a two-stage process, detailed as follows:
In the first step, commence by synthesizing trinitropyrene from an 
aqueous solution containing 4 g of pyrene and 320 mL of 70 % nitric 
acid at a temperature of 95℃. Subsequently, dissolve 0.1 g of the syn­
thesized trinitropyrene in 10 mL of a 2.5 % aqueous ammonia solution, 
ensuring uniform dispersion through ultrasonication on ice. Transfer the 
solution to a polytetrafluoroethylene-lined reactor and heat at 180℃ for 
a duration of 10 h. Post-heating, distill the solution to reduce its volume 
to 37.5 % of the initial, thereby purifying the precursor by removing 
impurities and un-reacted small molecules through a 48-hour dialysis 
process.
In the second step, for the synthesis of DAMs, prepare an aqueous 
solution containing 0.02 mmol CoCl2⋅6 H2O and 0.02 mmol of RuCl3. 
Combine this solution with 30 mL of the purified precursor solution at a 
concentration of 1 mg/mL and subject the mixture to freeze-drying. The 
resulting powder is then mixed with urea and calcined at a temperature 
of 500℃, with a heating rate of 10℃ per minute, to yield the black- 
colored DAMs.
To synthesize SAMs, modify the second stage by substituting the 
metal chlorides and RuCl3 with 0.04 mmol of RuCl3 alone, while 
maintaining all other procedural steps constant.
4.2. Materials characterization
The morphological characteristics and dimensions of DAMs were 
scrutinized employing dark-field transmission electron microscopy 
(TEM, JEM-F200, JEOL, Japan). The quantification of metal elemental 
content was executed using inductively coupled plasma mass spec­
trometry (ICP-MS: Agilent 7800, Agilent Technologies Ltd, Singapore). 
Atomic-level details of the surface-activated nanoparticles (DAMs) were 
probed via aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron micro­
scopy (AC-STEM, ARM200F, JEOL, Japan). X-ray diffraction (XRD, 
Smartlab, Rigaku, Japan) was utilized for the crystallographic identifi­
cation of DAMs. Raman spectroscopic analysis was conducted using an 
ultra-fast Raman imaging spectrometer (XploRA PLUS, HoribaJY, 
France) over a scanning wavenumber range of 500–3500 cm−1. 
Elemental composition and chemical states were elucidated through X- 
ray photoelectron spectrometry (XPS, Axis Supra, Kratos Analytical Ltd, 
UK). Magnetic measurements were carried out with an MPMS SQUID 
magnetometer.

4.5. NOX -like activity assay
The activity of NOX was ascertained using UV–vis spectrophotom­
etry. For the experimental group, a colorimetric dish was prepared by 
adding 180 μL of NADH (dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline, PBS) 
and 20 μL of the enzyme solution. The blank control group was estab­
lished by substituting the enzyme solution with an equivalent volume of 
PBS. The absorbance at 340 nm, indicative of NADH oxidation, was 
continuously monitored over time to assess the NOX activity.
To simulate the NOX catalytic process, the steady-state kinetic curve 
of the substrate NADH concentration was determined using UV–vis 
spectrophotometry. In the experimental setup, 170 μL of PBS, 10 μL of 
NADH, and 20 μL of the enzyme solution were combined in a colori­
metric dish. For the blank control, PBS was used in place of the enzyme 
solution. The absorbance at 340 nm was monitored at 1-minute intervals 
for a total of 10 min to track the reaction kinetics. The Km was derived by 
fitting the initial rates of the reaction to the Michaelis-Menten curve 
using Origin software. It is noteworthy that the molar extinction coef­
ficient of NADH at 340 nm is 6220 M−1 cm−1, which was utilized in the 
calculations. For quantitative analysis, enzyme activity was calculated 
based on NADH oxidation. One unit (U) of NOX-like activity is defined as 
the amount of nanozyme required to oxidize 1 μmol of NADH per minute 
under standard assay conditions (25℃).
4.6. NADPH oxidized activity assay
The activity of NADPH oxidized was ascertained using UV–vis 
spectrophotometry. For the experimental group, a colorimetric dish was 
prepared by adding 180 μL of NADPH (dissolved in phosphate-buffered 
saline, PBS) and 20 μL of the enzyme solution. The blank control group 
was established by substituting the enzyme solution with an equivalent 
volume of PBS. The absorbance at 340 nm, indicative of NADPH 
oxidation, was continuously monitored over time to assess the NOX 
activity.
4.7. DFT simulation
This work is simulated with Gaussian 09 with the exchange corre­
lation potential selected as B3LYP. The spin simulations are carried out 
by setting the spin multiplicity to a fixed value with geometry optimi­
zation applied. In addition, extra tools including Electron Localization 
Function, Charge Density Difference and Fukui Function are applied for 
a detailed discussion. Besides, the convergence criterion of the Self- 
Consistency Field (SCF) of electron wavefunction is set to be 10–8 
Hartree. The geometry relaxation convergence criterion is set to 
4.5*10−4 Hartree/Bohr.
4.8. Animal experiment
All animal experimental protocols were conducted in strict accor­
dance with the ethical guidelines established by the Animal Commission 
and adhered to the Principles of Animal Protection, Animal Welfare, and 
Ethics (YSY-DWLL 2023265). Male BALB/c mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were 
procured from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., LTD. The eczema model 
was meticulously established at Tianjin Haihe Hospital. The mice uti­
lized in the experiment were systematically allocated into six distinct 
groups: a control group (Con), an eczema model group (Eczema), the 
grous treated with DAMs (DAMs) and a group treated with tacrolimus 
(Tac), with each group comprising 8 mice.
4.9. Eczema models
The sensitization of the murine abdomen was initiated by applying a 
7 % solution of dinitrochlorobenzene (DNCB) in an acetone-olive oil 
(4:1) emulsion for a period of 2 days. In the control group, no inter­
vention was conducted. Following this sensitization phase, the dorsal 
region of the mice was challenged with 1.5 % DNCB every 3 days on two 
separate occasions to induce dermatitis. The control group remained 
untreated, thereby establishing the eczema model. Subsequently, DAMs 
was formulated at a concentration of 4 mg/mL in olive oil and topically 
applied to the eczematous lesions on the dorsal area of the mice every 
48 h for a total of 7 applications. In the Tacrolimus group, the ointment 
was applied as per the treatment protocol, while the eczema group 
received an equivalent volume of olive oil as a control. The untreated 
control group remained without any intervention. At the culmination of 
the treatment regimen, 48 h post the final application, the mice were 
euthanized, and skin samples from the eczematous lesions on their 
dorsal regions were harvested for further analysis.
4.10. Histological analysis
Various skin tissue samples were processed by fixation in neutral 
formalin solution for a duration of 2 days, followed by paraffin 
embedding and sectioning into 2 µm-thick slides. Hematoxylin and eosin 
(H&E) staining was employed to visualize the skin tissue affected by 
eczema on the dorsal aspect of the mice. In this staining procedure, the 
nuclei were stained blue-purple by the hematoxylin alkaline dye solu­
tion, while the cytoplasm, muscle, and connective tissues exhibited 
varying degrees of red or pink coloration due to the eosin dye solution. 
The inflammatory cells were quantified based on their morphological 
and chromatic distinctions from normal cells.
For the detection of vascular elements, IHC staining was conducted 
using a CD31 polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, catalog number 
28083–1-AP, diluted at 1:100) to delineate skin vessels. Furthermore, 
immunofluorescence (IF) staining was utilized to identify macrophages 
and anti-inflammatory cytokines within the skin tissue. This was ach­
ieved using CD68 polyclonal antibodies (Proteintech, catalog number 
28058–1 AP, diluted at 1:100) for macrophage labeling and a separate 
antibody for IL-10 (Proteintech, catalog number 28058–1 AP, diluted at 
1:100) to visualize anti-inflammatory cytokines.
4.11. Kit detection
The centrifugation of the homogenates was performed at 3000 rpm 
for 10 min, after which the supernatant was carefully collected and 
stored at −80 ℃ for subsequent analysis. Protein concentration in the 
homogenates was quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Biosharp, 
catalog number BL521A). Oxidative stress-related biomarkers, including 
malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and the gluta­
thione redox couple (GSH/GSSG), were assessed using specific assay kits 
from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. The MDA levels 
were determined with the Malondialdehyde (MDA) Assay Kit (catalog 
number A003–1–2), SOD activity was measured with the SOD Assay Kit

4.12. Flow cytometry
Freshly harvested tissues were initially rinsed with ice-cold phos­
phate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove any extraneous materials. Sub­
sequently, the tissues were enzymatically digested using collagenase IV 
(Solarbio, catalog number C8160) and a pancreatic enzyme cell diges­
tion solution (Biosharp, catalog number BL527A) at a temperature of 
37℃ for a duration of 30 min. This treatment facilitated the isolation of 
single cells, which were then prepared for flow cytometry analysis. For 
the flow cytometric analysis, a panel of fluorescently conjugated anti­
bodies was employed to characterize specific cell populations. These 
included: FITC-conjugated anti-mouse F4/80 antibody (BioLegend, 
catalog number 157310, diluted at 1:50), which is a marker for mac­
rophages, PerCP-conjugated anti-mouse/human CD11b antibody 
(Elabscience, catalog number E-AB F1081F, diluted at 1:20), indicative 
of myeloid cells, PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 antibody (Elabscience, 
diluted at 1:20), a marker for activation of antigen-presenting cells.
Additionally, macrophages were specifically labeled using: an un­
specified antibody (E-AB-f0994d, diluted at 1:20), and APC-conjugated 
anti-mouse CD206 antibody (Elabscience, catalog number E-AB-f1135e, 
diluted at 1:20), which is associated with M2 macrophage polarization.
4.13. Toxicological studies
Body weight fluctuations of the mice were meticulously documented 
on a daily basis commencing from the initiation of the treatment 
regimen. Euthanasia of the mice was performed the day subsequent to 
the final administration. The dorsal skin and blood samples were har­
vested from the mice for the assessment of drug elemental content and 
hematological parameters, respectively.
Hematological analyses encompassed a comprehensive evaluation of 
the following parameters: white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell 
count (RBCs), hemoglobin concentration (HGB), hematocrit level 
(HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin 
(MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), and 
platelet count (PLT). These assessments provided a detailed profile of 
the hematological status of the mice. Blood biochemical tests were 
conducted to assess liver and kidney function, including measurements 
of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), creati­
nine (CREA), and aspartate transaminase (AST). These parameters 
offered insights into the physiological integrity of the mice's hepatic and 
renal systems.
The quantification of Ru, Co elements in the skin, heart, liver, spleen, 
lungs, kidneys, and other vital organs of mice from both the control 
group and the DAMs treatment group was conducted using inductively 
coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). This analytical technique 
facilitated the precise determination of the elemental distribution and 
accumulation within the organs of interest.
4.14. Statistical analysis
The data are articulated as the mean value ± standard deviation (SD) 
or standard error of the mean (SEM). In instances where multiple 
comparisons were necessary, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) 
was employed, complemented by the post-hoc Tukey test to ascertain 
significant differences among the groups. Asterisks indicate significant 
differences (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

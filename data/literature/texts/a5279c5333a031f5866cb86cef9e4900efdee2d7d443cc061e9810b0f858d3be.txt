Atomically Precise Metal Nanoclusters for
Catalysis
Xiangsha Du and Rongchao Jin*
Department of Chemistry, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15213, United States
ABSTRACT: Recent eﬀorts in nanoscience to control nanoparticles
with atomic precision have met with success in solution-phase chemistry,
opening new opportunities. The products, atomically precise nano-
clusters (NCs), are not only compositionally well-deﬁned but also
structurally precise with unprecedented tailoring over the core and
surface for speciﬁc functionalities. In this Perspective, we ﬁrst highlight
recent work in metal−hydride NCs for applications in catalytic
hydrogenation and then reﬂect on the catalytic opportunities of
atomically precise metal NCs. Metal NCs, as a new class of material,
hold great promise for realizing the goals of understanding catalytic
mechanisms at the atomic/molecular level (e.g., construction of active
sites) and developing rules designing new catalysts with high activity and
selectivity for important reactions. Tailoring NC catalysts at the atomic
level will bring many exciting opportunities in future catalysis research.
T
he cluster state of matter has long been of tremendous
interest to both chemists and physicists, as it provides
answers to many fundamental questions including
metallic bond formation, evolution of electronic and magnetic
properties, and new structures of assembly, as well as many
other emergent phenomena.1−4 Research in cluster science has
evolved from the previous few-atom size regime to the current
nanometer sizes (e.g., 2.2 nm Au246(SR)80, where, −SR
represents thiolate).5 A rich library of nanoclusters (NCs) of
diﬀerent compositions, structures, and architectures has been
created by nanochemists.6 With respect to metal NCs, Au, Ag,
Cu, Pd, and alloys are of particular interest.7,8 These well-
deﬁned NCs with their structures characterized by X-ray
crystallography have served as important platforms for
addressing fundamental questions that are otherwise diﬃcult
to tackle with conventional, polydispersed nanoparticles and
are particularly relevant for work on fundamental catalysis.9−11
Nanocatalysis involves nanoparticles that are typically
supported on inert oxides.12 A traditional method for
nanocatalyst preparation is the wet impregnation approach,
i.e., soaking a powder of oxide (e.g., Al2O3, SiO2, TiO2, CeO2)
in a metal salt solution, followed by drying and calcination in a
muﬄe furnace. During the high-temperature process, the metal
salt decomposes, and the librated atoms rapidly aggregate into
nanoparticles. The as-prepared metal catalysts are polydis-
persed, and the non-uniform morphology and structures
render the precise structure−activity correlation impossible
to determine.12 Therefore, well-deﬁned catalysts are highly
desirable in order to achieve an atomic-/molecular-level
understanding of nanocatalysis.
Recent achievements in atomically precise NCs have opened
up exciting opportunities for fundamental catalysis.7 Herein,
we illustrate opportunities with copper−hydride (CuH) NCs
and their applications in catalytic hydrogenation. Research in
copper hydride clusters has a long history,13 with [CuH-
(PPh3)]6 being reported in the 1970s. In recent years, CuH
NCs with Cu(0) as opposed to Cu(I) have also been achieved,
such as [Cu25H22(PPh3)12]+.14 There are also reports on AgH
and AuH NCs,15,16 but the AuH system is less stable than CuH
and AgH due to the high electronegativity of gold.
Hydride (H−) is particularly relevant to H2 activation in
hydrogenation reactions.17 Generally, H2 follows two paths of
activation, homolytic and heterolytic, with the former leading
to the H· adsorbed state, whereas the latter gives rise to H−
and H+. The H−species is highly active in hydrogenation
reactions. The H−ligands in metal hydride NCs can be utilized
for catalytic hydrogenation, no matter the metal (M) in the
M(0) or M(I) state. Recent work by Sun et al. has pursued the
catalytic application of a Cu(I)25 NC in ketone hydro-
genation.18

synthesis and catalytic application of a thiolate (SR)-protected
[Cu25H10(SR)18]3−NC.18 Since the earlier report of
Au25(SR)18

reported their success with Ag25(SR)18,20 which was found to
share essentially the same structure as that of Au25(SR)18. The
next quest would be copper, with Sun et al. succeeding (Figure

With respect to the catalytic application of copper hydride
NCs, it is also worth mentioning a previous report on
electrocatalytic CO2 reduction over a Cu32H20L12 catalyst (L =
a dithiophosphate ligand).11 In that case, selectivity of CO2
reduction to HCOOH (as opposed to commonly CO) was
obtained at low overpotentials, which was ascribed to the
critical role of H−. Both this electrocatalytic CO2 reduction11
and the current work on ketone reduction18 demonstrate the
important role of hydride ligands in directing hydrogenation
reactions and highlight the promise of further studies of
copper−hydride NCs in catalytic hydrogenation processes.
FUTURE WORK ON COPPER−HYDRIDE
NANOCLUSTER CATALYSIS
For the application of NCs in catalytic hydrogenation, there
are interesting questions to address. For example, to what
extent is the mechanism proposed in the work of Sun et al.
applicable to other processes (e.g., chemoselective hydro-
genation)? To what extent does the metal component (e.g.,
Cu) contribute to the catalytic mechanism? Is Cu simply a
spectator or does it indeed contribute to the catalytic reaction?
Beyond the CuH system, the AgH and AuH systems15,16 also
deserve to be explored for hydrogenation reactions. In addition
to ketone hydrogenation, there are other important hydro-
genation reactions to investigate as well, such as hydrogenation
of nitro and alkynyl groups as well as chemoselective
hydrogenation reactions and energy-related reactions. A
particularly interesting comparison to make in future work
would be investigating the potential diﬀerence between
[Cu25H10(SR)18]3−and [Cu25H22(PPh3)12]+,14,18 as the latter
has a partial Cu(0) oxidation state (as opposed to Cu(I) in the
former). The potential eﬀects of intermediate Cu oxidation
state would likely exhibit some interesting eﬀects in catalysis.

THE FUTURE OF METAL NANOCLUSTER CATALYSIS
Metal NCs, as a new class of nanocatalysts, hold great promise
in future research. Here, we outline some potentially important
aspects for future work, in hopes of stimulating a wide interest
in the catalytic applications of atomically precise NCs, not only
for understanding catalytic mechanisms but also for developing
new rules toward high-performance catalysts for chemical
processes of environmental and energetic importance.21
Nanoparticle synthesis has experienced major progress in the
past two decades, which provided important opportunities for
catalytic investigations of size, shape, and composition
eﬀects.12 The recent advent of atomically precise nano-
chemistry has pushed the frontier forward an additional
step.7 It is now possible to look into atomic-level size, shape,
structure, and composition eﬀects (Figure 2).9
Quantum Size Eﬀects in Catalysis. Quantum size eﬀects
have long been an intriguing topic in metal-based nano-
catalysis. Taking gold as an example, recent work has mapped
out the nonmetal-to-metal transition that occurs precisely
between Au246(SR)80 and Au279(SR)84,21 which paved the way
for a detailed investigation on transition behaviors in catalysis.
Future investigations on a series of NCs spanning the
nonmetallic and metallic regimes will reveal fundamental
insights into quantum size eﬀects.

Single-Electron-Level Control of Nanoclusters. With
well-deﬁned NCs, precisely varying the charge state (q) has
become possible, although it is not easy to accomplish.22 The
ﬁrst system was [Au25(SR)18]q, in which Kauﬀman et al.
observed distinct eﬀects of charge state on the electrocatalytic
CO2 reduction.22 Future eﬀorts in controlling other NCs may
bring exciting new opportunities.
Ligand Engineering for Catalysis. The surface ligands
on NCs are often a barrier to catalysis, but, in some cases,
ligands can facilitate activation of reactants and provide a
means for activity and selectivity control.10,23 The double-
edged sword eﬀect of ligands constitutes an interesting topic
for future studies. Selectivity control is particularly intriguing
and deserves to be systematically studied because achieving
high selectivity is a major goal in catalysis.
Enantioselective Heterogeneous Catalysis. Typically,
catalytic reactions produce a mixture of enantiomers in equal
amounts, with the enantiomeric excess (ee) being zero. To
obtain one of the enantiomers selectively, a special catalyst
should be used in the reaction. Enantioselective catalysis has
long been practiced in homogeneous catalysis using chiral
ligand-modiﬁed organometallic complexes, but enantioselec-
tive nanocatalysts have not met with much success, except for a
few cases of chiral ligand-protected nanoparticles.12 Thus,
enantiomer selection remains a major goal in the ﬁeld of
nanocatalysis. Some chiral NCs have become available,24 but
preventing racemization and ﬁnding the appropriate reaction
conditions still pose problems.
Bi- and Multimetallic Nanoclusters. Metal NCs doped
with heteroatoms could be exciting in catalysis9,25 and permit
single-atom-level tailoring of nanocatalysts. For bi- or multi-
metallic catalysts, the starting point (e.g., the atomic locations
and the oxidation states of metal atoms) is well-deﬁned by the
X-ray structure of NCs, but monitoring NCs during the course
of reactions remains a challenge. Possible metal segregation
(e.g., from core to surface or vice versa) and its dynamics are of
particular importance if such a process indeed occurs. In situ X-
ray absorption spectroscopy may provide helpful informa-
tion,26,27 and other in situ techniques should also be employed
in probing the NC catalysts.
Nanocluster−Support Interactions. Although the sup-
port typically disperses metal nanoparticles for stabilization
only (i.e., no participation in the catalytic reaction), recent
research has reported cases in which the support could be
actively involved in certain reactions.7 Probing interactions
between ligand-protected NCs and the support is more
challenging than is investigating bare clusters on supports.
These challenges, including how to reduce the computational
demand and how to probe the interfacial processes
experimentally, remain to be overcome in future work.
Catalytic Active Sites: The Holy Grail in Nano-
catalysis. The active sites in catalysts have intrigued
researchers for many decades; it is a daunting task to pinpoint
catalytically active sites in nanocatalysts and to reveal both
their dynamics (e.g., reconstruction upon reactant adsorption
and product desorption) as well as the deactivation mechanism
of the active sites. With the advent of atomically precise NCs,
this goal is closer to being realized,9−11,18,28,29 albeit major
eﬀorts are still needed in the case of supported NC catalysts.17
The ability to tailor the inner core and outer surface separately
is particularly important for understanding catalytic mecha-
nisms and constructing active sites for speciﬁc reactions.9
Future research should devise strategies to enable observation
of the catalytic process over the NC catalysts at atomic
resolution and in real time. We expect the combined
experimental and theoretical eﬀorts to revolutionize nano-
catalysis in revealing the truly atomic origins of structure−
activity relationships, and, eventually, chemists will be able to
design active sites for speciﬁc reactions.

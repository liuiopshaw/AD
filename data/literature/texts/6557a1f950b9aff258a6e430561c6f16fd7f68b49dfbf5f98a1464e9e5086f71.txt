ABSTRACT: Au nanoclusters (Au NCs) with a unique size
eﬀect on the antibacterial performance provide a promising
nanoprobe for developing an eﬃcient nanomedicine. How-
ever, little progress has been made owing to the low quantum
yield and poor stability of Au NCs. In this work, protamine
(Prot) functionalized Au NCs (Prot/MTU-Au NCs) with
high stability were achieved through a simple mixing with 6-
methyl-2-thiouracil-capped Au NCs (MTU-Au NCs) due to
the hydrogen bonding between 5-methyl-2-thiouracil (MTU)
and the guanidine groups from Prot. Interestingly, a distinctly
enhanced photoluminescence from Prot/MTU-Au NCs (ca.
28-fold) was observed due to the formation of rigid host−
guest assemblies. We inferred that the cross-linked structure
and supramolecular hydrogen bonds both contributed to the ﬂuorescence enhancement and stability. The extra small size of the
NCs and the eﬃcient antibacterial capability from the capping shell of Prot encouraged us to probe its antibacterial performance
systemically. It was found that the Prot/MTU-Au NCs with highly stable loading of positively charged antibacterial reagents
were likely to penetrate into the bacteria and thus enhance the ability to kill both Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli) and
Gram-positive bacteria (multiple-resistant Staphylococcus aureus). The synergetic eﬀect between the unique size and the capping
layers enabled the minimal inhibitory concentration of the as-derived Prot/MTU-Au NCs reduced by ∼100-fold compared to
that with individual Au nanoparticle. The antibacterial mechanism further revealed that membrane injury occurred and reactive
oxygen species were generated after the incubation of the bacteria with Prot/MTU-Au NCs. Moreover, the highly luminescent
ﬂuorescence and positive surface charge of Prot/MTU-Au NCs could image the bacteria easily, which held great potential for
imaging-guided antibacterial platform.
KEYWORDS: Prot/MTU-Au NCs, host−guest recognition, antibacterial eﬀect, synergistic eﬀect
1. INTRODUCTION
Widespread use of antibiotics often cannot stop the spread of
bacterial infection due to its strong adaptability and rapid
regenerations. The overprescription and improper use of
antibiotics further accelerate the emergence of antibiotic-
resistant bacterial strains,1 which leads to the health threats and
increases the risks like sepsis, surgery, and even death.
Therefore, new antibiotics1 and nanomedicine are urgently
needed for exploration and development against multidrug-
resistant pathogens. With the development of the nano-
technology, more and more functionalized nanoprobes with
high eﬃciency2−5 and complex therapeutic regimen6−10 have
been designed to achieve the bacteria-killing ability due to the
improved chemical, biological, and physical characters.
Metal nanoclusters (NCs) with size approaching the Fermi
wavelength exhibited a lot of intriguing properties, such as
eﬃcient catalysis activity,11 distinctive ﬂuorescence,12 and
good biocompatibility,13 which attracted special interest in the
design of various sensors,14,15 bioimaging,16 and nano-
medicine.17 In 2017, Xie et al.17 demonstrated that enhanced
antimicrobial activity was achieved by reducing the size of Au
nanoparticles (NPs) down to the NCs range, which opened a
promising strategy for developing eﬃcient antibacterial nanop-
robes.8,18,19 However, the low quantum yield (QY) and poor
stability of NCs severely limited their large-scale applications.
Based on the fact that the ﬂuorescence of the NCs is not only
from the metal core but also closely associated with the surface
capping reagents, much eﬀort has been made to enhance the

photoluminescence of NCs. For example, designing alloy
NCs20 and developing aggregation induction approaches21,22
are commonly used strategies, which have been given summary
by Li et al.23 However, the QY of metal NCs still remained
relatively low and the products collected from the aggregation
induction approaches with large size suﬀered from poor
stability. Very recently, Chen et al.24 reported a facile blending
route for the fabrication of water-soluble and highly stable Au
NCs with high photoluminescence QY (QY = 65%) by
introducing L-arginine (Arg) into the capping layer of 6-aza-2-
thiothymine-stabilized Au NCs (ATT-Au NCs, QY = 1.8%).
The supramolecular hydrogen bonds formed between ATT
capped on the Au core, and the guanidine group from Arg
made the capping shell of ATT more rigid. However, the above
work excluded the enhancement possibility of 5-methyl-2-
thiouracil (MTU) capped Au NCs (MTU-Au NCs) by Arg
due to the absence of two adjacent nitrogen atoms in its six-
membered heterocyclic ring.
Protamine (Prot), a cationic peptide with abundant Arg
residues has an excellent capability to penetrate into bioﬁlms,
had been commonly used as an antiseptic substance in food
storage.25 Motivated by the structure and function of Prot, we
made a new attempt to modify MTU-Au NCs with Prot for
developing high-eﬃciency antibacterial nanoprobes (Prot/
MTU-Au NCs) with good stability. To our surprise, the
weak emission of MTU-Au NCs was lighted up (ca. 28-fold)
by the introduction of Prot. We inferred that the cross-linked
structure and supramolecular hydrogen bonds both contrib-
uted to the ﬂuorescence enhancement and stability. Utilizing
the unique tiny size eﬀect, reaction activity of the NCs, and the
eﬃcient antibacterial performance of Prot loaded on the
surface of Au NCs, we screened the antibacterial eﬃciency of
Prot/MTU-Au NCs. The minimum inhibitory concentration
(MIC) with our developed Prot/MTU-Au NCs toward both
Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus)
was obviously reduced by ∼100-fold compared to that using
individual Au NPs due to the synergetic eﬀect. Antibacterial
mechanism revealed that the membrane damage occurred and
reactive oxygen species (ROS) was generated, which resulted
in enhanced bacterial killing capacity.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Materials and Reagents. Gold(III) chloride trihydrate
(HAuCl4·3H2O) and 6-methyl-2-thiouracil were purchased from
Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Protamine and bovine serum album
(BSA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Milwaukee). Tetrakis-
(hydroxy-methyl) phosphonium chloride (THPC, P(CH2OH)4Cl,
80% solution in water) was obtained from J&K Chemical (Beijing,
China). Melamine was from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.
(Ningbo, China). Highly branched poly(ethyleneimine) (hPEI, MW
70 000) was bought from Alfa Aesar. NaOH was obtained from
Beijing Chemical Works (Beijing, China). Bacterial used Mueller
Hinton (MH) broth medium was bought from Hope Biotechnology
Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). Cell culture medium Dulbecco’s
modiﬁed Eagle’s medium (DMEM) and fetal bovine serum (FBS)
were from Gibco (Grand Land, New York). E. coli K-12 strain was
purchased from Yale University (New Haven). Pyridine iodide (PI)
and ROS Assay Kit was obtained from Beyotime (Guangzhou,
China). Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) was from Dojindo Molecular
Technologies (Shanghai, China). All of the glass vessels and
magnetons were washed with a bath of aqua regia and then rinsed
with ethanol and ultrapure water. Sterile ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ
cm−1) from Millipore system was used throughout the experiment.
2.2. Apparatus and Characterizations. Inductively coupled
plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, X Series 2, Thermo
Scientiﬁc) was used to determine the concentration of Au in the NCs
solution after dialysis. UV−vis absorption spectra and ﬂuorescence
spectra were recorded on a Cary 60 UV−vis (Agilent Technologies)
and Cary Eclipse ﬂuorescence spectrophotometers (Agilent Tech-
nologies), respectively. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and
high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) were
obtained by a JEM-2100F high-resolution transmission electron
microscope (Netherlands), which was operated at 200 kV. X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was obtained from an ESCALAB-
MKII X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (VG Scientiﬁc, U.K.). The
bright ﬁeld images and ﬂuorescence images were taken by a
ﬂuorescence microscope (Nikon TI-U, Tokyo, Japan) with an
autofocus (ProScan II, Prior) equipped with 100× phase-contrast
objective. Before imaging, bacteria were preloaded onto a microﬂuidic
device to distribute them in a monolayer. The Spark multimode
microplate reader (TECAN, Switzerland) was utilized to measure the
ﬂuorescence intensity of bacteria after dichloro-dihydro-ﬂuorescein
diacetate (DCFH-DA) was added. OD600 was measured on a YuanXi
UV5800 ultraviolet−visible spectrophotometer (Shanghai, China) to
determine the concentration of the bacteria solution.
2.3. Synthesis of BSA-Au NCs and THPC-Au NPs. To elucidate
the size and ligand eﬀect on the antibacterial performance, Au NPs
(∼6 nm) reduced by THPC (THPC-Au NPs) without the
participation of MTU and protamine- and BSA-capped Au NCs
(BSA-Au NCs) were also synthesized according to the approach
reported in the literature.12
2.4. Bacteria Culture. Two kinds of bacteria strains of S. aureus
and E. coli were grown in the sterile MH broth medium. Before the
experiment, a single colony of the two strains was picked from MH
agar plates and incubated in an MH culture medium overnight in a
shaking incubator at 37 °C. Bacterial cell suspensions were diluted by
fresh the MH culture medium before use.
2.5. Cell Culture and Cytotoxicity Assay. Human dermal
ﬁbroblasts (HDF) cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS
supplemented for 2 days. Then, they were digested with trypsin
enzyme (0.25% ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid added), washed, and
transferred into a 96-well plate with 1 × 104 cells dispersed in the
fresh culture medium in each well. After cultivation for 12 h, diluted
Prot/MTU-Au NCs using the fresh culture medium at MIC were
introduced to incubate the HDF cells. After incubation for 2 h at 37
°C, the Prot/MTU-Au NCs containing medium were removed and
the samples were washed twice with sterile phosphate-buﬀered saline.
Ten microliters of CCK-8 diluted in 90 μL of DMEM was added into
each well for further incubation to evaluate the cytotoxicity. Cell
viability was determined by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm
using a microplate reader. The cells were cultured in a humidiﬁed
incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2 supplemented.
2.6. MIC Determination. MIC was deﬁned as the minimum drug
concentration under which bacteria could not grow in 16 h.26 The
MIC toward S. aureus and E. coli was determined by broth
microdilution method, which was performed in 96-well microtiter
plates and MH broth solutions were added (100 μL). The 96-well
microtiter plates were arranged in a 2-fold dilution from left to right
and in triplicate. They were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. The results
were measured by the Spark multimode microplate reader at 600 nm.
The initial concentration of the bacteria solution was diluted 1000-
fold from OD600 = 0.5. It should be noted that the used BSA-Au NCs,
THPC-Au NPs, Prot/MTU-Au NCs, and MTU-Au NCs were
washed with 1 kD dialysis membrane to isolate the free molecules and
then the concentration of Au was determined by ICP-OES.
2.7. PI Staining. To estimate the killing eﬃciency and investigate
the antibacterial mechanism of Prot/MTU-Au NCs, PI was employed
as an indicator that could combine with DNA in membrane disrupted
cells. The concentration of the bacteria solution used in this work was
determined according to the value of OD600 (here, OD600 = 0.2,
corresponding to ∼2 × 107 bacterial cells17,27), and then the materials
were added into the solution. After 1 h uptake, a moderate amount of
PI (20×) was added into the solution.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Preparation and Characterization of Prot/MTU-
Au NCs. As shown in Scheme 1, the MTU-Au NCs were ﬁrst
prepared using MTU as the reducing and protecting reagents.
Brieﬂy, HAuCl4 (25 mM, 3.75 mL) solution was mixed with
MTU (80 mM, 3.75 mL) in the presence of 200 mM NaOH
under constant stirring. The above mixture was incubated for 1
h,24 and the colorless sample of MTU-Au NCs was achieved
with the faint pink ﬂuorescence emission (Figure 1a, inset left)
under UV light irradiation. After incubation with Prot (14 mg/
mL, 1 mL) for 3 h, Prot-functionalized Au NCs (Prot/MTU-
Au NCs) with a bright ﬂuorescent emission centered at 650
nm was observed (ca. 28-fold, Figure 1a, inset right) under the
UV-lamp irradiation. As a control (Figure S1), ﬂuorescence
responses from the mixing samples of Prot and MTU or
HAuCl4 and Prot were collected. Both of them emitted no
emission under the same conditions, suggesting that the
enhanced ﬂuorescence originated from Prot/MTU-Au NCs.
Recently, Shang et al.28,29 demonstrated that the interaction
between Au NCs and protein could achieve the enhancement
of ﬂuorescence. To test the inﬂuence of the protein, BSA was
chosen as a model to replace Prot for unraveling the enhanced
mechanism. We noted that the introduction of BSA cannot
alter the ﬂuorescence emission. One reasonable explanation for
the enhancement may be the supramolecular hydrogen bonds
between the enriched Arg active sites from Prot (ca. 60%) and
hydrogen bond accepters of MTU, which reduced the rate of
nonradioactive relaxation of the excited states.23,30 This result
was diﬀerent from the previously reported work24 since they
excluded the enhancement possibility of Arg to the MTU-Au
NCs system. The diﬀerence may be due to the diﬀerent
structures of Arg and Prot. Compared with Arg, Prot with a
cross-linked structure and enriched Arg active sites was
beneﬁcial for the rigid shells to protect Au NCs from direct

further supported by the inﬂuence of temperature on the
photoluminescence. When the temperature increased to ∼70
°C, the hydrogen bonds would rupture and the solution
become faintly ﬂuorescent again. Also, when the temperature
decreased, recovered photoluminescence was observed, clearly
manifesting the reversible process of the hydrogen bonds
formation. Of particular note, the formed hydrogen bonds
endowed the Prot/MTU-Au NCs with improved stability
compared to MTU-Au NCs. The strong ﬂuorescence of Prot/

(Figure S4). In sharp contrast, the photoluminescence of
MTU-Au NCs almost disappeared only after one day without
the protection of Prot. The result from the UV-adsorption
identiﬁed the production of small Au NCs without the absence
of the characteristic surface plasmon resonance at 520 nm of
Au NPs (Figure 1b). The TEM (Figure 1c) image

dispersed with a mean size of ∼1.5 nm, ruling out the
aggregation-induced enhancement mechanism. XPS was also

Au(0) and Au(I) existed in Prot/MTU-Au NCs. It was
reported that Au(I) contributed to the stability of the Au NCs,
and our result were in accordance with the conclusion.16,31
3.2. Antibacterial Eﬀects of Prot/MTU-Au NCs. Taking
S. aureus (Gram-positive) and E. coli (Gram-negative) as
examples, the antimicrobial activity of Prot/MTU-Au NCs was
ﬁrst evaluated by a growth-curve assay to monitor the optical
density at 600 nm. As a control, serials of samples including
THPC-Au NPs (Figure S5), THPC-Au NPs/MTU (prepared
by physical mixing THPC-Au NPs with MTU), THPC-Au
NPs/Prot (prepared by physical mixing THPC-Au NPs with
Prot), MTU-Au NCs;, and Prot/MTU-Au NCs with an
equivalent amount of Au were employed in the same initial
concentration of bacteria solutions. As shown in Figure 2, the
best inhibition eﬀect was obtained with Prot/MTU-Au NCs
treated group, and the bacteria could not grow throughout the
whole test period. In sharp contrast, physically mixing THPC-
Au NPs with an equal amount of MTU or Prot had similar
growth curves as that of the blank group, highlighting the role
of the size eﬀect on the antimicrobial activity. The diﬀerence in
antimicrobial activity between MTU-Au NCs and Prot/MTU-
Au NCs was mainly induced by the eﬀect of capping reagents.
On one hand, the introduction of Prot shell via the host−guest
recognition endowed the nanoprobes with a high-density
loading of positively charged antibacterial reagents, which
contributed a lot to the ﬁnal activity. On the other hand, On
the other hand, the enhanced stability of Prot/MTU-Au NCs
enabled the as-derived nanoprobes with a higher antimicrobial
activity in complex biological environments, which was
consistent with the previous reports.27
Moreover, the MIC is of crucial importance to assess the
antimicrobial activity of the nanoprobes (Table S1). The low
MIC reﬂected a strong antimicrobial activity. Compared to
that of MTU-Au NCs (Figure 3), the MIC of Prot/MTU-Au
NCs toward E. coli and S. aureus was reduced by ∼8-fold due
to the loading of Prot. Similar to the above growth curves, the
MIC of Prot/MTU-Au NCs was remarkably reduced by ∼100-
fold compared with that of THPC-Au NPs due to the
synergetic interactions between the unique size and the
bacteriostasis eﬀect from Prot capping shell. To get a better
comprehension of the capping reagents, a more revealing
comparison was made by employing BSA-capped Au NCs
(BSA-Au NCs). Both being biologically benign reagent (Figure
S6a), BSA- and MTU-protected Au NCs had apparently

agar diﬀusion assays26,34 to reveal the bactericidal activities

Au NCs treated samples showed better antimicrobial capability

the inhibition zones of E. coil and S. aureus treated by THPC-

Another unique feature of the Prot/MTU-Au NCs
originated from the intrinsic ﬂuorescence and the positive
charges of capping shell, which inspired us to use it as
molecular label for imaging bacteria. As shown in Figure 5, the
bacteria treated with MTU-Au NCs had very weak signal when
excited at 405 nm. However, bacteria after incubation with
positively charged Prot/MTU-Au NCs exhibited an intense
red emission, indicating the strong binding to the bacteria
membrane or internalization. This kind of imaging-guided
assays will be more beneﬁcial for the development of
antibacterial platform.
3.3. Antibacterial Mechanism. To have a better
comprehension about the bacteria killing process, we utilized
a dye of PI, which could combine with DNA when the
membrane was disrupted, to test whether the membrane of
bacteria was disrupted by Prot/MTU-Au NCs and diﬀerentiate
the alive and dead bacteria. Here, the bright ﬁeld was used to
reveal all of the bacterial cells. Similar to the control group
(Figure 6), negligible damage to the membrane could be
observed in the THPC-Au NPs treated bacteria, indicating an
inferior antibacterial eﬀect of THPC-Au NPs, which was
consistent with the above MIC results. In contrast, MTU-Au
NCs and Prot/MTU-Au NCs treated samples were stained
with PI obviously, suggesting signiﬁcant membrane damage.
Among all of the groups, Prot/MTU-Au NCs exhibited the
highest staining eﬃciency, indicating its improved antibacterial
eﬀect.

induced by ROS generation in metal NCs systems was thought
to be the pivotal mechanism of their antibacterial activity. To
verify whether ROS level was elevated after incubation with
Prot/MTU-Au NCs, intracellular ROS assay Kit35 was utilized.
The ROS probe of DCFH-DA exhibits no ﬂuorescence itself
and can penetrate the cell membrane freely to be hydrolyzed
into DCFH by intracellular esterase. DCFH can be easily

4. CONCLUSIONS
In summary, highly luminescent MTU-Au NCs functionalized
with Prot were obtained via host−guest interaction through
the formation of hydrogen bonds between MTU and the cross-
linked guanidine groups on the side chains of Prot. The
supramolecular assemblies endowed the Prot/MTU-Au NCs
with excellent optical properties and surface-stable loading of
high-density Prot, which leads to the best-performing growth
inhibition eﬃciency and lowest MIC to both Gram-negative
bacteria and Gram-positive ones. The reduced MIC (ca. 100-
fold) of Prot/MTU-Au NCs than individual Au NPs made the
former hold great potential as the nanomedicine against the
bacteria. Moreover, the bright ﬂuorescence and positively
charged surface of Prot/MTU-Au NCs could light up the
bacteria, making them a good candidate in the imaging.

ABSTRACT: Materials in nature feature versatile and program-
mable interactions to render macroscopic architectures with
multiscale structural arrangements. By rationally combining
metal−carboxylate and metal−organophosphate coordination
interactions, Au25(MHA)18 (MHA, 6-mercaptohexanoic acid)
nanocluster self-assembled structural color coating films and
phytic acid (PA)−metal coordination complexes are sequen-
tially constructed on the surface of titanium implants. The
Lewis acid−base coordination principle applies for these
metal−organic coordination networks. The isotropic arrange-
ment of nanoclusters with a short-range order is investigated via
grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering. The integration of robust M−O (M = Ti, Zr, Hf) and labile Cu−O coordination
bonds with high connectivity of Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters enables these artificial photonic structures to achieve a
combination of mechanical stability and bacteriostatic activity. Moreover, the colorless and transparent PA−metal complex
layer allows the viewing of the structural color and surface wettability switching to hydrophilic and makes feasible the
interfacial biomineralization of hydroxyapatite. Collectively, these modular metal−organic coordination-driven assemblies are
predictive and rational material design strategies with tunable hierarchy and diversity. The complete metal−organic
architectures will not only help improve the physicochemical properties of the bone−implant interface with synergistic
antibacterial and osseointegration activities but also can boost surface engineering of medical metal implants
KEYWORDS: metal−organic coordination, nanocluster, structural color, antibacterial activity, implant-associated infections
INTRODUCTION
Hierarchical material design principles in nature have evolved
to optimize performance in achieving external visual effects1,2
and/or internal mechanical properties.3,4 One of the visual
consequences of self-assembled biophotonic structures is the
so-called structural color,1 which exhibits vivid coloration in
birds,5 beetles,6 and butterflies.7 In marine mussels, histidine−
Zn2+ and catechol−Fe3+ coordination cross-links are proposed
to be responsible for the adhesive and protecting mechanical
behavior of the byssus core and cuticle, respectively.8 The
fabrication of mechanically reinforced and adhesive mussel
byssus fiber is a well-established role model for advanced
adhesives and coatings. Despite great progress in the
understanding of hierarchical design principles, there are still
huge gaps in mimicking the complex features of biological
materials that could account for the rich variations in nature.
Until now, the self-assembly of colloidal anisotropic
nanoparticles has been widely employed in generating
biomimetic structural coloration.9,10 However, such structural
colors typically exhibit iridescence that prevent use for general
purposes, whereas angle-independent non-iridescent structural
colors are identical in displays or coatings.11,12 The weak
noncovalent forces at work in traditional colloidal self-assembly
often result in restricted performance of color fastness and
chemical or/and mechanical robustness.13 Therefore, design-

of self-assembled architectures. Recently, atomically precise

metal-coordination-mediated assemblies of Au25(SR)18 nano-

scopic coloration.
To date, a vast majority of metallic biomedical implants are
made of titanium (Ti) and its alloys, owing to the
biocompatible characteristics, mechanical properties, and
excellent corrosion resistance.18 However, the formation of
bacterial biofilms at the bone−implant interface on and around
Ti implants has been particularly problematic, as implant-
associated infections frequently lead to the failure of the
implants.19 As the multiphasic and complicated process of
osteogenesis at the bone−implant interface is important, an
interfacial layer that combines antibacterial and osteogenic
activities is desirable for combating implant-associated
infections.20
Herein, biomimetic and metal−organic coordination-driven
supramolecular architectures with synergetic antibacterial and
osteogenic activities were constructed on the surface of a Ti
implant for re-establishing the bone−implant interface. The
multiscale self-assembled architectures were comprised of
Au25(MHA)18 (MHA, 6-mercaptohexanoic acid) nanocluster
self-organized amorphous photonic structures, which exhibit a
non-iridescent coloration similar to the appearance of the blue
color on male Eastern Bluebird feathers,21 as well as phytic
acid (PA)−metal complexes, contributing to a hydrophilic
surface wettability conversion and interfacial hydroxyapatite
biomineralization (Scheme 1). This bioinspired strategy

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Preparing of Structural Coloration Coating. Initially,
Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters were prepared and characterized
based on a reported method (Figure 1A−D, Figure S1).24,25
Inspired by the acid−base properties and solubility behaviors
of p-mercaptobenzoic acid-capped nanoclusters,26 we found
that fully protonated Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters are soluble in
ethanol. As shown in the UV−vis spectra, both protonated and
deprotonated Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters exhibited a charac-
teristic band at 670 nm and two shoulder peaks at 400 and 450
nm. Moreover, the precise composition of Au25(MHA)18 was
identified by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-
MS) (Figure 1D). Then, we first investigated the metal−
organic coordination behaviors of Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters
with tetravalent (Ti4+, Zr4+, Hf4+), trivalent (Fe3+), and
divalent (Cu2+) metal ions, respectively. Brilliant structural
color coatings were constructed through the coordination of
Au25(MHA)18 with tetravalent metal ions only (Figure S2).
Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images clearly show the
distinct surface morphologies of the assembled GNCs with
different metal ions (Figures S3−S7). A homogeneous-
distributed Ti/Zr/Hf element was observed respectively in
the elemental mapping of the films by energy-dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS) (Figures S3−S5). However, irregular
agglomerations with rough surfaces were formed during the
Fe3+- and Cu2+-mediated self-assembly process (Figures S6 and
S7). Since the linkages of the metal−carboxylate (ligand)
bonds are governed by the hard−soft acid−base (HSAB)
principle,27,28 the hardness of metal ions can be used as a

shown in the high-resolution O 1s XPS spectra (Figure 2B),

coordination with the metal ions. It was evident that the
assigned peaks of Ti 2p, Zr 3d, and Hf 4f were consistent with
the oxidation state of M4+ (M = Ti, Zr, Hf) (Figure S9).31−33
The deconvoluted XPS spectra of Cu 2p demonstrated two
dominant peaks, characteristic for Cu 2p1/2 (at ∼955.5 eV) and
Cu 2p3/2 (at ∼933.5 eV) peaks,34 respectively (Figure 2B).

clearly examine the distribution of mixed-metal ions in the
films, high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission
electron microscope (HAADF-STEM) imaging and EDS
mapping were performed. The MM-MON films were prepared
by dropping the mixtures onto carbon-coated TEM grids and
immediately evaporating the solvent, finally forming MM-
MON films. As shown in Figures S10−S12, HAADF-STEM
images and EDS mappings illustrate the regularly distributed
behaviors of the Cu and hard metal species (Ti, Zr, Hf) along
with the GNCs.

Mineralization Ability. As illustrated in Figure 4A, the
antibacterial activity, surface wettability, and mineralization
ability of the coatings were further explored. The antibacterial
activities against Gram-positive methicillin-resistant Staph-

(CFU) assays.38 The Ti/Zr/Hf-MON coating films exhibit
poor antibacterial efficiency of less than 5% toward MRSA and
E. coli (Figure S14). Comparatively, the Ti/Zr/Hf−Cu-MONs
films exhibited a much better bacteriostatic effect with
significantly lower levels of MRSA and E. coli colonization by
increasing the Cu content. The antibacterial efficiency of Ti/
Zr/Hf−Cu-MONs films with the highest Cu contents reached
up to about 98.86 ± 2.53%, 98.66 ± 2.0%, and 98.59 ± 1.84%
for MRSA, respectively (Figure 4B, Figure S15). Furthermore,
the biofilm eradication properties were verified by the live/
dead (green/red) staining assay. As shown in Figure S16, after

The compact nanostructures and protonated ligands endow
the MM-MON films with a hydrophobic surface with water
contact angles of >95° (Figure 4C). Inspired by the catechol−
Fe3+ coordination strategy for surface chemistry,39 the colorless
and transparent PA−Fe3+−Zn2+ (denoted as PA−metal)
complexes were introduced onto the coatings to improve the
surface wettability (Figure S17). After modifying with the PA−
metal complexes, the surface showed much lower water
contact angles (Figure 4C, Figure S18). The free P−OH
groups of PA−metal complexes render the coatings with high
water affinity,40 thus greatly improving surface hydrophilicity.
The introduction of PA−metal complexes had almost no effect
on germicidal efficacy and antibiofilm performance (Figures
S19 and S20). All of the divalent (Cu2+, Zn2+) and trivalent
(Fe3+) metal ions utilized in this study are environmentally
benign and indispensable for the human body.41 Moreover, the
bactericidal metal ions (Cu2+, Zn2+) have excellent broad-
spectrum antibacterial effects via multiple sterilization
mechanisms.42 With regard to safety, inductively coupled
plasma mass spectrometry was utilized to assess the release
behaviors of metal ions. The release of copper and zinc ions
exhibited a biphasic pattern featuring a relatively high release
within 3 days, followed by a gradual release for the next 4 days
when immersed in PBS (pH 7.4) (Figure S21). However, a
slow and sustained release behavior of iron ions was observed.
However, no Ti/Zr/Hf elements were detected, indicating the
high stability of the coating films.
Generally, the abundant phosphate groups on PA are
predictably active in inducing hydroxyapatite (HAp) mineral-
ization through donating adequate reactive sites for chelation
with Ca2+.43 The ability to induce bone-like HAp on the
surface of biomaterials in simulated body fluid (SBF) has been
widely used in predicting osteoinductive capacity.44 Therefore,
we soaked the metal−organic architectures coated samples in
SBF solution as an in vitro model for mineralization tests. After
immersion in the SBF for 7 days, the top-view SEM images
showed flake-like crystals, a typical morphology of HAp,45
homogeneously piled up on the whole surface of the MM-
MON film (Figure 4D,E; Figures S22 and S23). Correspond-
ing cross-sectional SEM-EDS elemental mapping demonstra-
ted that the Au and Ti/Zr/Hf elements (only from the MM-
MON film) were distributed in the bottom region, whereas the
P and Ca elements were more concentrated at the top layer,
indicating that the PA−metal complexes were consumed and
accompanied by the mineralization of HAp. In contrast, only a
few crystalline platelets of HAp were found if the MM-MON
film was not coated by PA−metal complexes (Figure S24).
The Raman spectroscopy technique was assessed to examine
the surface chemical composition after HAp mineralization. As
shown in Figure 4F, the most prominent peaks at ∼961 cm−1
are attributed to the ν1 P−O stretching vibration mode of PO4,
a representative demonstration of crystalline HAp.45 The peaks
at ∼430, 594, and 1043 cm−1 are ascribed respectively to the
bending ν2, antisymmetric bending ν4, and antisymmetric
stretching ν3 modes of PO4.46 Therefore, the PA−metal
complex constructed biointeractive interfaces are effectively
accounted for in the in situ mineralization of HAp.
Highly hydrophilic surfaces might be especially favored to
promote osteogenic cells to adhere, proliferate, and differ-
entiate.47 To evaluate the ability to support preosteoblast cell
adhesion, MC3T3-E1 cell morphology was characterized after
cells were stained for actin (red) and the nucleus (blue). The
cytoskeleton observations indicated that MC3T3-E1 cells on
the complete metal−organic architectures (abbreviated as
CMOA) exhibited a more spreading morphology and the most
extensive cytoskeletal organization when compared with
noncoated and hydrophobic Zr-MON groups (Figure 5A).
The proliferation of MC3T3-E1 preosteoblasts on the coatings
was further investigated by the CCK-8 assay. No obvious
cytotoxicity to cells was observed even with the highest
contents of Cu2+ (Figure 5B). The alkaline phosphatase (ALP)
activity and the extracellular matrix mineralization level of
osteoblasts were frequently evaluated as a typical early marker
and a late indicator of osteoblast differentiation,48 respectively.
After 7 days of culture, the ALP activity of MC3T3-E1 cells in
the PA−metal complex coated groups was significantly higher
than that in groups without PA−metal complex coating
(Figure 5C). The extracellular matrix mineralization level of
MC3T3-E1 cells was assessed by Alizarin Red S staining.48
After culturing on the coatings for 2 weeks, the reddish-black
coloration was much more evident in the PA−metal complex
coating groups (Figure 5D). The corresponding quantitative
analysis, through measurement of the absorbance, further
substantiates that the introduction of PA−metal complexes
was effective in calcium deposition and promoting osteogenic
differentiation (Figure 5E), which was consistent with the
alteration pattern of ALP activity. Additionally, the osteogenic-
related gene expression trends of ALP, osteopontin (OPN),
and osteocalcin (OCN) were similar to the trends of ALP
activities and mineralization levels (Figure 5F).49

and then soaked in culture medium. The culture plates and
mediums were further incubated at 37 °C for 24 and 12 h,
respectively. The bacterial colonies were spread densely on the
rolling trace of the noncoated Ti rod group, and the culture
medium became turbid with the growth of the residual bacteria
on the surface (Figure 6G). Comparatively, almost no bacterial
colonies were found in the CMOA group and the culture
medium was clear. This analysis result further indicated that
MRSA on the surface of the implants was substantially
eradicated (Figure 6H). Furthermore, H&E staining and
Giemsa staining were performed to investigate the inflamma-
tory response and the remaining bacteria in the bone tissues
and the surrounding soft tissues at 14 days after implanta-
tion.38 After H&E staining, numerous inflammation cells
infiltrated into the bone tissues around the noncoated Ti
implant (Figure 6I). However, only a few inflammation cells
were observed in the CMOA group, indicating that
inflammation reaction had basically subsided. In addition,
after Giemsa staining, there were still a lot of bacteria in the
noncoated group, whereas the remaining MRSA decreased
significantly in the metal−organic coating groups (Figure 6I).
The histological evaluation of surrounding soft tissues
demonstrated an alteration pattern similar to that indicated
in the bone tissues (Figure S26).
In Vivo Osseointegration. On the basis of the in vivo
results, the Ti rods coated with the CMOA were endowed with
highly effective antibacterial performance. The osteogenic
ability of the coatings was monitored by microcomputed
tomography (micro-CT) after 8 weeks of implantation.54 As
shown in Figure 7A, more bone mass was newly formed on the
interface of the bone implant in the CMOA group when
compared with the noncoated group and Zr−Cu-MON group,
indicating improved osteogenesis. The quantitative analysis of

CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we proposed an operationally facile method for
implant surface modification by in situ linking inorganic and
organic moieties together through a metal−organic coordina-
tion-driven self-assembly process. The implant−coating inter-
face was made up of Au25(MHA)18 nanocluster-based
amorphous photonic structures constructed by metal−
carboxylate coordination networks, while the coating−bone
interface was composed of PA−metal complexes via metal−
organophosphate bonding. Essentially, these two metal−
organic coordination networks share a Lewis acid−base adduct
nature when viewed upon as both of such constructs
connected by Lewis acidic metal ions to Lewis basic ligands.
The harmoniously fabricated self-assembling architectures
created a sophisticated toolset, combining the antibacterial
activity with osteoinductivity, to underpin the expectations for
future surface engineering of metal implants.
EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Materials. Gold(III) chloride trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O, 99%),
sodium borohydride (98%), methanol (99.9%), and ethanol (99.8%)
was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai,
China). The chemical compounds of 6-mercaptohexanoic acid
(MHA, 90%), titanium(IV) chloride (99.99%), zirconium(IV)
chloride (99.9%), hafnium(IV) chloride (99.5%), iron(III) chloride
hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, 99%), copper(II) chloride dihydrate
(CuCl2·2H2O, 99.99%), zinc chloride anhydrous (ZnCl2, 98%), and
phytic acid (70 wt.% in H2O) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent
Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus
(MRSA, ATCC 43300) and Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC 35218)
were obtained from the Department of Microbiology Laboratory,
Shanghai Jiao Tong University Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital. The
live/dead BacLight bacterial viability kit was purchased from Thermo
Fisher Scientific. All chemicals were used directly without additional
purification. All substrates including silicon wafers, Ti disks, and Ti
rods were ultrasonically cleaned in acetone, ethanol, and ultrapure
water sequentially for 10 min each to remove surface contaminants.
Preparation of Au25(MHA)18 Nanoclusters. The synthesis of
Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters was based on a reported method.24,25 The
fully protonated Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters were prepared by adding
a large excess of 1 M HCl. The precipitated nanoclusters were
separated by centrifugation (6000g, 2 min) and washed with water
three times to remove NaCl. The fully protonated nanoclusters were
then dispersed in ethanol for subsequent experiments unless specified
otherwise.
Preparation of GNCs-Based Films. For the preparation of
structurally colored films via the metal−carboxylate bonds directed
self-assembly of Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters, the protonated
Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters were first dissolved in ethanol/methanol
(1:1, v/v) solvent at a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL. For the
preparation of Ti-, Zr-, or Hf-based homometallic films, TiCl4, ZrCl4,
and HfCl4 were dissolved in methanol, respectively. As an example,
0.2 mL of fully protonated Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters (2.5 mg/mL)
and MCl4 (100 mM) (M = Ti, Zr, Hf) with the desired amount and
mole ratio were mixed. Next, the mixture was dropped onto the Si
wafer or Ti disk, which was placed on a 42 °C hot plate. As the
solvent rapidly evaporated, the non-iridescent structurally colored
films were obtained within 2 min and left to dry for another 2 h.
Similar procedures were performed to prepare Ti−Cu-, Zr−Cu-, or
Hf−Cu-based mixed-metal metal−organic network films with the
desired blend mole ratio. After drying, the surface-supported films
were washed with deionized water and dried with a stream of
nitrogen. This drop-casting procedure was used for all experiments
reported herein. The MM-MON films used for the HAADF-STEM
measurement and elemental mapping were prepared by dropping an
ethanol/methanol (1:1, v/v) solvent dispersion of the mixtures onto
300-mesh carbon-coated TEM grids, allowing to evaporate in the air
and forming MM-MON films.
Fabrication PA−Metal Complex Coating. As a typical
preparation, the PA−Fe−Zn complex solution was prepared by
adding the ethanol solutions of FeCl3·6H2O and ZnCl2 to the PA
solution (0.13 M, prepared via the addition of 0.25 mL of the 70 wt %
PA solution to 2.25 mL of ethanol) to give 0.065 M PA, 0.13 M Fe3+,
and 0.13 M Zn2+. The GNCs-based structurally colored film was
immersed in the PA−Fe−Zn complex solution for 10 min at room
temperature. The samples were then removed and rinsed with copious
amounts of water to remove most residual weakly bound PA
molecules and metal ions. Afterward, the three-cycle assembly was
performed by repeating of immersing in the PA−Fe−Zn complex
solution and rinsing with water. Finally, the complete metal−organic
coatings were rinsed with copious amounts of water to remove most
residual weakly bound PA molecules and metal ions and dried with a
stream of nitrogen.
Characterization. SEM analyses were performed on a Tescan
MAIA3 and a Zeiss Utral 55 at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. The
corresponding EDS elemental mapping was acquired on a Tescan
MAIA3 operated at 20 kV. Atomic force microscope images were
collected using an Asylum MFP-3D system in intermittent contact
mode at room temperature in ambient air conditions. XPS analyses
were performed with a Krato Axis Ultra DLD spectrometer using
monochromatic Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV). All XPS spectra
were referred to the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. HAADF-STEM images
and EDS mapping were acquired on a FEI Talos F200X S/TEM
operated at 200 kV with a Super-X EDS detector. The water contact
angles of the surfaces were measured by the contact angle
measurement device (DSA100; KRÜSS, Germany). In situ GIWAXS
was measured at the 13A beamline of the National Synchrotron
Radiation Research Center (NSRRC, Taiwan). All samples for
GIWAXS measurement were radiated with 12.12 keV X-rays with an
incident angle of 0.15°. The GISAXS intensity patterns were recorded
by a two-dimensional charge-coupled device detector. The 2D
GIWAXS patterns were analyzed using the Fit2D software and
displayed in scattering vector q (q = 4π sin θ/λ). Raman spectra were
obtained with a Renishaw inVia Qontor confocal Raman microscope
system equipped with a solid-state laser (λ = 532 nm) as the
excitation source.
Statistical Analysis. All data are shown as means ± standard
deviations with n ≥3. Significant differences among sample groups
were determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and
Student’s t-test. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001 were
considered statistically significant.

SUMMARY
Microglia actively monitor the neighboring brain microenvironments and constantly contact synapses with
their unique ramiﬁed processes. In neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), micro-
glia undergo morphological and functional alterations. Whether the direct manipulation of microglia can
selectively or concurrently modulate synaptic function and the response to disease-associated factors re-
mains elusive. Here, we employ optogenetic methods to stimulate microglia in vitro and in vivo. Membrane
depolarization rapidly changes microglia morphology and leads to enhanced phagocytosis. We found that
the optogenetic stimulation of microglia can efﬁciently promote b-amyloid (Ab) clearance in the brain paren-
chyma, but it can also enhance synapse elimination. Importantly, the inhibition of C1q selectively prevents
synapse loss induced by microglia depolarization but does not affect Ab clearance. Our data reveal indepen-
dent microglia-mediated phagocytosis pathways toward Ab and synapses. Our results also shed light on a
synergistic strategy of depolarizing microglia and inhibiting complement functions for the clearance of Ab
while sparing synapses.
INTRODUCTION
Microglia are the innate immune cells of the central nervous sys-
tem. They play important roles in clearing pathogens and in
maintaining brain homeostasis.1,2 Microglia are also necessary
for proper brain maturation and synapse elimination during
development.3,4 In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), microglia are asso-
ciated with pathological b-amyloid (Ab) deposits.5–8 Genome-
wide association studies in humans have revealed that a large
fraction of risk genes associated with increased AD susceptibility
is highly enriched in microglia.9–11 In disease conditions, deﬁ-
cient or exacerbated functions of microglia have been largely
described, strongly implicating these cells in the pathogenesis
of neurodegeneration.12–14 Although the involvement of micro-
glia in disease progression and synapse loss has been investi-
gated, only a few studies have directly manipulated the micro-
glial activity, probably due to a lack of tools2,15–17 and
difﬁculties in using virus-based methods to target microglia.18
Given the close interactions between microglia, Ab, and synap-
ses,19–25 manipulating microglia may represent a proactive strat-
egy to exert inﬂuences on the neural circuits and the disease-
associated protein aggregates.
Optogenetic
approaches
are
targeted
cell
stimulation
tools.26,27 When activated, channelrhodopsins (ChRs) allow
ions into the cells, thus causing depolarization. They are widely
used to excite neurons for neural circuit manipulation.28
Although microglia are non-excitable cells, few attempts have
been made to modulate them with electrical stimulation or opto-
genetics, showing that these cells can be depolarized.29–31 Mi-
croglia also display spontaneous calcium transients measured

In this study, we employed optogenetic methods to stimulate

with microglial depolarization, complement C1q plays a crucial
role in selective synapse removal but not in amyloid clearance.
Optogenetic stimulation not only provides an efﬁcient method
to manipulate the membrane potential of microglia but also of-
fers a potential therapeutic strategy for clearance of protein ag-
gregates without synapse loss when combined synergistically
with inhibition of the complement pathway.
RESULTS
Depolarization of microglia by optogenetics enhances
phagocytosis
Depolarization of microglia has been linked to chemotaxis, ra-
miﬁcation, and phagocytosis.35 To understand how depolarization
can
contribute
to
microglial
functions,
we
expressed
the
engineered opsin gene ChETA (ChR2 E123T/A)36 in BV2 cells
and
primary
microglia
using
lentivirus
carrying
the
CMV
(cytomegalovirus)-ChETA-EYFP
(enhanced
yellow
ﬂuorescent
protein) construct or a control construct CMV-GFP (Figure 1A).
The lentiviral vector successfully expressed the ChETA-EYFP in
the BV2 cells with high efﬁciency. To verify that the ChETA could
depolarize the BV2 cells, we used patch-clamp electrophysiology
to record the membrane potential from single cells while inducing
simultaneous stimulation with a blue laser (Figure 1B). Blue-light
pulses induced periodic depolarization currents (Figure 1C).
Compared with the ChETA-expressing BV2 cells, no change in cur-
rents was detected in the control BV2 cells that expressed only
GFP. Therefore, we conﬁrmed that optogenetic stimulation with
blue
light
in
the
ChETA-expressing
BV2
cells
leads
to
depolarization.
We then asked whether depolarizing microglia using optoge-
netics can change their phagocytosis capacity. We used
ChETA-expressing and GFP-expressing primary microglia and
placed a custom-designed blue-light LED (light-emitting diode)
panel above the cells to perform stimulation (Figure 1D). After
1 h of light stimulation, we found that the levels of the ﬂuores-
cence-labeled microbeads internalized in the ChETA group
were signiﬁcantly higher than in the control group (Figure 1E).
The amount of ﬂuorescent microbeads was unchanged in the
two groups without light stimulation (Figure 1E). We observed
similar increased uptake of ﬂuorescent microbeads when we
stimulated the BV2 cells with blue light (Figures S1A and S1B).
Increased phagocytosis was also conﬁrmed using ﬂow cytome-
try analysis (Figure S1C). A similar increase in phagocytosis of
synaptic structures was observed as ﬂuorescently labeled syn-
aptosomes were internalized in the ChETA-expressing primary
microglia (Figure 1F) or BV2 cells (Figure S1D) upon light expo-
sure compared with controls or in the absence of light. These re-
sults indicate that optogenetic stimulation leads to enhanced
phagocytic activity in microglia.
In AD, microglia are the major resident cells in the brain to clear
the toxic Ab protein aggregates. We then treated primary micro-
glia with oligomeric Ab and performed optogenetic stimulation.
After 1 h of light stimulation, we also observed enhanced uptake
of the ﬂuorescently labeled Ab in the ChETA group (Figure 1G);
by contrast, no enhancement was observed in the control group
with light stimulation or in the two groups without light stimula-
tion (Figure 1G). We also observed enhanced Ab phagocytosis
in the ChETA-expressing BV2 cells when they were stimulated
by the light (Figure S1E).
Oligomeric Ab from human AD patients has been reported
to be more toxic than the synthetic oligomers.37,38 We then
treated the ChETA-expressing microglia with Ab oligomers
derived from human samples (Figures S2A and S2B). Light stim-
ulation enhanced phagocytosis of Ab oligomers derived from
three different human patients, both in primary (Figures 1H and
1I) and BV2 microglia (Figure S1F). Together, these data show
that blue-light stimulation of microglia in vitro enhances the
clearance of Ab.
Phagocytosis consists of discrete steps of internalization and
degradation. We then investigated the digestion of the Ab oligo-
mers after their internalization. 3 h after the light stimulation (Fig-
ure S2C), the ChETA-expressing BV2 cells showed enhanced
clearance of Ab compared with the other control groups (Fig-
ures S2D and S2E). These ﬁndings indicate that optogenetic
stimulation of the microglia leads to increased uptake of Ab olig-
omers, followed by efﬁcient intracellular degradation.
To understand what molecules may regulate the phagocytosis
after the optogenetic depolarization, we performed quantitative
mass spectrometry in the ChETA-expressing and GFP-express-
ing BV2 cells after the light stimulation. Interestingly, we found
protein changes involved in endocytosis or phagocytosis (Fig-
ure S3A; Table S1). Gene Ontology enrichment analyses suggest
that biological processes after the light stimulation were associ-
ated with endosomal transport, regulation of anatomical struc-
ture size, and macroautophagy (Figure S3B).

To understand the effects of optogenetic stimulation in vivo, we
implanted optic ﬁber in the hippocampus of Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27
mice after the induction of ChR2 expression by tamoxifen (Fig-
ure 2C). We also implanted the ﬁber in the Cx3cr1-creER mice
and used them as controls. After 2 weeks post optic ﬁber implan-
tation, we performed optogenetic stimulation using blue light in
Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27 and control mice (Figure 2B). We conﬁrmed
that optogenetic stimulations induced depolarization of microglia
in the hippocampus of the Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27 mice using patch-
clamp recording in acute brain slices (Figures S5A–S5G). We
found that optogenetic stimulation induced signiﬁcant changes
in the number and the morphology of microglia. Quantiﬁcation
of cell density underneath the ﬁber tip revealed distance-depen-
dent changes (Figure S4D). In the hemisphere of the Cx3cr1-
creER::Ai27 mice that received the light stimulation, the microglial
density increased gradually further away from the ﬁber tip (Fig-
ure 2C), reached a maximum value about 500 mm away from the
ﬁbertip,and returned tobaselinelevel at about 800 mm (Figure 2D).
The number and morphology of microglia remained unchanged in
the contralateral side, where no ﬁber was implanted (Figure 2C), or
in Cx3cr1-creER control mice that received light stimulation (Fig-
ure 2C), ruling out non-speciﬁc effects of the light per se. In addi-
tion, in the mice that were implanted with the optic ﬁber but did not
receive light stimulation, the number and morphology of microglia
were not changed (Figure S4E). The increase of microglial density
in a distance-dependent manner indicates that the observed
changes were speciﬁc to the light stimulation within the illumi-
nated region.
Enlargement of the microglia cell body with less ramiﬁed pro-
cesses was seen after the optogenetic stimulation (Figure 2C).
Compared with the control group, the body size of the individual
microglia increased about 3-fold (Figure 2E). The processes re-
tracted signiﬁcantly with a much shorter length in the Cx3cr1-
creER::Ai27 mice compared with the control mice or unstimu-
lated sites (Figure 2E). Increased cell body size and reduced
ramiﬁcation suggest that optogenetic stimulation could be asso-
ciated with alterations in microglial states. The phagolysosomal
marker CD68 exhibited a signiﬁcant increase only in the micro-
glia
of
Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27
mice
after
light
stimulation
(Figures 2F and 2G). Quantitative real-time PCR analysis of the
brain tissue underneath the optic ﬁber and the brain tissue
from the contralateral site revealed that optogenetic stimulations
led to an increase of multiple inﬂammatory factors (interleukin
[IL]-12, IL-6, IL-18, IL-1b, interferon-gamma [IFN-g], and tumor
necrosis factor alpha [TNF-a]), phagolysosomal marker (CD68),
complement (C1qa, C3), and a decrease of synaptic markers
(PSD95, VGlut1; Figure 2H). Therefore, selective optogenetic
stimulation in microglia is sufﬁcient to induce broad responses,
including profound morphological and functional alterations.
Enhanced clearance of Ab by the optogenetic
stimulation of microglia in vivo
We next investigated whether optogenetic stimulation of micro-
glia in vivo can lead to enhanced phagocytosis of Ab oligomers.
To this end, we crossed the Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27 mice with
5xFAD mice, an established mouse model of AD.41 We im-
planted the optic ﬁbers in the hippocampus and performed op-
togenetic stimulations afterward (Figure 3A). Similar to the opto-
genetic stimulations in the Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27 mice, we also
found an increased number and body size of microglia with
reduced processes in triple transgenic Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27::5x-
FAD mice compared with Cx3cr1-creER::5xFAD control mice
(Figures 3B–3E). Strikingly, optogenetic stimulation led to
enhanced clearance of oligomeric Ab (Figures 3B and 3F). The
levels of Ab in the control mice were comparable to the contra-
lateral side without optic ﬁber implantation (Figures 3B and 3F).
These data indicate that, similar to the enhanced phagocytosis
of amyloid in primary microglia or BV2 cells in vitro, optogenetic
stimulation of microglia in vivo also leads to enhanced clearance
of Ab in an AD mouse model.
Next, we employed the Ab-infusion model to study the clear-
ance effect after the optogenetic stimulation of microglia in
Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27 mice. Oligomeric Ab was infused into the
lateral ventricles, and optic ﬁbers were implanted in the hippo-
campus (Figure 4A). 2 weeks later, optogenetic stimulation
was performed. As expected, the light stimulation increased
the number and body size of microglia with reduced processes
in Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27 mice treated with Ab (Figures 4B–4E).
Compared with the Cx3cr1-creER mice, the light stimulation
reduced oligomeric Ab in Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27 mice (Figures 4B,
4F, and S6A). The clearance of Ab was also conﬁrmed by west-
ern blot and ELISA analyses (Figures S6B and S6C). Together,
these data indicate that optogenetic stimulation of microglia
leads to enhanced clearance of Ab in vivo.

Clearance of synapses by the optogenetic stimulation of
microglia
Microglia play important roles in synaptic pruning by elimina-
tion of synaptic elements in excess.3,4 Therefore, we tested
whether optogenetic stimulation can affect the phagocytosis
of synaptic elements. We followed the same optogenetic stim-
ulation protocol (Figure 2B) and examined the synapses at
both presynaptic and postsynaptic sites, assessing the levels
of VGlut1 and PSD95, respectively. Immunostaining analysis
revealed that optogenetic stimulation was associated with
decreased levels of synaptic markers in the double-transgenic
Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27 mice compared with the Cx3cr1-creER
control mice, both for PSD95 (Figure 5A) and for VGlut1 (Fig-
ure 5B). Furthermore, levels of presynaptic marker synapto-
physin were also reduced (Figure S7A). We conﬁrmed these
results using western blot analysis in brain tissues extracted
from the light-stimulated area of Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27 mice,
with a signiﬁcant decrease in the levels of PSD95, VGlut1,
and synaptophysin (Figures 5C and 5D). These results demon-
strate that optogenetic stimulation of microglia results in syn-
apse loss.

The initial stage of synaptic elimination comprises the con-
tact or interactions between the synapses and the microglia.
We thus asked whether these contacts and subsequent engulf-
ment of synapses can be modulated by optogenetic stimulation
of microglia in vivo. We speciﬁcally investigated whether the
duration of optogenetic stimulation may impact microglia-syn-
apse interactions. We performed light stimulation with different
protocols of duration: 15, 30, 45, and 60 min. We quantiﬁed the
levels of the presynaptic marker VGlut1 internalized by micro-

In Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27 mice, longer stimulation duration re-
sulted in higher engulfment of VGlut1 by microglia (Figure 5G).
The number and the volume of VGlut1-positive structures in-

tion, and a 60-min-long light stimulation resulted in the most
prominent engulfment (Figure 5G). By contrast, no duration-
dependent changes were observed in Cx3cr1-creER mice or
the unstimulated regions (Figures S8A and S8B). Together,
these data demonstrate that targeted optogenetic stimulation
in microglia enhances microglia-synapse interactions and sub-
sequent synapse elimination.

Synapse elimination by the optogenetic stimulation of
microglia is dependent on C1q
The complement pathway is involved in the modulation of syn-
aptic pruning.44 In AD models, the level of complement C1q is
elevated at early pathological stages, and it is associated with
increased phagocytic ability of microglia.21 The loss of C1q
function or blocking C1q using antibodies can prevent syn-
apse loss in AD models.45,46 We therefore tested whether
the enhanced phagocytosis of synapses by microglia depends
on C1q. We ﬁrst examined using immunostaining the C1q
levels in the brain areas that were stimulated by the light in
the Cx3cr1-creER::Ai27 mice, and we found a signiﬁcant in-
crease as compared with Cx3cr1-creER controls and the un-
stimulated areas (Figure 6A). Furthermore, colocalization anal-
ysis between C1q and PSD95 or VGlut1 revealed an increase
in the fraction of synaptic puncta that colocalized with C1q
(Figures 6B and 6C). These data suggest that C1q mediates
the synapse elimination induced by the optogenetic stimula-
tion of microglia.

creER::Ai27 brain to inhibit the C1q function in vivo before
the optogenetic stimulation (Figure 6D). As a control experi-

Selective clearance of Ab without synapse loss using
combined optogenetic stimulation of microglia and C1q
antibody treatment
We reasoned that the selective enhancement of synapse phago-
cytosis by the C1q could be exploited to design a combination
strategy of optogenetic stimulation of microglia and complement
inhibition to enhance Ab clearance without synapse loss. To test
this hypothesis, we used the Ab-infusion model in which we
infused oligomeric Ab into the brain ventricles of Cx3cr1-
creER::Ai27 mice. Afterward, we performed the optogenetic
stimulation of microglia while concurrently infusing C1q antibody
into the brain (Figure 7A). In the light-stimulated region of the
mice that received C1q antibody treatment, we observed an
increased number of microglia and enlargement of the cell
body with reduced processes compared with the unstimulated
region (Figures 7B–7E). In the mice that received IgG treatment,
we also observed a similar change in microglial density and
morphology (Figures 7B–7E). This indicates that optogenetic
stimulations in the Ab-infusion model induced microglia alter-
ations independent of C1q signaling. Furthermore, Ab levels
were reduced by the optogenetic stimulation in both treatment
groups (Figures 7B and 7F), indicating that the reduction of Ab
was not affected by the C1q antibody treatment. These data
were consistent with the in vitro results, demonstrating that
in vivo blockade of C1q functions also did not prevent the
enhanced clearance of Ab induced by the optogenetic stimula-
tion of microglia.
Finally, we quantiﬁed the synapses in the Ab-infused Cx3cr1-
creER::Ai27 mice that received combined optogenetic stimula-
tion and C1q antibody treatment. The levels of PSD95 and
VGlut1 in the stimulated brain region were comparable to those
in the unstimulated region (Figures 7G and 7H). On the contrary,
optogenetic stimulations in the IgG treatment group signiﬁcantly
reduced the levels of PSD95 and VGlut1 in the illuminated region.
These results show that blocking C1q can prevent microglia-
mediated synapse loss leaving unaltered the increased Ab
clearance by optogenetic stimulation. Taken together, using op-
togenetic control of microglia, we are able to achieve efﬁcient
clearance of Ab and protection of synapses in vivo by establish-
ing a synergistic strategy of microglial depolarization and C1q
inhibition.
DISCUSSION
Microglia play a key surveillance role under normal physiological
conditions, ensuring the maintenance of brain homeostasis.
They constantly scan the brain parenchyma with their highly
ramiﬁed processes, making continuous contact with synapses
and neighboring cells. Furthermore, microglia are the profes-
sional phagocytes of the central nervous system, efﬁciently en-
gulﬁng and degrading potential toxic protein aggregates, which
might otherwise accumulate and contribute to brain diseases.
Here, we show that targeted optogenetic stimulation controls
the morphology of microglia and enhances their phagocytic ca-
pacity, both in vitro and in vivo. The response of microglia is
conﬁned to the area of light illumination in the hippocampus,
indicative of a targeted region-speciﬁc effect. In mice infused
with oligomeric Ab, optogenetic stimulation enhances the clear-
ance of both amyloid and synapses. We show that microglial de-
polarization is accompanied by an increase in the complement
molecule C1q, which is selectively required to mediate synapse
loss. Finally, we demonstrate that the inhibition of C1q protects
from the synapse loss induced by the optogenetic stimulation
of microglia while leaving the efﬁcient clearance of Ab unaltered.
Microglia maintain a hyperpolarized membrane potential
mediated by the THIK1 channels. Knockout or inhibition of
THIK1 depolarizes microglia and leads to a decrease in ramiﬁca-
tion and reduction of surveillance. Membrane potential is there-
fore important for maintaining a healthy monitoring state of mi-
croglia.31,47–49 Depolarization in macrophages can also lead to
enhanced phagocytosis.50 Consistent with the above ﬁndings,
our optogenetic stimulation approach effectively depolarized mi-
croglia and reduced morphological complexity. Besides the
known role of membrane potential in ramiﬁcation and surveil-
lance, our results also reveal that depolarization induced by light
stimulation leads to a rapid enhancement of phagocytosis by mi-
croglia. Therefore, membrane potential manipulations in micro-
glia may effectively mobilize microglia when the homeostatic
functions are impaired in the aging brain51 or in neurodegenera-
tive diseases.13,52
Synapse elimination occurs in the developing brain and is crit-
ically dependent on microglia.3,4,19 Transient decrease of micro-
glial density during development is associated with impaired
synapse elimination and with an excessive number of dendritic
spines.19 Here, we show that optogenetic stimulation leads to
an increased number of microglia and enhancement of synapse
elimination. Therefore, the increased number of highly phago-
cytic microglia contributes crucially to synapse elimination. We

The classical complement signaling pathway C1q is involved
in synaptic pruning during development. Classical pathways
can induce C3 activation, and C3 can also act through the alter-
native pathways.53 C1q and C3-CR3 have been implicated in mi-
croglia-dependent synaptic pruning. Indeed, microglia are
considered to be the dominant cell types that synthesize C1q
in the central nervous systems.54 Increased C1q levels in micro-
glia
were
associated
with
disease
conditions,
including
ischemia, focal brain injury, and virus infections.55–58 Apart
from C1q, complement receptors may also contribute to the
phagocytosis or clearance of Ab. Microglia can express
C1aRp.59 C1q can bind to C1qRp and other proteins; however,
C1qRp serves as a cell-surface receptor and is not speciﬁc for
C1q.59,60 In C3 and CR3 deﬁcient mice during early postnatal
development, reduced synaptic pruning is observed.4 In AD
mouse models, C3 knockout and CR3 ablation were associated
with decreased Ab load.61,62 It is worth noting that despite the
decreased Ab level, the phagocytosis of ﬁbrillar Ab and ﬂuores-
cent beads has been reported to be reduced in the CR3
knockout microglia.63 This implies that deﬁciency of CR3 and
Ab clearance may not solely depend on microglial phagocytosis,
and CR3 deﬁciency was found to increase the secretion of Ab-
degrading enzymes by microglia.61 On the other hand, C3
knockout or inhibition can lead to increased Ab levels with
diverse synapse outcomes in genetic AD models.64,65 Therefore,
besides the roles of C1q in mediating synapse elimination,
blocking the complement cascade might have more profound
consequences.
In AD models, the complement pathway plays an important
role in microglia-mediated early synapse loss.21,45,62 Oligomeric
Ab was shown to exacerbate the production of complement and
promote synapse elimination by microglia.21 By employing opto-
genetic tools selectively in microglia, we are able to separate
C1q-independent and C1q-dependent phagocytic pathways
that exert differential clearance functions toward Ab and synap-
ses, respectively. Our results support a role for C1q in the iden-
tiﬁcation of target synapses, possibly through binding mecha-
nisms
that
do
not
affect
the
removal
of
amyloid.
The
phagocytic capability of microglia is often impaired under
diseased conditions. Altogether, active boosting of microglial
phagocytosis by optogenetics may be a powerful strategy to
promote clearance of Ab and other toxic aggregates, in combi-
nation with anti-complement therapies to protect synapses.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS
Animals
Cx3cr1-creER mice,39 Ai27 mice40 5xFAD mice41 were bred to obtain the double or triple transgenic animals for local optogenetic
stimulation in the hippocampus. The age of the mice (heterozygous for Cx3cr1-creER and ROSA26) was 3 months old at the begin-
ning of the experiments. Both male and female mice were used. All mice were group housed under standard housing conditions
(12:12-hr light/dark cycle) with food pellets and water ad libitum. For primary microglia, C57BL/6J mice were used. Animal husbandry
and experimental manipulation in this study were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committees at the Shenzhen Institute of
Advanced Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China. The experimenters were not blind to the genotype of the animals
when the optogenetic stimulation experiments were performed. However, they were blind to the genotypes of the brain slices
when the antibody staining was performed. They were also blind to the genotypes for the image acquisition and the analysis of image
data.
BV2 cells
BV2 cells were planted in a 24-well plate with a density of 23103 cells per well. The medium contained MSFM (Invitrogen, Carlsbad,
CA, USA) with 10% FBS and 100 U mL-1 penicillin/streptomycin, at 37C and 5% CO2 in a humidiﬁed incubator. 18-24 hours later,
lentivirus was added to the culture with a MOI of 1, 10 or 100 supplemented with polybrene (6 mg/ml). 48 hours later, we could observe
the expression of ﬂuorescent proteins. The media were changed every 2 to 3 days.
Primary microglia
Primary microglia were harvested from newborn pups (postnatal day 3). Both male and female C57BL/6J mice were sued. The brain
was cut with scissors and placed in digestion buffer (Sigma, P3125). The solution was placed in 37C incubator for 90 mins and the
tissue was homogenized every 30 min. Then HBSS was added to the solution to ﬁnish the digestion. The solution was ﬁltered with a
40 mm-diameter membrane to ﬁlter out large size cells. The solution was centrifuged for 7 mins. Different concentrations of Percoll
solution were added to the ﬁltered cells. The solution was centrifuged for 40 mins. The cells between the 70%–37% layer separations
were sucked out by the pipette. The cells were centrifuged again. The cells were planted in a well with DMEM solution.
METHOD DETAILS
BV2 assay and optogenetic stimulation
We expressed the optogenetics of ChETA in the BV2 cells using lentivirus. The lentivirus was packaged by in-house virus production
service. In brief, the CMV promoter was cloned into the pLenti-CaMKIIa-ChETA-EYFP or pLenti-CaMKIIa-GFP plasmid (provided by
Dr Karl Deisseroth). Then 293FT cells were transfected with the lentiviral vector pCMVDR8.74 and pMD2G, and a high titer lentivirus
was produced. 24 hours after the transfection, 293FT cells were transferred to serum-free medium containing 5 mM sodium butyrate;
the supernatant was collected and concentrated by ultracentrifugation, then phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used to re-sus-
pend the viral pellets.
After lentiviral transfection, the optogenetic stimulation was performed using a custom-design LED panel. The device contained a
LED panel cover with a panel of LED lights directly facing the 24-well plate. The device was placed inside the incubator. The power
was 5 mW and the frequency was 10 Hz with pulse duration 5 ms. The stimulation duration was 1 hour.
For the phagocytosis experiment, ﬂuorescent beads, synaptosomes or ﬂuorescence-labeled Ab were added to the BV2 culture
before the light stimulation. BV2 cells were planed to a small coverslip placed in the well for future immunohistochemistry. The diam-
eter of the ﬂuorescent beads was 200 nm. The synaptosome was extracted from the mouse brain. Cortical and hippocampal brain
tissues were harvested. The synaptosome was extracted with the synaptosome kit (HL10482, Shanghai Haling Biotech). The synap-
tosomes were then labeled with ﬂuorescent probe FM 1-43FX (Thermo Fisher). We used ﬂuorescently labeled amyloid (Beta-Amyloid
1-40, HiLyte Fluor 647, Anaspec) for the phagocytosis of Ab. After 1-hour light stimulation, the plate was removed from the incubator.

Extraction of human Ab
Human specimens were originally obtained from the Massachusetts ADRC Neuropathology core at the Massachusetts General Hos-
pital in accordance with Partners Institutional Review (Walsh BWH2011). Brain extracts from three end-stage AD patients were pre-
pared as described previously37 and kindly provided by Dr. Dominic M. Walsh. All the individuals were female, AD1955 was 69-year
old, AD414 was 75-year old, and AD1849 was 68-year old at death. All individuals were clinically and neuropathologically conﬁrmed

A B S T R A C T   
Cyclodextrins are extensively employed in drug delivery systems like inclusion complexes, metal-organic 
frameworks, functionalized or PEGylated conjugates, and other nanocarrier systems such as nanosponges or 
hydrogel nanoparticles for targeted effect or prolonged release action. Applications of CDs range from drug- 
loaded nanocarrier systems useful for disease conditions (such as cancer, diabetes, and bacterial infections, 
etc.) to supramolecular chemistry, diagnostics, imaging, biosensors, and medical devices. However, there is a 
limited data and information on the adverse effects caused by cyclodextrins and their toxicities in the medical 
field. Various in-vitro and ex-vivo toxic effects such as cytotoxicity, ototoxicity, etc. as well as the adverse and 
toxic effects depend on the role of administration of cyclodextrins. This review article focuses on the advance­
ment of characteristics, properties and chemistry of cyclodextrins and addresses the new challenges faced in 
cyclodextrin-based delivery systems and the various toxicities induced by them.   
1. Introduction to cyclodextrins 
Cyclodextrins are cyclic oligosaccharides comprising of 6 or more 
units of α-1,4-linked D-glucopyranose units produced from starch by the 
action of cyclodextrin glucosyltransferase, via enzymatic degradation 
and intramolecular rearrangement reaction (Crini, Fourmentinn, & 
Lichtfouse, 2018; Topuz & Uyar, 2018). The cyclodextrins (CDs) of 
pharmaceutical interest are α-, β-, and γ-CDs subunits and there are 
found in a variety of drug delivery systems for cancer and gene therapies 
as well as in diagnostics, imaging, biosensors and medical devices. CDs, 
as the name suggests, are ring or truncated-cone shaped molecules 
which connect adjacent units by hydrogen bonding on the secondary 
hydroxyl groups (Crini et al., 2018). The size of cavity is determined by 
the number of glucopyranose units, for example, α-CDs form a hexago­
nal lattice structure, β-CDs comprise of a monocyclic parallelogram and 
γ-CDs form a quadratic prism crystal structure (de Miranda et al., 2011). 
The hydroxyl groups on the external surface of the cone render CDs with 
hydrophilicity whereas the hydrogen (H-3, H-5) and oxygen atoms of 
the glycosidic link impart hydrophobicity to the chemical structure 
(Topuz & Uyar, 2018). This amphiphilic nature of CDs, with a hydro­
phobic interior or core and a hydrophilic shell, allows them for inclusion 
(inclusion complexes) of a diverse range of drugs and genes, especially 
lipophilic molecules and compounds with a poor aqueous solubility (as 
per the Biopharmaceutics Classification System, BCS Class II and IV 
drugs) (Brewster & Loftsson, 2007; Crini, 2014). The properties of these 
3 parent CDs are further modified or functionalized for the applications 
as carriers or enhancers in the pharmaceutical and food industries 
(Jansook et al., 2018). The applications of CDs are extensive in almost all 
the pharmaceutical dosage forms such as oral, nasal, parenteral, topical, 
etc. due to their pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamics characteris­
tics as well as capacity to develop inclusion complexes with potent drugs 
in the biomedical field. The advances of CDs in novel delivery systems 
show a wide range, e.g. higher encapsulation efficiency, better solubil­
ity, greater bioavailability, stimuli-responsiveness, sustained or 
controlled release, site-specific action, biocompatibility, protection of 
drugs from degradation, etc. For example, drugs formulated with the 
help of nanotechnology to develop new carrier systems such as micelles, 
nanoassemblies such as liposomes, nanosponges (Cavalli et al., 2006; 
Pawar et al., 2019; P. Shende, Deshmukh, Trotta, & Caldera, 2013; P.K. 
Shende et al., 2012; P.K. Shende, Gaud, Bakal, & Patil, 2015), hydrogels 
(Wintgens et al., 2015; Omtvedt et al., 2021; Narayanaswamy & 
Torchilin, 2019), metallo-organic structures, graphene-conjugates,

Naturally, CDs have been considered as safe and there is a less 
amount of research studies and information on the adverse effects 
caused by pure CDs. In nanomedicines, wherein the CDs are used to 
encapsulate drugs as an excipient, offer controlled release of drugs and 
encompass better drug loading efficiency, which is an important aspect 
that should be considered while evaluating the toxicity profile of CD- 
based delivery system. For example, high loading efficiencies (low 
levels of CDs used) reduce the toxicity of CDs and are usually acceptable, 
however lower loading efficiencies, the chances of toxicity may increase. 
CDs have demonstrated few toxicities via various routes of administra­
tion or in higher concentrations (e.g. the LD50 value for β-CD is <12,500 
mg/kg with oral administration in mice and rats) (Hedges, 2009). 
Furthermore, the applications of CDs offered in various delivery sys­
tems, were initially associated with certain limitations like: 1. Some 
drugs may be unsuitable to form complexes with CDs due to the in­
compatibilities of their structures and inability to fit in the cavity of CDs 
(de Miranda et al., 2011); 2. In oral dosage forms, the chemical prop­
erties of the actives are also considered, such as complexation properties 
and dosage, for e.g. CDs are only used for drug doses <200 mg 
depending on the volume of formulation(de Miranda et al., 2011). Also, 
the inclusion complex stability depends on the hydrophobicity of the 
drug molecule, wherein they readily form complexes with CDs, but 
hydrophilic molecules bind to CDs with weaker bonding (Martin et al., 
2018). One reason that CDs are not effective in transporting BCS Class III 
drugs is due to hydrophilic nature and inability to form complexes with 
CD structures (Loftsson et al., 2007). The formation of CD-aggregates 
and self-assembling properties are also affected by intramolecular 
hydrogen bonding and the concentration (Cova et al., 2018); 3. As for 
solubility limitations, the water-solubility of natural CDs (α-, β-, and 
γ-CDs), especially β-CD with limited solubility (~18.4 mg/ml) is 
restricted due to the relative bonding strength in crystal state (Sharma & 
Baldi, 2016); 4. Human physiology also plays a crucial role, since the 
drugs released from CD-complexes through gastro-environmental me­
dium may face challenges in absorption due to media dilution. The 
aqueous diffusion layer also acts as a challenge towards the permeation 
of drugs across the targeted tissues in nasal, pulmonary or ocular dosage 
form since volume in this layer is small compared to the other routes and 
hence the dose is dissolved in limited aqueous volume (Jansook et al., 
2018). To overcome such limitations, CD-derivatives are employed to 
improve solubility and inclusion capacity, such as lipophilic derivatives 
of CDs (like methylated derivatives), which can easily pass through 
biological membranes (Sharma & Baldi, 2016), but show insignificant 
side effects such as nausea or vomiting. This, restricts the amount of CD 
that is used in a formulation to a lower dose. Furthermore, the potential 
nasal ciliary damage induced by high doses of certain CD such as 
dimethyl-β-CD (DM-β-CD) also leads to limited applications of such CDs 
in nasal and pulmonary formulations (Stella & Rajewski, 1997). 
The novelty of this manuscript is demonstrated by the extent and 
implications various toxicities induced by CDs in higher concentrations 
such as ototoxicity, cytotoxicity, cellular modifications, nephrotoxicity, 
interactions with lipid structures and extraction, etc. This manuscript is 
a pioneer in assembling these challenges of cyclodextrins in terms of 
toxicity and adverse effects in a single review, instead of single toxicity 
issues tackled separately. Furthermore, as per the literature search, this 
review article provides the updated information via compilation of 
toxicity studies of cyclodextrins that are not reported previously. 
2. In-silico studies 
Toxicological studies play a very important role in evaluating the 
nature, characteristics, suitability, and safety of various drugs (anti­
cancer, anti-inflammatory, etc.) and excipients. Since CDs are 
extensively explored in almost every pharmaceutical, biomedical, and 
food segments, it is important to evaluate their adverse reactions, toxic 
effects and calculate the related dose requirements for safety. CD-based 
toxicities are those which may cause serious adverse effects or disrupt 
the natural physiological conditions of the body, and may arise due to 
use of CD in various delivery systems or nanocarriers. The toxic and 
adverse effects are summarized in Fig. 1. 
In-silico studies are used in research and drug discovery to study the 
pharmacokinetics of various drugs and excipients and for pre- 
development predictions to understand the nature and structure of ac­
tives, their interactions, and isolating potential toxicities using various 
techniques and computational models such as molecular dynamics and 
simulations, docking, Quantitative Structure-Activity Relationship 
(QSAR), etc. Earlier research studies were, therefore, performed on CDs 
to understand the complexation properties, identifying the risk factors 
associated with them and to obtain basic pharmacokinetics profile, for 
example, log K (stability constant) values, experimental crystallographic 
and NMR data obtained for β-naphthyloxyacetic acid/β-CD as ligand- 
receptor inclusion complex using docking strategy(Ermondi et al., 
2007). Such computational techniques also provide the cost- and time- 
effective method to recognize the pharmacological properties of the 
drug and target patient population. In-vivo disposition of potential ac­
tives are also identified based on Physiologically-based Pharmacokinetic 
(PBPK) models, which simulate pharmacokinetics profiles of drugs and 
complexes; establish evidence of potential interactions on exposure to 
biological tissues and fluids; as well as in conjunction to pharmacody­
namic models, they predict and generate data on the time course of the 
drug and dosing regimens (Alqahtani, 2017). Moreover, structural and 
physicochemical behavior of drugs in human body and complex ratios of 
the actives in the human body are also recorded and considered as 
important parameters in toxicity. In one of the research studies, the 
pharmacokinetics and bioavailability parameters of the formulation 
consisting of erlotinib and sulfobutyl ether-β-CD (S-β-CD) complex were 
evaluated (Devasari et al., 2015). The drug was docked into S-β-CD and 
β-CD structures through the Genetic Optimisation for Ligand Docking 
(GOLD) program and the complexes were further optimized with the 
Hartree-Fock method. The in-silico models compared the interactions of 
the drug compound with S-β-CD and β-CD and concluded better impact 
on dissolution rate and bioavailability parameters. Here, the lower 
binding energy of erlotinib in the process demonstrated the ease of 
complex formation and suggested better pharmacokinetics potential of 
the complexed drug(Devasari et al., 2015). Similarly, another study 
highlighted the systematic structural and thermodynamic natures of the 
inclusion complexes formed between β-CD and phenanthrene (Phe) 
through molecular dynamics simulation and adaptive biased force 
methods. The experiment was focused on the association of β-Phe 
complexes in water and membrane environments to improve the uptake 
of the hydrophobic Phe by bacteria for effective biodegradation of soil 
contaminants such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH). The 
Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations predicted that the drop in 
conformational entropy resulted in the formation of the inclusion 
complexes and the in-silico results displayed that Phe easily passed 
through the membranes without encountering huge energy barrier in 
comparison to β-CD complexes which demonstrated only the bioavail­
ability enhancing properties of β-CDs and disregarded its penetration 
abilities. Results further suggested dose-dependent biodegradation since 
Phe uptake by bacteria was dependent on optimal molar ratios of β-CD: 
Phe (Ren et al., 2015). 
In-silico methods are also used by US FDA as predictive tools to 
compute toxicology of various pharmaceuticals and to record the data 
for generating regulatory procedures. Furthermore, it becomes impor­
tant to detect the potential toxicity issues of excipients used in medicine. 
Identification of toxicophores and potential toxicity of actives through 
various modelling techniques also prevents failures in clinical drug 
development for various diseases (Alqahtani, 2017). A study computed 
the MD simulation of CDs: α-CD, HP-β-CD, and trimethyl-β-CD (TM-

randomly and cross-validating them with computational functions and 
constant coefficients (Toropov et al., 2010; Zhu et al., 2009). In another 
example, a natural alkaloid, harman, was complexed with β-CD and its

the formulation. It provided the information about the ideal geometries 
and position of drug functional groups in relation to β-CD i.e. distance 
between the host and the guest, the energy profiles, and hydrogen bonds 
formed in the structure. It was also reported that this complex increased 
apoptosis, cell arrest, and cytotoxicity, as well as enhanced the activity 
of caspase-3, a pro-apoptotic agent, which was produced due to an in­
crease in the solubility of harman due to the β-CD complexation. Thus, 
predicting the optimized structure of CD-based complexes can prove to 
be effective in increasing the apoptotic and anti-cancer properties 
(Ferraz et al., 2020). On the other hand, significant drug solubility and 
bioavailability may also result to increase in drug penetration into cells 
and tissues and further lead to toxicity caused due to excessive accu­

3. Toxicity studies 
Although, CDs and novel CD-based delivery systems and nano­
carriers are examined for their cytotoxic nature, fundamentally, there is 
a lack of exclusive toxicological data causing on CDs that cause adverse 
reactions in the human body. There is also insufficient information on 
the toxicity of CDs caused by acute and repeated use on these cells for 
longer period. CD-based nanocarriers loaded with drugs and other de­
livery systems, where actives occupy the empty cavity of CDs, exhibit 
cytotoxicity due to higher permeability and uptake of these drugs, rather 
than the impact of CDs. On the other hand, an unwanted increase in the 
immunogenicity can be produced by oligonucleotides for gene therapy 
due to better permeation and solubility (Lai, 2014; Onishi et al., 2015; 
Tiwari et al., 2010). Similar to this enhancement of toxicity of actives, 
CDs may also possess the potential of accumulated adverse effects over 
time and therefore, further research is required to determine the 
authentic and inherent toxicities of CDs in various pharmaceutical 
dosage forms. 
3.1. Role of the routes of administration 
CDs are used for their diverse applications in encapsulation of actives 
in drug delivery systems with non-toxic nature and are considered in 
GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) category. CDs do not readily pass 
through the biological and lipophilic membranes due to their low 
partition coefficient, presence of a high number of hydroxyl and 
hydrogen groups, and larger molecular weight (>970 Da) (Loftsson & 
Duchene, 2007). However, lipophilic derivatives of CDs (like methyl­
ated derivatives) can easily pass through these membranes (Sharma & 
Baldi, 2016) and cause insignificant side effects such as nausea or 
vomiting; and even ciliary damage when administered through the nasal

3.1.1. Oral administration 
Oral administration of CDs such as unsubstituted γ-CD is followed by 
its hydrolysis in the GIT by the digestive juices containing amylases, 
which is facilitated by the larger internal cavities of γ-CD. Conversely, α- 
and β-CDs with a smaller internal structure are resistant to amylases but 
are digested by the intestinal bacteria in the colon (Jansook, Ogawa, & 
Loftsson, 2018; Laza-Knoerr, Gref, & Couvreur, 2010). The absorbed 
CDs on the other hand are excreted in the urine without degradation. 
The use of CDs in low oral doses are non-toxic, inert, easily absorbed, 
and safely used in drug delivery systems, however, larger doses cause 
adverse effects such as kidney dysfunctions, pancreatic hyperplasia, etc. 
(Carneiro et al., 2019; Irie & Uekama, 1997) The LD50 value for β-CD is 
approximate <12,500 mg/Kg for acute oral dose in rats and mice 
(Hedges, 2009). While the toxicity symptoms via oral administration are 
extremely few and not varied, but in case of larger doses CDs cause 
diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, etc. On the other hand, crosslinked β-CD 
nanosponges without any loaded actives (dose up to 2000 mg/kg) were 
tested for acute and repeated dose toxicity in-vivo in rats according to 
the OECD guidelines and found to be safe with respect to hematological, 
hepatological, and renal issues (Shende, Kulkarni, et al., 2015). 
3.1.2. Parenteral administration 
Parenteral administration generates similar conditions since CDs 
form complexes with cholesterol in the blood which aggregates in high 
doses. Depending on different types, intravenously administered CD 
doses are excreted into the urine intact without degradation, for 
example, γ-CD undergoes degradation to a certain extent, however, α- 
and β-forms are intact, which further causes problems in the kidney and 
urinary tract (renal toxicity). Nephrotoxicity induced through any 
administration of α- and β-CDs is due to CDs-induced cellular modifi­
cations and structural changes to the tubular cells of kidneys (Stella & 
He, 2008). Modifications such as introducing hydrophilic groups to the 
structure of CDs (as in the case of HP-β-CD) were made to reduce such 
renal toxicity since increased solubility showed greater chances of ease 
of elimination. However, increased solubility of methylated CD de­
rivatives (e.g. DM-β-CD) failed to reduce nephrotoxicity. Moreover, 
systemic toxicity is caused by such CDs depend on their high surface 
activity and interaction with phospholipids. High doses of β-CD through 
intravenous (i.v.) administration also lead to hemolysis via deformation 
of red blood cell (RBC) membrane (Irie & Uekama, 1997). Another 
example is observed in a sham-operated and ovariectomized rat model,

hepatotoxicity are caused by HP-β-CD at 200 mg/Kg (Kantner & Erben,

3.1.3. Dermal delivery 
CD-based drug delivery systems for dermal delivery are considered 
safe and non-irritant, however, the ability of certain lipophilic CD 
(methylated derivatives) to interact and extract the lipids present in the 
stratum corneum layer of the skin is a matter of concern. Further, the 
safety of CD using dermal and subcutaneous routes is hindered by dose 
limitations, for example, i) higher concentrations of CDs caused skin 
irritation in the order of γ-CD < α-CD < β-CD in guinea pigs, and ii) the 
activity of effector caspases −3 and −7 may be triggered in-vitro by β-CD 
and randomly methylated β- cyclodextrin (RM-β-CD); while β-CD (0.5 % 
w/v) and RM-β-CD (1 % w/v) caused significant increase in release of 
LDH. Although such effects are rarely observed within concentrations 
<0.1 % w/v (European Medicines Agency, 2017). Higher concentrations 
of CDs (such as DM-β-CD) in animals are associated with skin irritation 
since they possess a greater affinity for interaction with the lipids 
(cholesterol and triglycerides) present in the skin (Irie & Uekama, 1997). 
HP-β-CDs are known to cause extraction of 5–10 % cholesterol and some 
proteins from the lamellar structure of stratum corneum and disruption 
of lipid-bilayer structure (Bentley et al., 1997). 
3.1.4. Ocular administration 
Ocular administration is associated with very few reported cases of 
toxicity, however, α-CD with >4 % caused epithelial toxicity involving 
microerosions of the cornea in rabbits, and in another case, RM-β-CD 
(concentrations of ~5 % and 12.5 %) leads to irritation of cornea and 
conjunctiva in rabbits (European Medicines Agency, 2017). Another 
example is the study that recorded the use of different CDs in eye drop 
formulations to increase solubility and bioavailability of riboflavin. 
Here, the penetration of CDs was studied on excised bovine corneas, 
revealing disruption of epithelia due to the extraction of cholesterol by 
β-CD from the corneal tissue. This interfered with its structure, per­
taining to long exposure time; whereas no such effect was reported for α- 
and γ-CDs (Morrison et al., 2013). 
3.1.5. Nasal route of administration 
Nasal route of administration mainly employs CD complexes of 
protein or peptide drugs, used for their effective delivery and to avoid 
the degradation of such drugs by physiological processes such as first- 
pass metabolism (Rasheed, 2008). However, the interaction of mucosa 
with the CD surface leads to certain adverse effects like nasal ciliary 
damage (Stella & Rajewski, 1997), mucotoxicity, etc. For example, 
while 10 % concentration of RM-β-CD was non-toxic, 20 % RM-β-CD 
concentration demonstrated severe nasal mucosal damage of rats. On 
the other hand, the concentration of β-CD should be kept <1.5 % to 
render its non-toxic nature (European Medicines Agency, 2017). 
Another study observed the local toxicological effects of various CDs on 
nasal epithelia, and while most of them were found to be inert, 8 % HP- 
β-CD was reported as slightly more toxic in rats than the control group 
(Marttin et al., 1998). This again leads to the conclusion that only low 
doses are enough to transfer the encapsulated drugs and maintain the 
efficacy. 
Similar examples of toxicity are recorded in animals, however, the 
safety profiles detailing human physiology is inadequate for derivation 
and interpretation of useful results. Hence, the use of CDs should be 
restricted according to the dosing specified for each route of adminis­
tration. Moreover, the requirements of doses (LD50) of CDs to cause such 
toxicity is very high, whereas the amounts of CDs used in novel drug 
delivery systems and other pharmaceutical applications are relatively 
low, thereby depleting the incidences of such adverse effects. Further­
more, in most cases, the adverse effects were reversible, and the animals 
returned to the normal state after the withdrawal of CD administration. 
However, the type of CDs used, their chemical structure, surface density, 
and the charge should be kept in consideration to avoid the occurrences 
of toxicity as a result of interactions between physiological components 
and chemical properties of CD. Fig. 2 summarizes the toxicities occur­
ring via different routes of administration along with their 
concentrations. 
3.2. Cytotoxicity studies 
Cytotoxic compounds are fatal or dangerous to cells because of 
reduction in cell viability and their mechanism of action comprises of 
either inducing apoptosis (genetically programmed cell death) or 
through cell oncosis(Cummings & Schnellmann, 2004). Necrosis occurs 
to a group of cells and is marked by rapid swelling of cells, loss of 
structural integrity, high permeabilization of plasma membrane, and 
elevation in inflammation of cells (Proskuryakov et al., 2003); whereas 
apoptosis involves cellular shrinkage and convolution, chromatin 
condensation and DNA fragmentation followed by phagocytosis 
(Elmore, 2007). A variety of cell lines were employed in studying the 
cellular interactions and toxic effects of CDs and CD-based delivery 
systems to measure the suitability of their use in pharmaceuticals. 
3.2.1. Cytotoxicity of CDs 
The toxic effects of α-CD and its derivatives were analyzed on Caco-2 
cell lines, and its hemolytic action was measured on human RBCs (R´oka 
et al., 2015). Unlike the cytotoxicity of β-CD, which is associated with its 
ability to extract cholesterol from cellular membranes, cytotoxicity of 
α-CD is measured by its ability to bind to other lipids and phospholipids, 
such as phosphatidylcholine, that fit tightly into the smaller cavity of 
α-CD (R´oka et al., 2015). Here, the toxic effects were correlated to the 
CD structure, since the methyl (TM-α-CD, RM-α-CD) and succinyl sub­
stitutions on glucopyranose rings of α-CD were observed to increase the 
toxic effects, while hydroxypropyl and acetylated derivatives showed a 
decrease in cytotoxicity. It was found that the degree of substitution of 
methyl groups increased the toxic effects by reducing the free hydroxyl 
groups. Similarly, MTT assay observed concentration-dependent he­
molysis with methylated (HC50 values: ~1.9 mM for trimethylated, 
~15.5 mM for randomly methylated α-CD derivatives) and succinylated 
(HC50 value: ~9.6 mM) derivatives as compared to hydroxylated and 
acetylated derivatives (HC50 values: >100 mM). The lower HC50 values 
than IC50 showed that α-CD possessed greater affinity to the RBCs 
membrane rather than intestine, which further suggested that the 
cytotoxic effects differ between different cell types depending on the 
different lipid compositions and extraction mechanisms (R´oka et al., 
2015). Similar studies are also conducted to gauge the toxicity of β-CD 
pertaining to their abilities of solubilizing and extracting cholesterol and 
lipid as well as the correlation with their structural and functional 
properties (Kiss et al., 2010). Furthermore, earlier studies evaluated CDs 
and their derivatives as excipients in ophthalmic solutions in immor­
talized human corneal epithelial (HCE) cell lines. Herein, they found 
that the cytotoxicity of the CD solutions decreased in the order: α-CD >
DM-β-CD > SB-β-CD (sulfobutyl ether β-CD) ≅HP-β-CD > γ-CD, indi­
cating the highest cytotoxic effect lined with α-CD. The cytotoxic ac­
tivity of the CDs was found to be related to their interactions with 
cellular components such as phospholipids, cholesterol, and proteins, 
etc., and their extraction (Saarinen-Savolainen et al., 1998). 
Methylated-β-CDs (M-β-CD) were analyzed for cytotoxic effects in- 
vitro against nerve growth factor-differentiated PC12 (NGFDPC12) 
cells, wherein significant loss of cell viability at higher concentration 
(more than 0.12 %) was observed. Furthermore, the study demonstrated 
an increase in caspase-3 activity and high expressions of Bax and Bcl-XL 
proteins for apoptosis and nuclear condensation at 0.25 % concentra­
tion. This concentration of methylated CDs also induced significant 
toxicity against immortalized Schwann cells with different dose-time 
dynamics. The mechanism of action of toxicity was suggested to be 
the depletion of cholesterol stores from the neuronal cells (Ulloth et al.,

2007). Similarly, various types of cationic β-CD polymers and other 
quaternary ammonium-β-CD polymers were assessed for cytotoxicity 
against neuronal (PC12) and intestine epithelial (Caco-2) cell lines, 
displaying dose-, charge- and molecular weight dependent toxic activ­

compared to the protonable compounds; and cationic polymers with 
higher molecular weights were found to be more cytotoxic when sub­

In another study, supramolecular complexes of chlorhexidine were 
formulated with α-, β- and HP-β-CD in varying concentrations and their 
cytotoxic effects were analyzed against rat osteoblasts, murine fibro­
blasts, and human tumoral Caco-2 cell lines in-vitro. In this study, CDs 
exhibited higher cytotoxicity through a high ergosterol solubilization 
capacity, i.e. high extraction and desorption ability for sterols in the 
bilayer membranes, which disrupted the membranes and enhanced 
permeability. The results revealed that: 1) α-CD was found to be the most 
toxic against all cell lines as compared to β-CDs because of its lipid 
solubilization ability, 2) treatment with plain CDs stimulated prolifer­
ation of fibroblast and osteoblast, and 3) HP-β-CD showed more cyto­
toxicity against Caco-2 cells, however, the effect was lesser in 
comparison to the other complexes, and all complexes showed lesser 
cytotoxicity than free drugs. The study also suggested that the suitability 
of HP-β-CD is higher for complex formation where the cytotoxic effects 
should be minimal or absent (Teixeira et al., 2015). These studies sug­
gest that the internal toxicity of CDs is due to the solubilizing ability of 
lipids, phospholipids, and cholesterol and their extraction from the 
cellular membranes which alters their structural characteristics, in­
creases permeability, and thus leads to the increase in cytotoxicity. The 
effects also increase/decrease with structural modifications and deriv­
atization is observed in the case of methylated or hydrophilic CDs. The 
selectivity of CDs towards cancer and tumor cells is also supported by 
the fact that these cells contain more cholesterol and lipid content than 
the normal cells. 
3.2.2. Cytotoxicity of CD-based nanocarriers 
Various CD-based nanocarriers, especially nanosponges, and other 
drug delivery systems such as inclusion complexes and conjugates used 
in the delivery of anti-cancer drugs and chemotherapy were also 
assessed for their cytotoxic effects. Inclusion complexes of amygdalin 
with β-CD synthesized via 3 different methods were analyzed for their 
in-vitro cytotoxic and anti-proliferative effects against cervical cancer 
(HeLa) and normal fibroblastic epithelial (HFL1) cell lines. The com­
plexes prepared by the freeze-drying method produced greater cytotoxic 
effects which were induced due to greater solubility of amygdalin 
compared with the other methods. The cytotoxic effects of freeze-dried 
inclusion complexes against HeLa cell lines were greater at 24 h (IC50 
value ~63 μM) than against HFL1 cell lines (IC50 value ~116.21 μM). 
Here, the sensitivity of amygdalin to HeLa cell lines was increased in a 
dose-dependent manner, and apoptosis was induced via intrinsic path­
ways (Marian et al., 2020). Even though the toxic effects against 
cancerous cell lines were only slightly greater than the normal one, the 
overall enhancement in the cytotoxicity of actives by CD against normal 
cell lines should not be ignored. Cytotoxic effects were also observed on 
human lung cancer cell (A549) of CD-conjugated PEG-grafted gold 
nanoparticles. This nanocarrier (25–35 nm) was conjugated to all 3 
types of parent CDs to improve the solubility, delivery, and bioavail­
ability of curcumin (BCS Class IV). The cytotoxic effect was found to be 
related to the increased activity of curcumin since the nanoparticles 
without curcumin did not produce any effects. The results also reported 
that curcumin solution contains limited cytotoxicity, however, the 
nanoparticles conjugated with CD (all 3 parent CD) showed higher or 
comparable cytotoxicity by increasing its bioavailability. Furthermore, 
the high specific gravity of the nanocarriers increased their cell inter­
action and thus, improved the cellular uptake in-vitro, whereas the 
localization of nanoparticles in the endosomes and controlled drug 
release via endocytosis further showed rapid cytotoxicity (Hoshikawa 
et al., 2018).

3.3. Ototoxicity 
Ototoxicity is a drug- induced adverse reaction that affects the inner 
ear and leads to cochlear or vestibular dysfunction causing hearing loss 
as well as balance impairment (Ganesan et al., 2018). Several drugs such 
as antibiotics, NSAIDs, etc. are known to cause ototoxicity mainly by 
generating ROS, activating hair cell apoptosis in the inner ear and 
impairment in mechano-sensory function of outer hair cells(Tabuchi 
et al., 2011). 2-hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (2-HP-β-CD) was found to 
be ototoxic in many studies. The risk of ototoxicity caused by HP-β-CD 
was observed in the treatment of Niemann-Pick Disease Type C (NPC) 
where large concentrated doses of 2-HP-β-CD were required. NPC is a 
inherited lipid metabolic disorder (rare autosomal recessive disorder), in 
which unesterified cholesterol are stored in harmful quantities in lyso­
somes, along with abnormal intracellular cholesterol transport and 
affect the functions of the spleen, lungs, liver, bone marrow, and espe­
cially the brain (Vanier, 2010). 
A study searched for this toxic property on nine different CDs (2-HP- 
α-CD, 2-HP-β-CD, 2-HP-γ-CD, M-β-CD, S-α-CD, S-β-CD, S-γ-CD, etc.), 
wherein they administered NPC-diseased mice with CDs for 2 weeks and 
analyzed them for disease amelioration parameters and measured the 
Auditory Brainstem Responses (ARBs) for ototoxicity. The results 
showed that 2-HP-β-CD induced extreme hearing loss with ≥100 dB 
sound pressure level (SPL) which persisted throughout the study (20 
weeks). 2-HP-γ-CD showed a threshold at ~88 dB SPL, but the effects 
fluctuated between normal hearing and extreme hearing loss, while 
others showed a normal threshold (~40 dB), thus, in comparison to HP- 
β-CD and its varying degree of substitution, other CDs showed reduced 
ototoxicity. The mechanism of action was suggested to be the in­
teractions between CD and unesterified cholesterol, causing the death of 
outer hair cells in the ear (Davidson et al., 2016). A similar study dis­
played that administration of 2-HP-β-CD to adult rats with NPC disease 
induced dose-dependent (approximately ≥1 %) damage and extreme 
hearing loss by destroying outer ear hair cells and increasing ABR 
thresholds. Further, treatment of 2-HP-β-CD on post-natal cochlear and 
vestibular organ cultures showed more severe damage as compared to 
the mice. In-vitro, it induced the destruction of auditory nerve fibers and 
other ganglion, whereas vestibular neurons along with inner and outer 
hair cells via CD-mediated apoptosis, activation of caspase-8 and 
caspase-3 induced cell death. This study demonstrated the selective 
destruction and ear damage of 2-HP-β-CD in-vitro (cell cultures) than in 
in-vivo (adult rats) studies. This selectivity was attributed to: 1) the 
greater bioavailability and permeability of 2-HP-β-CD in organelle cul­
tures, 2) a significant increase in prestin expression during the first 2 
weeks after birth, thus the susceptibility to ototoxicity in post-natal 
cultures could be more as compared to adult rats (Ding et al., 2020). 
Another study tested hearing loss and damage caused by 2-HP-β-CD in 
mice subcutaneously and via stereotactic intracerebroventricular (ICV) 
delivery. The dose administered was based on human equivalent dose 
used in a clinical trial. Here, 2-HP-β-CD caused ABR ~60 dB threshold 
shift and a dose-dependent outer hair cell loss. After 7 days post sub­
cutaneous injection, 83 % of mice showed >85 % loss of outer hair cells. 
At low ICV doses 500 mg/kg brain weight affected 22% mice with shifts 
of 40-60 dB, while in high doses of 3000 mg/kg influenced 100% ani­
mals with large threshold shift (Cronin et al., 2015). 
In human clinical trials, similar results were observed with patients 
suffering from NPC. The clinical trial was non-randomized, open-label 
and dose-escalation phase 1/2a trial wherein 50–1200 mg of 2-HP-β-CD 
were administered intrathecally in 14 volunteers for 12–18 months. 
Here, 2-HP-β-CD demonstrated significant delaying of disease progres­
sion, however, it was accompanied by hearing loss due to ototoxicity, 
while no other side effects were observed. The cause was indicated to be 
outer hair cell death and it was suggested that 2-HP-β-CD-induced 
ototoxicity was higher in individuals with intact hearing and not yet

The possible mechanisms of action of this toxic action are similar to 
cytotoxicity, wherein: 1), HP-β-CD interacts with cholesterol in the cell 
membranes followed by its extraction, which leads to membrane 
disruption and loss of integrity, 2) HP-β-CD enters the cell through 
endocytosis, depletes cholesterol in the mitochondrial membranes and 
increases the oxidative stress or cytochrome-C release, which in turn 
activates outer hair cell death, 3) along with cholesterol, the interaction 
of HP-β-CD with motor proteins such as prestin resident in outer hair 
cells, leads to the changes in the polarization of the proteins and loss of 
membrane integrity, 4) alteration of hair cell fluidity which is triggered 
by cholesterol depletion, and 5) perturbations in membrane cholesterol 
by HP-β-CD alters electrical activity, channel density, and depolariza­
tion and loss of certain channels like potassium channel – KCNQ4 type 
which leads to excitotoxic cell death (Cronin et al., 2015; Crumling 
et al., 2017; Tabuchi et al., 2011). However, more research is required in 
each aspect to validate the potential mechanisms of ototoxicity by HP- 
β-CD, since the interactions between HP-β-CD and outer hair cells are 
not properly verified. Extensive study is also significant to determine: 1) 
if the ototoxicity is correlated with the disease itself or is based on the 
concentration, 2) the impact of long-term use of HP-β-CD, and 3) the 
identifications of optimal concentrations in such disease conditions 
where higher amounts of CD is required. 
Notably, the mechanisms of action in terms of cholesterol solubili­
zation and extraction, thereby leading to membrane disruption are 
similar in cytotoxicity and ototoxicity caused by CDs (as shown in 
Fig. 3). In cytotoxicity, HP-β-CD was not observed to be cytotoxic, and 
instead methylated and lipophilic derivatives were rendered more toxic 
to cells. However, for cells and neurons in the inner ear, HP-β-CD is 
noticed to be toxic. Furthermore, a high concentration of HP-β-CD is 
known to cause skin irritation as previously discussed in dermal appli­
cations. This suggests that the CDs interact in different fashions with the 
cellular contents and such inconsistencies should further be researched 
to validate the exact mechanism of action or to determine the cause of 
discrepancies between the two toxicity parameters. 
3.4. Miscellaneous toxicity 
A series of studies that researched the toxicological impact of CDs 
reported negative results and a safe profile for CDs supporting their 
applications as pharmaceutical excipients. A few examples are 
discussed: 
3.4.1. Hepatotoxicity 
β-CD-based GSH-responsive nanosponges encapsulating doxorubicin 
were assessed for hepatotoxicity on human HepG2 (hepatocarcinoma) 
cell lines in-vitro, on rat liver slices ex-vivo, and accumulation of 
nanosponges was recorded in-vivo. The hepatotoxicity of nanosponges 
was found to be comparable in terms of the free drug in in-vitro and ex- 
vivo studies. Here, the CD-based system enhanced the uptake of doxo­
rubicin in the HepG2 cells through endocytosis, and provided efficient 
intracellular accumulation, avoided drug resistance by escaping from 
the efflux Pg pump. However, the the hepatic accumulation of drug 
loaded in the nanosponges was similar to that of the free drug, 
demonstrating no increase or decrease in the toxic effects (Daga et al., 
2020). 
3.4.2. Teratogenicity 
A study experimenting on embryotoxicity and teratogenicity of γ-CD 
in 16 artificially inseminated white rabbits reported that even at the 
highest dose level (intake corresponding to: 5–7 g/kg body weight/day; 
from day 0 to 29 of gestation), no maternal toxicity was observed 
(Waalkens-Berendsen et al., 1998). Similar study analyzed teratoge­
nicity of α-CDs in 25 pregnant female Wistar rats and observed α-CD 
(intake corresponding to: 13 g/kg body weight/day; from day 0 to 21 of 
gestation) was generally well tolerated with no maternal toxicity, feto­
toxicity, embryotoxicity or teratogenicity and no deaths observed 
(Waalkens-Berendsen & B¨ar, 2004). On the other hand, oral adminis­
tration (from day 6 to 18 of gestation) of β-CD showed anti-teratogenic 
effects in Wistar rats against tolbutamide-induced teratogenicity, 
demonstrating its application in curbing or preventing the toxic effects 
of therapeutic drugs used in diseased conditions (Kale et al., 2012).

4. Future aspects and potential methods to reduce toxicity 
Even with the long and extensive history of different applications 
and related advancements, CDs still accommodate the possibility of 
additional development evolution. CDs, applied as excipients in various 
segments of pharmaceuticals, are usually considered inert and versatile. 
There are discrepancies in reports, since various in-vivo studies exhibit 
negligible and insignificant toxicological implications of CDs, however, 
inadequacy in knowledge about the side effects and toxic nature of such 
excipients demonstrates the need for further research for their safety and 
efficacy. New techniques of evaluation, modifications, and advance­
ments in the in-silico methods and assessment are to be developed to 
analyze the characteristics of CDs in terms of toxicity. Changes in their 
parent structures, modifications or attachments of new functional 
groups to fabricate novel derivatives, incorporation of inclusion com­
plexes into novel nanocarriers to mimic physiological conditions, as well 
as the development of biodegradable CDs to prevent their direct and 
intact excretion in the urine, may also render CDs free from the toxic 
activity and avoid renal or hepatic damage. Novel systems can also be 
developed for limiting the number of CDs in the body, e.g. crosslinking 
the properties of CDs with an inert polymer, and also to provide pro­
longed or controlled release (Cosgun & Tuncaboylu, 2021), which in 
turn reduces acute or accumulated toxicity. Methods of complex for­
mation can also be revised to curb the side effects, e.g. grinding methods 
that utilize the green chemistry approach and prove to be sustainable 
and eco-friendly, while curbing the toxic effects arising from the use of 
solvents (Jug & Mura, 2018). Furthermore, extensive research in the 
field of toxicological studies, and testing the impact of acute and 
repeated doses according to the OECD guidelines is required, as incon­
sistency in reports detailing the impact of CDs also exist, e.g. HP-β-CD 
was not found as cytotoxic, however, it was found to be ototoxic while 
both toxicological profiles act via similar mechanism of action. Since the 
side effects of toxic drugs are prevented by complexation with CD de­
rivatives, it is also equally important to derive new ways to prevent the 
dosage and route of administration-related side effects of CDs. Change in 
the functionalization parameters, attaching inert polymers, effective 
complexation with low dosage of CD as well as incorporating drug- 
complexed CDs into carrier systems such as liposomes, solid lipid 
nanoparticles (Pires et al., 2019), etc. may help in preventing the toxic 
effects via controlled or stimuli-responsive release (Peng et al., 2017). 
5. Conclusions 
CDs are oligosaccharides that aid in improving the delivery and 
pharmacokinetic parameters of drugs and bioactive molecules. CDs are 
considered safe, if used within the permitted dose range, however, 
greater concentrations than the restricted range of CDs show toxicity 
and adverse side effects. Methylated and other lipophilic derivatives of 
parent CDs tend to show a higher affinity for phospholipids, proteins, 
and cholesterol and induced their solubilization and extraction from the 
cellular membrane and further lead to structural disruption of cells and 
tissues. The concentration and dose regimen also depends on the route of 
administration as the quantity required for each type of CD differs with 
the composition and physiochemical processes of the organs it is 
dispensed. Other inherent toxicities of CDs such as cytotoxicity, 
ototoxicity, etc. are produced with similar mechanisms, while the tox­
icities of drugs are also significantly enhanced due to increased solubi­
lity and permeability of the actives across the tissue membranes as well 
as the combined effect (synergism) of the characteristics in actives with 
CDs. On the other hand, inconsistent reports for some CDs, such as HP- 
β-CD, in cases of cytotoxicity should also be considered for future 
research. However, the concentrations required to cause such toxicities 
generally exceed the amounts used in pharmaceutical or formulations 
and hence are considered as inert and non-toxic excipients.

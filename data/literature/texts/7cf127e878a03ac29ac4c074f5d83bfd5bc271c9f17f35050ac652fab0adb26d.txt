Observation of Body-Centered-Cubic Cu Nanocluster
with Partial Cu(0) Character
Pu Gao, Jing Du, Weijie Hong, Mingzai Wu,* Yan Feng, Yanjun Ding,*
and Manzhou Zhu*
Atomically precise coinage-metal nanoclusters (NCs), a key
bridge between molecular complexes and plasmonic nanopar-
ticles, have garnered signiﬁcant research interest over the past
two decades due to their intriguing physicochemical properties,
wide-ranging potential applications, and remarkable level of
precision at the atomic level.[1–7] Because of the huge difference
of metal NCs with different crystalline phases,[8–10] the active
exploration of novel physicochemical properties and functionali-
ties through the modulation of their crystal structure has thus
emerged as a vibrant area of research.[11,12] Normally, coinage
metals in the bulk phase mostly crystalize
in face-centered-cubic (FCC) due to the
highest packing density and a thermody-
namically stable state.[13] Au and Ag NCs,
however, have proved to adopt non-FCC
lattices in crystalline arrangement where
a dozen or more metal atoms are encap-
sulated
by
appropriate
ligands.[14]
For
example, Au30 NC stabilized by a thiolate
ligand has revealed a Au18 kernel assem-
bled into a ABAB pattern (hexagonal close
packed, HCP).[15] Au38 and Ag15 NCs also
both exhibited body-centered-cubic (BCC)
structure respectively.[16,17] These and other
inspiring discoveries in the ﬁeld have dem-
onstrated the ligand structure plays a crucial
role in regulating both the kernel and sur-
face structures of NCs.[5,18–24]
Despite these signiﬁcant advancements,
the exploration of non-FCC Cu NCs is still
lagging.[25] In fact, comparable Cu NCs
have remained scarce, presumably due to their instability, result-
ing in only a few examples of fully characterized Cu NCs.[26] To
date, the continuous efforts in pursuit of Cu NCs with Cu(0)
character have produced several results. Cu25H22(TPP)12Cl
was ﬁrst reported with an interesting Cu13-centered icosahedral
core.[27] The alkynyl-protected Cu53 and superatomic Cu31 NCs,
as well as a Cu23 NC-incorporating tetrahedral [Cu4]0 kernel,
were also observed very recently.[11,28,29] Unfortunately, none
of them provided a BCC structure in Cu NCs, making the whole
puzzle of Cu NCs miss an important piece in their crystalline

The structure of nanoparticles is a key determinant of their material properties.
The synthesis of non-face-centered-cubic (FCC) Cu nanoclusters (NCs)
with Cu(0) character is still challenging due to their instability. Herein, a
Cu(0)-containing Cu NC is ﬁrst reported to contain a metallic framework of
23 Cu atoms with a body-centered-cubic Cu@Cu8 core, stabilized by 6 triphe-
nylphosphine (TPP) and 18 4-methylbenzenethiolate ligands, as determined by
single-crystal X-ray crystallography. The observed structure stands in stark
contrast to the FCC and other structures reported for Cu NCs. Moreover, this
newly formed NC could react with Auþ ion with controlled concentration, rapidly
yielding another identiﬁable and bright red-emitting AuCu14 cluster upon irra-
diation. Based on this structural conversion, a new “turn-on” type of photolu-
minescent probe can be developed for the detection of Auþ ion with an ultrafast
response time (30 s) at a low detection limit (0.13 ppm). This work provides a
new way of understanding the growth of metal clusters and exploring their
applications in luminescent sensors.

Herein, we report the ﬁrst observation of a novel Cu(0)-
containing Cu NC that features a Cu@Cu8 BCC core. The
observed structure stands in stark contrast to the FCC and other
structures reported for Cu NCs.[25] Additionally, this NC could

Auþ ion upon irradiation, accompanied with a high-contrast

regarding
the
synthesis
were
provided
in
the
Supporting Information (SI). The obtained products were sub-
jected to crystallization in CH2Cl2/n-hexane at room temperature
for 3 days. Yellow crystals appeared and were meticulously
collected (Figure S1, Supporting Information) and subsequently
utilized for single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD), which indi-
cated its crystallization in the P-1 triclinic space group as a
dichloromethane solvate (Table S1, Supporting Information).
On the basis of the crystallographic structural analysis, the
cluster showed a C2 symmetry, revealing only singular half of
the cluster in the asymmetric unit. The exact structure was
elucidated to contain 23 Cu atoms co-protected by 6 triphenyl-
phosphine (TPP) and 18 4-methylbenzenethiolate (abbreviated
as SR) ligands (Figure 1A and S2, Supporting Information) with
one SbF6
 counterion, formulated as [Cu23(SR)18(TPP)6](SbF6)
(abbreviated as Cu23). The high-resolution electronic support-
ing information (ESI)-time of ﬂight-mass spectrum technique
with positive-ion mode was employed to precisely determine
the chemical composition of Cu23 in solution. As shown in
Figure 1B, the mass curve of Cu23 had four main peaks. The max-
imum peak of 3520.19 Da was attributed to the Cu19(C7H7S)14
(C18H15P)2(CH3OH)2 with a positive charge (calc. 3520.26 Da).
Another three peaks corresponded to the gain or loss of TPP
ligand on the basis of maximum peak.
Structurally, Cu23 contained a metallic framework of 23 Cu
atoms and possessed a BCC Cu@Cu8 core structure at the ﬁrst
glimpse (Figure 2). The centroid Cu atom had a coordination
number of 8 (Figure 2A), lower than that in metallic Cu.[11]
When looking into the Cu@Cu8 core (Figure S3, Supporting
Information), the average Cu─Cu bond length between the
central Cu atom and the Cu8 cube was 2.667 Å, which slightly
exceeded those in metallic Cu (2.556 Å),[31] but was still compa-
rable to those in Cu(0)-containing clusters,[27,29] suggesting their
strong bonding between the central Cu atom and the Cu8 cube.
Moreover, the Cu8 cube exhibited elongated opposite sides
(indicated by broken bonds) with an average Cu–Cu distance
of 3.746 Å, while the remaining sides (indicated by solid bonds)
displayed Cu─Cu bond lengths ranging from 2.575 to 2.781 Å.
Therefore, the Cu8 cube was not a regular cube, but an irregular
one elongated along one axial direction.[17,32] Two opposite faces
of the Cu@Cu8 core were ﬁrst wrapped by two Cu2(SR)3 units to
make up a Cu13(SR)6 structure (Figure 2A–C). The obtained
structure was further covered by two Cu4(SR)4(TPP)2 motifs at
another two opposite terminals (Figure 2D,E), giving the overall
structure after connecting two Cu1(SR)2TPP units in the outer
enclosure (Figure 2F–H).In the case of the surface ligands,
the 4-methylbenzenethiolate ligands presented ﬁve different
coordination environments, including μ2, μ3, and μ4 bridging
modes evidenced by several distinct chemical shifts of the
methyl group in the 1H NMR spectrum (Figure S4, Supporting
Information), while two peaks were observed in the 31P NMR
spectrum (Figure S5, Supporting Information), exhibiting two
different coordination environments of the P atoms from TPP
ligands. The speciﬁc coordination modes and their correspond-
ing numbers are detailed in Table S2 and S3, Supporting
Information. Of note, no distinct signal in the 1H NMR spectrum
could be attributed to Cu hydride. A sample of Cu23-D prepared
by NaBD4 only showed the solvent peak in the 2H NMR spectrum
(Figure S6, Supporting Information), further proving that no
hydrides were incorporated into Cu23-D.
When Cu23 crystals were dissolved in dichloromethane, the
UV–vis absorption spectrum of Cu23 displayed a shoulder peak
at 270 nm (Figure 3A). Based on the photo energy spectrum,
the optical HOMO–LUMO gap was measured to be 2.68 eV
(Figure 3A, inset). Interestingly, Cu23 seemed to have a photo-
luminescence (PL) and would give a weak emission at 470 nm
(2.64 eV) upon excitation at 270 nm, affording a ﬂuorescence
with a short PL lifetime (2.8 ns) according to the analysis
of PL decay traces (Figure S7, Supporting Information). The
extremely low difference in energy (0.04 eV) hinted the PL might
originate from the electronic transition from the LUMO to the
HOMO.[1,33] Furthermore, Cu23 in solution was not stable and

With respect to the BCC Cu@Cu8 core structure in Cu23, simi-
lar to that in the previously reported one with high phosphores-
cence,[36] we wondered whether the centroid Cu atom could be
replaced by other metallic atoms to form alloy NCs as alloying
has proven to be a highly effective approach for synthesizing novel
functional nanomaterials.[19,37] Au(TPP)Cl was ﬁrst added into
the Cu23 solution, resulting in the color change from light yellow
to red in solution within 12 h. Amazingly, the cluster transforma-
tion could rapidly happen within 30 s under UV light irradiation
(Figure 4A and S11, Supporting Information). Three peaks at
410, 510, and 580 nm with a shoulder peak at 455 nm gradually
appeared in the time-dependent UV–vis spectrum (Figure 4B,C),
indicating the decomposition of Cu23 to reconstruct a new NC with
Au(TPP)Cl. As identiﬁed by SCXRD, the as-obtained red NC
after crystallization (Figure S12A, Supporting Information), for-
mulated as [AuCu14(SR)12(TPP)6](SbF6) (abbreviated as AuCu14),
held almost the same metallic framework to previously reported
NCs, apart from the surface ligands (Figure S13, Supporting
Information).[36]
Structurally, AuCu14 also crystallized in the triclinic space
group P-1 and possessed a BCC structure with Au@Cu8 core
(Table S1, Figure S14, Supporting Information). Different from
Cu23 in free valence electrons, AuCu14 had two free valence elec-
trons (2 = 14–12–1), identical to that in the previous report.[36]
The Au─Cu bond lengths were in the range of 2.594 to
2.791 Å with an average length of 2.706 Å in AuCu14, suggesting
strong interactions between the central Au and Cu8 cube, while
the corresponding Cu─Cu bond lengths in Cu8 cube ranged
from 3.008 to 3.295 Å with an average length of 3.127 Å, much
longer than that in metallic Cu (2.556 Å) and Cu(0)-containing
clusters,[27,29,31] leading to no existence of metal–metal bonds
in the Cu8 cage. To ﬁgure out the formal oxidation state of
Au atom in AuCu14, we conducted the XPS measurement
with the crystal samples of AuCu14 (Figure S15, Supporting
Information). Based on the XPS analysis, the Au 4f region
showed a doublet 4f7/2 (83.9 eV) and 4f5/2 (87.7 eV). This doublet
was observed to be shifted toward the negative side by 0.1 eV
compared to Au 4f7/2 peak of metallic Au (84.0 eV). Thus, the
oxidation state of Au in AuCu14 has been determined to be
zero. The structural anatomy revealed that Cu23 and AuCu14
both shared Cu1(SR)2TPP motifs (Figure S13B, Supporting
Information), demonstrating the cluster transformation was
plausible. However, the 4-methylbenzenethiolate ligands in
the Cu1(SR)2TPP motifs stabilized the metallic framework
via μ3 bridging mode in AuCu14 (Figure S13D, Supporting
Information), while the 4-methylbenzenethiolate ligands in the
Cu1(SR)2TPP motifs displayed three types of bridging modes
(μ2, μ3, and μ4) in Cu23 (Table S2, Supporting Information).

Based on the above experiments, it has been conﬁrmed that
the Cu23 exhibited a high sensitivity to Auþ ion compared with
other metal ions under light irradiation, and the PL spectrum
allowed for visual monitoring, offering valuable opportunities
for the quantitative analysis of Auþ ion. When Au(TPP)Cl was
added into Cu23 solution (0.2 mg mL1), the PL intensity
increased progressively with rising concentrations of Au(TPP)
Cl from 0 to 18 μM (Figure 5A). The color of the solution under-
went a swift transformation from light yellow to red, accompa-
nied by the emergence of red PL when excited by 365 nm UV
light (Figure 5A, inset photo). Moreover, a strong linear correla-
tion was observed between the PL intensity of Cu23 solution at
≈620 nm and the concentration of Au(TPP)Cl (Figure 5B).
Another two Auþ compounds (AuSMe2Cl and Au-SR) were also
selected to test as the “turn-on” type of photoluminescent probe.
Both ﬁnished the rapid structural transformation of Cu23 into
AuCu14 under light irradiation after their addition in solution,
exhibiting a new photoluminescent emission, which was the
same to the addition of Au(TPP)Cl (Figure S18, Supporting
Information), though Au-SR had a very low solubility in solution.

of Cu23 in solution to form a precipitate. As a result, Cu23 exhib-
ited remarkable stability against other metal ions and can serve as
a highly stable “turn-on” photoluminescent probe for selectively
detecting Auþ ion (Figure 5D).
In summary, we prepared a new Cu(0)-containing Cu23 NC
that featured a BCC Cu@Cu8 core, enriching the library of
non-FCC Cu NCs. An ultrafast cluster transformation of Cu23
stimulated by Auþ ion was observed under light irradiation
and further analyzed through time-dependent UV–vis and PL
spectra. The conversion process was accompanied by a highly
efﬁcient “turn-on” luminescence phenomenon, which not only
conﬁrmed the quantitative detection of Auþ ion but also exhib-
ited rapid response time (30 s), exceptional sensitivity, and a low
detection limit for Auþ ion. This signiﬁcant ﬁnding not only ﬁn-
ished the whole puzzle of Cu NCs with Cu(0) character in their
three common crystalline phases, but also provided valuable
insights into cluster transformation and greatly propelled the
promising future application of atomically precise clusters.

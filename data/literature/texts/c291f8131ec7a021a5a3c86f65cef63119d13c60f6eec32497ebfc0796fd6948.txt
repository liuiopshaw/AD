Capturing the Monomeric (L)CuH in NHC-Capped Cyclodextrin:
Cavity-Controlled Chemoselective Hydrosilylation of a,b-Unsaturated
Ketones
Guangcan Xu, S8bastien Leloux, Pinglu Zhang, Jorge Meijide Su#rez, Yongmin Zhang,
Etienne Derat, Micka]l M8nand, Olivia Bistri-Aslanoff, Sylvain Roland, Tom Leyssens,*
Olivier Riant,* and Matthieu Sollogoub*
Abstract: The encapsulation of copper inside a cyclodextrin
capped with an N-heterocyclic carbene (ICyD) allowed both to
catch the elusive monomeric (L)CuH and a cavity-controlled
chemoselective copper-catalyzed hydrosilylation of a,b-unsa-
turated ketones. Remarkably, (a-ICyD)CuCl promoted the
1,2-addition exclusively, while (b-ICyD)CuCl produced the
fully reduced product. The chemoselectivity is controlled by the
size of the cavity and weak interactions between the substrate
and internal C@H bonds of the cyclodextrin.
Introduction
Encapsulating a metal center inside a well-defined cavity
is a promising way to induce selectivities in chemical reactions
and to stabilize highly reactive intermediates[1] by roughly
imitating metallo-enzymes.[2] However, relatively few exam-
ples are reported in the literature where the cavity both
includes the metal and is demonstrated to be responsible for
the selectivity. Quite a few systems display the possibility to
select a substrate over another, based mainly on steric
hindrance around the metal induced by the cavity.[3] A few
regioselective reactions have been reported[4–6] with this kind
of systems where, again, the steric hindrance of the cavity
governs the approach of the substrate and therefore the
outcome of the reaction. Following the same approach, chiral
cavities have been used to induce enantioselective reac-
tions.[7–9] An example where both enantio- and regioselectiv-
ities are induced has been reported by Reek[10] for a hydro-
formylation reaction using an asymmetric container.[11]
Some of us developped N-heterocyclic-carbene(NHC)-
capped cyclodextrin (CD) ligands called ICyDs, a-ICyD for
the one derived from a-CD and b-ICyD for the one derived
from b-CD (Figure 1A).[7] Functional CDs have been used as
metal ligands before by several researchers,[11,12] including
us,[13] but the uniqueness of the ICyD ligands is that they place
the metal center in the middle of the cavity and force the
substrate to be influenced by this cavity. We showed that the
bridging induced a change in the morphology of the cavity,
introducing both an additional degree of asymmetry and
different access to the metal, depending on the nature of the
CD (Figure 1B). Hence, we could promote enantioselective
gold-catalyzed cycloisomerisations,[7,8] and demonstrated that
the shape of the cavity could change the regioselectivity of
a copper-catalyzed hydroboration.[5,14] This system seemed
therefore well-fitted to study the effect of a cavity and its
shape on an archetypical chemoselective reaction: the
reduction of a,b-unsaturated ketones.
Copper complexes are well-known catalysts that promote
1,4-additions of hydrides. However, very few examples of 1,2-
additions have been reported.[15] We therefore wondered if it
was possible to force the system towards 1,2-addition due to
the cavity of the ICyD ligand. The Osborn complex or
StrykerQs reagent [(Ph3P)CuH]6 is the first reported stabilized
complex of a copper hydride. It is a hexamer[16] and was used
in conjugate 1,4-hydrogenation of a,b-unsaturated ketones

with high chemoselectivity.[17] Besides, a rare switching from
1,4- to 1,2-selectivity was observed by changing the ligand in
this system.[18,19] However, the preparation, storage, and use
of this reagent is not straightforward. As an alternative,
Lipshutz and Buchwald suggested to generate the active
“Cu@H” species in situ using a stoichiometric quantity of
silane.[20] With [(Ph3P)CuH]6 or a copper salt, a base, and
a ligand of choice, hydrosilylation reactions have been
performed, including enantioselective versions with chiral
phosphine[21] or NHC ligands.[22] There are also very few
reports of 1,2-selective hydrosilylations. An enantioselective
version was presented by Lipshutz,[23] who initially showed
that a-substitution of unsaturated ketones was needed to
form secondary allylic alcohols. This substrate control of the
1,2-reduction over the 1,4-reduction was further exemplified
by Lipshutz with b,b-disubstituted enones,[24] and very re-
cently by Mankad[25] and some of us[26] with a’-hindered
ketones. By using a bulky and basic phosphepine ligand,
Beller[27] described two examples of such selectivity, while
a mixture of reduction products was observed when benzy-
lideneacetone was used as a substrate. Finally, Waidmann and
Gordon showed one example of 1,2-selectivity.[28] Hence, the
1,2-selectivity of the copper-catalyzed hydrosilylation of a,b-
unsaturated ketones is clearly dependent on the structure of
the substrate, and particularly on the substitution of the
double bond, but the bulkiness of the ligand also plays a role.
This situation appeared perfect to test our system, as the
sterics around the reactive Cu@H moiety would be very well
controlled and different in a- and b-ICyDs. We therefore
embarked to study the cavity dependency of the chemo-
selectivity of the hydrosilylation of a,b-unsaturated ketones
using (ICyD)CuCl catalysts.
When dealing with CuH complexes, a recurrent question
turns around its nuclearity. Indeed, CuH complexes are
usually oligomeric, and the monomeric Cu@H is notoriously
elusive.[29] It was invoked by many, but never isolated and
characterized on its own. Only recently, and for the first time,
Bertrand spectroscopically described it in equilibrium with its
dimeric form in solution.[30] Owing to the high steric
hindrance of a-ICyD, we performed the same study as
Bertrand hoping to observe monomeric CuH on its own at
last.
Results and Discussion
We followed the reaction by NMR and first converted (a-
ICyD)CuCl into its CuOtBu counterpart by use of tBuOK.[5]
We then added 7 equiv of polymethylhydrosiloxane (PMHS)
or of tetramethyldisiloxane (TMDS; Scheme 1).[31] The for-
mation of a single new product, identical for both silanes, was
observed by NMR spectroscopy (Supporting Information,
Figure S4). The 13C spectrum of this product showed a single
carbene signal at & 180 ppm, which appears as a doublet in
the proton-coupled
13C NMR with a coupling constant of
40.2 Hz, characteristic of a trans 2J(13C@1H) coupling, and it
was already shown to be indicative of the monomeric copper-
hydride species by Bertrand.[30] HMBC experiments showed
cross-correlations between this carbene C and protons of the
imidazole ring, H6s of the sugars bearing the NHC, and
a proton at 1.5 ppm attributed to the hydride, in analogy with
the work by Bertrand.[30] Finally, NOESY experiments
revealed through-space correlations between the hydride
and intra-cavity H3s and H5s (see Figure 2 and the Supporting
Information). We therefore fully characterized the elusive
monomeric copper-hydride on its own by NMR. It appears
relatively stable in solution in the NMR tube for 24 h at room
temperature.
To probe its reducing activity, we first added acetophe-
none 1 and obtained the alkoxide 2 as expected. But much to
our delight, when we added the a,b-unsaturated ketone
benzylideneacetone 3 we obtained the product of the 1,2-
addition, 4 (Scheme 2).

Initial experiments and optimization of the catalytic
reaction parameters were carried out on the benchmark
asymmetric reduction of acetophenone 1 to 1-phenylethanol
5[32] using various ICyD-copper complexes (Table 1). Initially,
the reaction was set up using (a-ICyD)CuF·HF (entry 1),
which was designed to avoid the use of a co-catalyst such
a metal alkoxide and facilitates the transfer of the hydride
from silicon to copper to generate the active copper-hydride
catalyst.[33] However, with methyldiethoxysilane as a reducing
agent, problems of reproducibility regarding both yields and
enantiomeric excesses were observed. Moving to (a-ICyD)-
CuOH[5] under the same conditions gave a better conversion
with increased ee values (entry 2). It should be noted that no
additional base is required even when (a-ICyD)CuCl is used
as a catalyst, and that inert atmosphere is not mandatory. To
our knowledge, this is the first example in the literature where
a CuCl catalyst is used without a base additive in hydro-
silylation. With respect to the nature of the silane, PhSiH3 led
to excellent yields upon basic cleavage of the corresponding
silyl ether (entries 2–5). Besides, when other silanes were
used, lower conversions were observed (entries 7–13). For the
remaining part of this study, phenylsilane was chosen as the
hydrosilylating agent. Overall, b-ICyD gave similar yields but
significantly lower ee values (entries 3, 6, 12). After optimi-
zation (see Supporting Information), hydrosylations of ke-
tones were carried out using 1 mol% of the (a-ICyD)CuCl
complex in THFat room temperature. Those conditions are in
contrast with previously reported ones which implied the use
of an excess of base, low temperatures, and inert atmos-
phere.[20–28] We then performed an exemplification of this
reaction with various ketones using (ICyD)CuOH and
(ICyD)CuCl, affording ee values up to 91% (see Supporting
Information).
We next studied the hydrosilylation of a,b-unsaturated
ketones using both a- and (b-ICyD)CuCl catalysts. We first
looked at the reduction of benzylideneacetone 3, which
previously gave mixtures in that context.[27] This is a partic-
ularly challenging substrate as it is not functionalized in a-, in
a’-, nor in b,b-positions, which were shown to favor 1,2-
reduction.[23–25] It is therefore particularly noticeable that we
observed a selective 1,2-reduction to allylic alcohol 6 when
using the (a-ICyD)CuCl catalyst and a total reduction (1,2
and 1,4) of the a,b-unsaturated ketone to 7 when using (b-
ICyD)CuCl (Scheme 3). While the 1,2-reduction with (a-
ICyD)CuCl gave a respectable yield and enantiomeric excess,
a drastic drop in ee was observed when (b-ICyD)CuCl was
used, along with an increase of the yields, in line with the
reduction of ketones (see Supporting Information). Interest-
ingly, when using only 0.5 equiv of phenylsilane instead of 1.5
with (b-ICyD)CuCl, ketone 8, resulting from the 1,4-reduc-
tion of 3, was observed along with some starting material and
no traces of alcohol 7 (Scheme 2). Hence, the total reduction
of the a,b-unsaturated ketone using (b-ICyD)CuCl first
operates in the expected 1,4-fashion, leading to the ketone,
then an excess of silane affords the reduction of the carbonyl.
However, when (a-ICyD)CuCl was used, no matter the
quantity of silane, only 1,2-reduction was observed.
We then used this reaction on a range of a,b-unsaturated
substrates (Table 2). Various aromatic, cyclic, and aliphatic
a,b-unsaturated ketones were tested with a- and (b-ICyD)-
CuCl as well as a benchmark (iPr)CuF·HF catalyst described
elsewhere by some of us.[34] On this range of substrates,[35]
complete chemoselectivity is observed solely depending on
the nature of the catalyst. (iPr)CuF·HF always gave 1,4-
reduction, (a-ICyD)CuCl operated the 1,2-reduction with
variable enantiomeric excesses, and (b-ICyD)CuCl afforded
the fully reduced compounds.

of the reduction of a,b-unsaturated ketone only relying on the
size and shape of the cavity of the ICyD catalyst. To
investigate the reasons underlying this chemoselectivity
through cavity control in more detail, we performed a theo-
retical study using density functional theory (DFT) calcula-

addition transition state for (a-ICyD)CuH), the distance
between the copper and oxygen atoms is 3.6 c, while it is
2.3 c in TS-b-1,4 (Figures 3 and 4). Hence, the reaction is
more concerted and more likely to proceed in b-ICyD than in
a-ICyD. This is clearly due to the differences in size and shape
between the two cavities. In the case of b-ICyD, there is more
room for the ketone to interact with the metal, while in the
case of a-ICyD, the ketone does not have as much space. This
difference is also manifested in the different orientations of
the ketone compared to the cavity, as seen in Figure 4; the
ketone can enter the cavity in TS-b-1,4, while it stays flat in
the entry area in TS-a-1,4. (Figures 4 and 5)
We then performed a non-covalent-interaction analysis
(NCI)[37] on the TSs, looking for additional weak interactions
in these catalytic pockets, and could indeed detect attractive
interactions between the C@H groups of the CD and the
carbonyl oxygen of the substrate. The activation of a C=O
bond by a C@H bond has been rarely invoked,[38] usually with
a polarized C@H bond next to a quaternary ammonium
group[39] or in conjunction with another H-bond.[40] For a-
ICyD, this interaction is much stronger in TS-a-1,2 than in TS-
a-1,4. On the contrary, for b-ICyD, TS-b-1,2 has one such
interaction while TS-b-1,4 has two (Figure 5). So while TS-a-
1,2 and TS-b-1,2 are very similar, TS-a-1,4 and TS-b-1,4 differ
in many ways, in particular the fact that there is only one weak
hydrogen bond in the case of a-CD and two strong ones for b-
CD. Furthermore, in b-CD, the interactions involve H-5
atoms, which are located deep inside the cavity. The discrim-
ination between TSs is therefore due to both attractive
noncovalent interactions[41] inside the cavity and steric
hindrance imposed by the topography of the pocket.
Conclusion
We were able to induce chemoselective reductions of a,b-
unsaturated ketones based only on the structure of a- or b-
ICyD ligands. Moreover, we were able to observe an isolated
monomeric (L)CuH moiety inside the cavity, which confers
this very reactive species with remarkable stability. The
difference in accessibility to the metal in both cavities is
responsible for the difference in selectivity. Additional
interactions with the cavity itself seem to favor 1,2-addition
in a-ICyD and 1,4-addition in b-ICyD, making this system
even more enzyme-like.

A B S T R A C T
Alzheimer’s disease (AD), a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, poses significant therapeutic challenges due 
to its complex etiology and limited treatment options. Traditional pharmacotherapies targeting amyloid-β (Aβ) 
and cholinergic pathways offer modest benefits and are often associated with adverse effects. Emerging evidence 
implicates gut dysbiosis and the gut–brain axis in the pathogenesis and progression of AD. This review explores 
the multifactorial pathophysiology of AD and evaluates the therapeutic potential of gut-based interventions such 
as probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, metabiotics, postbiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in 
mitigating disease pathology. Emphasis has also been given on role of miRNA released from FMT in management 
of AD. Preclinical and clinical studies demonstrate that these strategies can restore microbial homeostasis, reduce 
neuroinflammation, enhance gut barrier integrity, and improve cognitive outcomes. The regulatory aspects with 
use of probiotics based products and FMT is also highlighted. The modulation of neuroimmune, neuroendocrine, 
and neural pathways through microbiota-derived metabolites offers a promising avenue for AD management. 
Despite encouraging findings, further research is needed to address interindividual microbiome variability, de­
livery challenges, and the requirement for large-scale, randomized trials. Personalized gut-targeted approaches 
may open new horizons for the prevention and treatment of AD.
1. Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD), a neurodegenerative disease, manifests as 
long-term mental deterioration, impairing brain function and thinking 
abilities, along with basic cognitive performance (Kumar et al., 2024). 
AD is the most prevalent form of dementia. An estimated 55 million 
cases of dementia in 2020 were reported globally, and are expected to 
increase by 78 million in 2030. It is believed to reach 139 million by 
2050(“Dementia statistics | Alzheimer’s Disease International (ADI)”). 
In the US, 6.9 million cases were reported aged 65 or older in 2024, and 
this number is expected to increase to 13.8 million by 2060 (Alzheimer’s 
disease facts and figures, 2024). In India, 60 % of dementia patients 
currently reside in low- and middle-income nations, and this percentage 
is predicted to be 71 % by 2050 (“Dementia statistics | Alzheimer’s 
Disease International (ADI)”). The risk of AD stands at 1 in 13 people for 
ages 65–84 and 1 in 3 people for ages 85 and above. AD is the fifth 
leading cause of death, with cases increasing by 141 % between 2000 
and 2021 for individuals aged 65 or older. (Alzheimer’s disease facts and 
figures, 2024).The morbidity of AD is significant in people with AD who 
receive a diagnosis at age 70. About 61 % of AD patients are more likely 
to die before the age of 80 than people without the disease (Alzheimer’s 
Facts and Figures, 2025). According to the WHO, 1.8 million died due to

2. Pathogenesis of AD
The pathogenesis of AD is multifactorial, involving genetic, molec­
ular, and neuroinflammatory mechanisms. Mutations in APP, PSEN1, 
and PSEN2 increase the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, enhancing amyloid plaque 
aggregation (Li et al., 2019;Lanoisel´ee et al., 2017). The tau pathway 
also plays a central role, where hyperphosphorylation by kinases such as 
GSK-3β and CDK5 leads to neurofibrillary tangles (Gong and Iqbal, 
2008). Mutations in CHRNA2 impair acetylcholine signaling, contrib­
uting to memory deficits (H. H. H. Ferreira-Vieira et al., 2016).

circularAPP-mediated regulation via the miR-1906/CLIC1 axis as 
drivers of neuroinflammation and Aβ accumulation (Wu et al., 2025).
The middle temporal gyrus serves as an early transition zone where 
somatostatin and neprilysin regulate Aβ clearance. Loss of somatostatin- 
expressing neurons reduces neprilysin, increasing amyloid deposition, 
neuronal hyperactivity, and excitotoxic calcium influx, which further 
activates tau kinases (New study, 2024). Genetic risk is strongly linked 
to the APOE ε4 allele, with heterozygotes having a 2–3 fold increased 
risk and homozygotes up to 12-fold (Yarns et al., 2022)
Additional pathogenic factors include mitochondrial dysfunction, 
where ROS production, oxidative stress, and calcium dysregulation 
impair ATP synthesis (Bhatia et al., 2022), and glutamate excitotoxicity, 
where NMDA receptor overactivation promotes neuronal death and 
accelerates Aβ toxicity. Together, these mechanisms illustrate the 
complexity of AD beyond the amyloid hypothesis, involving genetic, 
metabolic, and neuroimmune interactions (Wang and Reddy, 2017). The

3. Signalling pathway through the gut-brain axis
Gut dysbiosis is strongly linked to neurological disorders such as AD, 
PD, depression, and stress. The human gut hosts over 100 trillion mi­
croorganisms encoding ~3.3 million genes, which influence metabolic, 
immune, and neurological functions. Early-life factors such as delivery 
mode, breastfeeding, antibiotic use, and environmental exposure shape 
the gut microbiota, with disturbances increasing the risk of allergies, 
obesity, autoimmune, and neurodegenerative conditions. Gut microbes 
not only aid digestion but also regulate bile acids, inflammation, car­
bohydrate metabolism, and vitamin production (Arneth, 2018).
In AD, gut dysbiosis triggers both central and peripheral immune 
responses, elevating proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IFN- 
γ) and inflammatory biomarkers (TNF-α, NLRP3, IL-10, TGF-β, CXCL2, 
CRP), which worsen amyloid and tau pathology (Rubio-Perez and 
Morillas-Ruiz, 2012; Novoa et al., 2022). AD patients show reduced 
levels of anti-inflammatory bacteria (Eubacterium rectale, Bifidobacte­
rium) and increased proinflammatory taxa (Escherichia/Shigella), along 
with glucose hypometabolism detected by FDG-PET. Microbiota 
composition also varies by APOE genotype, with APOE2 linked to higher 
SCFA-producing Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae than APOE4 
(Agirman et al., 2021).
Animal studies demonstrate limitations in traditional transgenic AD 
models, which overproduce Aβ40, unlike human pathology. More 
advanced knock-in models, such as App^NL-G-F^ mice, better mimic 
human AD, including neuroinflammation (Saito and Saido, 2018). The 
gut–brain axis, operating through neural, endocrine, and immune 
pathways, is therefore critical in linking microbiome imbalance to AD 
progression (Ashique et al., 2024).

processes, and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) by receiving signals from 
the gut. The primary targets of AD brain disease are the hippocampus,

3.2. Neuroendocrine pathways
Another pathway, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, is a 
neuroendocrine mechanism that regulates cortisol in the body. The 
healthy gut-brain relationship causes elevated cortisol levels to affect 
the hypothalamus negatively, resulting in the reduction of body cortisol 
levels (Levine, 2012). When gut dysbiosis overstimulates the HPA axis, 
leading to elevated cortisol levels, it results in depression, mood 
disturbances, and anxiety. The pro-inflammatory cytokines such as 
TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β become activated by an extended period of HPA 
axis function. It has been demonstrated that Lactobacillus rhamnosus has 
an inhibitory role in stress responses through the HPA axis, along with 
other research on animal subjects(Wauters et al., 2022). The research 
team explained that the increased concentrations of Proteobacteria and 
Escherichia coli combine with LPS to induce neuroinflammation (Rizzatti 
et al., 2017). Enteroendocrine cells (EECs) can be classified into five 
main groups that produce hormones such as ghrelin, 5-hydroxytrypta­
mine (5-HT or serotonin), somatostatin (also called glucagon-like pep­
tide-1, or GLP-1), cholecystokinin (CCK), also known as neurotensin, 
and gastric inhibitory peptide (GIP). The kynurenine pathway, a key 
pathway for tryptophan metabolism, plays a crucial role since its me­
tabolites can be neurotoxic (e.g., quinolinic acid) or neuroprotective (e. 
g., kynurenic acid), and disruptions in this pathway have been associ­
ated with conditions like AD (Lu et al., 2024).
Gut dysbiosis affects hormone levels, that directly impacts on brain. 
TMAO (trimethylamine N-oxide Synthesis), a metabolite derived from 
the gut microbiota, contributes to ageing and neurocognitive disorders 
by promoting neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial 
dysfunction through the metabolism of dietary nutrients like choline, 
carnitine, and betaine, It has also been connected to metabolic and 
cardiovascular diseases. which all lead to harm to neurons. Additionally, 
elevated TMAO levels may aggravate tau and Aβ pathology, disrupt the 
blood–brain barrier, and hasten cognitive decline (Chen et al., 2022).

3.3. Neuroimmune pathways
In an additional pathway of neuroimmune signaling, approximately 
70–80 % of the body’s immune cells are located in the gut. GALT ( Gut- 
associated lymphoid tissue) includes structures like Peyer’s patches in 
the small intestine, the appendix, and tonsils. It’s a crucial part of the 
immune system, helping to defend against harmful microbes that enter 
the body through the digestive system, and MALT (mucosal-associated 
lymphoid tissue) is a system of lymphoid tissue that protects the body 
from pathogens entering through mucosal surfaces like the respiratory, 
digestive, and urogenital tracts. Since GALT encounters both invasive 
antigens and triggers bodily reactions as well as native T and B cells for 
antigen identification (M¨orbe et al., 2021). In the gut system, MALT 
serves as an essential immunological basis that connects to mucosal 
surfaces throughout the body. The three primary roles of the beneficial 
bacteria are to produce SCFAs butyrate via Butyryl-CoA: Acetate 
CoA-Transferase and acetate and propionate), reduce inflammation and 
preserve gut integrity. The immune system and gut bacteria must work 
closely together to maintain mucosal homeostasis. The central nervous 
system’s (CNS) immune cells, particularly microglia and astrocytes, are 
triggered by a variety of endogenous variables, including ageing and 
genetic changes, as well as external triggers, such as infections and 
environmental toxins (Shi et al., 2017). These types of cells activate and 
release inflammatory mediators, including cytokines, chemokines, 
reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to disruption of neural homeo­
stasis. Pathological protein aggregation is caused by chronic neuro­
inflammation and includes tau and Aβ, alpha-synuclein (α-syn) in AD, 
and TDP-43(Transactive response DNA binding protein of 43 kDa) in 
ALS (Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis) (Zhang et al., 2023). The LPS 
induced the expression of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) and cell 
migration in astrocytes via the activation of TLR4/P13K/Akt/MAPKs 
pathways. LPS-treated astrocytes exhibited increased motility compared 
to untreated controls. The results showed that NF-κB signaling regulates 
MMP-9 expression after LPS stimulation, and blocking this pathway 
reduced MMP-9 production as well as cell migration. (Yang et al., 2020). 
The microglia expression of marker TMEM119, along with Iba1 acti­
vation levels, enables researchers to differentiate genuine CNS microglia 
from invading macrophages when detecting CX3CR1 +CD11b+CD45int 
microglia (Bennett et al., 2016). The signalling pathway through

4. Relationship between Gut-Brain Axis
4.1. Healthy gut and brain
Certain microbes, such as curli from E. coli, contribute to forming 
harmful proteins such as α-syn for PD progression and Aβ for AD pro­
gression, which trigger neuroinflammation (Mahbub et al., 2024). The 
GIT consists of more than 10 ¹ ⁴ bacteria, which is approximately ten 
times the total number of human body cells. These gut microbes play 
several vital functions, such as assisting in the breakdown of food, 
controlling immunity, generating vitamins, and providing defence 
against infections. The gut microbiome’s composition is influenced by 
external factors, such as infections, antibiotics, stress, and dietary 
intake, which can either enhance or diminish a person’s resistance to 
disease (Zhang et al., 2015). The immune system development of infants 
relies on specific probiotics such as Bifidobacteria and Enterococcus spe­
cies, which help prevent inflammatory health problems. Bacillus and 
Lactobacillus species also regulate memory and learning. The breakdown 
of mucins along with the management of the gut epithelium protects the 
mucus barrier by Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Akkermansia mucini­
phila. It enables both bacteria to support gut homeostasis and produce 
anti-inflammatory components. Inadequate levels of both bacteria Fae­

disease, and potentially to atopic dermatitis as well (Wang et al., 2018).

4.2. Gut dysbiosis and impact on AD
Gut dysbiosis, an imbalance between beneficial and harmful mi­
crobes, plays a significant role in the onset and progression of AD. 
Reduced levels of protective bacteria such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii 
and Akkermansia muciniphila impair gut barrier integrity and increase 
inflammation. At the same time, pathogenic bacteria like E. coli, Clos­
tridium difficile, and Salmonella release toxins and metabolites that 
disturb neurotransmitter pathways, trigger systemic inflammation, and 
promote amyloid-β aggregation. These processes link gut imbalance 
directly to neurodegeneration in AD (Keshavarzian and Sisodia, 2024; 
Liang et al., 2024). The illustration of the dysbiotic gut resulting from

4.3. Gut microbiome-based interventions
Given the central role of gut dysbiosis in AD pathophysiology, stra­
tegies aimed at restoring microbial homeostasis have gained significant 
therapeutic attention. Probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, dietary modifi­
cations, and FMT represent the major microbiome-based approaches 
currently under investigation.
Probiotics, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus strains, have 
been shown to enhance SCFA production, suppress proinflammatory 
cytokines, and improve cognitive performance in AD animal models and 
early clinical trials (Bonfili et al., 2025). Prebiotics, including inulin and 
fructooligosaccharides, selectively promote the growth of beneficial 
bacteria, thereby enhancing neuroprotective metabolites. When com­
bined, synbiotics provide synergistic effects by simultaneously deliv­
ering probiotics and their supporting substrates, offering a more stable 
modulation of the microbiome. Dietary interventions such as the Med­
iterranean diet and ketogenic diet also hold promise, as they enrich 
SCFA-producing bacteria and reduce systemic inflammation. Mean­
while, FMT has emerged as a potential approach to reset the gut 
ecosystem by introducing a healthy microbial community from donors. 
Preclinical studies have shown that FMT from young, healthy mice to 
aged or AD-model mice improves cognition, reduces Aβ burden, and 
restores microbial balance (Zhang et al., 2025).
Beyond conventional interventions, postbiotics (bacterial-derived 
metabolites and components) are being explored as direct therapeutic 
agents due to their stability and defined bioactivity. In addition, engi­
neered probiotics capable of secreting neuroprotective molecules such 
as γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) or anti-inflammatory peptides are under 
development as precision microbiome therapeutics. While these in­
terventions offer substantial promise, challenges remain, including 
inter-individual variability in microbiome composition, long-term 
safety, and standardization of clinical protocols. Nevertheless, 
microbiome-based therapies represent a holistic and complementary 
strategy to current AD treatments, targeting not only brain pathology 
but also its systemic and environmental modulators (Sun et al., 2025).

One of the emerging molecular regulators in this process is the class of 
small non-coding RNAs known miRNAs. These molecules, typically 
18–23 nucleotides long, regulate gene expression at the post- 
transcriptional level by binding to messenger RNAs, leading to either 
their degradation or translational repression. In addition to their intra­
cellular functions, miRNAs are secreted into the gut lumen in exosomes, 
particularly from intestinal epithelial cells and Hopx-positive cells, 
where they interact directly with gut microorganisms (Ayyanar and 
Vijayan, 2025).
Evidence suggests that fecal miRNAs can penetrate bacterial cells 
and influence microbial physiology by targeting bacterial genes 
involved in growth and metabolism. This process provides a mechanism 
of host–microbiota cross-kingdom communication, wherein the host 
actively shapes the microbial ecosystem through miRNA secretion. 
Experimental studies show that synthetic human miRNAs such as hsa- 
miRNA-515–5p and hsa-miRNA-1226–5p can selectively promote the 
growth of Fusobacterium nucleatum and E. coli, respectively, supporting 
the hypothesis that miRNAs are capable of modifying microbial com­
munity structure in a targeted manner (Kandpal et al., 2022).
The miRNA–gut–brain hypothesis, therefore, proposes that dysre­
gulated host miRNA production may drive gut dysbiosis, which in turn 
contributes to Alzheimer’s pathology. Altered miRNA expression could 
favor the expansion of pathogenic over beneficial bacterial populations, 
triggering systemic inflammation, generating neurotoxic metabolites, 
and disturbing neurotransmitter precursor balance. These downstream 
changes may impair neuronal health, promote amyloid and tau pa­
thology, and ultimately accelerate neurodegeneration. Furthermore, 
certain circulating miRNAs have the ability to cross the blood–brain 
barrier, where they can directly regulate neuronal gene expression, 
linking peripheral dysregulation to central nervous system dysfunction 
(Caradonna et al., 2024).
Therapeutically, miRNAs offer an attractive target because of their 
dual role in shaping gut microbial composition and modulating host 
gene expression. Correcting dysregulated miRNAs could, in principle, 
restore microbial balance, reduce inflammation, and improve neuronal 
resilience. However, several limitations currently hinder clinical trans­
lation. First, miRNA activity is highly tissue- and context-specific, 
making it difficult to design broad therapeutic strategies. Second, each 
miRNA can regulate multiple targets, raising the risk of off-target effects 
and unintended gene silencing. Third, delivering miRNA-based thera­
pies to precise targets such as gut epithelial cells, neurons, or across the 
blood–brain barrier remains a formidable challenge. Finally, miRNAs 
are inherently unstable in circulation due to nuclease degradation, 
necessitating advanced delivery platforms such as lipid nanoparticles or

5. Effect of Gut-based interventions in the Management of AD
5.1. Probiotics in AD
According to the International Scientific Association for Probiotics 
and Prebiotics (ISAPP), probiotics are defined as “live microorganisms 
which, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit 
on the host”. This definition evolved from the original FAO/WHO 
classification. To qualify as probiotics, products must contain live mi­
crobial strains and demonstrate proven safety and scientifically vali­
dated health benefits (Hill et al., 2014). These microorganisms are 
commonly found in functional foods, dietary supplements, or fermented 
foods. For example, Lactobacillus is found in kimchi, Bacillus in miso, 
Lactobacillus and Saccharomyces in kefir, and both Lactobacillus and 
Bifidobacterium in yogurt (Syngai et al., 2015).
Probiotics exert beneficial effects through multiple mechanisms. 
They modulate gut microbial composition, enhance mucosal immunity, 
and inhibit pathogen growth by promoting the synthesis of β-defensin 
and secretory IgA. They also modulate immune responses via cytokine 
activation, influencing NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways. This re­
sults in improved epithelial and immune cell function, including T-cell 
responses (Hemarajata and Versalovic, 2013). The use of probiotics may 
serve as a non-invasive strategy to support brain health by rebalancing 
the gut microbiota and reducing systemic and neuroinflammatory 
markers in individuals with or at risk for AD. It has been demonstrated 
that lactic acid bacteria (LAB), such as L. plantarum, L. rhamnosus, and 
L. casei, which are naturally present in kefir and yogurt, are important 
for digestion and immunomodulation. These bacteria support immune

5.6. Postbiotics in AD
Postbiotics are the preparations of inactivated microorganisms and/ 
or their components that confer health benefits to the host. Unlike 
probiotics, postbiotics do not require live microbial viability, making 
them more stable and safer, especially for immunocompromised and 
elderly populations. According to the International Scientific Associa­
tion for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP), postbiotics may exert bene­
ficial effects through various mechanisms, including immune 
modulation, enhancement of epithelial barrier function, and anti- 
inflammatory activity. Postbiotics have shown promise in modulating 
the gut–brain axis, reducing neuroinflammation, and regulating neuro­
transmitter levels such as GABA and serotonin. Specific heat-inactivated 
strains like Lactobacillus helveticus, L. paracasei, and L. gasseri have been 
associated with improvements in mood, sleep, and systemic stress 
markers. Clinical trials have observed improvements in mood, sleep, and 
stress markers using heat-killed strains such as Lactobacillus helveticus, 
L. paracasei, and L. gasseri. While direct benefits for cognitive decline or 
dementia are not yet well established, postbiotics hold promise due to 
their interaction with microbiota-derived neuroprotective compounds 
like SCFAs (Scott et al., 2022).
Postbiotics such as heat-inactivated Akkermansia muciniphila and 
Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. Lactis CECT8145 are beneficial for 
metabolic health. It has demonstrated benefits in clinical studies, 
including reduced insulin resistance, lower cholesterol, and decreased 
visceral fat. They also appear effective in gastrointestinal disorders like 
irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), where inactivated strains like B. bifidum 
MIMBb75 improved symptoms significantly. Additionally, postbiotics 
are being explored for use in skin conditions, such as acne and eczema, 
with products containing extracts from Lactobacillus plantarum, 
S. thermophilus, and other species showing improvements in skin hy­
dration, elasticity, and inflammation (Thorakkattu et al., 2022).
Postbiotics also exhibit immune-modulatory properties, enhancing 
host defense mechanisms and reducing the severity of respiratory in­
fections. Products like IMMUSE™ (Lactococcus lactis strain Plasma) have 
been shown to stimulate beneficial immune responses in both younger 
and older adults. Moreover, they have been incorporated into skincare 
products and eyedrops, with early trials indicating benefits in conditions 
like dry eye disease and allergic rhinitis (Kyowa Hakko Bio Co., Ltd 
(“IMMUSETM Lactococcus lactis strain Plasma,”).
Another study used C57BL/6 J mice were fed with a high-fat diet 
(HFD) to induce obesity and were either supplemented with 0.002 % 
heat-killed Lactobacillus plantarum L-137 (HK L-137) or given no sup­
plementation over a period ranging from 4 to 20 weeks. The researchers 
observed that mice receiving HK L-137 gained significantly less weight 
than those on the HFD alone, even though food intake was comparable 
between the groups. In addition to reduced weight gain, the HK L-137- 
treated mice exhibited improved metabolic parameters, including lower 
plasma glucose, total cholesterol, and liver enzyme levels (ALT and 
AST), which are markers of metabolic health. Furthermore, analysis of 
adipose tissue revealed decreased expression of inflammatory genes 
such as F4/80, CD11c, and IL-1β, suggesting reduced tissue inflamma­
tion (Yoshitake et al., 2020)

biotics, with applications in immune support, gut, metabolic health, and 
potential brain-related benefits. However, more robust, large-scale 
clinical trials are needed to establish their efficacy across different

6. Regulatory Perspectives
Probiotics face significant regulatory and marketing challenges 
worldwide. On the regulatory side, there is no harmonized global 
framework, with probiotics being classified variably as foods, supple­
ments, or drugs, leading to inconsistencies in approval, labeling, and 
safety requirements. Authorities such as the FDA and EFSA demand 
strong clinical evidence for health claims, but many probiotic products 
struggle to meet these stringent standards due to limited large-scale 
trials, while additional hurdles include ensuring strain identification, 
stability, and accurate labeling of viable counts. On the marketing front, 
companies face obstacles such as low consumer awareness, skepticism 
due to inconsistent evidence, and restrictions on making specific health 
claims. Furthermore, market saturation, product instability during 
storage and transport, high research costs, and regional cultural differ­
ences make differentiation difficult. Together, these regulatory barriers 
and marketing challenges highlight the need for robust scientific vali­
dation, transparent labeling, and targeted consumer education to 
establish credibility and sustainable growth in the probiotic industry.
The regulatory perspective on the use and marketing of microbiome- 
based therapies such as prebiotics, probiotics, synbiotics, metabiotics, 
and FMT is complex and evolving. It involves multiple aspects of over­
sight, including safety, ethical, and market authorization considerations. 
In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (USFDA) regu­
lates FMT as an investigational new drug (IND) (Arora and Baldi, 2015). 
Physicians must obtain patient consent that clearly outlines both known 
and potential risks. Manufacturers producing standardized stool prepa­
rations must also submit Biologics Master Files detailing facilities, pro­
cesses, and quality controls. Additionally, donor screening is one of the 
most tightly regulated aspects. To minimize infection risks, stool donors 
undergo rigorous health questionnaires, serological tests, and stool 
analysis for pathogens such as Clostridium difficile, Salmonella, Shigella, 
E. coli O157, parasites, hepatitis viruses, HIV, and drug-resistant or­
ganisms like ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae, VRE, CRE, and MRSA. 
Any positive test excludes the donor. The FDA requires continuous 
vigilance, periodic retesting, and quarantining of stool samples until 
results confirm safety. Moreover, the manufacturing of fecal products 
involves significant regulatory oversight. The composition and prepa­
ration must preserve microbial viability while maintaining sterility. 
Variables such as storage temperature, oxygen exposure, lyophilization, 
and freeze-thaw stability directly influence efficacy (Venugopalan et al., 
2010). The FDA mandates Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls 
(CMC) data to ensure product identity, purity, strength, and stability 
before clinical use. Regulatory pathways emphasize the need for potency 
assays and long-term stability testing to support clinical trials. 
Furthermore, the regulation is heterogeneous outside the US. Some 
agencies classify FMT as a biologic drug, while others treat it as a human 
cell or tissue product, medicinal product, or even a medical practice, 
depending on jurisdiction. This variability complicates globalization 
and marketing since companies must navigate different classification 
systems. Harmonized international standards are lacking, raising bar­
riers for cross-border clinical trials and product distribution (Arora and 
Baldi, 2015).
Beyond technical regulations, ethical aspects are emphasized. 
Informed consent for both donors and recipients is mandatory, partic­
ularly regarding the unknown long-term risks and potential pathogen 
transmission. FMT cannot yet be commercially marketed as a routine 
therapy outside approved clinical contexts (Thanush and Venkatesh, 
2023). Companies exploring microbiome-based therapeutics must often 
move toward defined microbial consortia or synthetic microbiota 
products, which may have clearer regulatory pathways compared to raw 
donor stool. Further, from a commercialization perspective, the primary 
hurdles such as lack of harmonized international guidelines for 
approval, high compliance burden due to donor screening, pathogen 
testing, and manufacturing standards, unclear market categorization, 
and requirement of advanced delivery platforms to meet quality and 
stability demands are the major barriers limiting widespread utilization, 
clinical adoption, and global market expansion of these therapies 
(Carlson, 2020).
Conclusively, while FMT and related microbiome therapies hold 
significant promise, regulatory frameworks remain restrictive and 
fragmented. The FDA currently allows limited use under enforcement 
discretion, while broader applications require IND approval. The glob­
alization of marketing is challenged by varied classifications, 
manufacturing complexities, and safety concerns, underscoring the need 
for harmonized regulatory frameworks before these therapies can be 
widely commercialized.
7. Challenges and future directions
Gut-based interventions for AD hold significant promise, but they 
face several challenges. One major hurdle is the complexity and vari­
ability of the gut microbiome among individuals, which makes it diffi­
cult to design one-size-fits-all probiotic or dietary therapies. 
Additionally, most existing studies are preclinical or based on small 
sample sizes, limiting the strength of clinical recommendations. The 
mechanisms linking gut microbiota to brain health, such as immune 
modulation, production of neurotransmitter-like metabolites, and 
reduction of neuroinflammation, are still not fully understood. More­
over, the BBB role in mediating microbial metabolite effects remains a 
critical area for investigation. Concerns also exist regarding the safety, 
sustained effectiveness, and regulatory approval of microbiome-based 
therapies.
Future directions include developing personalized gut-based thera­
pies using specific strains or metabiotics tailored to individual micro­
biome profiles, conducting large-scale clinical trials, and using advanced 
omics technologies to identify microbial biomarkers for early AD 
detection and treatment response. Integration of microbiome science 
with neurology could open new preventive and therapeutic avenues for 
this devastating disease.
8. Conclusion
Gut-based interventions such as prebiotics, probiotics, synbiotics, 
metabiotics, postbiotics, and FMT have demonstrated promising thera­
peutic potential in mitigating AD pathology. These strategies modulate 
the gut microbiota composition, reduce neuroinflammation, improve 
intestinal barrier integrity, and influence the gut-brain axis, thereby 
contributing to cognitive improvement and neuroprotection. Preclinical 
studies have consistently shown attenuation of Aβ accumulation, tau 
pathology, and oxidative stress, while clinical trial evidence supports 
improvements in cognitive function and inflammatory markers. How­
ever, despite these encouraging outcomes, further large-scale, long-term 
randomized controlled trials are required to validate efficacy, determine 
optimal formulations, and understand the mechanistic pathways 
involved. Emerging evidence highlights miRNAs as key regulators of 
host–microbiota interactions and AD pathology, offering potential for 
biomarkers and therapies. At the same time, regulatory standardization 
and safety frameworks are essential for translating microbiome-based 
interventions into clinical practice. Nonetheless, large-scale, long-term 
trials are still needed to confirm efficacy and mechanisms.

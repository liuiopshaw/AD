ABSTRACT: Cu nanoclusters represent a type of atomically precise
nanomaterial that exhibits excellent catalytic performance. So far, exploration
of the structural chemistry and study of the electronic (e.g., dielectric
property), magnetic, and mechanical properties of Cu nanoclusters still remain
in the early stage. In this work, we report a Cu nanocluster,
[Cu19(PPh3)4(PET)16] (PPh3 = triphenylphosphine; PET = 2-phenyl-
ethanethiol), which contains a special orthogonally packed triangular kernel
with an unexpected three-valence-electron, open-shelled system. This result
significantly differs from the polyhedral structures and the superatomic
electronic configurations in previously reported Cu nanoclusters. The unpaired
electrons in Cu19 lead to paramagnetism, which is confirmed by a
superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID). Of note, the
solution-assembled Cu19 crystal displays an unexpectedly high dielectric
constant over a wide frequency range (∼75 at 1 kHz−1 MHz), which arises from the displacement of the valence electrons and the
sharply increased dipole under the electric field. This performance exceeds that of most of the currently used solution-processable
dielectric materials (e.g., polymers). Our work demonstrates the rich structural chemistry and the distinct electronic, magnetic, and
mechanical properties of Cu nanoclusters and unveils their potential applications as high-performance, solution-processable
dielectric materials.
KEYWORDS: nanocluster, Cu nanocluster, structural transformation, paramagnetism, dielectric material
■INTRODUCTION
The field of atomically precise metal nanoclusters1 has grown
rapidly in the past two decades, with most of the research
focusing on coinage metals, including gold,2,3 silver,4,5 and more
recently, copper.6−21 In comparison to gold (Au) and silver (Ag)
nanoclusters, copper (Cu) nanoclusters offer several advantages,
such as significantly reduced cost and superior reactivity. The
development of Cu nanoclusters has opened additional
opportunities for sustainable synthesis and catalytic applica-
tions. Currently, understanding of the structural stability,
conversion, and structure−property relationships of Cu nano-
clusters still remains in the early stage. To date, there are only a
limited number of reports on the X-ray structures of Cu
nanoclusters featuring the Cu(0) character, and almost all these
Cu(0)-containing nanoclusters exhibit polyhedral kernel
structures.18−44 Cu(0)-containing nanoclusters are prone to
oxidation, and the polyhedrons are theoretically predicted to be
the most stable structures against oxidation.9,45,46 Due to their
relative instability,18−35 only a few works have reported Cu(0)-
containing Cu nanoclusters that were synthesized from
structural conversion (particularly from Cu(I) to Cu(0)).28,47
The lack of structural diversity limits the enhancement of their
properties and hinders the wide applications of Cu nanoclusters
in cutting-edge technologies.
Electronic structures also play a crucial role in determining the
stability of metal nanoclusters.22,26,38,48−50 According to the
widely accepted superatom model, metal clusters with a “magic
number” of electrons (n* = 2, 8, 18, 20, 34, 40, 58, 92, 138, etc.)
have high stability. So far, open-shelled structures with a
nonmagic number of valence electrons (e.g., n* = 1) have been
barely found in Cu nanoclusters.24,39,41 It remains unknown
whether and how these open-shell electronic structures could
broaden the properties (e.g., magnetism) and application space
of Cu nanoclusters.
In previous studies, Cu nanoclusters were mainly applied in
catalysis and photoluminescence,9,16,45,51−59 yet their electronic
applications were rarely explored. Ideal electronic devices
require a low supply voltage and a simple fabrication process.

This can be achieved by the utilization of high dielectric constant
and solution-processable materials as the dielectric layers.60−62
However, in current dielectric materials (e.g., inorganic metal
oxides, polymers), high dielectric constant and good solution-
processability are usually mutually exclusive.63,64 Dielectric
materials are generally insulators with appropriate band gaps,
and their polarizability under an electric field determines their
dielectric constant. Generally, the wider band gap impedes
electron excitation, thereby enhancing insulation performance
but weakening polarization capability. Besides, among the four
polarization modes (electron, ionic, dipole, and space charge
polarizations), electron polarization has the shortest polarization
time, which leads to a fast response under high frequency.
Nanoclusters with valence electrons in the metal kernel can be
easily polarized under an electric field, and the insulated organic
ligand shell and appropriate band gap could prevent the electric
leakage. Based on this analysis, we anticipate that solution-
assembled crystal materials of metal nanoclusters with the
above-mentioned characteristics could serve as good candidates
for dielectric materials. This may offer an opportunity to address
the long-time trade-off between the dielectric constant and
solution-processability.

which comprises an orthogonally packed triangular kernel. The
Cu19 nanocluster was synthesized via the structural trans-

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Crystallography and Characterization
[Cu19(PPh3)4(PET)16] (Cu19) cluster has a Cu15 orthogonally
packed triangular kernel structure, with two CuPS3 and two
CuPS2 linked to the kernel motif by the Cu−S−Cu bonds
(Figure 1A−C). The orthogonal kernel is constructed by eight
Cu3 triangles, with four triangles (light blue-filled triangles, 1−4)
in the center, which share one central Cu atom (light blue atom).
The other four triangles (yellow-filled triangles, 5−8) connect to
the four central triangles by sharing four vertex Cu atoms
(yellow atoms). Among all of the Cu atoms, the “Cu5” atom has
static discrete disorder with the same probability in two
positions. Different from the other Cu(0)-containing nano-
clusters which normally display polyhedral structures (summar-
ized in Figures S1 and S2), the kernel of Cu19 has a nondense
stack mode. The coordination number of the central Cu atom is
6, which is only found in large Cu nanoclusters
([Cu61S6(SR)26Cl6H14]+ and [Cu75(SAdm)32]2+).19,27 Details
of the eight Cu3 triangles are displayed in Figure 1D.
Interestingly, the six Cu−Cu bond lengths related to the central
Cu atom are extremely short, which are in the range of 2.264−
2.546 Å. The shorter Cu−Cu bonds probably arise from the
encapsulation of outer atoms and the presence of more bonding
electrons. The short bond length means high bond energy,
which can be conducive to the stability of the inner Cu(0)
atoms.19,66 Other Cu−Cu bonds have longer distances (2.720−
2.888 Å). For clarity, all of the Cu−Cu bond lengths are
summarized in Figure 1E.
No counteranion can be determined by X-ray analysis of the
Cu19 crystal. Energy-dispersive spectroscopic (EDS) analysis of
the Cu19 crystal confirmed the existence of elements Cu, S, P,
and C. Other elements, such as N, F, Cl, or Br, were not detected
(Figures 2A and S3). This further confirms that there is no
counteranion in the crystal and Cu19 is a neutral nanocluster.
Electrospray ionization mass spectroscopy (ESI-MS) of Cu19
(crystal redissolved in CHCl3) has no corresponding signal peak
in either positive or negative ion modes, suggesting that the Cu19
cluster is neutral, which is thus hard to ionize under mass
spectrometer conditions (Figure S4). This observation is similar
to previous reports of neutral Cu nanoclusters.23,26,37 The
addition of CsOAc (cesium acetate) also did not yield relevant
results corresponding to the intact Cu19 cluster.
The valence state of the Cu atoms in Cu19 was examined by X-
ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The Cu 2p spectrum of
Cu19 has only two peaks at 932.9 and 952.8 eV, which
correspond to the binding energies of Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2,
respectively. There is no satellite signal at around 943 eV, which
excludes the existence of Cu(II) (Figure S5). The Cu LMM X-
ray-excited Auger electron spectroscopy was performed to
further verify the Cu valence in Cu19. The Cu LMM Auger
spectrum is shown in Figure 2B; a shoulder peak at 918.5 eV
could be attributed to Cu(0) LMM.33,37−39,41,42 The other two
peaks at 916.0 and 911.4 eV are assigned to Cu(I) LMM and
other electron orbitals. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
spectroscopy was conducted to exclude the existence of metal
hydride in Cu19. There is no copper-hydride signal in 1H NMR
spectra of Cu19,24,26,29,31,37,38,41 and the integration of the
correlated peak areas is in accordance with the stoichiometric
ratio of Cu19 (Figure S6). Moreover, the Cu19 cluster was
synthesized via the transformation of [Cu15(PPh3)6(PET)13]-
(BF4)2 without the addition of a hydride reducing agent. Thus,

Magnetic Properties
The number of valence electrons in the [Cu19(PPh3)4(PET)16]
(Cu19) cluster is 3. The DFT calculations suggest that the 3
valence electrons in the Cu19 cluster exhibit the “one electron
pair + one unpaired electron” configuration. The existence of an
unpaired electron leads to the paramagnetism of Cu19. Electron
paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy is based on the
Zeeman effect of unpaired electrons in an external magnetic
field. Therefore, EPR was employed to confirm the existence of
the unpaired electron and investigate the magnetic properties of
Cu19. The crystals of Cu19 display an intense EPR signal with a g-
value of 2.03 at 100 K (Figure 3A), which confirms the presence
of an unpaired electron in Cu19. Furthermore, the paramagnetic
nature of Cu19 was investigated by superconducting quantum
interference device (SQUID) measurements. Although Cu19 did
not reach saturation magnetization at the maximum magnetic
field intensity (±70 kOe) of the equipment, the M−H plot of
Cu19 crystals at 1.8 K clearly shows paramagnetic behavior
without hysteresis (Figure 3B). Compared with open-shelled Ag
and Au nanoclusters (3−30 kOe),68−70 the Cu19 nanocluster
requires a higher magnetic field intensity (>70 kOe) to reach
saturation magnetization. The Cu atom has fewer electron shells
(3d104S1), which exert a stronger confinement effect on the
electrons, thus making the Cu19 nanocluster more difficult in
magnetic moment directional alignment.
Dielectric Property
[Cu19(PPh3)4(PET)16] (Cu19) has an ∼2.96 eV band gap and 3
valence electrons. This relatively wide band gap and insulated
organic ligand shell could prevent electric leakage. The unpaired
electron in Cu19 properly reduces the band gap, thereby
enhancing the polarization capability of Cu19. Therefore, Cu19
has the potential to serve as a dielectric material. A Cu19 single
crystal capacitor was fabricated, and the dielectric property of
the Cu19 crystal was measured by an impedance analyzer (Figure
4A). The solution-grown Cu19 single crystals were picked out
from the mother liquor and transferred onto the ITO (indium−
tin oxide) glass. The Au (gold) electrodes with a thickness of 70
nm were deposited on the Cu19 single crystals via thermal
evaporation. An optical microscope image of the Cu19 single
crystal capacitor is shown in Figure 4B. Interestingly, the Cu19
single crystal shows a stable and high dielectric constant (κ) of
∼75 over a wide frequency range (1 kHz−1 MHz, Figure 4C).
Usually, fabrication of high dielectric constant materials (e.g.,
inorganic metal oxides) requires high-temperature calcination,
vapor deposition, and other high-temperature processes, which
are energy-consuming and inappropriate for the fabrication of
flexible devices.64 By contrast, solution-processable dielectric
materials (e.g., polymers) are easily fabricated and suitable for
fabricating flexible devices, but they generally have lower
dielectric constants (κ < 60, polyvinylidene fluoride copoly-
mer).63 The dielectric constants and processing methods of
common dielectric materials are summarized in Table S2. The
assembled crystals of Cu19 lead to the successful combination of
a high dielectric constant (∼75) and solution processability.
DFT simulations were used to reveal the electron polarization
of Cu19 (Figure 4D). The permanent dipole of Cu19 is only 0.46
D. The crystal face of Cu19 was determined by X-ray diffraction
(Figure S8). The electric field applied to the Cu19 single crystal
capacitor was perpendicular to the (−1,1,1) crystal face (Figure
4B). Therefore, a static electric field, which has a similar
direction as the Cu19 cluster and the electric field in the Cu19
single crystal capacitor, was added to simulate the electron
polarization. After the addition of the electric field (0.001 au),
the induced dipole sharply increases to 5.60 D, and the dipole
moment deflects toward the direction of the electric field. This
significantly increased dipole (i.e., electron polarization) under
the field leads to the high dielectric constant of the Cu19 crystal.

Mechanical Property
The mechanical properties of the [Cu19(PPh3)4(PET)16]
(Cu19) crystals were investigated by an ultranano hardness
tester (UNHT). Mechanical properties of metal nanocluster
crystals have only been studied in a few previous studies.74−76
The Cu19 single crystals were scattered over a silicon slice for
measurement without any additional treatment. The load−
displacement curves of a Cu19 single crystal were measured.
After fitting the loading curve, Young’s modulus (Er) of 2.45 ±
0.46 GPa and hardness (H) of 104 ± 6.08 MPa were obtained
(Figure 6A). The crystal structure of Cu19 is shown in Figure 6B.
The Cu19 crystal belongs to the monoclinic system (a = 27.662
Å, b = 27.055 Å, c = 28.581 Å, α = 90.00°, β = 92.895°, γ =
90.00°), which has a nondense packing mode. The longer
intercluster distances and the lack of intermolecular forces may
result in the relatively lower values of Er and H.
Byproduct
In addition, the reaction of [Cu15(PPh3)6(PET)13](BF4)2 and
Au−PET complex simultaneously leads to the formation of
another [Au1Cu14(PPh3)6(PET)12)]+ (Au1Cu14 for short here-
after) alloy nanocluster byproduct with a relatively lower yield.
After washing the mixture of Cu19 and Au1Cu14 crystals with
tetrahydrofuran (THF), the Cu19 crystals can be purified, while
the Au1Cu14 crystals are dissolved into THF. The character-
ization and analysis of Au1Cu14 are shown in the Supporting
Information.
■CONCLUSION
So far, most of the reported Cu(0)-containing nanoclusters are
superatoms (e.g., 2e, 8e) with polyhedral kernel structures, and
the majority of previous reports on Cu-nanocluster-based
applications focus on catalysis or luminescence. In contrast,
the current work studies the atomic structure, magnetic
property, dielectric performance, and mechanical property of a
nonpolyhedral, 3-electron Cu nanocluster. The main findings of
our work can be summarized as follows:
We report a Cu19 nanocluster with partial Cu(0)
character. Of note, the Cu19 nanocluster displays an
orthogonally packed triangular Cu15 kernel and a 3-
valence-electron, open-shelled system, which significantly
differs from the polyhedral kernel structures and
superatomic configurations reported in previous Cu(0)-
containing nanoclusters.

■METHODS
Chemicals and Materials
All of the chemicals are commercially available and were used as
received. Cuprous oxide (Cu2O, ≥99%), tetrachloroauric acid
trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O, 99%), and 2-phenylethanethiol
(C6H5CH2CH2SH, ≥99%, PET) were purchased from Shang-
hai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Acetonitrile
(CH3CN, AR), fluoboric acid (HBF4, 40%, AR), trichloro-
methane (CHCl3, AR), triphenylphosphine (C18H15P, CP,
PPh3), sodium borohydride (NaBH4, AR), ethanol (C2H5OH,
anhydrous, AR), methanol (CH3OH, anhydrous, AR), dichloro-
methane (CH2Cl2, AR), and hexane (C6H14, AR) were
purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.
(China).
Synthesis of Au−PET Complex
100 mg of HAuCl4·3H2O was dissolved in 5 mL methanol, and
100 μL of 2-phenylethanethiol (PET) were added to the
solution under vigorous stirring for 30 min. After centrifugation
at ∼5000 rpm, the solution was removed, the precipitate was
washed 3 times with ethanol to remove the redundant PET, and
the Au−PET complex (powder) was obtained. The composition
of the Au−PET complex was characterized by electrospray
ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS, Figure S18). The Au−
PET complex is a series of (AuPET)n.

Fabrication of Cu19 Single-Crystal Capacitor
The Cu19 single-crystal capacitors were fabricated in an
anhydrous and oxygen-free environment. The Cu19 single
crystals were dispersed in hexane. Then, the suspension was
dripped onto an ITO (indium−tin oxide) glass. Au electrodes
with a thickness of 70 nm were deposited on the Cu19 single
crystals via thermal evaporation. After the fabrication, the ITO
and Au electrodes partly protect the Cu19 crystal from oxidation,
enabling impedance analysis to be conducted in an ambient
atmosphere.
Characterization
X-ray Single Crystal Diffraction. A suitable crystal was
selected and mounted on a Bruker D8 Venture diffractometer
with Ga Kα radiation (λ = 1.34139 Å) for cell determination and
subsequent data collection at 170 K.
UV−Vis Spectroscopy. UV−vis spectra of the nanoclusters
(dissolved in CH2Cl2) were acquired by using a Shimadzu UV-
2600i diode array spectrophotometer.
Mass Spectroscopy. ESI mass spectra were recorded by
using a Synapt G2si quadrupole ion mobility time-of-flight mass
spectrometer (Waters, Manchester, UK) in TOF mode. Mass
calibration was performed using a 5 mg/mL cesium iodide
aqueous solution. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy was carried
out on an Oxford X-Plore Compact 30.
X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy. X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy measurements were performed on an AXIS
SUPRA, equipped with a monochromated Al Kα (1486.8 eV),
120 W X-ray source.
Electron Paramagnetic Resonance Spectroscopy.
Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were
carried out using a Bruker EMX plus EPR spectrometer (Bruker
Biospin GmbH, Germany), with a variable nitrogen temperature
control system (Bruker ER 4141 VTM). The EPR spectra were
all recorded at 100 K.
Superconducting Quantum Interference Device. Mag-
netic measurements were performed at 1.8 K from −7 T to 7 T
on a Quantum Design MPMS3 SQUID magnetometer. 1H
NMR spectra were measured on a Bruker AVANCE NEO 400
MHz NMR spectrometer.
Impedance Analysis. Impedance analyzer (Keysight
E4990A) was employed to carry out the capacitance−frequency
(C−F) measurements, and an AC amplitude of 500 mV in a
frequency range of 1 kHz to 1 MHz was applied to the capacitor
with zero DC bias.
Transient Absorption Spectroscopy
The femtosecond transient absorption measurements were
carried out using a Yb:KGW regenerative amplified femto-
second laser system (Light Conversion, 1027 nm, 190 fs, 200 μJ/
pulse, and 100 kHz repetition rate) and a Femto-TA100
spectrometer (Time-Tech Spectra LLC). The nanosecond
transient absorption measurements were conducted with a
Nano-TA100 spectrometer (Time-Tech Spectra LLC). The
pump beam was operated at 1 kHz. The nanosecond white-light
laser (380−1600 nm, 0.5 ns pulse duration, 2 kHz; Disco,
Leukos) was used to serve as the probe beam.

Computational Details
All calculations in this study were performed using density
functional theory (DFT) in Gaussian 09 with the B3LYP
functional. The effective-core basis set SDD was used for Cu
atoms, and the all-electronic 6-31G(d, p) basis set was used for
C, H, P, and S atoms. To reduce the computational cost, the
alkyl side groups of 2-phenylethanethiol were replaced with
methyl groups. Under the convergence criteria of DFT
calculations (int = ultrafine, int = acc2e = 12, and SCF =
maxcyc = 128), the doublet state (one unpaired electron)
successfully achieved convergence. However, the quartet state
(three unpaired electrons) cannot reach convergence under the
same condition, even after testing multiple wave functions as
initial guesses. Consequently, this result suggests that the 3
valence electrons in the [Cu19(PPh3)4(PET)16] (Cu19) cluster
exhibit the “one electron pair + one unpaired electron”
configuration. The excited state energies were calculated based
on the fully optimized ground state structures. Polarizabilities of
Cu19 were calculated by an optimized structure with or without
an electric field. The crystal face of Cu19 was determined by X-
ray diffraction (Figure S8). The electric field applied to the Cu19
single crystal capacitor was perpendicular to the (−1,1,1) crystal
face (Figure 4B). Therefore, a static electric field, which has a
similar direction as the Cu19 cluster and the electric field in the
Cu19 single crystal capacitor, was added to simulate the electron
polarization. The calculations were performed with the cluster
geometry held fixed, allowing only the electronic structure to
relax. The Gaussian09 software package was employed for the
whole set of calculations.

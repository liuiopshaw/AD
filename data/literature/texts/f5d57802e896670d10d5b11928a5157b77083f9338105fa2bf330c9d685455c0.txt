Glomerular barrier behaves as an atomically
precise bandpass ﬁlter in a sub-nanometre regime
Bujie Du1, Xingya Jiang1, Anindita Das2, Qinhan Zhou1, Mengxiao Yu1, Rongchao Jin2 and Jie Zheng1*
The glomerular ﬁltration barrier is known as a ‘size cutoff’ slit, which retains nanoparticles or proteins larger than 6–8 nm
in the body and rapidly excretes smaller ones through the kidneys. However, in the sub-nanometre size regime, we have
found that this barrier behaves as an atomically precise ‘bandpass’ ﬁlter to signiﬁcantly slow down renal clearance of
few-atom gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) with the same surface ligands but different sizes (Au18, Au15 and Au10-11).
Compared to Au25 (∼1.0 nm), just few-atom decreases in size result in four- to ninefold reductions in renal clearance
efﬁciency in the early elimination stage, because the smaller AuNCs are more readily trapped by the glomerular
glycocalyx than larger ones. This unique in vivo nano–bio interaction in the sub-nanometre regime also slows down the
extravasation of sub-nanometre AuNCs from normal blood vessels and enhances their passive targeting to cancerous
tissues through an enhanced permeability and retention effect. This discovery highlights the size precision in the body’s
response to nanoparticles and opens a new pathway to develop nanomedicines for many diseases associated with
glycocalyx dysfunction.
A
mong the many factors involved in the retention and elimin-
ation of engineered nanoparticles (NPs)1–3, size is known to
play a key role4–6. Depending on particle size, engineered
NPs interact differently with a variety of physiological barriers, are
retained in the body for different periods of time, and follow distinct
clearance pathways7–10. For example, engineered NPs can end up in
the spleen if their sizes are comparable to the inter-endothelial cell
slits of the spleen (200–500 nm)9 or accumulate in the liver if their
sizes are close to the vascular fenestration of the liver (50–100 nm)9.
Uptake by the reticuloendothelial system (RES) (liver, spleen and so
on) is the most common pathway for the body to remove engineered
NPs with sizes above 6 nm from the blood stream, but it often takes
much longer for the body to completely eliminate them if they are
not biodegradable11. On the other hand, engineered NPs with
sizes below 6 nm can be readily eliminated through the kidneys
by crossing a unique multiple-layer structure of glomeruli12,13.
Choi et al. developed a class of zwitterionic quantum dots
(Qdots), and found that Qdots with hydrodynamic diameters
(HDs) of <6 nm cleared from the body through the urinary
system much more efﬁciently than larger ones, with the renal clear-
ance efﬁciency of Qdots increasing from 45 to 75% injection dose
(%ID) (4 h post injection, p.i.) with just a slight decrease of HD
from 5.64 to 4.64 nm (ref. 14). A similar trend was also observed
for renal-clearable silica NPs15, where a decrease of the HD from
6 to 3.3 nm resulted in a signiﬁcant increase in renal clearance
efﬁciency from 55 to 67.5%ID (24 h p.i.). We also developed
renal-clearable AuNPs with HDs of 3.0 nm (ref. 16) and 2.2 nm
(ref. 17), which were eliminated from the body at efﬁciencies of
43 and 53%ID (24 h p.i.), respectively. These studies are also
consistent with a general observation of protein ﬁltration through
the glomeruli, where smaller proteins such as 5.3 nm single-chain
variable fragment (ScFv) can be eliminated from the body at an

Moreover, the renal clearance of the smaller 3.0 nm inulin is
nearly 30% more efﬁcient than that of 3.4 nm myoglobin14. With
these ﬁndings, it has generally been accepted that the glomeruli
serve as a one-directional ‘size cutoff’ slit, where engineered NPs
or proteins with sizes smaller than 6 nm can pass through the
kidneys, and smaller ones clear out of the body more efﬁciently
than larger ones.
Size-dependent glomerular ﬁltration is mainly observed for
engineered NPs or proteins with sizes ranging from 2 to 6 nm.
A fundamental question thus arises: is this size dependency in
glomerular ﬁltration still valid at an even smaller size scale?
Investigating how the glomeruli ﬁlter out engineered NPs in the
sub-nanometre regime not only helps us gain a more comprehen-
sive understanding of glomerular ﬁltration and kidney diseases in
general, but is also of fundamental importance to the precise
control of nanomedicines that can break into much smaller
fragments in vivo. Here, we report that, in the sub-nanometre size
range (∼1 nm), the glomerulus is no longer a ‘size cutoff’ slit18 but
can become an atomically precise ‘bandpass’ barrier to signiﬁcantly
slow down renal clearance of sub-nanometre gold nanoclusters
(sub-nanometre AuNCs). In the sub-nanometre regime, renal clear-
ance of AuNCs increases exponentially with increasing number of
atoms in a particle. Unlike the size dependency observed in the
glomerular ﬁltration of engineered NPs larger than 2 nm, which is
mainly dictated by the pore size of the glomerular basement
membrane (GBM) and podocyte19, the inverse size dependency in
glomerular ﬁltration in the sub-nanometre regime originates from
the smaller sub-nanometre AuNPs being more easily physically
retained by the endothelial glycocalyx of the glomerulus.
Atomically precise glutathione-coated AuNCs
The observation of size dependency in glomerular ﬁltration in the
sub-nanometre size regime was made possible by a recent break-
through in the precise control of the number of gold atoms in
sub-nanometre AuNCs20–23. Atomically precise AuNCs coated
with glutathione (GSH) were synthesized by controlling the
reduction kinetics of gold ions in the presence of GSH in aqueous
solutions, based on a reported method24 (for details see literature
from ref. 24 in the Supplementary Information), and electrospray
ionization mass spectra conﬁrmed the successful synthesis of

unique opportunity to non-invasively image the renal clearance of

podocyte. Podocytes are covered by 200 nm glycocalyx. Generally, the fenestration between endothelial cells is 70–90 nm, the GBM junction is 2–8 nm,

Because these AuNCs are highly physiologically stable and resistant
to serum protein adsorption for both short (30 min) and long (24 h)
periods (Supplementary Figs 4 and 7), the observed differences in
bladder/kidney ratios clearly indicate that the kidney ﬁltration is
highly sensitive to cluster size in the sub-nanometre regime. To
more quantitatively understand the renal clearance of these atomic-
ally precise AuNCs, BALB/c mice were intravenously injected
with 100 µl phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing Au25,
Au18, Au15 and Au10-11, respectively, at the same molar concen-
tration of 100 µmol l–1, followed by quantiﬁcation of the amount
of Au in the urine with inductively coupled plasma–mass
spectrometry (ICP-MS) at 2, 5, 8, 12 and 24 h p.i. Consistent with
X-ray imaging studies, renal clearance of sub-nanometre AuNCs
was highly sensitive to the number of Au atoms (Fig. 1c and
Supplementary Table 2). A signiﬁcant difference in renal clearance
was observed between Au25 (46.71%ID, 2 h p.i.) and Au18 (to
10.79%ID, 2 h p.i.), indicating that a seven-Au-atom decrease in
size can lead to a fourfold decrease in renal clearance efﬁciency at
2 h p.i. Furthermore, the renal clearance efﬁciency declined to
6.03 and 5.29%ID at 2 h p.i. when the cluster size was further
reduced to Au15 and Au10-11, respectively. At 24 h p.i., urinary
excretion of these AuNCs also decreased with decreasing number
of Au atoms in the clusters (51.57, 26.82, 22.90 and 19.07%ID for
Au25, Au18, Au15 and Au10-11, respectively). Comparing those
results with our previously reported renal clearance efﬁciencies of
∼1.7 (ref. 17), ∼2.5 (ref. 16) and ∼6 nm glutathione-coated gold
nanoparticles (GS-AuNPs) (ref. 17) (Fig. 1d), our results for Au25
(∼1.0 nm) are almost identical to those for ∼1.7 nm GS-AuNPs
(Au201)17 in terms of renal clearance efﬁciency at 24 h p.i. (∼52%
ID for Au25 and 53%ID for 1.7 nm AuNPs), despite the eightfold
difference in the number of Au atoms, but ∼2.5 nm GS-AuNPs16
(Au640, ∼43%ID, 24 h p.i.) cleared out of the body more slowly
than the smaller Au25 and Au201. In addition, only 4%ID of ∼6 nm
GS-AuNPs17 (Au8856, 24 h p.i.) were cleared renally. Combining
these results, it is clear that there is an inverse size dependency in
the renal clearance of AuNCs in the sub-nanometre region, distinct
from the prevailing understanding that smaller NPs always clear
more rapidly through the urinary system than larger NPs14,15. This
indicates that the glomerulus (Fig. 1e), composed of glycocalyx, endo-
thelial cells, GBM and podocyte12,27, is no longer a one-directional
‘size-cutoff’ slit18, but becomes an atomically precise ‘bandpass’
barrier to signiﬁcantly slow down renal clearance of ultrasmall
AuNPs with sizes in the sub-nanometre regime (Fig. 1d).

blood retention and slower extravasation and glomerular ﬁltration.
Passive tumour targeting of AuNCs
The passive tumour targeting of sub-nanometre AuNCs is also
size-dependent. Using nude mice bearing MCF-7 breast xenografts
as a model, we quantiﬁed the tumour-targeting efﬁciencies of these
AuNCs at 24 h p.i., which were measured as 8.17 ± 0.23, 6.07 ± 1.18,
4.89 ± 0.72 and 2.28 ± 0.13%ID g−1, for Au10-11, Au15, Au18 and
Au25, respectively (Fig. 4a; for accumulation in other organs see
Supplementary Fig. 12). These results clearly indicate that the
efﬁciency of passive tumour targeting in the sub-nanometre regime
decreases linearly with increasing number of Au atoms in the clus-
ters. Once the AuNPs are larger than Au25, this linear relationship
vanishes; instead, the tumour uptake of ∼1.7 nm GS-AuNPs (Au201,
∼1.97%ID g−1) and ∼2.5 nm GS-AuNPs (Au640, ∼2.00%ID g−1)
becomes comparable to that of Au25 (2.28%ID g−1). To unravel
the origin of the observed size-dependent tumour targeting
of sub-nanometre AuNCs, we correlated the tumour targeting efﬁ-
ciency with blood retention (area under pharmacokinetics curve,
AUC). The linear relationship between the targeting efﬁciency
and the AUC (Fig. 4b) indicates that the size-dependent tumour
targeting in the sub-nanometre regime is fundamentally due to
size-dependent blood retention of sub-nanometre AuNCs, with
the smallest AuNC, Au10-11, having the longest retention in the
blood and therefore having the highest accumulation in tumours.
Because the tumour/blood ratios of all the sub-nanometre AuNCs
are larger than 1 and much higher than muscle/blood ratios at
24 h p.i. (Fig. 4c), the selective accumulation of sub-nanometre
AuNCs is fundamentally because of the enhanced permeability
and retention (EPR) effect34, where the leaky and dense vasculature
of tumours leads to the long retention of nanosized particles. Further
investigation of the distribution of Au18 and Au25 in tumours at 24 h
p.i. (Fig. 4d) conﬁrmed that both AuNCs crossed the leaky tumour
vasculature and entered the tumour microenvironment. More impor-
tantly, because Au18 has a much longer blood retention than Au25,
more Au18 clusters were retained in the tumour microenvironment,
consistent with the observed size-dependent tumour targeting in
the sub-nanometre regime. Combining these results with our
previous observation of passive tumour targeting of 2.5 nm renal-
clearable AuNPs26,35 conﬁrms that the EPR effect is indeed retained
in renal-clearable AuNPs with sizes down to a few atoms, which
could further broaden the EPR effect, a major strategy used with
large non-renal-clearable NPs in tumour targeting5.
Unlike the tumour/blood ratios, the ratios of the liver, spleen and
intestine to blood at 24 h p.i. were all close to 1 (Fig. 4e), suggesting
that the ultrasmall AuNCs have little interaction with those RES
organs. The ratio of the kidney to blood was much larger than 1
(Fig. 4e), and kidney accumulation of these AuNCs decreased
continually over time (Supplementary Fig. 13), clearly indicating
that kidneys remain the major organ for eliminating these ultrasmall
AuNCs. After one month, the major organ accumulation of

In summary, by investigating the in vivo behaviour of sub-
nanometre AuNCs with high resistance to the binding of serum
protein and blood cells, we discovered that the glomeruli can
serve as an atomically precise barrier to slow down the renal clear-
ance of clusters with size below 1 nm: a few-atom decrease in cluster
size results in a nearly one order reduction in renal clearance. This
surprising observation fundamentally originates from the fact that
the smaller AuNCs are more easily and physically retained by the
glycocalyx of the glomeruli, similar to the separation principle
used in gel ﬁltration or size-exclusion chromatography. Such a
size-dependent interaction between sub-nanometre AuNCs and
the glycocalyx also dictates the extravasation of sub-nanometre
AuNCs from normal blood vessels and signiﬁcantly impacts their
accumulation in cancerous tissues through the EPR effect. This discov-
ery highlights how precisely the glomerulus and the body could
response to ultrasmall AuNPs, which might be generalized to many
other
renal-clearable
nanosystems14,15,36
and
further

our comprehensive understanding of glomerular ﬁltration in the
sub-nanometre regime. Considering the similarity of rodents and
humans in terms of the pore sizes of the glomerular ﬁltration mem-
brane (Supplementary Table 1), these ﬁndings will also open a new
pathway to the design of clinically translatable precision nanomedi-
cines that can target many endothelial-dysfunction-associated diseases
such as strokes, atherosclerosis, hypertension and chronic renal
failure, once the biocompatibility of these renal-clearable smaller
AuNCs has been fully investigated.

Methods
Materials and equipment. Hydrogen tetrachloroaurate (Fisher Scientiﬁc) was used
for the synthesis of AuNCs. All other chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich
and used as received, unless speciﬁed. Gold in the urine, blood and organs was
detected by an Agilent 7900 ICP-MS. The luminescence spectra of Au18SG14,
Au25SG18 and Au22SG16, Au22SG18 were collected with a PTI QuantaMaster 30
Fluorescence Spectrophotometer. Blood-vessel images and optical images of kidneys
and tumours were taken with an IX-71 microscope (Olympus). EM images of the
kidney were recorded with a 120 kV Tecnai G2 spirit transmission electron
microscope (FEI) equipped with a LaB6 source. Animal studies were performed
according to the guidelines of the University of Texas System Institutional Animal
Care and Use Committee. Female BALB/c mice (BALB/cAnNCr, strain code 047,
6–8 weeks old, weighing 20–25 g) were purchased from Envigo. Female nude mice
(Athymic NCr-nu/nu, strain code 069, 6–8 weeks old, weighing 20–25 g), were also
purchased from Envigo for the tumour-targeting study. All mice were randomly
allocated and four mice were housed per ventilated cage under standard
environmental conditions (23 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 5% humidity and a 12/12 h light/dark
cycle) with free access to water and standard laboratory food.
Synthesis of GSH-coated AuNCs. The AuNCs were synthesized according to
previously reported procedures with slight modiﬁcations37–42. The Au10-11SG10-11
and Au15SG13 nanoclusters were synthesized using carbon monoxide (CO) as
reducing agent under controlled pH conditions37. Brieﬂy, aqueous solutions of
HAuCl4·3H2O (6.25 ml, 20 mM) and GSH (GSH, 5 ml, 50 mM) were added
to 114 ml water. This reaction mixture was stirred vigorously for 2 min, followed by
adjusting the pH to 7 and 9 with 1 M sodium hydroxide for the synthesis of
Au10-11SG10-11 and Au15SG13, respectively. CO gas was bubbled through the mixture
at 1 atm pressure to reduce Au(I)-SG for 3–4 min. The reaction ﬂasks were then
sealed and the solutions stirred for 24 h at room temperature.
For the synthesis of Au18SG14 (ref. 38), aqueous solutions of HAuCl4·3H2O
(2 ml, 10 mM) and GSH (3 ml, 20 mM) were added to 15 ml water and stirred
vigorously for 5 min. A solution of borane tert-butylamine in toluene
(20 ml, 10 mM) was then added to the mixture and the reaction mixture stirred
overnight (∼16 h). After completion of the reaction, the aqueous phase was
concentrated under reduced pressure, followed by precipitation of the crude cluster
product with excess methanol. The as-obtained precipitate was washed repeatedly
with methanol and ﬁnally dried under vacuum to yield Au18SG14 clusters in
powder form.
For the synthesis of Au25SG18 (refs 39,40), as a ﬁrst step, HAuCl4·3H2O (0.090 g,
0.228 mmol) was dissolved in 5 ml water, then added to a toluene solution of
tetraoctylammonium bromide (10 ml, 0.145 g, 0.265 mmol). The two-phase
solution was vigorously stirred for 15 min for phase transfer of Au(III) salt from the
aqueous to toluene phase. The aqueous layer was then removed and
triphenylphosphine (PPh3) (0.180 g, 0.686 mmol) was added under vigorous
stirring. A freshly prepared ethanol solution of sodium borohydride (NaBH4)
(0.026 g, 0.684 mmol, 5 ml) was rapidly added to the whitish suspension to reduce
Au(I)-(PPh3)X (X = Cl or Br) to the clusters. After 6 h, toluene was removed by
rotary evaporation. The resulting reddish-brown product was washed with water
followed by repeated hexane:dichloromethane (6:1) washings to remove excess
reactants. The Au:PPh3 clusters were ﬁnally extracted with dichloromethane, to
which an aqueous solution of GSH (5 ml, 0.400 g, 1.3 mmol) was added. This
reaction was stirred at 55 °C for 36 h. Excess acetone was then added to the aqueous
phase to precipitate out the crude Au25SG18 cluster product. The as-obtained
precipitate was then repeatedly washed with excess methanol and dried under
vacuum to yield Au25SG18 clusters in powder form.
For the synthesis of Au22SG16 and Au22SG18 (refs 41,42), 1.0 mmol of GSH
was added to a methanol solution (50 ml) of HAuCl4 (0.25 mmol). The mixture was
cooled to 0 °C for 30 min. The aqueous solution of NaBH4 (0.2 M, 12.5 ml) was
added into the mixture, after which this mixture was stirred at 1,600 r.p.m. for
60 min. The resulting precipitate was washed with methanol repeatedly, then
puriﬁed with a NAP-5 column in water to remove the impurities. The as-synthesized
AuNCs were separated using polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis.
Stability test of AuNCs in the physiological environment. To test whether these
AuNCs bind to serum protein or not, Au10-11SG10-11, Au15SG13, Au18SG14 and
Au25SG18 were incubated with either PBS or PBS supplemented with 10% (vol/vol)
fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C for 30 min. In addition, Au10-11SG10-11, Au18SG14
and Au25SG18 were incubated with PBS or PBS supplemented with 10% (vol/vol)
FBS at 37 °C for 24 h to test whether these AuNCs bind to serum protein after
long-term (24 h) incubation with FBS. To identify the colourless protein band,
FBS-incubated Au10-11SG10-11, Au15SG13, Au18SG14 and Au25SG18 and pure FBS
were stained by 10% (vol/vol) Coomassie Brilliant Blue 250 (CBB 250). All samples
were analysed using 2% agarose gel electrophoresis (Mini-sub cell GT gel
electrophoresis system from Bio-Rad). The amount of gold in each band was
quantiﬁed using ICP-MS.
To quantify the long-term physiological stability of these AuNCs, Au10-11SG10-11,
Au18SG14 and Au25SG18 were incubated with PBS solution supplemented with
10% (vol/vol) FBS for 24 h incubation at 37 °C. Their UV–vis absorption spectra
were collected at different incubation time points (0, 1, 12 and 24 h).
Blood cell binding test. Whole blood was collected from female BALB/c mice
(6–8 weeks, 20–25 g) and then stored in an anticoagulation BD Vacutainer. This
blood was divided into 4 × 3 groups for incubation with Au10-11SG10-11, Au15SG13,
Au18SG14, Au25SG18 at a 10% vol/vol ratio at room temperature for 30 min,
followed by centrifugation at room temperature at 500g to separate the plasma
and blood cells. Finally, the amounts of gold in plasma and blood cells were
quantiﬁed by ICP-MS.
X-ray kidney images of mice after intravenous injection of Au10-11, Au18 and
Au25. All X-ray images of kidneys were taken using Bruker In-vivo Xtreme Imaging.
Parameters: X-ray, 0.8 mm, KVP: 45; exposure type, standard; mode, high speed;
exposure time, 15 s; bin: 1×1; FOV, 12; fStop, 5.6; focal plane, 0 mm. BALB/c mice
were imaged with the above parameters after being intravenously injected with
Au10-11SG10-11, Au18SG14 and Au25SG18 for 40 min (Au10-11SG10-11, n = 3;
Au18SG14, n = 4; Au25SG18, n = 3).
In vivo renal clearance kinetics study. BALB/c mice were intravenously injected
with Au10-11SG10-11 (n = 3), Au15SG13 (n = 3), Au18SG14 (n = 6) and Au25SG18
(n = 3), respectively (∼100 µmol l–1, 100 µl) and then placed in metabolism cages.
Mouse urine was collected at 2, 5, 8, 12 and 24 h p.i. The urine was completely lysed
in freshly made aqua regia in a plastic centrifuge tube (15 ml) for 2 days, then
diluted to 10 ml using ultrapure water. All samples were centrifuged at 4,500 r.p.m.
for 5 min and analysed by ICP-MS.
Optical kidney images of mice after injection of Au18SG14 and Au25SG18.
Au18SG14 and Au25SG18 (300 µl, ∼0.7 mmol l–1) and 300 µl PBS (control) were
i.v. injected into the mice, respectively. The mice were killed at 10 min and 7 days
p.i. and kidneys were then collected and ﬁxed by 4% formalin, followed by
dehydration and parafﬁn embedding. The ﬁxed kidneys were then sectioned into
4 μm slices. To image these ultrasmall gold clusters under bright-ﬁeld optical
microscopy, silver staining was used to enhance their size and contrast. Before silver
staining, the slices were dewaxed using xylene and then dried in a laboratory oven at
65 °C. Each kidney slice was incubated in silver staining solution containing 40 µl
0.1 M silver nitrate and 40 µl 0.2 g l–1 hydroquinone for 30 min. The silver-stained
slices were washed with ultrapure water and dried in the laboratory oven at
65 °C, again. The ﬁnal samples were imaged with an IX71 optical microscope in
bright-ﬁeld mode.
EM images of the glomerulus of mice after i.v. injection of Au18SG14 and
Au25SG18. The mice were killed and ﬁxed by transcardial perfusion at the 10 min
time point after i.v. injection of Au25SG18 (∼0.7 mmol l–1, 300 µl), Au18SG14
(∼0.7 mmol l–1, 300 µl) or PBS (300 µl, control). Brieﬂy, 10 ml heparinized normal
saline (as initial perfusant) was used to remove the whole-body blood under a
perfusion speed of 75 ml h–1, then 10 ml 4% paraformaldehyde/PBS ﬁxative, as
second perfusant, was injected to ﬁx the whole animals at the same perfusion speed.
After ﬁxation perfusion, the kidneys were collected and cut into 1 mm3 pieces, which
were then immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde/PBS in scintillation vials. Fixation was
continued by immersion with agitation at 4 °C for 24–72 h. The kidneys were then
rinsed with 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer and post-ﬁxed with 1% osmium
tetroxide and 0.8% potassium ferricyanide in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer for
1.5 h at room temperature, followed by rinsing with water and en bloc staining with
4% uranyl acetate in 50% ethanol for 2 h. The kidney tissues were then dehydrated
with increasing concentrations of ethanol, transitioned into resin with propylene
oxide, inﬁltrated with Embed-812 resin and polymerized in a 60 °C oven overnight.
The blocks were then sectioned with a diamond knife (Diatome) on a Leica Ultracut
6 ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems) and collected onto grids, followed by
silver-staining with the same method as described above. Finally, EM images were
taken using a Tecnai G2 spirit transmission electron microscope.
Glycocalyx binding test of Au18SG14 and Au25SG18. To test whether these AuNCs
chemically bind to the glycocalyx, we incubated Au18SG14 and Au25SG18 with a 10%
(vol/vol) major component of glycocalyx-heparan sulfate (HS) proteoglycan from
Sigma-Aldrich in PBS at 37 °C for 30 min and 24 h, respectively. To identify the
colourless HS proteoglycan band, HS proteoglycan incubated Au18SG14 and
Au25SG18 were stained by 10% (vol/vol) Coomassie Brilliant Blue 250 (CBB 250). All
the samples were analysed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis using the Mini-sub cell
GT gel electrophoresis system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). The amount of gold in each
band was quantiﬁed using ICP-MS.
In situ blood vessel imaging. The BALB/c mice were placed on the imaging station
of an IX-71 microscope (Olympus) under isoﬂurane anaesthesia after removal of
ear hair. Bright-ﬁeld images of the blood vessels in the ear were taken with a
×10 objective and Photon Max 512 CCD camera (Princeton Instruments). Once the
breathing rate of the mice was controlled at ∼16–20 breaths per minute, the mice
were injected with Au18SG14 (∼0.7 mmol l–1, 300 µl) or Au25SG18 (∼0.7 mmol l–1,
300 µl) through the tail vein (n = 3 for Au18SG14; n = 3 for Au25SG18). Fluorescence
images were taken with a ×10 objective under Hg-lamp excitation
(ex. 480/40 nm; em. 700 Long Path; dichroic mirror, 560 nm; exposure time, 2 s)
immediately after injection.

Pharmacokinetics study. BALB/c mice (n = 3 for each nanocluster) were
intravenously injected with Au10-11SG10-11, Au15SG13, Au18SG14 and Au25SG18,
Au22SG16, Au22SG18 (∼100 µmol l–1, 100 µl), respectively, followed by collection of
∼30 µl blood samples retro-orbitally at 2, 5, 10, 30 min, 1, 3, 5, 8, 12 and 24 h and
48 h p.i. The blood samples were weighed and completely lysed in 1 ml freshly made

to 10 ml using ultrapure water and transferred into 15 ml plastic centrifuge tubes,

Tumour-targeting efﬁciency study. For tumour implantation, the human breast
cancer cell line MCF-7 (purchased from ATCC) was cultured in minimum essential
medium (MEM) with 10% (vol/vol) FBS and 1% (vol/vol) penicillin–streptomycin
at 37 °C in a humidiﬁed atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The cell suspension
(in MEM with 10% (vol/vol) FBS) was then mixed 2:1 (vol/vol) with matrix gel and
injected subcutaneously (upper) near the mammary fat pad (MFP) area of the nude
mouse with a volume of 100 µl dense suspension (containing ∼1 × 106 cells) for each
mouse. The tumour was allowed to grow for ∼2 weeks and reach a size of ∼6–8 mm
before the study. Au10-11SG10-11, Au15SG13, Au18SG14 and Au25SG18 (∼100 µmol l–1,
100 µl) were intravenously injected into MCF-7 tumour-bearing nude mice (n = 3),
respectively. The tumours were collected and weighed after 24 h p.i. The amount of
gold in the tumour tissues (%ID g−1) was determined by ICP-MS.
Biodistribution study. To quantify the distributions of Au10-11SG10-11, Au15SG13,
Au18SG14 and Au25SG18 in the tissues and organs over time, the mice were killed at
12 h p.i. (the whole blood was perfused before the mice were killed), 7 days and
1 month after i.v. injection of Au10-11SG10-11, Au15SG13, Au18SG14 and Au25SG18
(∼100 µmol l–1, 100 µl), respectively (n = 3). The major organs and tissues were then
collected and measured by ICP-MS to determine the gold concentration (%ID g−1).
Statistical analysis. Data are reported as mean ± s.d. Differences between groups
were compared by Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for
the results shown in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs 3 and 5. A P value of <0.05 was
considered statistically signiﬁcant (NS, not signiﬁcant; *P < 0.05, signiﬁcant;
**P < 0.005, highly signiﬁcant). Investigators conducting the experiments were not
blinded. Dixon’s Q test was used for the identiﬁcation and rejection of outliers in the
data analysis.

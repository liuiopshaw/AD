A B S T R A C T
This study aimed to mask ﬂuconazole (FLU) taste and improve its rheological properties by an eﬃcient process
of cyclodextrin complexation. For this, hot-melt extrusion (HME) was used to obtain extrudates composed of
FLU, hydroxypropylcellulose, and one of two diﬀerent cyclodextrins (β-cyclodextrin or hydroxypropyl-β-cy-
clodextrin) maintaining the drug:cyclodextrin molar ratio at 1:0.3 or 1:0.2, respectively. Samples were char-
acterized by physicochemical tests, palatability using e-tongue and antifungal assays. Drug stability was pre-
served after HME, according to spectroscopy test (correlation coeﬃcient > 0.9) and HPLC-assay (100–107%).
Flowability was improved in HME systems with compressibility of < 12%. Similarly, ﬂoodability exhibited
signiﬁcant enhancement (dispersibility < 10%). Whereas extrudates of FLU containing only the polymeric
matrix led to a slow drug dissolution eﬃciency (18.6%) and a partial drug taste masking; extrudates containing
cyclodextrin accelerated FLU dissolution (dissolution eﬃciency approx. 30%) and provided a complete drug
taste masking. Moreover, HME process could produce drug complexes with high complexation eﬃciency and
preserve its antifungal activity.
1. Introduction
Immediate release oral tablets containing ﬂuconazole (FLU) are the
most common dosage form used in the treatment of fungal infections
worldwide (Ahmad, Khan, Khan, & Manzoor, 2010). Yet, pharmaceu-
tical technology could ameliorate antifungal therapy by developing
more convenient and eﬀective administration forms, e.g., buccal pre-
parations with masked drug taste to be used by pediatric patients with
oropharyngeal candidiasis (Saito, Watanabe, Igarashi, Ogata, & Edo,
2000; Shawahna, 2016; Steele, Russo, & Thomas, 2006). Indeed, several
studies have proposed FLU modiﬁed release systems with important
therapeutics advances (Gratieri, Gelfuso, De Freitas, Rocha, & Lopez,
2011;
Moazeni
et
al.,
2016;
Nirmala,
Allanki,
Mukherjee,
&
Chandrasekaran, 2013). However, these systems present clear limita-
tions for industrial use such as scale-up diﬃculties, stability problems
and high costs of production.
One approach to improve biopharmaceutics properties of FLU is the
inclusion complex with cyclodextrin (Ahad, Sreeramulu, Padmaja,
Reddy, & Prakash, 2011; Li, Zhang, Zhou, Guan, & Zhang, 2016; Li and
Gao et al., 2016; Upadhyay & Kumar, 2009; Yurtdaş, Demirel, & Genç,
2011). Nevertheless, the amount of cyclodextrin used in the formula-
tions is one of the most challenging aspects in obtaining inclusion
complexes in solid state. Because of the complex labile behavior,
complexation is easily undone after common drying methods such as
freeze-drying and spray-drying. Thus, large amounts of cyclodextrins
are needed, increasing administrable pharmaceutical unit weight and
often m+aking the industrial use unfeasible (Cunha-Filho, Dacunha-
Marinho, Torres-Labandeira, Martinez-Pacheco, & Landin, 2007).
Hot-melt extrusion (HME) is a technological process for developing
solid dispersions that has been capable of producing accelerated
(Malaquias et al., 2017), sustained or vectorized drug delivery systems
(Thiry, Krier, & Evrard, 2015; Tiwari, Patil, & Repka, 2016). Remark-
able industrial advantages of this technique includes: avoidance of or-
ganic solvents; possibility of continuous manufacturing; high uni-
formity of extrudates and easy processing and scale-up (Saerens,
Vervaet, Remon, & De Beer, 2013; Thiry et al., 2015). The palatability

HME has demonstrated to be an alternative method to produce in-

this technique (Thiry et al., 2017). Due to the high proportion of cy-

patient oral administration, especially when high dose of the drug is
demanded.
The goal of the study presented in this paper was to produce solid
inclusion complexes of FLU with cyclodextrins to mask drug taste and
to improve its rheological properties. Here, we challenged the eﬃciency
of the HME process in promoting the formation of inclusion complexes
by associating equimass mixtures of drug-cyclodextrin. β-cyclodextrin
(βCD) and 2-hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (HPβCD) were selected
once the cavity size of beta-cyclodextrin presents the best ﬁt for en-
capsulation of FLU (Li and Zhang et al., 2016; Li and Gao et al., 2016;
Upadhyay & Kumar, 2009).
2. Material and methods
2.1. Material
FLU (306.3 Da, lot FLP0490512, 99%) was purchased from Galena
Química e Farmacêutica Ltda (Brazil). The polymer Klucel ELF (lot
40915, 2-hydroxypropyl ether cellulose, HPC) and the cyclodextrins
βCD (Cavamax W7 Pharma, 1135 Da, lot 662911) and HPβCD (Cavasol
W7 HP, 1410 Da, lot A1306A0054, substitution degree of 4.1–5.1) were
kindly gifted by Ashland Specialty Ingredients (USA). Hydroxypropyl
methylcellulose (HPMC; METHOCEL) was donated by Dow (USA) and
sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) was purchased by Synth
(Brazil). All other chemicals and solvents were of analytical grade.
2.2. Preformulation studies
A preliminary solubility study to select the polymer matrix and its
proportion to be used in FLU HME formulations was performed. Three
hydrophilic cellulosic polymers were tested: HPC, CMC and HPMC.
Hydroethanolic solutions of binary mixture of drug and polymer in the
proportions of 25:75 and 75:25 (w/w) were prepared and oven-dried at
a temperature of approximately 120 °C until complete removal of the
solvent. The presence of precipitated FLU in the systems was then
veriﬁed by optical microscopy using a stereoscope (Laborana/SZ,
China).
2.3. HME processing
Samples were prepared initially combining FLU with the polymer
HPC in binary systems (25:75 w/w), and then in ternary systems con-
taining

βCD or

2.4. Extrudates characterization
2.4.1. Drug content
A reverse-phase HPLC-UV method was used for quantiﬁcation of
FLU as follow: 50 μL of the samples were injected into a liquid chro-
matograph (model LC-20AD, Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a C18
column (150 mm × 4.6 mm, Shim pack, Shimadzu, Japan) maintained
at 40 °C with detection at 261 nm. The mobile phase consisted of a
mixture of water and acetonitrile (80:20 v/v) and the ﬂow rate was
settled to 1.0 mL/min. The validation of the method was performed in
accordance to FDA guidelines. The method was selective against all
excipients used and was linear (r = 0.999) according to the standard
curves prepared with the concentrations of 3, 10, 20, 40, 70, 100, 150
and 200 μg/mL.
2.4.2. Water determination
Water content of the samples was determined by thermogravimetric
analyses performed using a DTG-60H (Shimadzu, Japan). The analyses
were carried out in platinum pans under nitrogen atmosphere (ﬂow rate
of 50 mL/min). Samples were heated from room temperature up to
105 °C at a heating rate of 20 °C/min, followed by an isothermal step for
3600 s (United States Pharmacopeia and National Formulary, 2017).
2.4.3. Morphological analysis
Morphological characteristics of the individual compounds, their
physical mixtures and correspondent extrudates were assessed with
both a stereoscope (Laborana/SZ, China) and with a scanning electronic
microscope (SEM −Jeol, JSM-7001F, Japan). Prior to SEM analyses,
samples were metallized with gold.
2.4.4. Diﬀerential scanning calorimetry (DSC)
DSC curves were obtained in a DSC-60 (Shimadzu, Japan) using
aluminum-sealed pans. Analyses were performed under nitrogen at-
mosphere (ﬂow rate of 50 mL/min) at a heating rate of 10 °C/min from
30 to 300 °C. Tests were carried out in approximately 3 mg samples on
the individual compounds, their physical mixtures and the produced
extrudates. FLU original crystalline form percentage in the HME sys-
tems was calculated based on melting enthalpy (Cunha-Filho et al.,
2007).
2.4.5. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)
The FTIR spectra were recorded on a Varian 640-IR FTIR spectro-
meter (Varian/640-IR, USA) between 4000 and 400 cm−1 at an optical
resolution of 4 cm−1 using an ATR imaging accessory. Tests were car-
ried out on the individual compounds, their physical mixtures and
produced extrudates. The resulting spectra were analyzed considering
the correlation coeﬃcient (r) between physical mixtures and HME
systems based on the bands corresponding to the functional groups of
the drug and the excipients using the Essential FTIR software (Operant
LLC, USA) (Mecozzi, Pietroletti, & Monakhova, 2016).
2.4.6. X-ray powder diﬀraction (XRPD)
Miniﬂex diﬀractometer 600 (Rigaku, Japan) equipped with high
speed
scintillation
detector
and
graphite

(λ = 1.5418 Å) was used to perform the X-ray diﬀraction analyses of
the samples. The diﬀraction patterns were obtained from individual
compounds, their physical mixtures and the extrudates using angles
between 5° and 60° (θ–2θ).
2.5. Rheological characterization
A PT-N powder characteristic tester (Hosokawa Micron Powder
Systems Co., USA) was used to perform rheological measurements in
triplicate for each sample (Schuck, Dolivet, & Jeantet, 2012). Com-
pressibility was determined using the aerated and packed density data.
For aerated density analyses, the powder was sieved through a vi-
brating sieve and chute to ﬁll a volumetric measuring cup. The mass
was weighed to calculate the aerated density. The cup containing the
same sample was dropped 180 times in order to obtain the packed bulk
density. Compressibility (%) was determined by the diﬀerence between
packed density and aerated density divided by the packed density and
multiplied by 100.
Dispersibility was assessed using 10 g of each sample. The samples

2.6. Dissolution rate
Dissolution proﬁles of FLU raw material and its extrudates were
performed in a dissolutor Ethik model 299 (Brazil) using 900 mL of
0.1 mol/L HCl medium at 37 °C, and the apparatus 1 operating at
75 rpm. Experiments were performed in quadruplicate under “sink”
conditions using samples containing 150 mg of FLU placed in gelatin
capsules. Dissolution proﬁles were evaluated using dissolution eﬃ-
ciency at 30 min (DE30) (Khan & Rhodes, 1975).
FLU determination was performed using a UV–vis Lambda XLS
spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, USA) set at 260 nm. The speciﬁcity of
the method was evaluated comparing the absorbances of FLU alone at
the concentration of 200 μg/mL and in the presence of HPC, βCD and
HPβCD, in the same concentration, in sextuplicate. No statistical in-
terference with excipients was identiﬁed according to ANOVA (sig-
niﬁcance level of 0.05). Linearity correlation coeﬃcient was 0.9988
with slope diﬀerent from zero and residues randomly distributed
without tendency.
2.7. In vitro taste masking evaluation (Astree e-tongue)
The taste masking of the samples was evaluated, applying an Astree
e-tongue system equipped with an Alpha MOS sensor set #2 composed
by seven sensors and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode (Alpha-MOS,
France). Conditioning and calibration were performed before the ana-
lyses. Equivalent amounts of 100 mg of FLU of each extrudate were
dispersed into 50 mL of distilled water, gently shaken for 30 s and ﬁl-
tered using ﬁber glass of 1 μm, to assay electronic tongue samples.
Acquisition times were set at 120 s, at 25 °C. Ten replicates of each
sample were measured and the average values between 80 s and 120 s.
2.8. Antifungal activity following the microdilution method
Microdilution tests were performed in triplicate for each sample
using FLU concentration ranges from 1.6 to 0.0008 mg/mL. Positive
control of FLU and HME formulations were tested against Candida
krusei strains INCQS 40095 ATCC 34135 and Candida albicans INCQS
40175 ATCC 14053, both provided by FIOCRUZ (Brazil) according to
M27-A3 (CLSI, 2008). Microdilution plates were incubated at 36.5 °C
for 18 h and analyzed using an ELISA test (Polaris model, Celer, Brazil)
with detection at 630 nm.
2.9. Antifungal activity following the disc diﬀusion method
This assay was performed in triplicate for each sample according to
the M44-A2 with modiﬁcations (CLSI, 2009). Plates of Müeller-Hinton
agar supplemented with glucose 2% containing perforations of 6 mm of
diameter were spread with the same inoculums described at section 2.8.
Fifty μg of FLU as supplied and an equivalent amount of HME for-
mulations were placed inside the perforations and incubated at 36.5 °C
for 48 h. The inhibition halos obtained were measured using a pachy-
meter.
2.10. Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM,
USA). Rheological parameters (compressibility and dispersibility), dis-
solution results (DE30) and antifungal activity following the disc dif-
fusion method (inhibition halos) were expressed as the mean ±
standard deviation. Statistical signiﬁcance test was performed with
ANOVA one-way and diﬀerence between mean values was considered
signiﬁcant at p ≤0.05. Tukey’s multiple comparison test was applied to
discriminate among the means.
Data from Astree e-tongue were processed and analyzed using an
Alpha software 14.0 (Alpha-MOS, France), which allows representing
the responses of the seven sensors in a two-dimensional map, namely
principal component analysis (PCA), based on multivariate statistic al-
gorithms. Based on the repeatability of measurement (RSD = 0.08 to
1.55), the method was considered ﬁtted for the study (Li and Zhang
et al., 2016; Li and Gao et al., 2016). The distances between extrudates
and the placebo and between extrudates and the unprocessed drug were
calculated as indicative of the taste masking power of each formulation.
For the antifungal activity by microdiluition, the inhibitory eﬃ-
ciency of formulations was evaluated by their IC50 values (drug con-
centration required for a growth inhibition of 50%). They were de-
termined by ﬁtting experimental data to a sigmoidal curve with
variable slope using GraphPad Prism 6.01 (USA).

drug embedded into the matrix at a drug:polymer ratio of 25:75 (w/w),
producing a homogeneous system without precipitate. Drug:polymer
ratio of 75:25 (w/w) for both polymers revealed an intense FLU pre-
cipitation for both polymers (Supplementary data 1). Once HPC glass

3.2. Physicochemical characterization
All samples demonstrated appropriate extrusion behavior, with
continuous extrusion ﬂow, and without any signal of decomposition
based on HPLC determinations (Table 1). Drug content was within the
usual acceptable limits for pharmacopeial assays and the moisture
content of the samples was between 2 and 3% (Table 1). Processing
temperature used was ﬁxed at the range of 137–140 °C, considering that
this temperature range is above both the drug melting and the glass
transition of the HPC, and that extrusion in temperatures above 140 °C
caused signiﬁcant darkening of the samples, which indicates their de-
composition.
Binary extrudates presented translucent aspect, suggesting complete
drug dispersion into the polymeric matrix, and the ﬁlaments exhibited a
slightly yellowish aspect, which is characteristic of HPC (Table 1). On
the other hand, ternary systems containing cyclodextrins showed an
opaque appearance, which indicates there was an immiscible phase in
the extrudate. According to photomicrographs (Fig. 1), while in phy-
sical mixtures each component is individually identiﬁed – crystalline
drug and amorphous excipients – after HME process homogeneous
products were obtained with dense aspect and no pores.
Diﬀractograms for FLU as supplied showed a typical crystalline
proﬁle with main peaks at 10.1°, 16.7° and 20.2° (in 2Ɵ), whereas an
amorphous behavior was predominantly observed for the formulations
after HME process (Fig. 2a). Although the most representative peaks of
the original drug crystalline phase remained detected in some ex-
trudates, their intensities did not diﬀer from the baseline noise
(Fig. 2a), conﬁrming the drug amorphization after HME.
According to our DSC results, drug-melting peak occurred at 142 °C
(Fig. 2b) and this melting event was still observed in the correspondent
physical mixtures with minor changes. Instead, FLU-HPC extrudate
showed an accentuated shifted of the drug-melting peak to lower
temperatures together with a marked reduction in its enthalpy
(Fig. 2b). Based on enthalpy data, the crystallinity degree of this sample
was reduced to 41.9%. This result indicated a signiﬁcant concentration
of the drug in its amorphous form is dispersed in the HPC along with the
FLU original crystalline phase.
In occasion of ternary systems containing cyclodextrin, the crys-
talline fraction of the drug completely disappeared. DSC has been de-
scribed as a more sensitive tool than XRPD in determining drug/ex-

(Bruni, Berbenni, Milanese, Girella, & Marini, 2010; Marini et al.,

3.3. Rheological characterization
The rheological behavior of a solid is multidimensional and de-
pendent of multiples powder characteristics (Alves, Sá-Barreto, Souza,
& Cunha-Filho, 2011). Several processes in pharmaceutical manu-
facturing involve powder manipulation, like blending, sieving, trans-
ferring, storage, ﬂuidization and compaction; hence, reaching an ap-
propriate powder ﬂow is quite relevant during the formulation
development (Gupta, Sharma, & Pathak, 2013).
In our study, we used the compressibility to address ﬂowability of
the samples (Fig. 4). The extrudates produced by HME have improved
the drug ﬂow, which is in agreement with several works that use this
technique (Alshetaili et al., 2016; Dalziel, Nauka, Zhang, Kothari, & Xie,
2012; Maddineni et al., 2015). Extrudates compressibility showed re-
sults almost three times better compared to the drug (Fig. 4).
Samples ﬂoodability was determined by the dispersibility, which is
a direct measure of the powders tendency to scatter in the air (Schuck
et al., 2012). In a production line of solids, a high dispersibility of the
powders increases the risk of cross-contamination and should be
avoided. HME is a powerful process for solid densiﬁcation in which the
polymer works as a thermal bonder, dismissing the necessity of com-
pressing agents in formulations (Maniruzzaman et al., 2012).

A signiﬁcant improvement in dispersibility was obtained in the FLU
HME preparations compared to the drug as supplied (Fig. 4). Dispersion
can be controlled by modifying the characteristics of the particles
(Schuck et al., 2012) and, in fact, FLU HME powders showed to be very
dense and with a smooth surface, favoring a laminar fall of the particles
without spreading.
The most common methods for producing drug inclusion complexes
with cyclodextrins in solid-phase use drying techniques such as spray-
drying and freeze-drying. Generally, such techniques lead to products
with low density, cohesive and with poor ﬂow properties. In addition,
cyclodextrins
themself
have
inappropriate
ﬂowability
(Garcia-

additional technological advantage in the preparation of cyclodextrin
solid inclusion complexes, once rheology of the powders was sig-
niﬁcantly improved.
3.4. Dissolution proﬁle
“Sink” conditions obtained with acid dissolution medium were used
to evidence possible diﬀerences of FLU dissolution rate in HME systems
(FDA, 1997), which allows examining more clearly the possible for-
mation of inclusion complexes and their inﬂuence on the drug dis-
solution kinetics. FLU raw material in its powder form showed a rapid
dissolution proﬁle, dissolving > 90% in the ﬁrst 15 min of experiment
(Fig. 5). FLU HME binary system (FLU-HPC) produced a compact ma-
trix that even presenting the drug partially in amorphous form led to a
slow release of the FLU, deeply modifying the drug dissolution proﬁle
(Fig. 5). In this sample, less than 20% of the dose was released in 15 min
and just around 80% of the drug was dissolved after 2 h. The high in-
teraction of the drug with the polymeric matrix may have linked its
dissolution to the matrix erosion; thus, drug dissolution occurs as the
HME matrix is being dissolved (Punčochová et al., 2015).
On the other hand, the addition of cyclodextrin into polymeric
systems caused signiﬁcantly changes in drug release compared to the
binary system FLU-HPC (p < 0.005), accelerating the FLU dissolution
(Fig. 5). Moreover, there was no statistical diﬀerence in DE30 between
the samples containing the two cyclodextrins (FLU-HPC-βCD and FLU-
HPC-HPβCD, p = 0.95). The presence of cyclodextrin in the HME ma-
trix modiﬁed the interaction of the drug with the polymer and conse-
quently modulated the FLU dissolution rate, as previously described for
other drugs (Sá-Barreto et al., 2013). This eﬀect reinforces the expected
formation of FLU inclusion complexes with cyclodextrins using HME.
3.5. In vitro taste masking
E-tongue sensors imitate taste bud on human tongue by initiating
changes in electric potentials that can be compared to physiological
action (Maniruzzaman et al., 2012). This in vitro assay presents a high
correlation with the human sensory test and it is until 100 times more
sensitive
for
bitter
taste
than
human
panel
(Maniruzzaman
&
Douroumis, 2015). According to the results obtained in this study, e-
tongue was capable of identifying taste diﬀerence between the for-
mulations and the FLU raw material. Moreover, diﬀerences between the
extrudates themselves were observed, which corroborates with the
physicochemical ﬁndings discussed before.
In PCA chart of Fig. 6, a successful taste masking is achieved when
the formulations signals are far from the drug and close from the pla-
cebo. In this sense, the binary system (FLU-HPC) kept a reasonable
distance from the drug being able to improve the preparation palat-
ability. However, although this sample was close to placebo, they are
not in completely coincident regions. On the other hand, ternary sam-
ples containing cyclodextrins (FLU-HPC-βCD and FLU-HPC-HPβCD)

4. Conclusions
This paper described a more eﬃcient approach for the production of
solid cyclodextrin inclusion complexes of FLU by means of the use of
HME, in which the complexation process was 3-fold more eﬀective than
traditional methods of complexation that uses an equimolar ratio of
drug/polymer. Mechanical extrusion of formulations in temperature
ranges after polymer glass transition and drug melting possibly creates
an environment capable of dissolving the cyclodextrin and favoring the
formation of the inclusion complexes. Furthermore, this work presented
for the ﬁrst time a palatable FLU system produced by HME with po-
tential for intraoral drug release in which the use of minimal cyclo-
dextrin concentrations in the ﬁnal formulation brings evident ad-
vantages in cost and manufacturing feasibility.

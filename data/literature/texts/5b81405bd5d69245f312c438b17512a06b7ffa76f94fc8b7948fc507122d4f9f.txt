Cu(I) complexes with aggregation-induced
emission for enhanced photodynamic antibacterial
application†
Zhongxiang Zuo,‡ Xinxin Pan,‡ Ge Yang, Yuemin Zhang, Xingwen Liu, Jinrun Zha
and Xun Yuan
*
This communication reports the design of aggregation-induced
emission (AIE)-featured PEG-condensed Cu(I)-p-MBA aggregates
(PCuA). Beneﬁting from the AIE trait and intrinsic antibacterial
property of Cu species, the as-developed PCuA exhibits enhanced
photodynamic antibacterial activities against broad-spectrum bac-
teria, providing a paradigm in the design of novel antibacterial
agents.
Developing high-eﬃcacy and broad-spectrum antibacterial
materials is a perpetual task for chemists and materials scien-
tists because of the intensive demands of public health and
environmental sanitation.1–5 Among conventional antibacterial
agents, metal nanomaterials as a class of inorganic antibacter-
ial agents are very attractive in terms of wide applicability and
broad-spectrum antibacterial activity.6–9 For example, ultra-
small coinage metal nanoclusters (e.g., Ag and Au NCs) as a
novel class of functional nanomaterials10–27 have been demon-
strated to be eﬃcient in killing bacteria based on their con-
sumption via metal ion release or cell internalization.28–30
However, the practical application of these antibacterial agents
is usually curtailed by the high expense and self-consumption-
induced short eﬃcacy.31,32 To address the issue of short
eﬃcacy, researchers developed aggregation-induced emission
(AIE)-featured
Au
NCs
as
photodynamic
antibacterial
agents,33,34 which rely on the continuous photocatalytic pro-
duction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that is hazardous to
bacteria, providing a feasible way to realize long-lasting anti-
bacterial function. It is noteworthy that the AIE trait35,36 of
metal NCs is significantly beneficial for their photodynamic
antibacterial performance due to the visible-light-driven elec-
tron/hole separation, strong photoluminescence (PL), large
Stokes shift, and long PL lifetime (in μs scale).33,37,38 On this
basis, we hypothesized that if we can design an AIE-featured
Cu nanomaterial, we may achieve low-cost yet long-lasting
photodynamic
bacterial
killing,
promoting
the
practical
deployment in downstream antibacterial applications.
Herein we report the design of an AIE-featured PEG-con-
densed Cu(I)-p-MBA aggregate (PCuA) for enhanced photo-
dynamic antibacterial application (here PEG denotes polyethyl-
ene glycol, while p-MBA denotes 4-mercaptobenzoic acid).
Firstly, it was found that the Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes display
interesting AIE attributes, i.e., the loose-structured non-lumi-
nescent Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes could be condensed into
highly luminescent ones by decreasing the solvent polarity. On
this basis, we innovatively employed long-chained PEG mole-
cules with a molecular weight of 10 000 Da to confine the Cu
(I)-p-MBA complexes into a stable aggregation state, which
eliminates the solvent-dependent AIE influence but makes the
formation of stable yet luminescent PCuA in water possible. In
addition, this strategy has been demonstrated to be versatile,
which is reflected not only by the fact that other macro-
molecules such as PVA (polyvinyl alcohol 1788) and chitosan
can also be applied to confine the Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes to
form AIE-featured ones, but also the fabrication of other
ligand-protected Cu(I) complexes with AIE. Benefiting from the
AIE trait and intrinsic antibacterial property of Cu species, the
as-developed PCuA exhibits enhanced photodynamic antibac-
terial activities against broad-spectrum bacteria due to the
photodynamic production of ROS, providing a paradigm in the
design of novel antibacterial agents. In addition, the photo-
dynamic ROS production pathway of the PCuA was also eluci-
dated, which deepens the fundamental understanding of the
antibacterial mechanisms.

form a yellowish solution of Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes (insets of

As shown in Fig. S2,† the sample solution (pH = ∼9.0) is
transparent with a colloidal hydrodynamic size of ∼0.21 μm,
indicating the dispersive state of Cu(I) complexes. In contrast,
the solution (pH = ∼3.0) shows turbidity with a colloidal
hydrodynamic size of ∼11.6 μm, suggesting the aggregated
state of Cu(I) complexes. When the pH was adjusted from 9.0
to 3.0, the surface Zeta potential (ζ) of Cu(I) complexes
changed from −27.6 mV to −14.63 mV, which shows that the
carboxyl group of p-MBA ligands on the complexes was depro-
tonated at such a high pH and caused negative-electrostatic
repulsion between Cu(I) complexes, leading to dispersive col-
loidal solutions of Cu(I) complexes.39,42 However, similar to
the Au(I)-thiolate complexes reported by Luo et al.,39 the Cu(I)-
p-MBA complexes could be aggregated together by adjusting
the pH = ∼3.0 and concurrently decreasing the solvent polarity
via varying the ethanol-to-water ratio, which leads to an intri-
guing AIE phenomenon with strong red emission at 640 nm
(Fig. S3† and Fig. 1c, d).
Specifically, when the pH = ∼3.0, the surface carboxyl
groups of the Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes tend to be protonated,
which enables the π–π and Cu(I)–Cu(I) interactions between Cu
(I)-p-MBA complexes to drive their aggregation into relatively
loose entities.43,44 In contrast, increasing the volume fraction
of ethanol ( fethanol) in the reaction solvent could condense the
Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes into compact ones, which is supported
by the decreased Rayleigh scattering and hydrodynamic size
(Fig. S4†). It is documented that the introduction of less polar
ethanol (dielectric constant of ∼25.3) into polar water (dielec-
tric constant of ∼80.1) could disrupt the hydration layer of the
M(I)-thiolate complexes (here M denotes metal),39 and further
endow the surface of the complexes with neutral charges, pro-
moting the aggregation of the complexes into a smaller one
with a condensed structure and less Rayleigh scattering.39
Consequently, the highly aggregated Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes
could emit red PL at 640 nm under UV irradiation due to the

In addition, it is found that the generation of the AIE pro-
perties of the Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes is not limited to the trig-
gering of ethanol, and other non-polar solvents such as metha-
nol, glycerol, DMF, and acetone can also induce the AIE of
Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes with intensive red emission (Fig. S6†).
Moreover, besides p-MBA, other water-soluble ligands such as
glutathione are also capable of forming AIE-featured Cu(I)
complexes (Fig. S7†). However, both the AIE and the aggre-
gated structure of Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes are highly dependent
on the variation of solvent polarities, which is not friendly for
relevant applications where AIE-featured nanomaterials with a
stable structure and a pure water environment are required,
prompting us to develop AIE-featured Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes
with a stable structure for convenient use. Inspired by the
interfacial chemistry,47 PEG polymer molecules were selected
to interact with the Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes at an elevated
temperature (80 °C) and pH = ∼3.0, which allowed PEG mole-
cules to confine the complexes into a stable aggregation state
through their bulky steric hindrance (Fig. 2a and Fig. S8†).
Consequently, the highly aggregated Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes
could be in situ formed in the intra-space of PEG, resulting in
the generation of the AIE attribute in pure water with the same
red emission (640 nm, Fig. 2b) as that of solvent-induced AIE-
featured complexes (Fig. 1d).47 A control experiment discloses
that the absence of PEG would not make the Cu(I)-p-MBA com-
plexes emit PL in pure water (Fig. 2b), confirming the eﬀective
influence of PEG on the aggregation of the complexes. In
addition to PEG, using other polymer molecules such as chito-
san and PVA can also help achieve the condensation of Cu(I)-p-
MBA complexes for AIE generation in water, demonstrating the
robust applicability of the newly developed strategy in the syn-
thesis of AIE-featured metal nanomaterials (Fig. S9†).
It is noteworthy that the PL lifetime decay profile reveals
that the as-formed PCuA (i.e., PEG-condensed Cu(I)-p-MBA
complex aggregates) exhibits phosphorescence with a long PL
lifetime of 0.812 μs (Fig. 2c), which may be caused by the long-
time consumption of inter-system crossing (ISC). It is known
that copper as a heavy metal could enhance the spin–orbit
coupling of electrons and simultaneously relieve the spin for-
bidden eﬀect, i.e., copper can promote the transition from S1
(excited singlet state) to T3 (excited triplet state) of the PCuA.47
Moreover, we also analysed the excitation spectrum of the
PCuA and found that the PCuA could be eﬀectively excited by a
wide-spectrum light from 340 nm to 500 nm (Fig. S10†), which
indicates good visible light-responsive properties, facilitating
visible light-driven photodynamic antibacterial application.
Benefiting from the intriguing AIE trait and the intrinsic
antibacterial activity of Cu species, the as-developed PCuA
displays excellent photodynamic antibacterial performance
against Gram-positive S. aureus, Gram-negative E. coli and
methicillin resistant S. aureus (MRSA). As shown in Fig. 3a and
Fig. S11,† the PCuA ([Cu] = 0.4 mM in the medium) under air
atmosphere achieves high photodynamic antibacterial activi-
ties of 97.67 ± 1.23% for S. aureus, 97.83 ± 0.42% for E. coli
and 97.56 ± 0.79% for MRSA with the visible light irradiation
of 60 min (100 mW cm−2), which are higher than the values of
those treated with the PCuA but without light (<86%). In
addition, the control experiment excluded the possibility that
the light itself is the main killer of bacteria, which is sup-
ported by its negligible antibacterial activity (<1%). Taken
together, two major conclusions could be obtained: (1) the
PCuA has decent antibacterial activity even without the light
irradiation, which originates from the intrinsic antibacterial
property of the Cu species; (2) the AIE trait of PCuA could con-
tribute to the enhancement of its photodynamic antibacterial

To uncover the photodynamic antibacterial mechanism, we
examined the role of oxygen (O2) in bacterial killing by bub-
bling O2 or N2 into the system during the photodynamic anti-
bacterial process, and found that the bactericidal activities of
the PCuA are further improved to 99.47 ± 0.14% for S. aureus,
99.58 ± 0.42% for E. coli, and 99.27 ± 0.44% for MRSA under
an O2 atmosphere, but decreased to 84.42 ± 1.93% for
S. aureus, 85.5 ± 1.62% for E. coli, and 83.91 ± 3.01% for MRSA
under a N2 atmosphere (Fig. 3a). This result clearly suggests
the participation of O2 in the photodynamic antibacterial
process, which probably contributes to the photocatalytic for-
mation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that are hazardous for
bacteria through oxidative damage of their nucleic acids and
proteins. Naturally, the ROS productivity of the PCuA was
assessed under diﬀerent conditions by using 2,7-dichlorohy-
drofluorescein (DCFH) dye as an indicator because the DCFH
can be oxidized by ROS to 2,7-dichlorofluorescein (DCF) that
could emit green PL emission at 525 nm with the excitation of
488 nm light.32 As shown in Fig. 3b, the ROS yield (∼0.34) of
the PCuA under light irradiation (without O2 bubbling) is one
time higher than that under the dark conditions (∼0.15),
which indicates that the higher antibacterial activity of the
PCuA under light irradiation is derived from the photo-
dynamic production of additional ROS. In addition, with the
light irradiation and O2 bubbling, the PCuA produces two
times more ROS than that under light irradiation (i.e., 1 vs.
0.34); with the light irradiation and N2 bubbling, the PCuA
produces less ROS than that under the conditions of darkness
and air exposure (0.09 vs. 0.15). These ROS levels are consistent
with the trend of the photodynamic antibacterial results of the
PCuA, further supporting the key role of ROS in bacterial
killing (Fig. 3b).
It is well known that the ROS includes •O2
−, H2O2, •OH, and
1O2,33,48 and thus we are curious about the specific ROS
species corresponding to bacterial killing in this system.
Therefore, we firstly studied the visible light harvest of the
PCuA by recording its UV-vis diﬀuse reflectance spectrum
(DRS). As shown in Fig. S12,† there are two absorption peaks
at 459 nm and 755 nm in the UV-vis DRS, revealing the good
ability of the PCuA in visible light harvest in a wide range of
400–750 nm. Under visible light irradiation, the soluble 3O2
(triplet oxygen) could transform into more reactive 1O2 (singlet
oxygen) by absorbing the energy transferred from the excited
PCuA.34 Furthermore, we also deciphered the valence band
(VB) and conductive band (CB) of the PCuA based on the
VB-XPS spectrum (Fig. S13†), which determines the pathway of
O2 reduction.32–34 Consequently, the VB and CB of the PCuA
are estimated to be 2.11 V vs. RHE and−0.58 V vs. RHE,
respectively (Fig. S13b, S14 and ESI Note I†). Such energy
bands of the PCuA allow the photodynamic reduction of O2 to
•O2
−and H2O2 by photoinduced electrons. Moreover, the VB
potential of the PCuA (+2.11 V vs. RHE) is higher than that of
holes oxidizing H2O to O2 (2H2O + 2h+ →O2 + 4H+, +0.82 V vs.
RHE, pH = 7), which means that the holes can oxidize H2O to
produce O2, providing O2 supply for photodynamic production
of ROS.49 Taken together, ROS species including 1O2, •O2
−, and
H2O2 are responsible for the photodynamic bacterial killing.
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images confirmed that
the PCuA could kill bacteria through utilizing these photo-
generated ROS to destroy the cell wall and membrane of bac-
teria, which is supported by the severely deformed mor-
phologies of Gram-negative E. coli and Gram-positive S. aureus
upon PCuA treatment under light irradiation (Fig. S15†).
The cytotoxicity of PCuA was examined on L929 cells using
MTT
(3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium
bromide) assay (Fig. 4a). Although 0.4 mM Cu2+ species shows
high cytotoxicity towards L929 cells with only 15.21 ± 13.25%
cell viability, the PCuA with the same Cu concentration exhi-
bits low cytotoxicity with 88.13 ± 10.34% L929 cell viability.
The marked diﬀerence in the cytotoxicity between Cu2+ species
and PCuA is probably due to the diﬀerent oxidation state of
the Cu component as well as the good passivation of p-MBA
ligands for PCuA. In addition, a Live/Dead viability test was
also performed on L929 cells. The cells that were stained green
were alive, while those stained red were dead with damaged
membranes. As seen in Fig. 4b, most of the L929 cells
remained alive when treated with PCuA ([Cu] = 0.4 mM). These
results suggest that the PCuA not only exhibits excellent anti-
bacterial activity but also has good biocompatibility.
Conclusions
In summary, we have successfully fabricated AIE-featured
PCuA for enhanced photodynamic antibacterial application.
The key aspect of this strategy is the use of bulky PEG polymer
molecules to confine the Cu(I)-p-MBA complexes into a stable

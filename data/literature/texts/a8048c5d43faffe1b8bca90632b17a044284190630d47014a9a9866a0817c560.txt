A B S T R A C T
Background: Integrating natural enzymes and nanomaterials exhibiting tailored enzyme-like activities is an 
effective strategy for the application of cascade reactions. It is essential to develop a highly efficient and robust 
glucose oxidase-catalase (GOx-CAT) cascade system featuring controllable enzyme activity, a reliable supply of 
oxygen, and improved stability for glucose depletion in cancer starvation therapy. However, the ambiguous 
relationship between structure and performance, and the difficulty in controlling enzyme-mimic activity, 
significantly hinder their broader application. Herein, the CAT-like activity of atomically precise Au25(MPA)18 
(MPA = 3-mercaptopropionic acid) nanoclusters (AuNCs) was modulated by incorporating N-acetyl-L-cysteine 
(NAC) in a series of ratio.

1. Introduction
Self-cascade, in which the product of upstream enzyme catalytic 
reaction can be directly used as substrate in the downstream reaction 
without the separation of intermediate [1,2], is commonly involved in 
multiple enzymes that are related to cell metabolism [3]. Compared to 
other methods to fight tumors, regulating metabolism is a therapeutic 
method with fewer side effects and significant therapeutic effects [4]. 
Given that glucose is the a main energy source for high-speed cancer cell 
metabolism [5] based on the warburg effect [6], cancer starvation 
therapy that deprives intratumoral glucose to disturb the glycolysis in 
cancer cells provides an alternative strategy that enable induce their 
death [7]. In this type of glucose-depleting strategy, the introduction of 
glucose oxidase (GOx), a natural enzyme could efficiently catalyze the 
oxidization of glucose into gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), 
thus regulating glycometabolism for achieving tumor starvation therapy 
and other related therapies [8]. However, GOx-mediated glucose con­
sumption is an oxygen (O2)-dependent process, leading to a restricted 
glucose-consuming efficacy in the hypoxic tumor, a typical feature of the 
tumor microenvironment (TME) [9]. To solve this dilemma, some efforts 
have been focused on achieving a self-cascade glucose-depleting reac­
tion by introducing catalysts such as catalase (CAT), which could cata­
lyze the decomposition of H2O2 into O2 to alleviate tumor hypoxia [10] 
and thus facilitate the glucose consumption [11]. However, the fragile 
nature of native enzymes, including their sensitivity to the environment 
due to the thermodynamic instability of their tertiary structure, is one of 
the major drawbacks that limit their application, especially in bio­
pharmaceutical and biomedicine areas [12]. Thus, developing an effi­
cient and robust GOx-CAT cascade system with controllable enzyme 
activity, supply oxygen, and enhanced stability for GOx-based starving 
therapy is necessary for tumor therapy and other related therapies.
Nanomaterials with enzyme-like activities (known as nanozymes or 
artificial enzymes) have been extensively explored [13–17]. The 
co-delivery of natural enzyme (GOx) and nanomaterial with CAT-like 
activity in a nano-system for synergistic cancer treatment by 
self-cascade reaction has attracted increasing attention [18,19]. Despite 
some progress, further modification and functionalization of these 
nanomaterials have been restricted by their ambiguous structur­
e–performance relationship to optimize functional nanomedicines.
Atomically precise thiolate-protected gold nanoclusters (AuNCs), 
which are descriptive by a molecular-like formula of Aun(SR)m (where 
–SR represents thiolate, n and m denote the gold atom and ligand 
numbers, respectively) [20], have emerged as a new family of potential 
enzyme-like catalysts for their abundant active sites, controllable elec­
tronic structure, and catalytic activity [21–23]. Compared to gold 
nanoparticles, Aun(SR)m exhibits a size smaller than 2 nm generally, and 
discrete electronic structures typically owing to the quantum size effect 
[24]. More importantly, the atomically precise composition and struc­
ture of Aun(SR)m provide great opportunities to explore the 
structure-performance relationship of biomimetic catalysis at the atomic 
and molecular levels [25]. Typically, Au25(SR)18 was chosen as a model 
cluster for engineering enzyme-like NCs study for its excellent stability, 
synthesis feasibility, biocompatibility, and known structures [26–28]. 
Recent efforts have found that changes in the metal core [29,30] or the 
surface ligands [31,32] in the external shell of Aun(SR)m may signifi­
cantly affect their electronic structure, functionality and adaptability 
against external stimuli. Despite the process, the study of 
metal-NCs-based enzyme mimics is still in its infancy, especially with the 
ligand effect on the modulation of enzyme mimic functionality of metal 
NCs related to cascade reaction remaining elusive. Various ligands have 
been employed to prepare a variety of AuNCs, it was found that surface 
ligands not only provide effective protection for the metal core but also 
affect the catalytic properties, especially activity and stability [33,34]. 
Among them, Aun(SR)m protected by the short-chain 3-mercaptopro­
pionic acid (MPA) containing one carboxyl group as a functional 
group was applied extensively in biocatalysis due to its favorable 
water-solubility and biomimetic activity [22,30]. Derived from MPA, 
N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) has a similar chain length and a hydrophilic 
acetyl amino group substituting on the α-carbon. The acetyl amino 
group in the side chain of NAC is beneficial for the formation of intra­
molecular hydrogen bonds in the molecular structure of Aun(SR)m, thus 
leading to higher structural stability and peroxidase mimic catalytic 
ability [25]. More importantly, compared with mono-thiolate-protected 
Aun(SR)m, bi-thiolate-protected Aun(SR)m offers more choices in 
modulating stability in solution [35,36]. Therefore, stability and 
enzyme mimics activity of Au25(SR)18 were modulated by incorporating 
the bi-thiolate-ligand in this study.
In this work, Au25(MPA)18 protected by mono-thiolate-ligand (MPA) 
was synthesized, along with the Au25NACxMPA18-x, which was protected 
by bi-thiolate-ligand by introducing the NAC ligand into Au25(MPA)18. 
Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1 exhibited superior CAT-like activity and 
structural stability than Au25(MPA)18 in our work, indicating that ligand 
incorporation is a feasible and effective method to modulate the enzyme 
mimics activity and stability of AuNCs. Guided by the nanoconfinement 
effect [37], GOx-Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1@ZIF-8 cascade system was 
constructed by co-immobilizing the GOx and Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1 
cluster in zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8), a typical metal­
–organic framework. ZIF-8 was commonly utilized for drug delivery 
systems due to its biocompatibility, high loading efficiency and mod­
erate synthetic conditions in the aqueous phase that can well maintain 
the activities of the encapsulated enzymes [38,39]. Given that ZIF-8 is 
pH-sensitive, gluconic acid generated in the GOx-mediated glucose 
consumption would enhance the acidity of the microenvironment, thus 
triggering the degradation of ZIF-8 and then the release of the encap­
sulated drug [40]. The accelerated O2-dependent depletion of glucose in 
the cascade system was due to the synergistic effect of the conversion of 
glucose to gluconic acid and generation of H2O2 catalyzed by GOx,

2. Methods
2.1. Reagents and chemicals
Gold chloride (HAuCl4⋅3H2O) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. 3-mer­
captopropionic acid (MPA) was obtained from TCI. N-acetyl-L-cysteine 
(NAC) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 2-Methylimidazole was 
purchased from Aladdin. Sodium borohydride (NaBH4) was purchased 
from HUSHI. D-(+)-Glucose (C6H12O6) was purchased from Aladdin. 
Glucose oxidase assay kit was purchased from Applygen. The catalase 
assay kit and BCA protein assay kit were purchased from Beyotime 
Biotechnology. HRP and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) were ob­
tained from Sangon Biotech. Trypsin and 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine 
(TMB) were purchased from Solarbio.
2.2. Characterization
The nitrogen adsorption and desorption isotherm are obtained by 
degassing the sample at 120 ◦C in a nitrogen atmosphere for 12 h and 
then analyzing it with the Microtrac BEL Automatic Multi-Station Spe­
cific Surface Area, Micropore and Mesoporous Pore Size Analyzer (Bel­
sorp Max). The Brunauer-Emmet-Teller (BET) is used to analyze the 
specific surface area, while the Nonlocal Density Function Theory 
(NLDFT) is used to analyze and calculate the pore size distribution. 
Ultraviolet–visible absorption spectroscopy (UV–vis) was performed 
with a microplate reader and tested using a PerkinElmer EnSight 
HH3400 spectrophotometer. The Fourier transform infrared spectros­
copy (FTIR) was performed using a Nicolet NEXUS 470-FTIR spec­
trometer, which was measured by drying the samples in a freeze dryer 
(SCIENTZ-12 N). Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and Zeta potential 
were tested with the SZ-100 Zeta potential analyzer. Scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM) is measured by dispersing samples in ethanol and 
drying them naturally, and then spraying them with gold. Transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM) images of samples were obtained by ultra­
sonic processing in absolute ethanol, diluting it to a certain concentra­
tion, and then dropping it onto an ultra-thin carbon support membrane, 
allowing it to dry naturally, and then using the FEI TECNAIG2F20–S- 
TWIN high-resolution transmission electron microscope. X-ray Photo­
electron Spectroscopy (XPS) was tested using the Thermo Scientific 
ESCALAB 250Xi X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy. The instrument used 
for the Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) test is the Rigaku B/Max-RB. 
The confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was measured by 
Leica TCS SP8X confocal microscopy with an excitation wavelength of 
488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm. For direct visualization, 
the enzyme was labeled with FITC, and then underwent a similar 
encapsulation process.
2.3. Synthesis of Au25MPA18 and Au25NAC14-17MPA4-1
The Au25MPA18 synthesis step was as follows: HAuCl4 (0.250 mL, 40 
mM) and MPA (0.200 mL, 100 mM) were added to ultrapure water 
(4.55 mL) with gentle agitation (500 rpm). After 5 min, adjust the pH of 
the reaction solution to 12.0 by adding 1 M sodium hydroxide solution 
dropwise. Half an hour later, NaBH4 (0.01 mL, 4.3 mg/mL) dissolved in 
0.2 M sodium hydroxide aqueous solution was added to it. Next, the 
container is sealed and the reaction is performed at room temperature 
with gentle agitation for 24 h.
The Au25NAC14-17MPA4-1 synthesis step was as follows: MPA (0.033 
mL, 100 mM) and NAC (0.167 mL, 100 mM) were added to ultrapure 
water (4.55 mL), followed by HAuCl4 solution (0.250 mL, 20 mM). After 
5 min, adjust the pH of the reaction solution to 12.0 by adding 1 M 
sodium hydroxide solution dropwise. Half an hour later, NaBH4 (0.01 
mL, 4.3 mg/mL) dissolved in 0.2 M sodium hydroxide aqueous solution 
was added to it. Next, the container is sealed and the reaction is per­
formed at room temperature with gentle agitation for 24 h. By adjusting 
the ratio of MPA to NAC, different proportions of doped Au25NAC18- 
xMPAx (x = 1–18) can be synthesized. For further purification of 
nanoclusters, a 3 kDa ultrafiltration tube at 3500 rpm/min was used to 
remove unmatched ligands. The obtained samples are stored in the dark 
at 4 ◦C for further analysis.

Pre- and post-catalytic stability experiments: Au25(SR)18 (2 mg/mL) 
and H2O2 (5 mM) were added to a centrifuge tube for a reaction for 5

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Synthesis and characterization of gold clusters
Au25MPA18 was synthesized by combining HAuCl4 and MPA 
following the reported protocol with minor modifications [41]. The 
mixture was reduced by a freshly prepared solution of NaBH4, and then 
was stirred for 24 h. To elucidate the role of ligand engineering in tuning 
the catalytic microenvironment, the reaction catalyzed by CAT was 
conducted as a model reaction, in which the decomposition of H2O2 was 
accompanied by the generation of O2 and H2O [10]. To explore the in­
fluence of ligand structure on CAT-like activity of Au25MPA18, different 
aliquots of MPA ligand were replaced by NAC through a similar process 
to synthesize Au25NACxMPA18-x, in which the proportions between MPA 
and NAC were 1:0, 1:1, 1:3, 1:5, respectively. Both MPA and NAC belong 
to the short alkyl chain, which is beneficial to the accessibility to the 
substrates and a higher catalytic activity [42].
Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectrum was first used to 
characterize the as-synthesized AuNCs samples. As shown in Fig. S1a, 
Au25NACxMPA18-x with various proportion of the MPA/NAC ligand 
exhibited the characteristic peaks at approximately 450 nm and 670 nm, 
which were in accordance with thiolated Au25NCs in former reports [30,
43], confirming their similar structure. The CAT-like activity of these 
samples was also compared subsequently. The content of NAC gradually 
increased from zero to 83 % (NAC: MPA = 5: 1), accompanied with the 
CAT-like activity increased by nearly 1.4-fold compared to that of 
Au25MPA18 (Fig. S1b). The CAT-like activity of Au25NACxMPA18-x is 
positively related to the NAC content in these samples. When the ratio of 
NAC and MPA reached 5:1, in which the sample was donated as 
Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1 according to the literature [35,41], the 
CAT-like activity is the highest in our work. As the ratio of NAC and MPA 
increased continuously, the UV–Vis absorption spectra of the sample 
were different from Au25NCs (data not shown), indicating the changes in 
the structure. Hence, Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1 was chosen for further 
analysis.
Further analyses were performed to investigate the structures, mor­
phologies, and compositions of the Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1, while 
Au25MPA18 was a control without the introduction of the NAC ligand. 
The absorption peaks were located at approximately 450 nm and 670 
nm in the UV–vis absorption spectrum in Au25MPA18 and Au25(NAC)14- 
17(MPA)4-1 (Fig. 1a), consistent with our reported results. After the 
introduction of NAC, the raw aqueous solution of Au25(NAC)14- 
17(MPA)4-1 also retained a similar reddish-brown color as that of 
Au25MPA18. The results indicated that there is no significant effect on 
cluster structure before and after ligand mixed, and also proved the 
successful synthesis of cluster Au25(SR)18. In addition, X-ray photo­
electron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted to investi­
gate the Au valence states of AuNCs before and after the incorporation of 
NAC ligand. As shown in Fig. S2, the high-resolution XPS spectra of Au 
4f reveal a negative shift of ~0.1 eV after the incorporation of NAC 
ligand, indicating a profound electron flow from NAC to Au25(NAC)14- 
17(MPA)4-1. Semi-quantitative analysis was conducted based on XPS 
spectroscopy to obtain the relative contents of elements and thus the 
ratio of NAC/MPA in samples. Based on XPS analysis, the ratio of NAC 
and MPA in Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1 was 4.9:1 (Table S1), which is close 
to the ratio of raw materials during the synthesis process (5:1), thus 
indicating the successful synthesis of this cluster. The zeta potential and 
particle size analysis were used to track the construction process of the 
composite. The zeta potential could be used to characterize the apparent 
surface charge density of the clusters. The zeta potential of Au25MPA18 
was −45 mV. After NAC ligand incorporation, Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1 
showed a more negative zeta potential (−48 mV) than Au25MPA18 
(Fig. 1b), which is probably due to the strong negative charge of NAC. 
Moreover, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of the 
clusters revealed a spherelike morphology with an average size of 2 nm 
(Fig. 1c and e). The hydrodynamic size of the clusters was determined by 
dynamic light scattering (DLS), with an average size of about 2 nm 
(Fig. 1d and f), which is close to the TEM results. As the UV–vis ab­
sorption spectra (Fig. 2a–b) and hydrodynamic size (Figs. S4a–b) shown, 
Au25MPA18 and Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1 remained stable when dis­
solved in water for 7 d, confirming the satisfied stability of the Au 
clusters even after NAC ligand mixed. Together, these findings suggest 
the ultra-small size, good dispersity stability and high uniformity of size 
distribution of the as-synthesized clusters.

solutions were observed compared to glucose (Fig. 5b), demonstrating

posites toward glucose.

With the depletion of glucose in the cascade systems, the simultaneously 
generated toxic intermediate H2O2 was also monitored. The lower re­
sidual H2O2 level in the system indicated the higher H2O2 conversation

system after reaction for 30 min compared to the latter two systems, 
respectively (Fig. 5c). To exclude the influence of AuNCs and the ZIF-8 
framework on the experimental results, a series of control groups were 
set up. The residual H2O2 level after treatment with GOx-Au25(NAC)14- 
17(MPA)4-1 and GOx-Au25(MPA)18 were about 68 % and 23 % lower than 
that after treatment with GOx alone, respectively (Fig. 5d). The residual 
H2O2 content after treatment with GOx-Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1@ZIF-8 
and GOx-Au25(MPA)18@ZIF-8 were about 70 % and 78 % lower than 
that after treatment with GOx@ZIF-8, respectively (Fig. 5d). Moreover, 
reaction system without GOx, including AuNCs (Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4- 
1 or Au25(MPA)18), ZIF-8 alone and the composites (Au25(NAC)14- 
17(MPA)4-1@ZIF-8 or Au25(MPA)18@ZIF-8), could not degrade glucose, 
thus negligible H2O2 was observed (Fig. 5d). These results confirmed 
that the synergistic merits of constructing a cascade system consisted of 
natural enzymes and NCs possessing tunable CAT-like activity, as well as 
co-immobilization in carriers, promote glucose-depletion efficiency, 
which holds the potential for further medical treatment.

4. Conclusions
In summary, a bi-thiolate-protected Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1 cluster 
containing two thiolate ligands with similar chain length but different 
functional groups was synthesized in this work. The acetyl amino group 
in the side chain of NAC is beneficial for the formation of intramolecular 
hydrogen bonds in the molecular structure of Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1, 
thus leading to higher structural stability and CAT-like activity. Co- 
encapsulation of GOx and Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4-1 within the ZIF-8 
could consume more glucose compared to that with GOx alone under 
the same conditions. After treatment with GOx-Au25(NAC)14-17(MPA)4- 
1@ZIF-8, the residual H2O2 level was about 93 % lower than that after 
treatment with GOx alone. Additionally, the composite showed higher 
catalytic activity, stability, and tolerance when applied to GOx-mediated 
glucose depletion. This versatile strategy offers valuable guidelines for 
engineering high-performing and controllable cascade systems in 
further applications. The mass transfer process of the constructed self- 
cascade system and the molecular mechanism in active sites need 
further research in the future.

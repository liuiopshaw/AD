2. Results and Discussion
The synthesis of super-AgNC included two key steps: (i) syn-
thesis of ssDNA/AgNC as building blocks, and (ii) construc-
tion of super-AgNC via Watson–Crick base paring based 
self-assembly. Using DNA as the scaffold to formulate the 
formation of AgNC relied on the specific affinity between 
cytosine (C) of DNA and silver cations.[5,26–28,37,38] Thus, we 
used C-rich ssDNA as scaffold to prepare ssDNA/AgNC. As 
demonstrated in Figure 1, ssDNA contained two functional 
moieties: (i) C-rich sequence which functioned as the scaffold 
for the formation of AgNC (the red part in Table S1 in the Sup-
porting Information), and (ii) the sticky-end for the formation 
of branched DNA through hybridization (the black part). Ag 
ions tended to aggregate at the site of C-rich part of ssDNA, 
and were reduced to Ag atoms by NaBH4, resulting in the for-
mation of AgNC. C-rich ssDNA played vital roles in the forma-
tion of AgNC: (i) offered active sites for Ag ions, (ii) constrained 
further growth of AgNC, and (iii) stabilized AgNC in solution. 
Afterward, branched DNA/AgNC (super-AgNC) was synthe-
sized through the hybridization of complimentary sticky-ends 
Metal nanoclusters (NCs) possess unique optical properties, and exhibit a 
wide variety of potential applications. DNA with robust molecular program-
mability is demonstrated as an ideal scaffold to regulate the formation of 
NCs, offering a rational approach to precisely tune the spatial structures of 
NCs. Herein, the first use of branched DNA as scaffold to regulate the forma-
tion of silver nanoclusters (super-AgNC) is reported, in which the spatial 
structures are precisely designed and constructed. Super-AgNC with tunable 
shapes and arm-lengths including Y-, X-, and (Y–X)- shaped super-AgNC is 
achieved. The molecular structures and optical properties of super-AgNCs 
are systemically studied. As a proof of application, remarkably, super-AgNCs 
exhibit superior antibacterial performance. In addition, super-AgNCs show 
excellent biocompatibility with three types of tissue cells including 293T 
(human embryonic kidney cells), SMCs (vascular smooth muscle cells), 
and GLC-82 (lung adenocarcinoma cells). These performances enable the 
super-AgNCs adaptable in a variety of applications such as biosensing, 
bioimaging, and antibacterial agents.

1. Introduction
Metal nanoclusters (NCs) are a unique category of nano-
materials, which are typically comprised of several to tens of 
metal atoms, and exhibit unique size-dependent fluorescent 
emission.[1–3] In particular, silver (Ag) nanoclusters (AgNC) 
have been widely developed due to their appealing fluores-
cent features and promising biomedical applications such as 
bioimaging and biosensing.[4,5] The synthesis, size, and fluo-
rescent properties of AgNC could be regulated by various scaf-
folds (or termed as templates),[6–9] such as carboxylate groups 
(–COO−),[10] thiol-compounds,[11] polymers,[12,13] amino acids,[14] 
and peptides.[15–17] Alternatively, DNA has been proven an 
excellent scaffold due to its unique molecular recognition 
and sequence programmability,[18–22] which could regulate the

We further studied the association of AgNC with DNA 
in super-AgNC by investigating the changes of ultraviolet 
(UV)–visible absorption spectra. Upon Ag+ complexation, the 
wavelength number of the maximum absorption (λmax) of 
ssDNA shifted from 259 to 263 nm (red shift). After reduc-
tion of the bound Ag+ to AgNC, λmax shifted from 263 back 
to 260 nm (blue shift) (Figure 3a). Y-, X-, and (Y–X)- super-
NC exhibited similar behavior: λmax of branched DNA shifted 
from 258 to 260 nm upon Ag+ complexation, with following 
shifting back to 258 nm after reduction (Figure 3b–d). The 
red shift in the electronic absorption induced by Ag+ indi-
cated that the Ag+ were associated with the bases; the blue 
shift caused by the formation of AgNC was attributed to the 
new, overlapping electronic bands for AgNC.[26] Furthermore, 
we studied the structure changes of branched DNA induced 
by Ag+ and AgNCs by circular dichroism (CD) spectra, which 
indicated the dipole coupling between the excited electronic 
states of DNA bases. We observed that the CD spectra of 
ssDNA, Y-DNA, X-DNA, and (Y–X)-DNA apparently changed 
upon Ag+ complexing and the reduction of the Ag+ to AgNC 
(Figure 4). Among which, the more branches of the branched 
DNA were, the less shift induced by Ag+ and AgNCs caused. 
We ascribed this difference to the increased rigidity of the 
branched structure.[39]

exhibited the similar regulation patterns upon 568 nm excita-
tion: increased first, then reached a peak and followed with a 
decline. The peak position of fluorescence intensity for each 
super-AgNC was slightly different. It was reasonable that 
with the increase of concentration of super-AgNC, the fluo-
rescence intensity in the bulk solution increased. However, 
when the concentration of super-AgNC further increased, the 
distance between single AgNCs reached a critical value, and 
consequently a phenomenon called concentration quenching 
occurred. In this case, the excitations of the electrons migrated 
over fluorescence centers. On the other hand, the resonant 
energy transferred from one fluorescence center to another 
one occurred until this energy was completely captured by 
the quenching center (such as impurities in solution).[40] As 
a result, with the increment of concentration of super-AgNC, 
the transfer of excitation energy led to the fluorescence 
quenching.
The programmability of branched DNA enabled remark-
able tunability of the spacial structures of super-AgNC, such 
that the arm-lengths of the double-stranded part of branched 
DNA could be precisely tuned. We therefore studied the fluo-
rescent properties of super-NCs with different arm-lengths. 
We tuned the arm-lengths through varying the bp number of 
the branch arms (Equation S1, Supporting Information). The 
electrophoresis results confirmed the increment of molecular 
weight with increasing the bp number of the branch arms, 
demonstrating the precise control of DNA nanostructures and 
successful preparation of Y- and X- super-NC with variable 
arm-lengths (Figure 6a). In this study, we fixed the concen-
tration of ssDNA at 3 µm (the optimization of concentration 
was illustrated in Figures S1 and S2 in the Supporting Infor-
mation). The bp number of branch arms varied from 10, 14, 
18 to 22, correspondingly the arm-length from 3.4, 4.8, 6.1 
to 7.5 nm. With the elongation of arm-lengths, the fluorescence 
intensity of Y-super-NC and X-super-NC showed the same 
trend: the peak value of fluorescent spectra increased first and 
then declined. Y-super-NC with 14 bp arm-length and X-super-
NC with 18 bp exhibited the strongest fluorescence intensity 
(Figure 6b,c). These results demonstrated that varying the arm-
lengths of branched DNA enabled precise tuning of spacial 
structure of super-AgNC, subsequently regulating the optical 
properties of super-AgNC.

tions, the cytotoxicity of super-NCs is important to evaluate. 
We carried out 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyl tetra-

muscle cells), and GLC-82 (lung adenocarcinoma cells) after 
incubation with ssDNA/AgNC, Y-super-NC, X-super-NC, and 
(Y–X)-super-NC with a wide range of concentration from 0, 
2.5, 5, 10, 15 to 20 × 10−6 m, respectively (Figure 9). For the 
normal tissue cells, the cell viability of 293T and SMCs with 
20 × 10−6 m super-AgNCs was higher than 85% and 83%, 
respectively. For the cancer cells, the cell viability of GLC-82 
with 20 × 10−6 m super-AgNCs was higher than 94%. The 
results indicated that our super-AgNC had low cytotoxicity to 
the three kinds of cells.

4. Experimental Section
Materials: Phosphate buffer (PB), sodium borohydride (NaBH4), and 
silver nitrate (AgNO3) were purchased from Solarbio Technology Co. 
Ltd. Desalted DNA oligonucleotides were purchased from GENEWIZ 
Biotechnology Co. Ltd. All the solutions were prepared with ultrapure water.
Preparation of DNA/AgNC: ssDNA/AgNC in PB (pH 6.8, 20 × 10−3 m) 
was prepared by reduction with NaBH4. The final mole ratio of NaBH4/
AgNO3 was 1:1. Second, ssDNA/AgNC was used as building blocks to 
construct Y-, X-, and (X–Y)- super-NC, and the final concentration of 
ssDNA/AgNC was 60 µm. The construction of super-AgNC was verified 
with staining in EtBr solution (0.5 µg mL−1) by 12% polyacrylamide gel 
(PAGE) which was prepared by mixing 4 mL of 30% acrylamide solution 
(The mole ration of acrylamide and bis-acrylamide was 29:1), 2 mL of 
5 × tris-borate-EDTA buffer, 75 µL of 10% ammonium persulfate, 3.5 µL 
of N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine and the volume was made up to 
10 mL with MilliQ water. (Y–X)-super-NC was verified with staining of EtBr 
solution (0.5 µg mL−1) by agarose gel electrophoresis (3%).

Spectroscopy Analysis: UV–visible absorption spectra were acquired 
using Quawell Q5000 micro volume spectrophotometer. Circular 
dichroism spectra were obtained from a Jasco J-810 spectropolarimeter. 
The time resolved fluorescence measurements spectra were carried 
out using a time-correlated single photon counting system (Horbia 
JobinYvon XH2162). Quantum yields were verified using FLS 920P. 
Fluorescence spectra were acquired on a BioTek SYNERGY H1 
spectrophotometer using 96 well plate. All spectra were obtained by 
taking the average of three times scans.
Dynamic Light Scattering Analysis: Particle size measurements were 
performed by DLS analysis adopting a Nano ZS instrument.
Antibacterial Activity Test: E. coli BL21 (DE3) and S. aureus FDA 209P 
were used as Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria models to study 
the antibacterial properties of DNA/AgNC. The susceptibility of E. coli and 
S. aureus to DNA/AgNC was determined by the growth curve method. 
Briefly, bacteria were cultured in Luria–Bertani medium until OD600 value 
reached 0.5, and the original bacterial concentration was 1.6 × 107 colony-
forming units (cfu mL−1). Bacteria were incubated with ssDNA/AgNC, 
Y-super-NC, X-super-NC, and (Y–X)-super-NC as antimicrobial agents at 
37 °C for 12 h. The DNA/AgNC concentration was adjusted to 2.5, 5, 10, 
15, and 20 µm based on Ag+ concentration in a final volume of 100 µL. 
Growth curves were determined by measuring OD600 every 5 min in 12 h.
MTT Assay: In order to evaluate the effect of DNA/AgNC dose 
on cellular toxicity, the cells were incubated with DNA/AgNC for 8 
h with a serial concentration. The culture medium was replaced by 
100 µL fresh medium containing 0.5 mg/mL MTT assay reagent, and 
then cells were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 4 h. The medium 
was removed and 110 µL dimethyl sulfoxide was added. Viability of 
non-DNA/AgNC-treated control cells was defined as 100%. Finally, the 
absorption at 490 nm of each well was measured by BioTek SYNERGY 
H1 spectrophotometer.

ABSTRACT: Acinetobacter baumannii is a Gram-negative
bacterium of increasing concern due to its virulence and
persistence in combat and healthcare environments. The
incidence of both community-acquired and nosocomial A.
baumannii infections is on the rise in foreign and domestic
healthcare facilities. Treatment options are limited due to the
acquisition of multidrug resistance to the few eﬀective
antibiotics. Currently, the most eﬀective pharmaceutically
based treatment for multidrug-resistant A. baumannii infections
is the antibiotic colistin (polymyxin E). To minimize side
eﬀects associated with administration of colistin or other toxic
antimicrobial agents, we propose the development of a nanotechnology-mediated treatment strategy. In this design-based eﬀort,
colistin-functionalized multilayered, inorganic, magnetoplasmonic nanoconstructs were fabricated to bind to the surface of A.
baumannii. This result, for the ﬁrst time, demonstrates a robust, pharmaceutical-based motif for high aﬃnity, composite
nanoparticulates targeting the A. baumannii surface. The antibiotic-activated nanomaterials demonstrated cytocompatibility with
human cells and no acute bacterial toxicity at nanoparticle to bacterial concentrations <10 000:1. The magnetomotive
characteristics of the nanomaterial enabled magnetic extraction of the bacteria. In a macroscale environment, maximal separation
eﬃciencies exceeding 38% were achieved. This result demonstrates the potential for implementation of this technology into
micro- or mesoﬂuidic-based separation environments to enhance extraction eﬃciencies. The future development of such a
mesoﬂuidic-based, nanotechnology-mediated platform is potentially suitable for adjuvant therapies to assist in the treatment of
sepsis.
KEYWORDS: magnetic nanoclusters, iron oxide, gold, bacteria capture, colistin, magnetic separation
■INTRODUCTION
Acinetobacter baumannii has emerged as a bacterial species of
importance due to its signiﬁcant virulence and enhanced
persistence in combat and healthcare environments.1,2 A Gram-
negative bacterium initially found in the Middle East, A.
baumannii possesses an enhanced ability to thrive in dry
environments3 and persist following conventional surface
cleaning4 and wound treatments.5 The recent increase in
incidence of A. baumannii infections over the past decade
stems, in part, from US military combat in Iraq and
Afghanistan.6 Incidence in both community-acquired and
nosocomial infection is on the rise in both foreign and
domestic US healthcare facilities7 which can be partially
attributed to the return of infected cargo and veterans.
Acinetobacter species can cause a multitude of ailments
including pneumonia, bacteremia, meningitis, urinary tract
infections, peritonitis, and skin and soft tissue infections.8
These infected tissues contribute to the incidence of
Acinetobacter species bloodstream infections (BSIs) which
account for over 3% of all nosocomial acquired BSIs.9 The
mortality rate associated with A. baumannii bacteremia is 52%,
and pneumonia-associated mortality ranges from 23% to 73%.10
Treatment options for A. baumannii infections are increas-
ingly limited due to the rapid acquisition of multidrug
resistance to the few eﬀective antibiotics.11 Currently, the
most eﬀective pharmaceutically based treatment for multidrug-
resistant A. baumannii infections is the antibiotic colistin
(colistimethate [colsitin sulfomethate] or polymyxin E).12
Colistin is a cationic polypeptide and potent broad-spectrum
antibiotic for Gram-negative bacteria. Previously prescribed
only as a last resort due to nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity,
colistin has been revisited as a ﬁrst-line treatment due to its

electrostatically with the outer membrane of Gram-negative
bacteria by competitively displacing divalent cations from the
negatively charged phosphate groups of membrane lipids. This
interaction disrupts the outer membrane, and lipopolysacchar-

sensitive strains at high dosage, dosage reductions are often
necessary due to the nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and
broncho-restrictive eﬀects that occur at the most eﬀective
dosage.15 Such dosage reductions limit treatment eﬃcacy
resulting in increased mortality. Dose limiting toxicity also
potentially contributes to the development of colistin-resistant
strains, a particularly worrisome outcome for A. baumannii.
As a possible remedy for the side eﬀects associated with
colistin and other toxic antimicrobial agents, many have
investigated therapies which function as adjuvants to increase
the eﬃcacy of conventional antibiotic regimens. Taking a
pharmaceutically centered approach, combinatorial delivery of
colistin with sulbactam, rifampin and tigecycline, proved
suboptimal as it has been shown to lead to the development
of resistance.16 Nonpharmaceutically centered, innovative
approaches have been described, including the development
of extracorporeal devices aiming to mechanically cleanse
bacteria from body ﬂuids. Most investigations utilize
pathogen-targeted and endotoxin-targeted magnetic nano-
technology with an external device acting as a ﬁlter or spleen
mimic.17 A blood-cleansing extracorporeal device, inspired by
the spleen, to continuously remove pathogens and toxins from
blood has been described.18 Magnetic nanoparticles conjugated

previously developed a novel colistin-functionalized nano-

saturation) binding kinetics between colistin-functionalized
nanoparticles and three diﬀerent A. baumannii strains, including
a colistin-resistant strain.20 This work details the synthesis and
provides the characterization of the colistin-functionalized
core/shell nanomaterials used to magnetically extract A.
baumannii from solution. For use in the design of an
extracorporeal system, our previous results suggest the use of
colistin as a robust motif for nanoparticulate targeting, with
respect to binding aﬃnity, to the A. baumannii surface. To
initiate the development of such a treatment, robust, inorganic
nanoparticulate platforms, comprised of materials approved for
human use, must be developed and their capture ability in
saline solution quantiﬁed. Using a design-based approach, gold
iron−oxide composite nanoclusters were, for the ﬁrst time,
biologically modiﬁed and utilized for both their optical and
magnetomotive properties. We also describe the use of colistin
as a targeting motif to bind the magnetic gold−iron oxide
nanoclusters to the surface of A. baumannii. Careful design was
employed to implement the nontrivial use of colistin as a
targeting motif to achieve eﬀective functionalization of the
active form of the antibiotic without introducing a signiﬁcant
impact on bacterial survival. In this way, we design to minimize
both the release of toxins from lysed bacteria and evolution of
resistant strains. As a whole, we describe the extraction
eﬃciency of A. baumannii using this antibiotic-enabled
nanoparticulate platform in a single-pass, macroscale system.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

characterized for their elemental composition and their optical,
magnetic, and morphological properties. Following sonicatory
amination of γ-Fe2O3 nanoparticles, there were no signiﬁcant
changes in the morphology of the nanomaterials (Figure 2A)
where the materials remained monomorphous. However, after
gold sonoplation, the formation of polymorphous, jagged,
oblong, and elliptical particles was apparent among many
spherical particles as shown in Figure 2A(iii). Furthermore,

Nanomaterial Characterization. The optical properties of
the nanomaterials change signiﬁcantly upon the sonoplation of
gold to the surface of the γ-Fe2O3 nanoparticles. The
appearance of a surface plasmon resonance peak at 564 nm is
consistent with the formation of gold clad nanomaterials. The
presence of FeOx/Au core/shell nanoparticles following
synthesis was substantiated via HRTEM-EDS of an isolated
nanoparticle. The micrograph in Figure 2C shows an isolated
nanoparticle with 2.4 Å fringe patterns indicative of a gold clad
material.22 This single nanoparticle was characterized for its
elemental composition via the EDS functionality of the S/
TEM-EDS microscope. Following 8 min of X-ray collection,
the EDS spectrum (Figure 2D) contained gold, as expected, but
also iron and silicon. The detection of elemental iron and
silicon substantiates the existence of both an iron core and the
intermediary aminosilane layers which comprise the core/shell
nanoparticle.
Conjugation Conﬁrmation. The conjugation of colistin to
the surface of the FeOx/Au nanoclusters was conﬁrmed by
changes in optical absorbance, zeta potential, and enumeration
of liberated colistin conjugates using nuclear magnetic
resonance and ELISA assays.Conjugation with PEG and colistin
shifts the absorbance peak by an additional ten nanometers (to
553 nm), consistent with the change in the surface plasmon
resonance due to the chemical conjugation or ligand binding on
the gold surface of the nanomaterial.23
Following conjugation, the 2-fold, statistically signiﬁcant (p <
0.05) positive shift in zeta potential between colistinated and
PEGylated nanoclusters is an indicator of conjugation as the
colistin molecule has a higher positive charge than PEG (Table
1). There was also a signiﬁcant positive shift in zeta potential of
both conjugated particle populations when compared to the
unconjugated particles, further conﬁrming conjugation. The
iodine-catalyzed decomposition of the colistinated nanomateri-
als ensured that bound colistin would be liberated into solution
from the gold surface. Thereafter, the iodinated gold
ﬂocculated, losing its colloidal properties, and settled in the
tube. The inset of Figure 3A depicts this observation, where
colloidal, colistinated FeOx/Au nanoclusters (left) are
compared to precipitated iodinated gold with the colistin
conjugates liberated into solution. Only purple-colored,
concentrated nanoclusters with no supernatant are present in
the tube on the left, whereas a yellow hued supernatant with
brown precipitate is present in the tube on the right. 1H NMR
spectra of stock colistin show strong methyl−methyl peaks
around 0.8−0.9 ppm, whereas primary amine groups are
detected around 8 ppm. Diamagnetic shielding generates a
spectral shift consistent with this observation. The additional
electrons surrounding the colistin, which are contributed by the
bound polymer, shield the molecule which causes a 0.5−1 ppm
shift in the resultant spectrum. As shown in Figure 3A, 1H
NMR spectra of the supernatant of this reaction depict the
presence of colistin substantiated by the appearance of methyl−
methyl peaks (0.5−1 ppm). Most importantly, the appearance
of primary amines (8−8.5 ppm) as shown in Figure 3B
substantiates the presence of liberated colistin.
To ensure no unconjugated colistin was contributing to the
1H NMR results, a free colistin ELISA assay was performed on
the ﬁrst-wash supernatants of both colistination and PEGyla-
tion conjugation reactions. The estimated free colistin
concentration (0.0015 ng/mL) was below the ELISA limit of
sensitivity (0.1 ng/mL). No colistin was detected in PEGylated
supernatants.
1H NMR was not employed alternatively to
detect unconjugated colistin at concentrations less than 10 μg/
mL as it requires quantiﬁcation below the limit of sensitivity of
the instrument. Furthermore, these NMR results were
substantiated using a free colistin ELISA assay which
enumerated the concentration of liberated colistin (32.6 ng/
mL), postconjugation unbound colistin (<0.5 ng/mL), and
unbound PEG concentration (<0.5 ng/mL) from nonliberated
nanomaterials. The combination of these results conﬁrms that
colistin was successfully conjugated to the surface of the
nanomaterials.
Bacterial Toxicity. PEGylated nanomaterials lacked sig-
niﬁcant toxicity (p < 0.05) against A. baumannii in vitro except
at the highest nanoparticle to bacteria ratio (100 000:1) as
shown in Figure 4. Colistinated particles demonstrated
signiﬁcant bacterial toxicity (p < 0.05) at nanoparticle to
bacteria ratios exceeding 10 000:1. A. baumannii susceptibility
to free, unconjugated colistin testing yielded a 0.5 μg/mL
minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). Colistinated par-
ticles at 100 000:1 and 10 000:1 nanoparticle to bacteria ratios
contained estimated surface-associated colistin concentrations
of 0.6 μg/mL and 0.06 μg/mL, respectively. These results
suggest that nanoparticle-bound colistin has greater bactericidal
properties than free colistin (∼28% increased eﬀectiveness).
However, it is possible that not all colistinated nanoparticles at
these high ratios will reach the cell surface to induce bacterial
toxicity and that some bactericidal eﬀects may be contributed
by the large number of nanoparticles in close proximity to each
bacterium. By estimation, a maximum of 1000 nanoparticles
with 12 nm diameter will completely coat the surface of a single
A. baumannii bacterium. Such high nanoparticle levels
associated with the bacterial surface may interfere with nutrient
exchange or other transport functions at the cell surface and

Functionalized Nanoclusters Are Biocompatible. Both
colistinated and PEGylated nanoclusters are biocompatible with
HUVEC and HepG2 cell lines in vitro with nanoparticulate and
bound colistin concentrations as high as 900 mM and 94 μM,
respectively (p < 0.5, n = 3). The lack of cytotoxic eﬀects in
these cell lines reﬂects compatibility with both vasculature and
liver tissue (Figure 5). Free colistin was tolerated without
toxicity in these mammalian cells up to 0.9 μM (p < 0.5, n = 3).
The colistin reagent used to derive the free colistin cytotoxicity
results was also used in colistination reactions. Similarly, both
colistinated and PEGylated nanoclusters were biocompatible
with red blood cells in vitro and did not cause hemolysis.
Colistinated and PEGylated nanoclusters lysed, at maximum,
0.1% ± 0.2 of red blood cells at nanoparticulate concentrations
ranging from 50 μg/mL to 400 μg/mL (Table 2). Free colistin
lysed no more than 0.0% ± 0.7 at concentrations up to 1 mg/
mL (Table 2). An amount of 100 μg/mL of colistinated
nanomaterials contributed to a statistically signiﬁcant diﬀerence
from the negative control. We do not attribute this to hemolysis
since the measured values were negative. The hemolysis results
demonstrate that colisitinated nanomaterials can be in contact
with blood for 1 h with no detectable hemolysis, consistent
with intravascular administration of colistin as practiced in
clinical treatment.

Magnetic Extraction of A. baumannii. Colistinated
FeOx/Au nanoclusters were used to decorate and magnetically
extract A. baumannii in vitro. Colistinated nanoclusters
separated 28.8% of A. baumannii in the sample, whereas the
PEGylated control nanoclusters separated only 9.6% of the
bacteria (Figure 6). The diﬀerence in percent capture was
highly signiﬁcant (p < 0.001) and suggests that the presence of
colistin on the surface of the nanomaterials enhanced binding
to A. baumannii. This enhanced binding is presumably
inﬂuenced by nanoparticulate surface charge, which is mediated
by the presence of conjugated colistin. This conjugate-mediated
selectivity is supported by a 20 mV positive increase in zeta
potential of nanoparticles following colistination as shown in
Table 1. Comparatively, nanocluster zeta potential remained
unchanged following PEGylation.
As previously determined using a rodent compartmental
sepsis model, bacterial extraction, in conjunction with antibiotic
treatment, with separation rates which exceed 20% are shown
to enhance clearance rates, thus providing biological beneﬁt.24
Here, maximal separation eﬃciencies exceeding 38% were
achieved that, following optimization, will provide biological
beneﬁt as an adjuvant therapy to antibiotic administration.
Separation eﬃciencies could be improved by optimizing the
magnetomotive properties of the nanomaterials. Through
engineering optimization, the ideal balance between the Au
layer thickness, colistin surface density, and iron core size can
be achieved in order to encourage great separation eﬃciencies.
Furthermore, these experiments were performed in a macro-
scale environment where the nanomaterials and bacteria were
required to travel distances (∼mm) potentially lowering the
separation eﬃciency. Implementation of this technology into
micro- or mesoﬂuidic-based separation environments could be
a potential solution to enhance extraction eﬃciencies. The
future development of such a mesoﬂuidic-based, nanotechnol-
ogy-mediated platform is potentially suitable for adjuvant
therapies to assist in the treatment of sepsis.
Quantiﬁcation of Bacteria Capture. Colistin-mediated A.
baumannii capture was conﬁrmed following compensation for
bacterial growth during the in vitro study (Figure 7). Capture
analysis was performed at a “per tube” level of quantiﬁcation to
isolate extraction from the potential confounding impact of
bacterial growth. Notably, colistinated nanoclusters captured
signiﬁcantly more bacteria than PEGylated treatments, even
after correcting for bacterial growth (Figure 7). Signiﬁcant
diﬀerences between supernatant CFU counts further validate
this strategy. Total bacterial concentrations were not diﬀerent
in paired samples treated with nanoparticles and control,
untreated samples. Bacterial concentration in treated samples
was obtained as the sum of nanoparticle-associated bacteria and
unbound bacteria in the supernatant. The sum of bacteria in
treated samples was not diﬀerent than paired, untreated
samples. This approach enabled conﬁrmation that all of the
bacteria in each sample was accounted for among all exposure
times, providing a mass balance of bacteria while accounting for
bacterial growth during the experiment. Acute toxicity was also

more, this result is of key importance as it demonstrates that
the diﬀerence between the captured and uncaptured samples is
attributed to nanoparticulate-mediated magnetic extraction and
not a modulated bacterial growth rate or loss of bacteria in the
samples (e.g., cell death).
As shown in Figure 6 and Figure 7, PEGylated nanoclusters
support A. baumannii separation. We hypothesize that exposed
portions of the Au shell or FeOx core, due to incomplete
PEGylation, may contribute to nonspeciﬁc A. baumannii
binding. In future work, increasing the lengths of the thiolated
PEG spacers may additionally passivate the nanoparticle surface
and reduce nonspeciﬁc binding. The force generated by the
bulk ﬂuid motion of the magnetic nanomaterials acting as a
ferroﬂuid may also contribute to nonspeciﬁc mechanical
entrapment of the bacteria. This phenomenon is comparable
to magnetophoretic, ferroﬂuid-guided systems where bulk
movement of a ferromagnetic ﬂuid in a magnetic ﬁeld has
been shown to trap nonmagnetic particulates.25 Future work
also includes modulating the nanoparticle to bacteria ratio to
determine if the nanoparticle concentration has a signiﬁcant
impact on the nonspeciﬁc ferroﬂuid separation potential
through this mechanical mechanism.
Colistin as an A. baumannii Targeting Motif. Nano-
cluster decoration of the A. baumannii surface is shown in the
STEM-EDS micrograph map in Figure 8A. The presence of the
bacterium is supported by the detection of phosphorus within
the cell as depicted using the color green in the EDS map
overlay. The location of the nanoclusters is substantiated by the
colocalized detection of iron and gold. The nanoclusters are
more apparent than the bacteria on the STEM image due to the
superimposition of the high-angle annular dark ﬁeld (HAADF).
Nanoclusters composed of elements higher in atomic number
of gold will appear to vary in contrast in comparison to lower
atomic number elements. The clustering eﬀect of the
nanomaterials is a feature of the technology which creates an
increase in magnetic relativity, thus improving the magnetic
susceptibility material. Based on the previously described
mechanism of action of colistin,12,13 we suggest that the
binding of the nanoclusters is mediated by colistin. The
electrostatic interaction of the colistin with the phosphate
groups occurs due to the presence of positively charged primary
amine groups. These positively charged groups, especially those
in the lariat of the molecule, displace divalent cations (Mg2+
and Ca2+) which compose the cell envelope of the bacterium
and, under normal conditions, stabilize the membrane lipids.26
Based on the mechanism of colistin−A. baumannii interaction,
our results suggest that nanoparticles are decorated with
colistin in an active conﬁguration. We recognize that colistin
can be functionalized on the nanoparticle surface in multiple
conformations, reﬂecting the multiple sites for reactive
chemistry on colistin. It can be presumed that multiple
colistin−lipid electrostatic interactions generate enough force
to resist detachment during magnetic extraction. Furthermore,
multiple nanoparticulate binding events may multiply this
binding force through cooperative association. The lipophilic
portion of the free colistin molecule is presumed to penetrate
the lipid layer of the bacterium.27 At high colistin
concentrations, these interactions destabilize the lipid bilayer
of the bacterium to the point of cell lysis.26 At suﬃciently high
nanoparticle concentrations, the colistinated nanoparticles
disrupt the cell membrane in a dose-dependent manner,28 as
evidenced in Figure 4. We presume that these binding events
are not speciﬁc to the colistin molecule alone but speciﬁc to the
polymyxin chemistry and amphipathic structure27 of the
antibiotic, where other cationic peptides or designer polymers
with similar chemistry could have similar utility.
Design Implications for Future Development. The
cytocompatibility results are of great importance with regard to
future development of a robust therapeutic. Failure to retain the
magnetic nanomaterials and allowing them to re-enter the
patient is a major regulatory and health concern. Upon entry
into the patient, the nanomaterials would have immediate
interaction with the vascular tissue and would accumulate in the
liver because of their size and charge.29 Due to their
cytocompatibility with these tissues, catastrophic failure of an
extracorporeal device would be better tolerated by a patient in
comparison to devices which utilize nanomaterials which are
cytotoxic.
The dual, magnetoplasmonic nature of the inorganic
nanomaterials utilized in this study enables their usefulness in
theranostic applications. Extracorporeal devices, while designed
foremost to be therapeutic devices, can also be utilized to
speciﬁcally detect the species of bacteria leading to rapid
diagnosis. In the future, such a theranostic approach could be
implemented in a multiplexed fashion on patients who present
with septic-like symptoms but remain undiagnosed due to
diagnostic limitations. Such a device could act as a “broad-
spectrum” therapeutic designed to extract a multitude of

■MATERIALS AND METHODS
All materials were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO) and
used as received unless otherwise noted. Deionized water (dH2O) was
obtained through a dedicated, dual-bed laboratory installation (Mar
Cor Puriﬁcation).30 FeOx/Au nanocluster synthesis protocol is brieﬂy
summarized in Figure 1A.
Fabrication of Oleic Acid Capped γ-Fe2O3 Superparamag-
netic Nanoparticles. γ-Fe2O3 with ∼10 nm diameter was fabricated
by a thermal decomposition, aeration, and reﬂux protocol by Woo et
al.31 Brieﬂy, octyl ether (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, 200 mL) and oleic
acid (Sigma-Aldrich, 28.5 mL) were mixed in a 500 mL round-bottom
ﬂask under reﬂux and Ar gas ﬂow, and Fe(CO)5 (Sigma-Aldrich, 2.0
mL) was injected at 100 °C before heating to 295 °C. The reaction
was aerated at ∼80(±10) °C for 14 h and reﬂuxed while boiling for an
additional 2 h. The γ-Fe2O3 nanoparticles were washed with ethanol
and dried under air for 48 h.
Sonicatory Amination of γ-Fe2O3 Core Nanoparticles.
Aminated γ-Fe2O3 ∼10 nm diameters were fabricated via a place
exchange reaction in an ultrasonic agitation reaction environment.
Oleic acid capped γ-Fe2O3 nanoparticles (10 mg) were dissolved into a
1 wt % (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES, Sigma-Aldrich)
solution in anhydrous toluene and reacted under ultrasonic agitation
(90 min, 20 °C) with an intermittent vortex. Using a 1 T neodymium
1″ cube magnet, the aminated γ-Fe2O3 was magnetically separated
from the toluene-based solvent, washed with EtOH, and stored at 4
°C.
Gold Sonoplation of Aminated γ-Fe2O3 Core Nanoparticles.
Au−(γ-Fe2O3) composite nanoclusters (FeOx/Au) were fabricated via
an electroless plating reaction in an ultrasonic agitation reaction
environment. Adapted from a protocol described previously,32 an
aqueous 1% HAuCl4 (150 μL, Sigma-Aldrich) solution was mixed with
an ethanolic solution of aminated γ-Fe2O3 nanoparticles (1 mg/mL, 2
mL). Under an ultrasonic agitation environment, aqueous sodium
citrate (12 mL, 20 mM, Sigma-Aldrich) was added in 800 μL
increments every 30 s. The resultant FeOx/Au nanoclusters were
puriﬁed via centrifugation (800 rcf, 10 min), resuspended into dH2O
(10 mL), and stored at 4 °C at a concentration of 200 μg/mL.
Conjugation of Colistin Antibiotic to Heterobifunctional
PEG Linker. Colistin (polymyxin-E; Sigma-Aldrich) was conjugated to
ortho-pyridine disulﬁde and N-hydroxysuccinimide heterobifunctional
polyethylene glycol polymer linker (OPSS-PEG3400-NHS; Creative
PEG Works).33 In a 100 mM NaHCO3 solution, OPSS-PEG3400-NHS
(0.044 mg/mL, 9 mL) was mixed with colistin (0.2 mg/mL; 900 μL)
and allowed to react for 2 h at 20 °C. OPSS-PEG3400-colistin
conjugates were stored at 4 °C for further use for no more than 24 h.
Fresh OPSS-PEG3400-colistin conjugates were prepared for conjuga-
tions separated by 24 h or more.
Functionalization of FeOx/Au Nanoclusters with OPSS-
PEG3400-colistin Conjugates and PEG2000-SH. FeOx/Au nano-
clusters were functionalized with OPSS-PEG3400-colistin conjugates
and thiolated PEG (PEG2000-SH; Laysan Bio, Inc.).33 To prepare Col-
PEG-FeOx/Au nanoclusters, FeOx/Au nanoclusters (200 μg/mL; 4
mL) were mixed with OPSS-PEG3400-colistin (100 μL; 18.8 μg/mL)
for 1 h and then mixed with PEG2000-SH (100 μL; 15.6 mg/mL) for an
additional hour. Simultaneously, PEG-FeOx/Au nanoclusters were
prepared by mixing FeOx/Au nanoclusters (200 μg/mL; 4 mL) with
PEG2000-SH (100 μL; 15.6 mg/mL) for 1 h and then mixed with
dH2O for an additional hour. To purify, both preparations were
centrifuged at 800 rcf for 10 min. The supernatant was removed using
magnetic separation to preserve the pellet and saved for unconjugated
colistin enumeration. The pellet was then resuspended in a 70%
aqueous EtOH sterilization solution to restrict bacteria contamination
and promote sterility. Preparations were stored at 4 °C until utilized
for bacterial separation experiments and characterization for no more
than 24 h thereafter.
Nanocluster Characterization. Both conjugated and uncon-
jugated FeOx/Au nanoclusters were characterized similarly. For
morphological analysis, TEM micrographs with EDX (energy-
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy) elemental analysis were obtained on
an FEI Tecnai Osiris S/TEM microscope (FEI, Hillsboro, OR).
Spectroscopic extinction measurements were performed in dH2O
using a Varian Cary 50 UV−vis−NIR spectrophotometer (Agilent
Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Relaxometric measurements were
obtained on a Maran DRX-II 0.5T NMR spectroscopic scanner
following sample preparation in Nanopure dH2O at a diluted
nanocluster concentration (40 μg/mL; 1 mL). Spectroscopic T2
relaxation measurements were performed in a Carr−Purcell−
Meiboom−Gill (CPMG) sequence with the following parameters:
relaxations time (TR) = 30 s, echo time (TE) of ﬁrst echo = 6 ms,
spacing of subsequent echos = 2 ms, and 1200 echos. Size and zeta
potential measurements were performed on a Malvern Zetasizer
(Malvern Instruments, Westborough, MA) following nanoparticulate
dilution (40 μg/mL; 1 mL) in dH2O. The size of the individual
nanoparticles which comprise the nanoclusters was estimated from
TEM images using ImageJ software.
Conﬁrmation of Ligand Conjugation. A colistin ELISA Test
Assay (Bioo Scientiﬁc, Austin, TX) was used to detect and quantify
free, unconjugated colistin in the supernatant of the reaction volume
and liberated colistin from conjugated nanomaterials. For quantiﬁca-
tion of the free, unconjugated colistin in the supernatant, the assay was
used as prescribed by the manufacturer with no deviation from the
provided protocol.
1H NMR spectroscopy was used to conﬁrm colistin conjugation to
the FeOx/Au nanoclusters.
1H NMR spectra were obtained in
deuterium oxide (D2O) and measured using a Bruker 600 MHz
spectrometer (Bruker, 600 MHz, Billerica, MA). Freshly conjugated
Col−PEG-FeOx/Au nanoclusters (2 mL, 2 mg/mL) were lyophilized
for 24 h. The sample was resuspended in 200 μL of D2O to prepare for
the iodine-catalyzed nanocluster decomposition reaction (iodination).
Brieﬂy,34 four to seven iodine crystals were added directly to the
suspension of nanoclusters. The iodinated sample was vortexed (10 s,
100%) intermittently for 10 s, every 20 min, for 1 h. Following a
nucleophilic substitution (place exchange reaction) between the iodine
molecules and the sulfur groups of the colistin-bound conjugate, the
nanoclusters spontaneously settled via precipitation out of solution.
This event caused the colistin-bound conjugates and free iodine to be
the only remaining reagents in the solution. The sample was stored at
20 °C for 12 h to allow the iodine crystals to settle to the bottom of
the suspension. Only colistin-bound conjugates remained in the D2O
suspension which was measured using 1H NMR and a colistin ELISA
assay within 24 h.
For quantiﬁcation of the liberated colistin, the colistin ELISA assay
protocol was modiﬁed to account for the contribution of the free
iodine to the resultant absorbance measurements required by the
assay. Iodinated standards were prepared by incubating 2−3 large
iodine crystals in 150 μL aliquots of each standard overnight. This
protocol ensures a consistent iodine concentration (0.29 mg/mL)
across all standards and liberated samples. Concurrently, noniodine
standards were prepared as prescribed by the manufacturer with no
deviation from the provided protocol. Iodinated samples were
compared against the standard curve of the iodinated standards,
whereas noniodinated samples were measured with no deviation from
the provided protocol.
Conﬁrmation of Nanocluster Binding to A. baumannii.
STEM-EDS (energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry) micrographs of
bacteria−nanocluster aggregates were also obtained from an FEI
Tecnai Osiris S/TEM Microscope using the following ﬁxation
protocol. Brieﬂy, following a 1 h incubation with A. baumannii in 1X
PBS, FeOx/Au nanoclusters bound to bacteria were centrifuged at
12 400 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet
was resuspended in sterile dH2O to wash the nanocluster-bound
bacteria. The washed sample was centrifuged at 12 400 rpm for 5 min
and resuspended in 2% formaldehyde for 30 min at 20 °C.
Approximately 2 μL of the nanocluster-bound bacteria was dropcast
onto carbon-ﬁlm-supported cooper grids (Ted Pella, Inc., Redding,

Mammalian Cell Culture. HepG2 cells (human liver carcinoma

In Vitro Cytotoxicity Studies. The cytotoxicity induced by Col-
PEG-FeOx/Au nanoclusters, PEG-FeOx/Au nanoclusters, and free
colistin on mammalian cell cultures was determined using the
alamarBlue (Invitrogen Co., Carlsbad, CA) assay. HepG2 and
HUVEC cells were seeded in clear, ﬂat 96-well plates at a density of
10 000 cells/well in 200 μL of medium and incubated for 24 h at 37
°C. Next, cells were treated with free colistin at concentrations of 900,
90, 70, 15, 1, and 0 μM. Similarly, culture medium was removed and
exchanged with medium containing fresh Col-PEG-FeOx/Au and
PEG-FeOx/Au at concentrations of 2, 1, 0.5, 0.25, and 0 mM,
respectively. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, cells were washed
three times with phosphate buﬀer solution (PBS), pH 7.4, and treated
with 200 μL of alamarBlue containing media. Cells were then
incubated for an additional 4 h at 37 °C. Fluorescence was quantiﬁed
using a plate reader (Tecan, Inﬁnite M1000 Pro, Switzerland), with an
excitation wavelength of 540 nm and emission wavelength of 590 nm.

collected from anonymous, consenting human donors in accordance

111251). Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated according to well-

were prepared in PBS, pH 7.4, respectively. Free colistin (900, 0.9, 0.4,
0.2, and 0 μM) was also prepared in PBS, pH 7.4. Col-PEG-FeOx/Au,
PEG-FeOx/Au, and free colistin were incubated with RBCs for 1 h at
37 °C, each respectively, at the concentrations outlined above.
Following incubation, the samples were centrifuged, and the
supernatant was spectrophotometrically analyzed for hemoglobin
release using a plate-reader (Tecan, Inﬁnite M1000 Pro) at 451 nm
in order to determine percent hemolysis relative to the positive control
(Triton X-100 detergent). The negative control used was PBS, pH 7.4.

Bacterial Cell Culture. An amount of 50 mL of LB broth (RPI,
Mt. Prospect, IL) was inoculated with a single colony of A. baumannii
(ATCC 17978) using standard sterile techniques. The culture was
incubated overnight (18 h) at 37 °C and agitated at 200 rpm in an
orbital shaker. Before using the bacteria for experiments, the culture
was diluted 1/100 in fresh LB broth and reincubated in the same
conditions for 2 h.
Bacterial Susceptibility Tests. For survival analysis, FeOx/Au
nanoclusters and A. baumannii were mixed and incubated in LB broth.
FeOx/Au nanoparticle concentration (both Col-PEG-FeOx/Au and
PEG-FeOx/Au) was varied from 0 to 1011 nanoparticles/mL, while the
concentration of A. baumannii was adjusted to be 106 CFU/mL in all
samples. After a 1 h incubation at 37 °C and agitation at 200 rpm in an
orbital shaker, cultures were diluted, plated on LB agar plates, and
incubated for 18 h at 37 °C. The percentage of surviving cells
compared to untreated controls was determined by CFU counting. A.
baumannii susceptibility to colistin was also analyzed using
concentrations from 0 to 2 μg/mL and following the same protocol.
Bacterial Magnetic Capture. To determine the magnetic capture
eﬃciency of 1010 nanoclusters of Col-PEG-FeOx/Au or PEG-FeOx/
Au, A. baumannii at 107 CFU in 1 mL of PBS was mixed and incubated
for 1 h at 37 °C and agitated at 200 rpm in an orbital shaker. The
magnetic capture process is summarized in Figure 1D. After
incubation, each sample was divided into two tubes (500 μL each).
One of them was kept at room temperature, as a control of the total
concentration of bacteria after incubation, and not introduced into the
magnetic ﬁeld (MF) and labeled as “−MF tube”. A permanent magnet
(1 T) was placed on the side of the other tube for 20 min at room
temperature. After magnetic separation, supernatants were collected
(+MF SN tubes), and nanoclusters were resuspended in 500 μL of
PBS (+MF NP tubes). An amount of 50 μL of alamarBlue was added
to each tube. All samples were then incubated for 30 min at 37 °C and
centrifuged, and supernatants were analyzed using a plate reader
(Tecan, Inﬁnite F500). Fluorescence was quantiﬁed with an excitation
wavelength of 535 nm and emission wavelength of 590 nm, and CFU/
mL in each sample was determined using a standard curve that
correlated the number of CFU/mL with the ﬂuorescence intensity
obtained from the alamarBlue assay. The standard curve was generated
and processed in parallel with all the samples. The percentage of
magnetic capture was determined by comparison of the CFU/mL in
the −MF tubes and the +MF NP tubes. The percentage of magnetic
capture was determined by dividing the captured CFU/mL by the
control CFU/mL with multiplication by 100.
Statistical Analysis. All experiments were performed in triplicate
unless otherwise noted. The results were expressed as the arithmetic
mean ± standard deviation (SD). A two-sided, unpaired t test was
used to determine statistical signiﬁcance, with signiﬁcance levels
ranging from p < 0.05 to p < 0.001 unless otherwise noted.
■CONCLUSION
Using a design-based approach, we have successfully demon-
strated that superparamagnetic FeOx/Au core/shell nano-
particles, which form magnetic nanoclusters, can be used to
magnetically capture and extract A. baumannii using colistin as
the microbial targeting ligand. The inorganic nanomaterials
were successfully synthesized and characterized for their
magnetoplasmonic properties and potential for use in
theranostic applications. Successful conjugation was conﬁrmed,
and nanocluster binding to the bacterial surface was observed.
This result supports our previous work20 by demonstrating a
robust, pharmaceutical-based motif for high aﬃnity nano-
particulate targeting to the A. baumannii surface. The antibiotic-
activated nanomaterials demonstrated cytocompatibility with
human cells, no toxic eﬀects on bacteria at bacterium to
nanoparticle concentrations <1:10 000, and bacterial toxicity at
higher levels. In a macroscale environment, maximal separation
eﬃciencies exceeding 38% were achieved. This result
demonstrates the potential for implementation of this
technology into micro- or mesoﬂuidic-based separation
environments to enhance extraction eﬃciencies. The future
development of such a mesoﬂuidic-based, nanotechnology-
mediated platform is potentially suitable for adjuvant therapies
to assist in the treatment of sepsis.
These results reveal an opportunity for the future develop-
ment of an adjuvant treatment option for patients suﬀering
from A. baumannii bacteremia and other Gram-negative
bacteria. Possible adjuvant treatments include the development
of extracorporeal devices to mechanically remove bacteria,
while intravenous injections of the antibiotic work concurrently
to stiﬂe the systemic infection. Our development and
characterization of a robust nanoparticulate platform for
targeting and capture of A. baumannii in solution paves the
way for the translational development of such an adjuvant
treatment. Furthermore, due to the dual, magnetophotonic

of “broad-spectrum” theranostics as well as other adjuvant
therapies based on this antibiotic-mediated nanotechnology
platform has the potential to signiﬁcantly improve the clinical
outcomes of septic patients.

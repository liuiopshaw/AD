Diatom-Inspired Scaﬀold for Infected Bone Defect Therapy:
Achieving Stable Photothermal Properties and Coordinated
Antibacterial-Osteogenic Functions
Xinyi Li, Yifei Yang, Mingxuan Chen, Xinyang Liu, Tianyi Chen, Fang Luo, Yijie Huang,
Yihua Liu, Hongbo Zhang, Song Chen,* Ding Bai, Chunmei Ding,* and Jianshu Li*
Bone defect therapy frequently encounters bacterial infections and chronic
inﬂammation, which impair bone regeneration and threaten implant stability.
Iron oxide nanoparticles have attracted attention due to cost-eﬀectiveness,
biocompatibility, and metabolic safety. However, iron oxide nanoparticles still
struggle to balance low-temperature eﬃcient antibacterial activity, eﬀective
immunomodulation, and bone regeneration. Therefore, inspired by diatoms, a
multifunctional bone repair scaﬀold based on chitosan-hydroxyapatite (CH) is
developed by integrating the multifunctional properties of copper-doped iron
goethite (Fe(Cu)OOH) nanoparticles and a mesoporous SiO2 protective layer
(CH/FeCu@SiO2). The “cytoskeleton” CH scaﬀold stabilizes the nanoparticles
and supports tissue growth. The “chloroplast” Fe(Cu)OOH and the “ frustule”
SiO2 layer synergistically capture near-infrared (NIR) light to generate localized
mild hyperthermia (≈42 °C), with the release of Cu2+ to achieve antibacterial
eﬀects (>99%). In addition, Fe(Cu)OOH exhibits enzyme-like antioxidant
activity, scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) (69.20%) and thereby
promoting M2 macrophage polarization (1.64-fold), which protects stem
cells and creates osteogenic immune microenvironment. Moreover, the mild
hyperthermia and ion release upregulate the TGF-𝜷signaling pathway and
inhibit osteoclast diﬀerentiation, promoting vascularized bone regeneration
and defect repair (1.3-fold). Overall, this biomimetic scaﬀold, enabling
synergistic antibacterial, immunomodulatory, and osteogenic activities,
oﬀers a promising therapeutic strategy for infectious bone defects treatment.

1. Introduction
With the improvement in living conditions
and extended human lifespans, bone dis-
eases caused by trauma, tumors, chronic
inﬂammation, and infections have become
increasingly common and are now a ma-
jor contributor to global disability.[1] Clini-
cal treatments for bone defects often face
signiﬁcant challenges due to implant site
infections, as persistent inﬂammation and
bacterial bioﬁlm formation not only impede
bone regeneration but also risk implant
failure, endangering patient health.[2,3] Al-
though antibiotic-loaded implants (e.g.,
ciproﬂoxacin and gentamicin)[4] demon-
strate antibacterial potential, their limi-
tations, including the risk of antibiotic
resistance, challenges in achieving sus-
tained and localized drug release proﬁles,
potential cytotoxicity at elevated concen-
trations, and incomplete eradication of
bioﬁlm-associated infection, remain sig-
niﬁcant obstacles. Additionally, the tran-
sient therapeutic window of antibiotics of-
ten fails to synchronize with the pro-
longed osteogenic processes required for
bone regeneration, further complicating
clinical outcomes.[5,6] Therefore, develop-
ing intelligent bone repair materials that
integrate environment-responsive antibacterial properties, con-
trolled degradation, and angiogenic-osteogenic synergy has be-
come a critical area for advancement.
Photothermal therapy (PTT) has gained attention for its
spatiotemporal controllable antibacterial properties and heat-
stimulated osteogenesis,[7–9] with the generated thermal energy
promoting vasodilation, nutrient delivery, and upregulation of
osteogenic protein expression.[10] Iron oxides have emerged
as promising components for bone repair due to their cost-
eﬀectiveness, biocompatibility, and metabolic safety, while re-
leased iron ions during degradation regulate cellular metabolism
and support bone regeneration.[11–13] Nevertheless, their appli-
cation in PTT faces limitations: 1) Eﬀective antibacterial action
typically requires high temperatures (≥55 °C), risking heat
damage to healthy tissues, impairing bone repair, and potentially
inducing bacterial heat shock protein expression that reduces

Diatoms, as one of the most prevalent planktonic algae, con-
tribute 45% of the ocean’s primary productivity.[23] Their eﬃcient
light capture and CO2 absorption play critical roles in material
and energy cycling while oﬀering unique insights into designing
therapeutic scaﬀolds with photothermal stability and antibacte-
rial capabilities. The photosynthetic pigment arrays (e.g., chloro-
phyll a/c-fucoxanthin complexes) within their chloroplasts eﬃ-
ciently capture light transmitted through water layers for pho-
tosynthesis, converting light into chemical energy.[24] Cytoskele-
tal structures control chloroplast positioning and coordinate or-
ganelle distribution for normal cellular processes.[25] Carotenoids
in chloroplasts, along with antioxidant enzyme systems (e.g.,
superoxide dismutase (SOD), ascorbate peroxidase (APX)) in
other organelles, remove reactive oxygen species (ROS) to main-
tain metabolic stability.[26] Most critically, diatoms’ silica (SiO2)
microporous frustules provide mechanical protection, chemi-
cal and thermal stability, and resistance to microbial attacks.[27]
This hierarchical structure (frustule-cytoskeleton-chloroplast) en-
ables protection, environmental adaptation, and energy conver-
sion through synergistic interactions, oﬀering new concepts for
constructing biomimetic bone repair scaﬀolds.
Inspired by diatoms, we developed a cascaded therapeutic plat-
form based on a 3D porous chitosan-hydroxyapatite (CH) scaﬀold
by integrating the multifunctional properties of copper-doped
iron goethite (Fe(Cu)OOH) nanoparticles and a mesoporous
SiO2 protective layer (CH/FeCu@SiO2), enabling synergistic an-
tibacterial, immunomodulatory, and osteogenic activities. The
3D interconnected porous network of the CH scaﬀold not only
provided robust anchoring sites to stabilize Fe(Cu)OOH/SiO2
and prevent nanoparticle aggregation, but also created permis-
sive channels for neovascularization and bone tissue ingrowth,
while preserving structural integrity at the defect site. Acting
as a functional “chloroplast” core, Fe(Cu)OOH harvested near-
infrared (NIR) light to generate local mild hyperthermia (≈42 °C),
which synergized with the sustained release of Cu2+ ions to form
a dual antibacterial barrier—disrupting bacterial metabolism and
bioﬁlm integrity—while simultaneously promoting vasodilation
and enhancing oxygen perfusion. Moreover, Fe(Cu)OOH mim-
icked SOD and catalase (CAT) activities to scavenge excess ROS,
alleviating oxidative injury to osteogenic stem cells. In paral-
lel, it promoted a shift in macrophage polarization from a pro-
inﬂammatory M1 state to a reparative M2 state, suppressing
TNF-𝛼expression and upregulating IL-10 to reconstruct an im-
munologically favorable microenvironment for bone regenera-
tion. To enhance long-term stability and performance, the SiO2
“frustule” served as a protective physical barrier under acidic
infectious conditions, modulating Fe(Cu)OOH degradation and
controlling Cu2+ release kinetics. Additionally, its low thermal
conductivity and infrared reﬂectivity minimized heat dissipa-
tion, thereby improving photothermal conversion eﬃciency and
enabling precise local temperature regulation. This multilevel
biomimetic platform, leveraging antibacterial activity, immune
modulation, and mechanical support, ultimately achieved inte-
grated repair of infectious bone defects and oﬀers promising
insights for the treatment of other complex tissue pathologies
(Scheme 1).
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Characterization of Diatom-Inspired Scaﬀolds
Bone tissue engineering scaﬀolds require a bone-mimetic porous
architecture to provide optimal spatial guidance for neotissue
formation. 3D porous CH scaﬀolds with pore sizes ≈100 μm –
comparable to natural bone matrix[28] – were fabricated through
lyophilization (Figure 1a). Leveraging the chelation capacity of
amino and hydroxyl groups in chitosan molecules, we imple-
mented in situ mineralization of Fe(Cu)OOH “chloroplast-like”
nanostructures, followed by silica protective layer deposition
via chemical vapor deposition, resulting in functionally graded
CH/FeCu@SiO2 biomimetic composite scaﬀolds. The hierarchi-
cal porous architecture remained intact after functionalization,
with Fe(Cu)OOH nanoparticles and silica layers uniformly dis-
tributed across the chitosan matrix. Elemental mapping con-
ﬁrmed successful integration of Fe, Cu, Si, and C within the com-
posite system (Figure 1b), while Cu constitutes 2.1% of the metal-
lic elements (Table S1, Supporting Information). Compared to
pristine CH scaﬀolds, the biomimetic CH/FeCu@SiO2 exhibited
enhanced surface roughness, a critical topological feature known
to potentiate cellular adhesion and proliferation during bone re-
generation processes.
The chemical architecture of the scaﬀolds was further in-
vestigated by FTIR spectroscopy (Figure 1c). Characteristic ab-
sorption bands at 2874 cm−1 (C─H stretching vibrations of
methylene groups), along with 1646 cm−1 (asymmetric N─H de-
formation) and 3457 cm−1 (N─H stretching) from protonated
amine groups, conﬁrmed chitosan’s molecular framework. In
goethite (FeOOH)-mineralized scaﬀold (CH/Fe), Fe(Cu)OOH-
mineralized scaﬀold (CH/FeCu), and CH/FeCu@SiO2, the emer-
gence of FeOOH’s diagnostic “W”-shaped hydroxyl bending vi-
brations at 890 and 790 cm−1 provided direct evidence of iron
oxide mineralization. Additional spectral signatures at 603 and
565 cm−1 corresponded to phosphate (PO4
3−) species, while the
1469 cm−1 band conﬁrmed carbonate (CO3
2−) incorporation, col-
lectively verifying successful hydroxyapatite (HA) integration. Fi-
nally, the distinct Si─O stretching vibration at 1100 cm−1 substan-
tiated the presence of the silica “frustule”. The crystalline archi-
tecture of mineralization layer was analyzed by XRD (Figure 1d).
Pristine CH scaﬀolds exhibited characteristic HA diﬀraction pat-
terns, while CH/Fe and CH/FeCu displayed new diagnostic peaks
at 21.2°, 33.2°, 36.6°, and 53.2°, corresponding to the (110), (130),
(111), and (221) crystallographic planes of FeOOH, respectively.

Copper doping induced isostructural substitution of lattice iron
atoms without signiﬁcant alteration of the crystalline framework.
XPS survey spectra revealed characteristic signals in CH scaﬀolds
(Figure 1e), with distinct Fe 2p and Cu 2p peaks emerging in min-
eralized scaﬀolds. High-resolution Fe 2p spectra demonstrated
Fe2+ signatures, while Cu-doped variants exhibited Cu+ states in
their respective 2p spectra (Figure 1f; Figure S1, Supporting In-
formation). The electron interaction between Fe/Cu in diﬀerent
valence states might generate catalytic sites for ROS scavenging.
The physicochemical properties of bone tissue engineering
scaﬀolds including porosity, mechanical strength, and degrada-
tion kinetics critically inﬂuence their therapeutic eﬃcacy. Both
CH and biomimetic CH/FeCu@SiO2 scaﬀolds exhibited inter-
connected porous architectures, with porosities of 70% and
60.6% (Figure 1g), respectively. While mineralization slightly re-
duced pore volume, the retained hierarchical porosity remained
conducive to cellular inﬁltration and nutrient diﬀusion. Mechan-
ical characterization revealed substantial enhancements in load-
bearing capacity following mineralization. The biomimetic scaf-
fold demonstrated a 3.58-fold increase in compressive stress
(369.31 kPa) and a 2.81-fold elevation in Young’s modulus
(457.01 kPa) compared to pristine CH (Figure 1h). These op-
timized mechanical parameters enable the scaﬀold to main-
tain structural integrity under physiological loads while pro-
viding a stable mechano-microenvironment for osteogenic ac-
tivities. Notably, mineralization with the SiO2 armor slightly
decelerated scaﬀold degradation. After 42 days of immersion,
CH/FeCu@SiO2 exhibited a controlled mass loss of 24.4%, in-
dicating enhanced structural retention (Figure 1i). Moreover,
the SiO2 armor modulates the dissolution kinetics of embedded
metal hydroxide species. Compared to the unprotected CH/FeCu
scaﬀolds, CH/FeCu@SiO2 reduced the initial 24-h burst release
of ions by 42.5%, ultimately achieving a cumulative concen-
tration of 4.42 μg mL−1 over 14 days (Figure 1j). This stabil-
ity primarily stems from the protective role of the SiO2 shell,
which acts as a diﬀusion barrier that limits aqueous erosion
and enzymatic degradation. Simultaneously, the Si–O–Fe/Cu co-
valent interfacial bonding stabilizes the Fe(Cu)OOH nanolayer,
further enabling the sustained release of metal ions (Figure
S2 and Table S2, Supporting Information). The measured cop-
per concentrations in both groups remained below the cytotoxic
threshold, suggesting biocompatible ion release ranges. Grad-
ual exposure of metallic ions stimulates angiogenesis while mit-
igates acute oxidative damage—a balance crucial for successful
osseointegration.
The silica “frustule” synergistically ampliﬁes the photothermal
conversion eﬃciency of Fe(Cu)OOH. Infrared thermography re-
vealed distinct thermal responses under 808 nm NIR irradiation,
with CH/Fe and CH/FeCu scaﬀolds reaching 55 and 63.5 °C
respectively after 240 s exposure, while pristine CH showed
negligible temperature elevation. Remarkably, CH/FeCu@SiO2
achieved a peak temperature of 69.5 °C, demonstrating a 9.5%
enhancement over CH/FeCu (Figure 1k,l). This thermal am-
pliﬁcation arises from the frustule’s multifunctional architec-
ture: its mesoporous siliceous framework permits optical pen-
etration while conﬁning photon absorption to the internal
Fe(Cu)OOH layer, where non-radiative relaxation generates local-
ized heat. Concurrently, the silica shell’s intrinsic thermal insu-
lation properties restrict conductive heat dissipation and create a
greenhouse-like infrared trapping mechanism, reducing convec-
tive/radiative losses to the environment. Cyclic irradiation tests
(3 on/oﬀcycles) demonstrated <5% photothermal eﬃciency de-
cay, conﬁrming structural stability for repeated therapeutic ap-
plications (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Crucially, the
composite maintained superior photothermal performance im-
mersed in PBS, achieving a therapeutically optimal tempera-
ture beneﬁcial to promote vasodilation and angiogenic factor re-
lease without inducing protein denaturation (Figure 1m). This
aqueous milieu-stable photothermal response, coupled with spa-
tiotemporal temperature controllability via laser power modula-
tion, positions the biomimetic scaﬀold as an on-demand thermal
therapy platform for bone regeneration. Moreover, we investi-
gated the eﬀects of mineralization and siliﬁcation conditions on
the photothermal performance of scaﬀolds. An optimal mineral-
ization time of 6 h and siliﬁcation time of 24 h were achieved,
which combine suitable structures of Fe(Cu)OOH and thermal
insulation of the SiO2 armor (Figures S4–S6, Supporting Infor-
mation).
To address the oxidative stress microenvironment induced
by bacterial infection in bone defects, we further evaluated the
scaﬀold’s antioxidative enzyme-mimicking activity. In vivo, an-
tioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and
catalase (CAT) eﬀectively eliminate superoxide radicals (·O2
−)
and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), respectively. Analogously, the
CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR group exhibited pronounced multi-
enzyme mimetic antioxidant capacity, eliminating 84.5% of ·O2
−
and 80% of ABTS+ within 15 min (Figure S7, Supporting Infor-
mation), markedly outperforming other groups. Furthermore, in
CAT-like activity assays, Michaelis–Menten kinetic analysis re-
vealed that the CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR group possessed the low-
est Km and highest Vmax, indicating superior substrate aﬃnity
and catalytic eﬃciency toward H2O2 (Figure S8 and Table S3 Sup-
porting Information). This enhanced antioxidant performance is
attributed to the enzyme-like catalytic centers formed by the syn-
ergistic redox activity of Fe/Cu species, and the surface electron
transfer is further augmented by NIR-induced photothermal ef-
fects. Collectively, these features endow the scaﬀold with potent
radical-scavenging capability, highlighting its promise in mitigat-
ing oxidative stress and modulating the inﬂammatory microen-
vironment in infected bone defects.
2.2. In Vitro Antibacterial Activity Assessment
The anti-infective potential of biomimetic scaﬀolds was systemat-
ically evaluated against pathogens S. aureus and E. coli, given the
clinical urgency of addressing implant-associated osteomyelitis.
In contrast to the negligible bactericidal activity of intrinsic CH
and CH/Fe, NIR irradiation enhanced the antibacterial eﬃciency
of CH/Fe to 74.89% and 78.84% against respective pathogen
(Figure 2a,b). Remarkably, CH/FeCu and CH/FeCu@SiO2 scaf-
folds demonstrated copper-dependent antibacterial superiority,
achieving >90% bacteria eradication (Figure 2c–f). All scaﬀolds
demonstrate remarkable antibacterial activity following miner-
alization and siliﬁcation (Figures S9–S11, Supporting Informa-
tion). Compared to conventional PTT protocols, this multimodal
strategy—integrating chemodynamic therapy (CDT) with PTT—
enables pathogen elimination at sub-cytotoxic thermal doses

systematically investigated through morphological and mem-

The bactericidal mechanism involves synergistic interplay be-
tween sustained Cu2+ liberation from Fe(Cu)OOH and NIR-
activated PTT. Continuous copper ion release facilitates electro-
static interactions with anionic bacterial membranes, disrupting
transmembrane potential and metabolic homeostasis through
metalloprotein interference.[26] Localized hyperthermia (≈42 °C)
alters membrane ﬂuidity dynamics, compromising structural in-
tegrity to accelerate Cu2+ inﬂux and cytoplasmic eﬄux. Intracel-
lular copper accumulation triggers oxidative stress cascades via
Fenton-like reactions, inducing protein denaturation and double-
strand DNA lesion.[27,28]
The recalcitrant nature of bioﬁlm-mediated infections was sys-
tematically addressed through engineered therapeutic strategies.
Mature bioﬁlms of S. aureus and E. coli, characterized by ro-
bust extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrices that im-
pede antibiotic penetration and promote antimicrobial resis-
tance, were subjected to combinatorial therapy. Live/dead-stained
bioﬁlms demonstrated that CH/FeCu@SiO2 with NIR irradia-
tion induced bioﬁlm disintegration, achieving 88% biomass re-
duction (Figure 2h–j). Structural analyses revealed marked EPS
matrix fragmentation and loss of bacterial cell-cell cohesion in
treated groups. Quantitative crystal violet assays corroborated
these ﬁndings, showing 88.6% and 76.3% bioﬁlm inhibition rates
for S. aureus and E. coli, under combined CDT/PTT treatment—a
3-fold enhancement over monotherapeutic modalities in group
CH/FeCu and CH/FeCu@SiO2 (Figure 2k,l; Figure S12, Sup-
porting Information). This therapeutic synergy stemmed from
photothermally induced localized hyperthermia, which disrupted
bioﬁlm matrices and bacterial cellular architecture through
thermal degradation of extracellular polymeric substances and
lipid bilayer ﬂuidization.[29] The enhanced membrane per-
meability enabled deep Cu2+ inﬁltration into bioﬁlm niches,
where intracellular accumulation exacerbated oxidative stress
via Fenton-like reactions, synergistically eradicating embed-
ded pathogens while disassembling bioﬁlm structural integrity
(Figure 2m).[30]
This approach achieved bioﬁlm eradication at relatively low
copper concentrations, mitigating resistance development risks
while maintaining mammalian cell viability. The dual physical
disruption (thermal) and biochemical attack (ROS/metabolic in-
terference) paradigm presents a solution for managing implant-
associated osteomyelitis without provoking inﬂammatory cas-
cades.
2.3. In Vitro ROS Scavenging and Stem Cell Protection
Upon conﬁrming that the CH/FeCu@SiO2 exhibited outstand-
ing and multifaceted antioxidative enzyme-like activity, we pro-
ceeded to evaluate the potential capacity to scavenge ROS and
protect stem cells using bone marrow-derived mesenchymal
stem cells (BMSCs) as experimental models. Based on estimated
physiological levels of H2O2 during inﬂammation, a concentra-
tion of 100 μM H2O2 was chosen.[31,32] Intracellular ROS levels
were quantiﬁed using the probe 2,7-dichlorodihydroﬂuorescein
diacetate (DCFH-DA). A marked reduction in green ﬂuores-
cence signal was observed in several treatment groups espe-
cially CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR, whose ROS levels were reduced
by ≈69.2% relative to the H2O2 group (Figure 3a,b; Figure
S13, Supporting Information). The photothermal-induced tem-
perature elevation further enhanced the enzyme-mimicking ac-
tivity of Fe(Cu)OOH, leading to the rapid decomposition of
H2O2 and other ROS. Subsequently, ROS-induced stem cell
damage was further demonstrated by the biomarker of oxida-
tive/nitrosation stress 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), indicating the
CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR treatment eﬀectively protects BMSCs
from ROS-induced injury (Figure S14, Supporting Information).
Excessive ROS induces DNA damage in BMSCs, often re-
sulting in cell death especially apoptosis when the cellular in-
jury becomes severe.[33] Therefore, we examined the potential of

2.4. In Vitro Immunomodulation and Macrophage Activation
Eliminating ROS to suppress oxidative stress can promote
the polarization of M1 macrophages to M2 macrophages,
thereby shaping an osteogenic microenvironment through
immunomodulation.[34] We then investigated immunomodula-
tion and macrophage activation using RAW 264.7 macrophages
pre-treated with 50 ng mL−1 lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Intense
DCFH-DA ﬂuorescence was detected under LPS stimulation, in-
dicating a high level of ROS generation, while a signiﬁcant re-
duction in ﬂuorescence intensity was observed in macrophages
shielded by NIR-assisted CH/FeCu@SiO2, demonstrating a po-
tent ROS scavenging eﬀect, which was conﬁrmed by ﬂow cytom-
etry (Figure 4a–c; Figure S26, Supporting Information). Then,
intracellular ROS was also examined via 4-HNE, and signiﬁ-
cantly increased ﬂuorescence was noted in the LPS group, while
CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR treatment signiﬁcantly decreased the
ROS level caused by LPS (Figure 4d,e). The redox cycling of
Fe(Cu)OOH, coupled with the van der Waals-mediated adsorp-
tion of LPS by silanol groups on SiO2, confers remarkable ROS-
scavenging eﬃciency. This synergistic interplay eﬀectively sup-
presses LPS-induced secondary ROS generation, thereby shield-
ing cells from oxidative damage and delivering immunomodula-
tory signals during the early stages of bone regeneration.
To examine macrophage polarization, the morphology of
the RAW 264.7 macrophage was ﬁrst analyzed. As shown in
Figure 4f, LPS-exposed macrophages displayed a spherically
spiny shape, resembling the M1 phenotype.[35] In contrast,
macrophages treated with CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR developed
pseudopod-like structures and elongated forms, suggesting a
shift toward the M2 phenotype.[36] Immunoﬂuorescence stain-
ing validated this transition, as Figure 4g,h showed strong
red ﬂuorescence of iNOS (M1 marker) under LPS treatment,
while CD206 (M2 marker) expression remained minimal. Con-
versely, the CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR group exhibited. a stronger
CD206 signal, reﬂecting enhanced M2 activation attributed to the
immunomodulatory eﬀects of CH/FeCu@SiO2 combined with
NIR. Flow cytometry (Figure 4i; Figures S27 and S28, Supporting
Information) further showed a signiﬁcantly higher proportion of
CD206+ M2 macrophages in the CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR group,
with an increase of ≈1.64-fold compared to the LPS group, con-
ﬁrming its superior capability to promote M2 macrophage polar-
ization.
As for anti-inﬂammatory eﬀects, utilizing qRT-PCR and West-
ern blot (Figure 4j,k; Table S4, Supporting Information), the
ﬁndings revealed M1-related genes were highly expressed with
LPS treatment, whereas M2-related genes were signiﬁcantly sup-
pressed. In contrast, macrophages treated with CH/FeCu@SiO2
+ NIR exhibited reduced proinﬂammatory M1-related gene
expression and increased anti-inﬂammatory M2-related gene
expression compared to the control group. Therefore, the
CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR group achieved superior immunomodu-
latory performance, eﬀectively promoting M2 macrophage polar-
ization under inﬂammatory conditions and contributing to the
formation of a bone-regenerative microenvironment (Figure 4l).

2.6. In Vivo Bone Regeneration Therapeutic Eﬃcacy
Given the remarkable in vitro anti-infection eﬃcacy and os-
teogenic bioactivity of bioinspired scaﬀolds, an infected femoral
defect rat model was established to further investigate the NIR-
mediated anti-infection and osteogenesis performance of the
CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR treatment in vivo (Figure 6a). Con-
sistent with the in vitro ﬁndings, the photothermal behavior
of CH/FeCu@SiO2 scaﬀolds in vivo exhibited power density-
dependent characteristics (Figure 6b). A laser power density of
2.0 W cm−2 was utilized (Figure 6c), and we evaluated the skin
temperature elevation under NIR irradiation at a laser power
density of 2.0 W cm−2, whose histological analysis conﬁrmed
that this irradiation did not cause any observable skin damage
(Figures S49 and S50, Supporting Information). Within 300 s,
the CH group temperature increased slightly from 34.0 to
37.4 °C, whereas the CH/FeCu@SiO2 group exhibited a rapid
rise from 34.3 to 41.8 °C and stabilized under further irradiation
(Figure 6d), which also conﬁrmed the ability of NIR light to pene-
trate skin tissue and induce CH/FeCu@SiO2 scaﬀold heating in
vivo.
After implantation for 2, 4, and 8 weeks, bone defects
in the CH groups were substantially larger compared to
those in CH/FeCu@SiO2 groups at all time points, and the
CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR group demonstrated superior repair
outcomes, with defects nearly healed after 8 weeks of implan-
tation (Figure 6e). To visualize osteogenesis, bone defect mi-
crostructures were reconstructed via micro-computed tomogra-
phy (micro-CT) imaging. The CH group with NIR showed infe-
rior repair compared to CH/FeCu@SiO2 group with NIR, con-
ﬁrming the importance of microenvironment-adaptive proper-
ties of the implants in promoting infected bone defect heal-
ing. Additionally, the combined eﬀects of Fe3+ ions and ROS
scavenging enhanced both interfacial bioactivity and osteoin-
ductivity, as highlighted by the superior biomineralization abil-
ity of CH/FeCu@SiO2 scaﬀolds. Under NIR irradiation, the
CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR group exhibited more newly formed
bone than the CH/FeCu@SiO2 group, supporting the hypothe-
sis that moderate photothermal stimulation is conducive to bone
regeneration (Figure 6f). From weeks 4 to 8, the CH/FeCu@SiO2
+ NIR group demonstrated signiﬁcantly enhanced bone volume
relative to tissue volume (BV/TV), along with elevated trabecular
number (Tb.N) and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), while exhibit-
ing reduced trabecular spacing (Tb.Sp) compared to the other
groups (Figure 6g–j). Notably, the scaﬀold under NIR achieved
a BV/TV value in bone defect repair that was 1.3 times higher
than that of the CH scaﬀold with NIR. Furthermore, fewer S. au-
reus colonies were detected in the CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR group
compared to other groups, demonstrating its potential to prevent
infection during bone regeneration (Figure 6k; Figures S51 and
S52, Supporting Information). Gram staining and H&E staining
showed that in the CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR group no visible bacte-
rial structures were detected after 7 days and inﬂammatory inﬁl-
tration was alleviated, whereas the control group exhibited dense
bacterial colonization and pronounced infection-related inﬂam-
mation (Figure S53, Supporting Information). These results sug-
gested that CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR group possessed excellent os-
teogenic ability in infected femoral defect (Figure 6l).

2.7. In Vivo Immunomodulatory Performance in Osteogenic
Microenvironment Formation
The involvement of immunomodulation plays pivotal roles in
establishing an osteogenic microenvironment for bone defect
repair.[39] Considering that macrophages are the central func-
tional cells in immune responses and are closely tied to the
repair phase, the representative markers of M1-type (iNOS)
macrophages (Figure S57, Supporting Information) and M2-type
(CD163) macrophages (Figure 7e,i) were stained after 8 weeks of
implantation, revealing more iNOS-positive and fewer CD163-
positive cells in defect sites of the CH group and suggesting a
heightened inﬂammatory state accompanied by accelerated in-
ﬁltration of pro-inﬂammatory M1 macrophages. Comparatively,
in the CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR group, a substantial population of
pro-healing M2 macrophages, characterized by increased CD163
expression, was observed in defect region. Furthermore, we ex-
plored the broader immune context and potential eﬀects of the
scaﬀold on other immune cell populations. The results indi-
cated that changes in the pro-inﬂammatory cytokine TNF-𝛼mir-
rored M1 macrophage ﬂuctuations, while changes in the anti-
inﬂammatory cytokine IL-10 paralleled M2 macrophage ﬂuctu-
ations (Figure S58, Supporting Information), and the scaﬀold
had no signiﬁcant eﬀect on the recruitment of B cells, T cells,
or neutrophils (Figure S59, Supporting Information). These ﬁnd-
ings indicate that CH/FeCu@SiO2 scaﬀolds combined with NIR
eﬃciently modulate the inﬂammatory response toward an M2-
dominant regenerative state.
Given that immunomodulation contributes to vascular de-
velopment, which is essential for generating an osteogenic
microenvironment, immunoﬂuorescence staining of angio-
genic gene CD31 was utilized, demonstrating that NIR-
treated CH/FeCu@SiO2 scaﬀolds exhibited the most eﬀec-
tive angiogenesis and notable immunomodulatory activity,
as evidenced by highly expressed CD31 ﬂuorescence signals
(Figure 7d,h; Figure S60, Supporting Information). This indi-
cates that CH/FeCu@SiO2 scaﬀolds, under moderate photother-
mal activation, eﬀectively promote angiogenesis, thereby con-
tributing to the establishment of an osteoimmunomodulatory
microenvironment.
Bone remodeling requires a precise interplay between
osteoblast-mediated bone formation and osteoclast-driven re-
sorption that is also a critical component in shaping the os-
teogenic microenvironment. As illustrated in Figure S61 (Sup-
porting Information), cells underwent tartrate-resistant acid
phosphatase (TRAP) staining, the marker speciﬁcally expressed
by osteoclasts, revealing a signiﬁcant reduction in TRAP-positive
osteoclasts in the CH/FeCu@SiO2 + NIR group and suggest-
ing that the scaﬀold reduced inﬂammatory osteoclast activity,
thereby promoting new bone formation and enhancing bone vol-
ume. Additionally, the histological safety of the groups was eval-
uated by performing H&E staining of major organs. As illus-
trated in Figures S62 and S63 and Table S5 (Supporting Infor-
mation), no signiﬁcant inﬂammatory pathological abnormality
or distinct copper levels were observed in the major organs, in-
dicating minimal systemic toxicity after the implantation proce-
dure. Collectively, these in vivo ﬁndings indicated that the favor-
able osteoimmunomodulatory microenvironment provided by
the CH/FeCu@SiO2 scaﬀold and NIR stimulation synergistically
accelerated bone repair by triggering a range of osteogenic and
angiogenic pathways.
3. Conclusion
In conclusion, inspired by the structure of diatoms, we devel-
oped a hierarchical, multifunctional porous bone repair scaf-
fold CH/FeCu@SiO2. On chitosan-hydroxyapatite scaﬀolds that
mimicked a “cytoskeleton”, we performed in situ mineraliza-
tion of “chloroplast” Fe(Cu)OOH and subsequently encapsulated
it with mesoporous SiO2 as a “frustule” that eﬀectively stabi-
lized the material in acidic infectious environments and enabled
sustained release of copper ions. Under NIR, Fe(Cu)OOH and
the SiO2 layer synergistically capture light energy, and together
with the release of Cu2+, the system exhibits excellent antibacte-
rial activity at low temperatures (>99%). Moreover, the enzyme-
like antioxidant ability of Fe(Cu)OOH reduces ROS (69.2%),
which further facilitates a 1.64-fold increase in macrophage po-
larization toward the M2 phenotype, protecting stem cells and
cultivating an immune microenvironment for bone regenera-
tion. Meanwhile, the mild hyperthermia and ion release activate
the TGF-𝛽signaling pathway and inhibit osteoclast diﬀerentia-
tion, signiﬁcantly enhancing vascularized bone regeneration and

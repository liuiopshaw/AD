ABSTRACT: Copper nanoclusters (CuNCs) are emerging as an interesting class of
materials for various biomedical applications. In this work, we have designed highly stable
nucleobase-capped luminescent CuNCs and studied the eﬀect of substituents on the cluster
composition and photophysical properties. The NCs exhibit exceptional stability in
ambient atmosphere and show signiﬁcant variation in the emission properties with a change
in position of substituents on the ligand, thiouracil. This study represents the ﬁrst example
of a nanocluster that functionally mimics the activity of a major antioxidant enzyme,
superoxide dismutase (SOD). In addition to their enzyme-mimetic activity, the CuNCs
evince controlled release of nitric oxide (NO), a key gaseous molecule of endothelial
system from S-nitrosothiol, S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO). Further, to a greater
signiﬁcance, these luminescent CuNCs are readily taken up by the mammalian cells and
exhibit low toxicity. The superoxide dismutase and NO releasing activity of the ﬂuorescent,
biocompatible copper nanoclusters suggest their potential application in both therapeutics
and bioimaging.
KEYWORDS: superoxide dismutase, nitric oxide, nanoclusters, denitrosylation, luminescence
1. INTRODUCTION
Metal-based nanoclusters (NCs) with molecule-like properties
are of paramount importance in biomedical ﬁeld due to their
ultrasmall size, unique optical properties, and excellent
biocompatibility.1−3 Over the past decade, synthesis and
applications of gold, silver, and copper metal nanoclusters
have been extensively investigated.4,5 Among these, the
synthesis and applicability of noble metal nanoclusters (i.e.,
Au and Ag nanoclusters) are most examined owing to material
properties such as exceptional electronic structures, wavelength
selective plasmon absorption resonances, conductivity, etc.
More speciﬁcally, the thiol-capped noble metal nanoclusters
have emerged as a paradigm of atomically precise materials
that ﬁnd immense application in diverse ﬁelds ranging from
catalysis to environmental and biomedical applications.6,7
However, in recent years, interest in copper atom-stabilized
nanoclusters (CuNCs) is growing rapidly due to its low cost
and high earth abundance. Nevertheless, the poor stability,
luminescence quenching or bleaching of CuNCs in an
oxidizing environment and ability to produce reactive oxygen
species (ROS) in cells, curtails their application in bioimaging,
sensing, and drug delivery.8 Further, diﬀerent synthetic
strategies have been employed using various capping ligands
such as thiolated small molecule, proteins, DNA, and
polymers.9 However, these synthetic methodologies are
complex and do not yield stable, ultrasmall sized NCs with
high quantum yields.10,11 Recently, the synthesis of stable,
ultrasmall luminescent copper NCs showing aggregation
induced emission (AIE) has been reported,12−16 but their
biological applications are not well-explored. Similar to their
noble metal analogues, the major reasons accounting for the
luminescent properties of CuNCs include quantum conﬁne-
ment, ligand to metal charge transfer (LMCT)/ligand to metal
to metal charge transfer (LMMCT).9,17−22 More importantly,
the luminescent nanoclusters with aggregation induced
emission (AIE) characteristics are of greater relevance as
compared to the nanoclusters exhibiting aggregation induced
quenching (ACQ).9,17−22 Furthermore, the restricted intra-
molecular vibration and rotation along with strong cuprophilic
interaction between CuNCs in the aggregated state accounts
for the bright luminescence properties, long ﬂuorescence
lifetime, and increased optical and chemical stability of the
reported AIE nanoclusters.23 Such stable multiatom encom-
passing NCs holds promise in various biomedical applications
due to their tunable properties.

In general, the NCs attract attention in the ﬁelds of
biosensing, bioimaging, biolabeling, and targeted delivery.
Meanwhile, in recent years, there has been a thriving interest in
the development of enzyme mimetic metal nanoclusters as
low-cost alternative for natural enzymes. The development of
antioxidant enzyme mimetics attract signiﬁcant interest as
ROS-mediated oxidative stress contributes to various patho-
logical conditions such as cancer, atherosclerosis, diabetes
etc.24−28 For example, protein and amino acid stabilized NCs
are reported to exhibit peroxidase mimetic activity.29,30
However, fewer reports of artiﬁcial enzymes especially
antioxidant enzyme mimetic NCs, in addition to their lower
catalytic eﬃciency remains major drawback. Further, the
enzyme mimetic activity of NCs is limited by their speciﬁc
catalytic function, whereas natural enzymes eﬃcaciously
perform a cascade of catalytic and noncatalytic functions in a
complex cellular environment.31,32 For instance, the antiox-
idant enzyme, superoxide dismutase (SOD) in addition to its
catalytic activity of dismutating superoxide radical, also
regulates the release of nitric oxide (NO) from nitrosylated
thiol, S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO), in cells.33,34 Therefore,
NCs capable of exhibiting multiple functions similar to the
cellular enzymes are of greater signiﬁcance. In this work, we
report the synthesis of thiol-modiﬁed nucleobase-capped
biocompatible CuNCs with intrinsic bright luminescent
property. The investigations reveal the ability of these NCs
to mimic the activity of primary antioxidant enzyme, SOD.
Furthermore, we also show that these nanoclusters similar to
SOD enzyme exhibits controlled release of NO, a critical
signaling molecule of endothelium from nitrosylated thiols.
This is the ﬁrst example of a NC capable of mimicking a crucial
antioxidant enzyme and performing multiple functions
analogues to the cellular enzymes.
2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
2.1. Chemicals. All reagents used in the study were of analytical
grade and used directly without any further puriﬁcation. Copper
sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O) and methanol were obtained
from S.D. Fine Chemicals Pvt. Ltd. 5-Methyl-2-thiouracil
(C5H6N2OS) was purchased from Lancaster Synthesis. 6-Methyl-2-
thiouracil (C5H6N2OS) was obtained from TCI. The SOD-assay kit,
Sulforhodamine B (SRB), glutathione (GSH), sodium nitrite
(NaNO2), sodium borohydride, and Rhodamine 6G were purchased
from Sigma-Aldrich. Milli-Q water was utilized for the synthesis of
nanoclusters and various experiments.
2.2. Synthesis of CuNCs@5MT. The synthesis of CuNCs@5MT
was carried out by reacting 5-methyl-2-thiouracil (5MT) (100 mg,
0.7033 mmol) with CuSO4·5H2O (175.60 mg, 0.7033 mmol) in
methanol (10 mL). The reaction mixture was allowed to stir at 34 °C
for 3 h. The progress of the reaction was monitored visually. The
color of the reaction mixture changes from dark brown to green,
ﬁnally to pale yellow with completion of reaction. The pale yellow
colored Cu(I)-5MT complex obtained was further reduced with
sodium borohydride (266.04 mg, 7.03 mmol) that resulted in the
formation of CuNCs@5MT as oﬀ-white precipitate. The obtained
precipitate was washed several times with methanol and water to
remove excess metal salt and unreacted ligand. CuNCs@5MT was
dried in air and stored at room temperature. ESI-MS: [Cu3(5MT)4 +
Li]+ m/z: 766.05.
2.3. Synthesis of CuNCs@6MT. In a 100 mL round-bottom ﬂask,
6-methyl-2-thiouracil (6MT) (100 mg, 0.7033 mmol) was dissolved
in DMF (7 mL). CuSO4·5H2O (175.60 mg, 0.7033 mmol) solution
was separately dissolved in 15 mL methanol. CuSO4·5H2O solution
was then slowly added in to 6MT solution with constant stirring, the
reaction mixture was further allowed to stir for 3 h at 34 °C. The
reaction mixture’s color changes from dark brown to pale green and
ﬁnally to pink. The pink-colored Cu(I)-6MT complex was reduced
with sodium borohydride to produce CuNCs@6MT as a dirty white
precipitate. CuNCs@6MT was centrifuged and washed many times
with DMF and methanol to remove excess metal salt and unreacted
ligand. CuNCs@6MT was then dried in air and stored at room
temperature. ESI-MS: [Cu2(6MT)2 + Na]+ m/z: 433.04.
2.4. Characterization Techniques. UV−visible spectra were
recorded on Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer. Fluorescence
emission spectra were obtained from Horiba Jobin Yvon, Fluoromax-4
spectroﬂuorometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data
were obtained from Axis Ultra DLD. Scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) images were recorded on Zeiss Ultra 55 and transmission
electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained from TEM-
TITAN, Themis 60−300 kV. The Zeta potential was determined
using Malvern Instruments-Zetasizer Nanoseries-ZEN 3690. The
Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) and Barrett−Joyner−Halenda
(BJH) measurements were carried out using Micromeritics ASAP
2020 instrument. Confocal images were captured on Zeiss LSM880
(Airyscan) confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and the
images were processed from Zen v2.3 (blue edition) software.

CuNCs@5MT and CuNCs@6MT were determined relative to
Rhodamine 6G (Rh6G).35−37 The ﬂuorescence quantum yield (Φf)
of Rhodamine 6G was reported to be 95% in ethanol solvent.

2.6. SOD-like Activity of CuNCs. The superoxide dismutase-like
activity of the CuNCs was determined using SOD-assay kit (Sigma)
as per manufacturer’s instructions. Brieﬂy, superoxide radicals (O2̇−)
that are generated by xanthine/xanthine oxidase system reduces the
tetrazolium salt, WST-1, to spectrophotometrically detectable
formazan at 440 nm.38 The water-soluble formazan produced can
be visualized by formation of yellow-colored solution. A decrease in
the color development corresponding to reduction in the percentage
of formazan produced is directly proportional to lesser superoxide
anions and thus, higher SOD-like activity of materials. The CuNCs
were well-dispersed in water using probe sonicator before performing
enzyme-like activity.
2.7. Synthesis of S-Nitrosoglutathione (GSNO). GSNO was
synthesized following the literature protocol.39 In brief, 0.325 mmol
(100 mg) of glutathione (GSH) was dissolved in 385 μL of milli-Q
water followed by addition of 38 μL of concentrated hydrochloric
acid. The reaction mixture was stirred in ice bath maintaining the pH
of the solution between 1 and 2. Followed by complete dissolution of
GSH, 0.325 mmol (23 mg) of sodium nitrite was added in dark
condition and the solution was stirred for another 10 min. The red-
colored solution obtained was stored in amber colored vial at −80 °C.
2.8. NO-Releasing Activity of NCs from GSNO. The
denitrosylation of GSNO was monitored by measuring a decrease
in the absorbance of GSNO at 335 nm (the molar extinction
coeﬃcient of GSNO: ε335 = 922 M−1 cm−1)39 spectrophotometrically
using a UV−vis spectrophotometer, operating in kinetic mode. A
simultaneous increase in the absorbance at 270 nm corresponding to
GSSG can also be observed. The reaction mixture contained CuNCs
(50 μM, 5 mol % of substrate) and GSNO (1 mM) in sodium
phosphate buﬀer (50 mM, pH 7.4) at 37 °C. The stock solution of
nanoclusters was prepared by sonicating them in milli-Q water for 1 h

2.9. Cell Culture Conditions. Human liver hepatocellular
carcinoma (HepG2) cells were procured from American Type
Culture Collection (ATCC), Washington, DC, USA. The cells were
grown in minimum essential medium (MEM) supplemented with
10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2.2 g L−1 of sodium bicarbonate,

at 37 °C.
2.10. Cell Viability Assay and Confocal Imaging. The cellular
viability of HepG2 cells in the presence of CuNCs@5MT and
CuNCs@6MT was determined using sulforhodamine B (SRB)
colorimetric assay. The cytotoxicity assay and confocal imaging
studies were carried out following similar protocol reported in our
previous study.14
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Synthesis and Characterization. Herein, we have
designed CuNCs stabilized by ligands, 5-methyl-2-thiouracil
(5MT), and 6-methyl-2-thiouracil (6-MT), resulting in the
formation of CuNCs@5MT and CuNCs@6MT, respectively
(Figure 1a and Scheme S1). The radical scavenging property,
interesting excited state photophysical properties and good
biocompatibility of the pyrimidine derivatives prompted us to
utilize them as a capping ligand for the synthesis of
CuNCs.40−42 The initially synthesized precursor Cu(I)
complexes exhibited micron-sized spherical ﬂakes and leaﬂet-
like assembly for Cu(I)-5MT and Cu(I)-6MT, respectively.
The morphologies of the precursor complexes were ascertained
by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission
electron microscopy (TEM) images (Figure S1). Subse-
quently, the Cu(I) complexes were subjected to reduction by
NaBH4 to obtain ultrasmall sized copper nanoclusters. The
chemical composition of these nanoclusters was analyzed by
electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) and was
found to be Cu3(5MT)4 and Cu2(6MT)2 for CuNCs@5MT
and CuNCs@6MT respectively (Figure 1b−c). The trans-
mission electron microscopy (TEM) images of CuNCs@5MT
and CuNCs@6MT show a few nanometer-sized particles in
the aggregated state (Figure 2a−b and S2). The surface
properties and oxidation state of the metal center in CuNCs
was determined by utilizing X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS). The high-intensity peaks in the region of 932.2 and
952.9 eV corresponding to Cu 2p3/2 and Cu 2p1/2 are in good
agreement with the literature values and conﬁrms the presence
of Cu in Cu(I)/Cu(0) states (Figure 2c−d).12−14 The absence
of a satellite peak around 940−942 eV corresponding to
Cu(II) ions attests the purity in oxidation state of the metal ion
in the NCs (inset Figure 2c−d).43 Further, the speciﬁc surface
areas of CuNCs@5MT and CuNCs@6MT as determined by
Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) were found to be 13.16 and
15.09 m2/g, respectively (Figure S3). The surface charge (i.e.,
zeta potential, ζ) of CuNCs@5MT was observed to be −30.5
± 4.5 mV and for CuNCs@6MT was −8.5 ± 4.45 mV (Figure
S4). The large negative value of CuNCs@5MT indicates less
tendency to aggregate as compared to CuNCs@6MT. The
UV−vis spectra of CuNCs@5MT and CuNCs@6MT shows a
broad absorption band around 250−420 nm (Figure S5).
However, the nonappearance of a surface plasmon resonance
(SPR) band at around 550 nm in the UV−vis spectra conﬁrms
the formation of nanoclusters instead of nanoparticles.14,44

3.2. Superoxide Dismutase-like Activity of CuNCs.
The enzyme superoxide dismutase (SOD) converts superoxide
radicals (O2
−•) to hydrogen peroxide and oxygen in a two-step
process and tightly regulates the cellular redox homeostasis.
More speciﬁcally, Cu−Zn−SOD forms the primary defense
line in the mammalian cells against oxidative/nitrosative
stress.46−53 In addition, one of the capping agent used in this
study, 6-MT, is previously reported to signiﬁcantly increase the
levels of Mn-SOD in thyroid tissues of rat model.54 Therefore,
we employed a colorimetric assay to determine the SOD-like
activity of these NCs.38 In brief, the xanthine-xanthine oxidase
system generates superoxide radical that reduces the
tetrazolium salt, WST-1 (2-(4-iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-
5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium) to spectrophotometri-
cally detectable formazan (Figure 3a). An inhibition in the
formation of yellow-colored formazan was observed in the
presence of water-dispersible CuNCs under physiologically
relevant conditions. The SOD-like activity increases with
increasing concentration of the NCs which is indicated by
decrease in the percentage of formazan production at 440 nm
(Figure 3b−c). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration
(IC50 value) of CuNCs@5MT and CuNCs@6MT was found
to be 106.4 and 87.42 μM, respectively. The SOD mimetic
activity of CuNCs could be supposedly due to the presence of
multiple redox active copper centers in NC, similar to the
naturally occurring Cu−Zn SOD enzyme. Such NCs with
SOD-like activity can be of therapeutic signiﬁcance in oxidative
stress mediated disorders.
3.3. Denitrosylation of S-Nitrosoglutathione by
CuNCs. In cells, SOD is known to control the levels of NO
by carrying out denitrosylation reactions. Also, copper based
enzymes, complexes, and materials are known to catalyze the
denitrosylation reaction.33,34,55−57 However, the release of NO
from nitrosothiols such as S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO) by
copper ions is more stoichiometric than catalytic.58 Therefore,
development of copper-based complexes and nanomaterials
that can catalyze the release of NO from GSNO is important.
In this study, we investigated the ability of the synthesized
CuNCs to catalyze the denitrosylation reaction of S-nitro-
sothiol, GSNO (Figure 4a), and monitored the reaction
progress by UV−vis spectrophotometry.58−62 We utilized S-
nitrosoglutathione in our study since it is an S-nitrosated
derivative of most abundant cellular thiol, glutathione (GSH).
The decrease in the absorbance of GSNO with time at 335 nm
indicates an eﬀective NO releasing ability of these nanoclusters
from nitrosylated thiol (Figure 4b). A parallel increase in the
absorbance at 270 nm corresponding to the formation of
GSSG further conﬁrms the disintegration of NO from GSNO
(Figure 4c−d).58−62 The autoxidation of GSNO was very less
as compared to the oxidation in the presence of nanoclusters
that eﬃcaciously mediates the denitrosylation (Figure S8).
Similar to the previously reported examples, the fast shuﬄing
of oxidation states at the copper center might be responsible
for release of nitric oxide from GSNO at physiological pH.56
3.4. Cytotoxicity and Luminescence Property in
Mammalian Cells. Facile oxidation of copper nanoparticles
(CuNPs) and copper nanoclusters (CuNCs) pose a major
hurdle in the development of copper-based materials for
biological applications.63−65 The synthesized nanoclusters were
found to be highly stable and least prone to oxidation over a
period of months in ambient atmosphere. Further, we
investigated the cytotoxicity of these NCs in human liver
carcinoma cells, HepG2 by utilizing classic Sulforhodamine B
(SRB) assay.65 The cells were incubated with diﬀerent
concentrations of CuNCs (CuNCs@5MT and CuNCs@
6MT) for 24 h followed by colorimetric analysis. The cell
viability was not aﬀected even at higher concentrations (IC50 >
100 μM) of CuNCs indicating good biocompatibility of these
NCs in cellular environment (Figure 5). The ﬂuorescent
copper-based nanoclusters are reported to have potential
application in biosensing.66−70 Therefore, we evaluated the
extent of uptake and ﬂuorescent property of these clusters in
cells by utilizing confocal laser scanning microscopy.
The confocal images were captured after incubating the
HepG2 cells with or without CuNCs for 4 h. The NCs
incubated cells show bright red emission emanating from
cytoplasm, whereas no noticeable emission was observed in the
absence of CuNCs (Figure 6). The images indicate localization
of NCs exclusively in cytosol. The inherent ﬂuorescence
property of these NCs which is retained in the mammalian
cells can be exploited for understanding their localization and
bioimaging in addition to their implication in therapy.
5. CONCLUSIONS
In this paper, we demonstrate the synthesis of highly stable
nucleobase capped luminescent CuNCs with intrinsic ability to
mimic the activity of major antioxidant enzyme, superoxide
dismutase. Interestingly, these nanoclusters also release NO
from S-nitrosothiol, GSNO, in a controlled manner similar to
the SOD enzyme. This is the ﬁrst example of a nanocluster

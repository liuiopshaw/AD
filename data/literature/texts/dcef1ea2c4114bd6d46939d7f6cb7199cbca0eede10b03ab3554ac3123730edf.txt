Metal nanoclusters (MNCs) have ultrasmall size (3 nm), precise molecular structure, strong
luminescence, size-sensitive physicochemical properties, engineerable metallic composition, and rich
yet tailorable surface chemistry, enabling them to be promising for the construction of luminescent sen-
sors for diversiﬁed sensing applications in ﬁelds of environmental protection, food safety, agriculture, dis-
ease diagnosis, biomarker identiﬁcation, etc. However, a systematic summary is urgently needed to
correlate the physicochemical properties of MNCs with their inﬂuences on sensing applications, to sum-
marize the synthetic strategies of highly luminescent MNCs towards sensor construction, and to discuss
the general conﬁgurations and sensing mechanisms of MNCs-based luminescent probes. In this review,
we ﬁrst discuss relevant physicochemical properties of MNCs that may have major inﬂuences on the
sensing mechanisms/performance of MNCs-based luminescent sensors, and then summarize the main
synthetic strategies of highly luminescent MNCs. Then, we brieﬂy illustrate the general conﬁgurations
of MNCs-based sensors, and elaborately clarify their sensing mechanisms with the exempliﬁcation of rep-
resentative sensing applications. We conclude the review with our perspectives on the opportunities/
challenges of MNCs-based luminescent sensors. It is believed that this review will shed light on the
design of MNCs-based luminescent sensors for future sensing applications.

1. Introduction
Optical sensors hold predominant advantages such as low cost,
fast response, high accuracy, and portable detection [1], which
have made them applicable in diverse ﬁelds including environ-
mental protection [2,3], disease diagnosis [4], healthcare [5], food
safety [6,7], agriculture [8,9], and security inspection [10,11]. Com-
paring with colorimetric probes, luminescent sensors are much
attractive because of their higher signal output and consequent
sensitivity. In recent years, various luminescent materials (e.g.,
quantum dots [12], conjugate polymers [13], rare-earth-doped
upconversion nanoparticles [14,15], metal–organic frameworks
[16], carbon dots [17,18], and metal nanoclusters [1,4,10,19]) have
been employed to construct luminescent sensors. Among these
materials, metal nanoclusters (MNCs for short; i.e., Au, Ag, Cu,
etc.) have emerged as a new class of luminophores [20,21]. MNCs
are composed of a metal core and an organic ligand shell (Fig. 1),
which are a subgenre of nanoparticles with core sizes of less than
3 nm [22–32]. Due to strong quantum conﬁnement effect in this
size regime, MNCs exhibit intriguing molecule-like properties such
as intrinsic chirality [33–40], magnetism [41], HOMO-LUMO tran-
sition [22,23], and strong luminescence [10,20,21,42–50], which
enable MNCs to be widely applied in catalysis [51–59], energy con-
version [60–64], environmental protection [65–69], biomedicine
[70–79],
optoelectronics
[80,81]
and
sensor
development
[1,4,10,19]. For example, MNCs-based luminescent probes have
gained amazing progress in the ﬁeld of analytical chemistry
[1,4,10,19], which greatly promotes the development of lumines-
cent sensors.
In the past two decades, numerous MNCs have been synthe-
sized or engineered for the construction of luminescent sensors,
which sufﬁciently satisﬁed the diverse sensing needs. For instance,
plenty of MNCs-based luminescent sensors were developed to
detect heavy metal ions (e.g., Hg2+, Cd2+, Cu2+, and Pb2+) in the ﬁeld
of environmental protection [82–87]; In the ﬁeld of food safety,
simple monitoring of speciﬁc chemicals and pesticide residues
(such as dinotefuran, phoxim, etc.) in food could be achieved with
the help of MNCs-based sensors [8,9,88]; In the ﬁeld of biomedi-
cine, MNCs-based luminescent probes are highly acceptable in dis-
ease
diagnosis,
biomarker
identiﬁcation
(e.g.,
amino
acids,
proteins, biological mercaptans, DNA, and RNA), detection of
important
molecules
in
cells,
etc.
[1,10,89].
In
addition,
MNCs-based
luminescent
probes
are
also
found
to
be
advantageous in some special applications such as intracellular
temperature and pH sensing [90]. Such a broad detection scope
of MNCs-based luminescent sensors mainly beneﬁts from the
ultrasmall size, precise molecular structure, strong luminescence,
size-sensitive physicochemical properties, ultrahigh surface-to-
volume ratio, engineerable metallic composition, and rich yet
tailorable surface chemistry of MNCs [10,19].
Being an excellent optical sensor should fulﬁll three criteria:
high sensitivity, high selectivity and fast response, which could
be fully catered by the ultrasmall sized MNCs-based luminescent
sensors [19]. In terms of sensitivity, all the sensing mechanisms
of such sensors rely on the change of luminescence output signals
upon the direct or indirect interaction between MNCs and analytes.
From this viewpoint, if the MNCs have stronger luminescence, they
can theoretically achieve lower detection limit for analytes. In par-
ticular, the strong luminescence of MNCs may guarantee sufﬁcient
signal output of sensors even at an extremely low MNC concentra-
tion (e.g., one MNC per volume unit), which means lower-
concentration analytes could trigger the variation of the lumines-
cence signals (e.g., luminescence quenching or enhancement) of
such MNCs via direct or indirect interaction, making high-
sensitivity sensing possible. Furthermore, the ultrasmall size (<3
nm) of MNCs renders them more like a molecule, and a single ana-
lyte molecule/ion may cause the signal change of the sensor
through the MNC-analyte interaction, empowering single mole-
cule/ion detection achievable and further highlighting the advan-
tages of MNCs as luminescent probes in analytical chemistry
[83,91]. For example, Chen et al. designed a novel Ag NC-based
luminescent sensor and achieved the single molecule detection
of N6-methyladenine [92].
Regarding the selectivity, the ‘‘metal core-organic shell” struc-
ture of MNCs with precise numbers of metal atoms and ligand
molecules provides opportunities to tailor their physicochemical
properties, which is beneﬁcial to improve their selectivity for ana-
lytes. It is documented that both the metal core and the organic
ligand shell of MNCs can be utilized to provide good selectivity
for speciﬁc analytes through unique interactions with analytes
[19]. Of note, the variation of a single metal atom or surface ligand
molecule in the MNCs can lead to a dramatic change in their
physicochemical properties such as luminescence and catalytic
activity [93,94], and current advances of MNCs have stepped into

example, a series of synthetic methods have been designed, and
a large number of MNCs with strong luminescence have been

their sensing applications. Moreover, while it is known that the
composition, oxidation state, catalytic property, luminescence
property, ligand’s stereostructure, and the aggregation state of
MNCs could impact on the performance of MNCs-based lumines-

still unclear. In addition, although currently several published
review articles in this area essentially promote the development
of MNCs-based luminescent sensors [1,4,10,19,100–102], it is
urgently needed to comprehensively correlate the physicochemical
properties of MNCs with their inﬂuences on detection applications,
to summarize the synthetic strategies of water-soluble highly
luminescent MNCs towards sensor construction, and to discuss
the general conﬁgurations and sensing principles of MNCs-based
luminescent probes.
In this review, we ﬁrst discuss relevant physicochemical prop-
erties of MNCs that may have major inﬂuences on the detection
mechanism as well as the detection performance of MNCs-based
luminescent sensors. Subsequently, owing to the importance of
luminescent MNCs as the key component of a sensor, we summa-
rize the main synthetic methods of highly luminescent MNCs with
the highlight of their mechanisms in enhancing their luminescence
intensities. Then, we sketch out the general conﬁgurations of main-
stream MNCs-based luminescent sensors. In the following section,
the sensing mechanisms of MNCs-based luminescent probes
towards various analytes are comprehensively summarized with
the exempliﬁcation of representative sensing applications. Finally,
an overall summary as well as the current opportunities/chal-
lenges of MNCs-based luminescent probes are presented. We
believe that this review will shed light on the design of MNCs-
based luminescent sensors for future sensing applications, and
stimulate more research creativities on the development of
MNCs-based luminescent sensors.
2. Correlating the physicochemical properties of MNCs to their
sensing applications
As shown in Fig. 1, MNCs are composed of a metal core and an
organic ligand shell (e.g., thiolate, selenolate, alkynyl, phosphate
ligand, DNA, and proteins) [22,23,103–106]. Both the metal core
and the organic shell of MNCs could largely dictate the physico-
chemical properties, which could further impact the design, detec-
tion performance and the application direction of MNCs-based
luminescent sensors [19]. Therefore, understanding these physico-
chemical properties of MNCs is favorable to the design of high-
performance MNCs-based luminescent probes. In this section, we
will discuss the physicochemical properties of MNCs based on their
core–shell structure: (1) the metal core-dictated physicochemical
properties; (2) the ligand shell-dictated physicochemical proper-
ties; (3) the MNC entity-dictated properties. It should be men-
tioned that not all the physicochemical properties of MNCs will
be discussed, but only those having inﬂuences on the performance
of MNCs-based luminescent sensors will be presented here.
2.1. The MNC’s metal core-dictated physicochemical properties
As the metallic part of the core–shell structured MNCs, the
physicochemical properties of MNC’s metal core play signiﬁcant
roles in affecting the luminescence output as well as the sensing
performance of the MNCs-based luminescent sensors. In this part,
several key attributes of MNC’s metal core including its composi-
tion, structure, oxidation state and catalytic activity will be
discussed.
2.1.1. The composition and structure of MNC’s metal core
The metal core of MNCs usually composes coinage metals (i.e.,
Au, Ag, and Cu) and their alloys [22,23,103], and sometimes is
doped with Hg, Cd, and Pt elements [107–109]. On one hand, such
components may affect the biosafety of MNCs-based luminescent
probes. For example, the in vitro and in vivo sensing applications
could be curtailed if the MNCs contain bio-toxic metal species
(e.g., Hg2+ and Cd2+). On the other hand, the composition of MNCs
also greatly affects their luminescence [20]. Recently, some alloy
MNCs were found to have stronger luminescence than the same
sized and structured mono-metallic MNCs [110,111]. For instance,
Mishra et al. reported that the luminescence of Au-doped (AuAg)29
NCs is 6 times higher than that of Ag29 (Fig. 2a) [111]. Wang et al.
found that after replacing 13 Au atoms of Au25 with Ag atoms
(Fig. 2b), its luminescence was enhanced up to 200 times [110].
The principle behind the luminescence enhancement may be that
doping hetero-metal species into Au NCs is conducive to the
ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) or ligand-to-metal–metal
charge transfer (LMMCT) [20], and detailed discussion on the metal
doping-induced luminescence enhancement of MNCs may refer to
Section 3.1.

In addition, the crystal structure of MNCs also has a major inﬂu-
ence on the luminescence property [112,113]. Taking glutathione
(GSH) protected Au NCs as an example, it was found that GSH-
protected Au10-12, Au15, Au18, and Au25 NCs are not luminous under
UV light irradiation [114,115], while GSH-protected Au22 NCs show
intensive red luminescence with an emission peak at 665 nm [93].
Recent studies revealed that the GSH-protected Au25 NCs show
strong luminescence in the second near-infrared region (1000–
1700 nm; NIR-II for short; see Fig. 2c) [78], which indicates that
the atomic packing structure of MNCs has great impact on their
luminescence. It should be pointed out that the NIR-II lumines-
cence has strong tissue penetration and can be easily distinguished
from the self-luminescence of biospecimens [73], which may
greatly promote the application of NIR-II MNCs in biosensing.
Overall, the composition and structure of NC’s metal core can
not only affect their biotoxicity, but also inﬂuence their lumines-
cence property, which will further impact the application scope
and performance of MNCs-based luminescent sensors.
2.1.2. The oxidation state of MNC’s metal core
The metal core of MNCs consists of the M(0) species inside the
kernel and the M(I) species on the surface motifs (here M denotes
metal), thereby making the valence state of the MNC’s metal com-
ponent between 0 and 1 [99]. Usually the relative increase of M(I)
species in the metal core will lead to a more positive valence state,
which would promote the LMCT effect and thus is conducive to
enhancing the luminescence of MNCs [99,116]. In addition, the
increase in the oxidation state of MNC’s metal core also favors
the resistance to the oxidation of MNCs by oxygen or other oxi-
dants in the environment, which not only improves the stability
of MNCs [99], but also enhances the luminescence signals of
MNCs-based luminescent probes, potentially prompting their
sensing performance.
Aside of this, the valence state of MNC’s metal core sometimes
can correlate to the selectivity of the MNCs-based sensors [19]. For
example, Au NCs with abundant Au+ species showed high selectiv-
ity for Hg2+ due to the metallophilic interaction between the 5d10
Au+ and the 5d10 Hg2+ [82], and the similar behavior was also found
in the Ag+-rich Ag NCs [83]. In addition, the valence state of MNC’s
metal core is also inﬂuential to the reactivity between MNCs and
some analytes, which may lead to the change of luminescence sig-
nal, and thus made the detection of such analytes possible. For
instance, Ag+ or Cu2+ ions can react with Au NCs via anti-galvanic
metal exchange [117], achieving the detection of Ag+ or Cu2+ ions
through luminescence enhancement or quenching [97,118]. There-
fore, the oxidation state of MNC’s metal core should be carefully
manipulated when designing MNCs-based luminescent sensors,
especially for those that the selectivity is originated from the attri-
butes of MNC’s metal core.
2.1.3. The catalytic property of MNC’s metal core
The high surface to volume ratio and unique molecule-like
properties of MNCs render their metal core to exhibit excellent cat-
alytic activity for speciﬁc reactions [51], which is also one of the
bases for some MNCs-based luminescent probes. MNCs usually
have high charge transfer efﬁciency and can act as excellent energy
acceptors/donors [67], enabling them to hold superior catalytic
performance. Speciﬁcally, recent studies have shown that MNC’s
metal core has some enzyme-mimic catalytic properties (including
reductase, catalase, etc.) [79,119], and a series of MNCs-based
luminescent sensors with excellent detection performance have
been successively developed based on their enzyme-mimic cat-
alytic properties [120–122]. For example, peroxides can be easily
adsorbed on the Au core of MNCs and catalytically decomposed
by the MNCs due to their outstanding catalase activity. In view
of this, Liu et al. developed an Au NC-based luminescent sensor
for Cu2+ detection in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)
as Cu2+ ions could inhibit the catalytic decomposition of H2O2 by
the Au NCs and accordingly trigger the chromogenic reaction of
H2O2, which is however unachievable by other ions such as K+,
NH4
+, Cr3+, Ag+, Al3+, Mg2+, Mn2+, Ca2+, and Hg2+ [120].
2.2. The MNC’s ligand shell-dictated physicochemical properties
As known, the ligand shell of MNCs is equally or even more
important for their applications because it directly dictates the
interface behavior between MNCs and a certain substrate in most
of their applications such as catalysis, biological application, and
sensor development [123]. In this regard, the interface properties
of MNCs in sensing applications can be markedly upgraded by
engineering the surface ligand shell of MNCs for desired function-
alities. In view of this, the physicochemical properties of MNC’s
ligand shell such as the ligand structure/steric hindrance, tai-
lorability, and biocompatibility will be brieﬂy presented in this
subsection, which could be useful for understanding the construc-
tion, application and the sensing mechanisms of MNCs-based
luminescent sensors.
2.2.1. The structure and steric hindrance of MNC’s ligand shell
The structure and steric hindrance of MNC’s ligands have con-
siderable inﬂuence on the application of MNCs-based luminescent
sensors [19,91]. Firstly, the structure of MNC’s ligands can be either
ﬂexible or rigid, which largely affects the luminescence of MNCs
[124,125]. Particularly, if the ligand of MNCs has a rigid structure,
the non-radiation energy loss caused by the ligand’s vibration,
stretching and rotation can be effectively inhibited, facilitating
the luminescence maintenance of MNCs [124,126]. Secondly, the
larger steric hindrance of MNC’s ligands means that there are more
uncoordinated metal atom sites on the MNC surface (Fig. 3a)

luminescent sensors.
Exactly as every coin has two sides, for those luminescent sen-

the larger steric hindrance of MNC’s ligand shell may hinder the
permeation of analytes into the ligand shell and delay the interac-

postpone the response of MNCs-based luminescent sensors for
analytes. In addition, in certain circumstance, the steric hindrance
of MNC’s ligands can be smartly regulated to selectively allow the

the selectivity of MNCs-based probes for speciﬁc analytes. For
example, Yuan et al. designed a novel luminescent GSH-protected

of the Ag NCs allowed the permeation of Cys into the ligand shell
to etch the Ag NCs for luminescence quenching, but prevented
the permeation of interfering species GSH with larger molecular
structure (Fig. 3b), achieving the high-selectivity detection of Cys
from GSH.
On the basis, it is essential to take the inﬂuence of MNC’s ligand
shell (in terms of structure and steric hindrance) into consideration
when designing a high-performance MNCs-based luminescent
sensors.
2.2.2. The tailorability of MNC’s ligand shell
The ligand shell of MNCs with rich yet tailorable surface
chemistry offers opportunities to expand the functionalities of
MNCs-based luminescent sensors [19]. In many cases, some speci-
ﬁc functional groups or molecules which have unique interaction
with certain analytes [10,19], can be grafted on the surface shell
of MNCs via physical or chemical interactions, which ensures the
selective detection of such analytes by MNCs-based luminescent
sensors [92,130]. In addition, researchers also often regulate the
ﬂexibility of MNC’s ligand shell through grafting some rigid mole-
cules, which may restrict the stretching/vibration of surface
ligands and reduce the possible loss of non-radiative energy,
resulting in enhanced luminescence (Fig. 4) [124–126]. Moreover,
researchers

of MNCs to prompt the emission of stronger luminescence from
MNCs [131].
2.2.3. The biocompatibility of MNCs from their ligand shell
While the composition of MNC’s metal core is inﬂuential to the
biotoxicity of MNCs, the biocompatibility of MNCs is mainly deter-
mined by their ligand shell [70,104]. Firstly, water-soluble surface
ligands of MNCs are favorable to retain their biocompatibility
[70,104], which makes the sensing of MNCs-based luminescent
sensors possible in aqueous environment. For example, it is
required to use water-soluble MNCs-based luminescent sensors
for the routine monitoring of toxic metal ions in water samples
or biospecimens [10,19]. Moreover, for in vitro or in vivo sensing
applications, luminescent MNCs armed with adequately biocom-
patible surface ligands are demanded for the sensor construction
[10]. For instance, biocompatible molecules such as GSH, Cys,
and lipoic acid with attributes of anti-oxidation, promoting angio-
genesis and inhibiting cell apoptosis are desired for directly stabi-
lizing MNCs in order to cater the requirements of biosensing
applications [104]. Furthermore, benign biocompatibility could
also be achieved by modifying the MNC’s ligand shell via grafting
biocompatible molecules on the surface [110].
2.3. The MNC entity-dictated physicochemical properties
It should be emphasized that some physicochemical properties
of MNCs are not merely dictated by either the metal core or the
ligand shell, but determined by the whole core–shell structured
MNC entity. For example, the luminescence and the renal clearance
of MNCs are comprehensively commanded by the whole MNC
entity [20,21,75,132,133], and such two attributes of MNCs will
be discussed in this subsection.
2.3.1. The luminescence of MNCs
The intensive yet tunable photoluminescence of MNCs as sensi-
tive output signal is the basis of constructing MNCs-based lumi-
nescent sensors. Theoretically, the luminescent signals of MNCs-
based sensors are usually stronger than the colorimetric signals
of conventional metal nanoparticles-based counterparts, which
indicates that the MNCs-based luminescent sensors may have
higher sensitivity for analytes. Moreover, the tunable lumines-
cence of MNCs from UV to visible and NIR-II range (Fig. 5) offers
broader applicability of MNCs-based sensors. For example, com-
paring with visible (400–700 nm) and NIR-I (700–900 nm) lumi-
nescence, the NIR-II (1000–1700 nm) luminescence has deeper
penetration in biological tissues and distinct difference from the
self-luminescence of biological environments [73,78,134], which
makes the MNCs-based NIR-II luminescent sensors advantageous
in biosensing and disease diagnosis.
In addition to luminescence intensity, luminescence lifetime is
also an important luminescence attribute of MNCs, which has been

2.3.2. Renal clearance
MNCs-based luminescent sensors have promising potential in
the in vivo sensing and disease diagnosis, which requires MNCs
to possess excellent renal clearance [132,137]. In addition to the
benign biocompatibility of MNCs such as good water-solubility
and low biotoxicity, the size of particles should be small enough
to avoid high accumulation in organs of the reticuloendothelial
system.
Generally,
particles
with
hydrodynamic
diameters
of <6 nm are considered to be suitable for excellent renal clearance
[74,132,137,138]. MNCs with ultrasmall core size and relatively
thin ligand shells fortunately have proper hydrodynamic sizes
(<6 nm) [132,137,138]. This enables MNCs to be adequate for con-
structing MNCs-based luminescent probes towards in vivo sensing
and diagnostic applications. Taken together, owing to the excellent
renal clearance and benign biocompatibility, MNCs-based lumines-
cent probes have great prospects in in vivo sensing applications.
3. Synthesis of highly luminescent MNCs towards sensing
applications
As aforementioned, for a superior MNCs-based luminescent
sensor, outputting adequate luminescence signals with a high
signal-to-noise ratio is vital to achieve high-sensitivity detection,
thereby deploying highly luminescent MNCs for sensor design is
extremely essential. With continuous research efforts for years,
several efﬁcient strategies have been developed to acquire highly
luminescent MNCs, including metal doping, surface motif engi-
neering, ligand engineering, and structure modulation. In this sec-

have stronger luminescence than their mono-metallic analogues

3.3. Ligand engineering
Ligand engineering is another efﬁcient strategy to enhance the
luminescence of MNCs, which is generally exert inﬂuences in the
following aspects: (1) grafting electron-rich functional groups onto
ligands to prompt the electron donation to the metal core
[112,131,165], thereby enhancing the luminescence of MNCs; (2)
grafting bulky yet rigid molecules on ligands to restrict the non-
radiative relaxation of the excited-state energy of MNCs [126,166].
In early years, Wu et al. initially found that Au25 NCs may have
dramatically luminescence intensities if they are protected by dif-
ferent ligands [131]. For example, the authors found that ligands
with electron-rich atoms (e.g., N, O) or groups (e.g., –COOH, NH2)
can efﬁciently promote the luminescence of MNCs, which was sup-
ported by the stronger luminescence of the Au25 NCs protected by
the GSH and long-chain peptide nucleic acid with electron-rich –
COOH and –NH2 groups (Fig. 8a). Similarly, Zhu and his co-
workers compared the luminescence of Au25(SePh)18, Au25(SC2H4-
Ph)18, and Au25(NAPS)18 NCs (here NAPS denotes 2-(naphthalen-2-

luminescence of Au25(SePh)18 than Au25(SC2H4Ph)18 is probably
due to the relatively poor contribution of the Se-Au bond to the
LMCT or LMMCT comparing to the S–Au bond, while the 6.5 times
higher luminescence of Au25(NAPS)18 than Au25(SC2H4Ph)18 is orig-
inated from the more powerful charge donating capability of NAPS
than SC2H4Ph. Later, Wu and his coworkers employed a series of
Au24 NCs protected by different ligands as research models to
investigate this issue, and further corroborated the correlation

3.4. Structure modulation of MNCs
Atom packing structure of MNCs play an important role in dic-
tating their properties such as optical and catalytic attributes
[20,22,23,51,56,172]. Actually, the luminescence of MNCs could
also be impacted by tuning the kernel atom packing of MNCs
[46,173]. For example, Zhuang et al. synthesized two types of iso-
meric Au42(TBBT)26 NCs (TBBT = 4-tert-butylbenzenethiolate), and
found that the face-centered cubic (fcc)-structured Au42 NCs dis-
play stronger luminescence than that of the non-fcc-structured
one [174]. Moreover, the authors also used three additional pairs
of Au NCs with similar sizes but different structures to compare
their luminescence, which all implied that the fcc-structure could
be more favorable for luminescence than the non-fcc ones.
Recently, Zhou et al. revealed that the body-centered cubic (bcc)
structured Au38 NCs exhibit an extremely long excited state life-
time of 4.7 ls, which is three-order magnitude higher than the
hexagonal close-packed (hcp) Au30 NCs (i.e., 1 ns; see Fig. 9) [28].
Based on theoretical and experimental analyses, the authors con-
ﬁrmed that the difference of MNCs in the excited state lifetimes
is due to the distinctive wave functions of the tetrahedral Au4 units
between the bcc Au38 NCs and the hcp Au30 NCs.
Above-mentioned two representative examples clearly con-
ﬁrmed that the structure modulation could be powerful in regulat-
ing the luminescence of MNCs. However, the advance in this aspect
is in a very early stage, and future research efforts are required to
systematically explore the delicate structure modulation strategies
of
MNCs
and
fundamentally
understand
their
structure-
luminescence correlations [46].
Overall, the above-mentioned synthetic strategies have been
demonstrated to be efﬁcient in fabricating highly luminescent
MNCs. However, no attempt was done to combine two or more
of such strategies together for further enhancing the luminescence
of MNCs, although it should be a feasible way in this issue. For
example, combining metal doping with surface engineering may
be practicable for further luminescence enhancement of MNCs. It
is believed that MNCs with higher luminescence intensity will be
reported soon via the combination of above-mentioned strategies.

accomplishing the sensing of the analyte in a luminescence
‘‘on-off-on”

into this category. By contrast, the MNCs-based ratiometric sensors
are usually irrelevant to luminescence quenching, but involve the
luminescence conversion from one color to another one triggered
by the analyte (Fig. 10c). Occasionally, the luminescent comple-

luminescent sensors with the ‘‘on-off-on” and ratiometric sensing
modes have been developed in the past few years.
5. The sensing mechanisms of MNCs-based sensors
In the past two decades, thousands of MNCs-based luminescent
sensors with diverse working mechanisms have been devised in
this ﬁeld. By dissecting such sensors for mechanistic insights, the
detection of analytes by MNCs-based sensors can be considered
as a chemical processing unit in which the MNC constituent or
the complementary constituent of the sensor will chemically react
or physically interact with the analyte and thus trigger the lumi-
nescence variation. As shown in Fig. 11, while the MNC constituent
of the sensor can act as the reactant, catalyst or product during the
sensing process, both the analyte and the complementary con-
stituent of the sensor could be employed as the reactant or catalyst
if considering the sensing process as a chemical reaction. There-
fore, it provides a number of combinations featuring different sens-
ing mechanisms for various sensing applications. For example,
pairs of MNCs + analyte, MNCs + complementary constituent,
analyte + complementary constituent can be deployed as reactants
for speciﬁc reactions to induce the change of luminescence signals
during a sensing process, and their sensing mechanisms are totally
different from that using MNCs or analytes or complementary spe-
cies as a catalyst for sensing applications.
In addition to chemical reactions, such combinations of MNCs,
analytes and complementary species (if needed) could also exert
functions for sensing applications based on their physical interac-
tions. Actually, a lot of MNCs-based luminescent sensors were
developed for sensing analytes based on the physical interactions
between the sensor and the analyte. It should be mentioned that
the speciﬁc afﬁnity between complementary base pairs of DNA
or RNA is considered as a physical interaction here for simplifying
the discussion in this regard [92,175–177]. In this section, we will
summarize the sensing mechanisms of MNCs-based luminescent
sensors into four categories based on the main roles of MNCs, ana-
lytes and the complementary species: (1) Chemical reaction-
directed sensing mechanisms of MNCs-based luminescent sensors,
(2) catalysis-directed sensing mechanisms of MNCs-based lumi-
nescent sensors, (3) MNCs production-directed sensing mechanism
of luminescent sensors, and (4) physical interactions-directed
sensing mechanisms of MNCs-based luminescent sensors.
5.1. Chemical reaction-directed sensing mechanisms of MNCs-based
luminescent sensors
Many MNCs-based luminescent sensors would participate in
chemical reactions when sensing analytes. Actually, a MNCs-
based sensing system composes luminescent MNCs, analytes, and
a complementary species (if needed), and any two of which could
react as reactants for luminescence signal change (Fig. 11). More-
over, as aforementioned, MNCs is composed of a metal core and
a ligand shell, and both of them may act as reactants to react with
analytes or complementary species, resulting in the luminescence
change. Therefore, the discussion about the chemical reaction-
mediated sensing mechanisms will divided into 4 parts in this sub-
section: (1) MNC’s ligands as reactants for sensing, (2) MNC’s metal
core as the reactant for sensing, (3) analytes as reactants for sens-
ing, and (4) complementary species as reactants for sensing.
5.1.1. MNC’s ligands as reactants for sensing
MNCs have rich surface chemistry, and their ligands could be
easily engineered for speciﬁc functions. For example, atomically
precise MNCs have been widely applied for disease therapeutics
and sensing after ligand engineering [10,70,71]. Regarding the
sensing applications, several MNCs-based luminescent sensors
have been developed for the detection of certain analytes through
chemical reactions by using MNC’s ligands as reactants.
In 2014, Zhuang et al. developed a ratiometric Au NCs-based
luminescent sensor for biosensing of hydroxyl radicals (OH) in live

response signal and speciﬁc reactant for chemically recognizing
OH (see Fig. 12a). The as-formed Au NCs@HPF initially emitted
the intrinsic red luminescence at 637 nm of the Au NCs, and the
conjugated HPF molecules were almost not luminous. However,

only several MNCs-based luminescent sensors are developed based

of more sensors with this sensing mechanism is greatly needed in
the future to meet diversiﬁed sensing demands.
5.1.2. MNC’s metal core as the reactant for sensing
Aside from the ligand shell, the metal core of MNCs offers addi-
tional reactivity for selectively detecting analytes. In this aspect,
MNC’s metal core may react or chemically bind with analytes or
complementary species, which could lead to the luminescence
change of MNCs, enabling the speciﬁc detection of the analyte.
At present, many MNCs-based luminescent sensors have been
developed based on this mechanism [91,181–190]. Yuan et al.

effectively differentiated Cys from the other 19 natural non-thiol-
containing amino acids. After that, several other MNCs-based lumi-
nescent sensors were successfully developed for thiols detection
[182,184,187,189,191], and the recent advance in this aspect
reached the fabrication of portable MNCs-based sensing proto-
types [181,190]. Later, luminescent detection of other analytes

other MNCs-based luminescent sensors acting up to this mecha-

5.1.4. Complementary species as reactants for sensing
In some sensing cases, MNCs have negligible reactivity or inter-

luminescent MNCs impossible, thereby requiring a complementary

(note: Cu2+ ions could be reduced into Cu(I) by MPA, while the
MPA would form a disulﬁde compound), achieving the quantitative
detection of a liver-speciﬁc microRNA with the detection limit of
0.73 pM.

MNCs-based luminescent sensors are still very rare, which may

5.3. MNCs production-directed sensing mechanism of luminescent
sensors
In recent years, several distinctive luminescent sensors were
developed, which simultaneously achieved the detection of ana-
lytes and the formation of luminescent MNCs. In other words,
the most attractive feature of this type of sensors is that the
analyte sensing is performed with the consequence of forming
luminescent MNCs [212–215]. Here a few examples about such
sensors will be enumerated to elucidate this issue.
In 2016, Hong et al. reported a label-free luminescent assay of
nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) through leveraging liga-
tion triggered NC beacon (NCB) [213]. As shown in Fig. 17a, the
presence of NAD+ could repair the split of NCB so that the C-rich
sequence could link with the enhancer sequence, which could
result in the lighting up of non-luminescent DNA-stabilized Ag
NCs that were formed via a routine NaBH4 reduction strategy.
Owing to the formation of luminescent DNA-stabilized Ag NCs as
luminescence signal, this assay showed a high sensitivity for
NAD+ with a detection limit of 0.002 nM. Similarly, Zhao et al. suc-
cessfully developed a luminescent assay for the detection of vari-
ous metabolites and enzymes (e.g., glucose/glucose oxidase, uric
acid/uric acid oxidase, sarcosine/sarcosine oxidase, choline/choline
oxidase, xanthine/xanthine oxidase, and lactic acid/lactic acid oxi-
dase) based on the biocatalytic reactions between them for the for-
mation of H2O2 [212]. The as-generated H2O2 could produce OH
under the catalysis of Fe2+, which induced the polymerization of
methacrylic acid (MAA) to form PMAA. Subsequently, using PMAA
as a stabilizer, the introduced Ag+ ions could be reduced into lumi-
nescent Ag NCs via a UV photoreduction strategy. In this way, the
detection of such metabolites and enzymes could be correlated to
the formation of luminescent Ag NCs.
Very recently, Zang and his coworkers employed a chain-like
thiolated Ag+ complex (i.e., Ag18 dimers assembled by NO3) for
ultrasensitive detection of H2S gas (Fig. 17b) [214]. The H2S could
react with the non-luminescent Ag18 dimers and promptly pro-
duced the red-emitting Ag62 NCs, which achieved the fast detection
of H2S in 30 s with a low detection limit of 0.13 ppm. This study is
very impressive because it is the ﬁrst report that realized the con-
current detection of gaseous H2S and the formation of atomically
precise MNCs. In addition, researchers also applied large-sized Au
nanoparticles for the detection of GSH, during which small-sized
luminescent Au NCs were formed via the GSH etching [215].
As discussed, this type of sensors usually works with a lumines-
cence turn-on mode because of the formation of luminescent
MNCs. Moreover, either metal ions, or MNCs, or large-sized metal
nanoparticles should be introduced as the metal precursors of
MNCs during the sensing process for the latter formation of lumi-
nescent MNCs. In view of this, it is pivotal to make sure that the
sensing systems cannot contain interference species having strong
coordination afﬁnity with the metal precursors, otherwise the
direct correlation between analyte sensing and luminescent MNCs
formation would not be established.
5.4. Physical interactions-directed sensing mechanisms of MNCs-based
luminescent sensors
In addition to the chemical reactions-directed sensing mecha-
nisms, there are also hundreds of MNCs-based sensors acting via
physical interactions between MNCs and analytes or between the
complementary constituent and analytes. For a straightforward
MNCs-based sensor, both the metal core and the ligand shell of
MNCs can be engineered to possess speciﬁc interactions with the
analyte, which consequently induces the luminescence change
and leads to selective detection of the analyte. However, for certain
analytes that have no apparent interactions with MNCs, a comple-
mentary species should be introduced into the sensing system to
interact with MNCs or analytes for sensor development. Therefore,
special engineering in the MNC’s metal core and ligand shell as
well as the complementary constituent of the sensor is generally
required
in
order
to
realize
the
high-selectivity
and
high-
sensitivity detection of analytes via their physical interactions. In
this section, we will discuss the physical interaction-directed sens-
ing mechanisms of MNCs-based luminescent sensors from the fol-
lowing three aspects: (1) Physical interaction between MNC’s
ligands and analytes or complementary species for sensing, (2)

between complementary species and MNCs or analytes for sensing.
5.4.1. Physical interaction between MNC’s ligands and analytes or
complementary species for sensing
In this category, the ﬁrst representative example is the MNCs-
based luminescent sensors for DNA and RNA identiﬁcation based
on the complementary base matching principles. Currently, a num-
ber of luminescent MNCs stabilized by functional biomolecules
such as DNA, RNA, aptamers, and antibody were designed and used
for the development of MNCs-based luminescent sensors, which
exhibited excellent selectivity and sensitivity for the detection of
DNA, RNA, adenine, antigen, and other analytes via the speciﬁc
complementary interaction between such functional molecules
and analytes [92,175–177,216–218]. For example, Xu et al devel-
oped a chain-substitution triggered self-assembly of Ag NCs for
ratiometric detection of intracellular miRNA-21, realizing single
base mismatch detection based on luminescence color variation
[217].
In addition, researchers also put effort into the engineering of
MNCs’ ligands for high-selectivity detection of speciﬁc analytes
via their physical interactions. For example, Liu et al. synthesized
an orange-emitting Cu NCs protected by speciﬁc Ca2+ ligands
which were modiﬁed by polyethylenimine via forming Schiff base
(590 nm emission), and the as-synthesized Cu NCs were further
grafted with a water-soluble red-emitting ester (690 nm emission)
for the development of a ratiometric sensor of Ca2+ (Fig. 18) [219].
Upon introducing Ca2+ ions, the luminescence emission at 590 nm
could increase due to the Ca2+-induced ligand shell rigidiﬁcation of
Cu NCs, while that at 690 nm was constant, realizing ratiometric
detection of Ca2+ with high accuracy and high sensitivity (detection
limit of 220 ± 11 nM).

of MNC’s ligands to strengthen their speciﬁc interaction with ana-

5.4.2. Physical interaction between MNC’s metal core and analytes or
complementary species for sensing
Different from the broad applicability of the above-mentioned
ligand-analyte interaction-involved MNCs-based luminescent sen-
sors, the sensors discussed in this subsection have a very narrow
application range due to the rare interactions between MNC’s
metal cores and analytes.
At present, the most successful exempliﬁcation of the MNCs-
based luminescent sensors in this regard is the detection of Hg2+
ions via the metallophilic interaction between MNC’s metal species
(e.g., surface Au+ and Ag+) and Hg2+ [82,83]. It is known that there
is a speciﬁc metallophilic interaction between the d10 centers of
closed-shell metals such as Hg2+ (5d10), Au+ (5d10), and Ag+
(4d10) due to their large dispersion forces [228], which could be
utilized for the selective detection of Hg2+. On the basis, Xie et al.
developed a red-emitting BSA-stabilized Au NCs with about 17%
of Au+ species on the NC surface and illustrated the exertion of
speciﬁc metallophilic interaction for Hg2+ detection for the ﬁrst
time [82]. Owing to the high speciﬁcity and afﬁnity of Hg2+Au+
interaction, Hg2+ ions could quench the red luminescence of the
Au NCs within seconds, which rendered the Au NCs with high
selectivity for Hg2+ (Fig. 19). Furthermore, intracellular sensing of
Hg2+ was also successfully demonstrated by using such Au NCs-
based luminescent sensors [229,230]. Later, Xie and co-workers
further designed a type of thiolated Ag NCs containing 100% of
surface Ag+ species, and found that the Ag NCs have both excellent
selectivity and outstanding sensitivity for Hg2+ sensing [83]. After
that, a series of luminescent MNCs have been developed for the
construction of Hg2+ sensors based on the luminescence quenching
through metallophilic interactions [85,231–236].
Overall, it is difﬁcult to extensively extend the application range
of these sensors through detecting more types of analytes due to
the relatively simplex metal composition of MNCs. However, the
unique metallophilic interaction offered excellent detection per-
formance for Hg2+ in terms of high selectivity, high sensitivity,
and fast response, which is beneﬁcial to the further development
of the portable and facile Hg2+ sensors with great commercial
potentials based on the recruitment of highly stable MNCs with
high surface active species (e.g., Au+ or Ag+).
5.4.3. Physical interaction between complementary species and MNCs
or analytes for sensing
If the above-mentioned MNCs-based luminescent sensors are
incapable of sensing the desired analytes, researchers may con-
sider to design one relying on the interaction between the comple-
mentary constituent and analytes or MNCs because introducing
the complementary constituent into the sensing system could
afford more chances to trigger the luminescence signal variation
of MNCs for analyte sensing via mutual interactions. Moreover, this
type of sensors usually behaves in a luminescence ‘‘on-off-on”
mode. Particularly, the introduction of the complementary con-
stituent would suppress or quench the luminescence of MNCs via
the interaction between them (i.e., the luminescence on-off step),
and then the incoming analyte could restore the luminescence of
MNCs due to its stronger interaction with the complementary spe-
cies or MNCs (i.e., the luminescence off-on step).
For example, Qin et al. reported the design of a ‘on-off-on’ lumi-
nescent probe for intracellular GSH detection based on the usage of
an endogenous Cys-assisted GSH-protected AgNCs/rGO nanocom-
posite (rGO denotes reduced graphene oxide; see Fig. 20) [237].

luminescent sensors featuring this sensing mechanism will be
developed for sensing diversiﬁed analytes in the future.
6. Conclusion and perspective
In summary, the past two decades have witnessed the explosive
advance of MNCs-based luminescent sensors, and thousands of
such sensors were developed to satisfy the diversiﬁed sensing
needs in ﬁelds of environmental protection, food safety, disease
diagnosis, biomarker probing, agriculture, and safety inspection.
Owing to the ultrasmall size, precise molecular structure, strong
luminescence, size-sensitive physicochemical properties, ultrahigh
surface-to-volume ratio, engineerable metallic composition, and
rich yet tailorable surface chemistry, MNCs have been recognized
as all-round luminous candidates for luminescent sensing applica-
tions where excellent selectivity, high sensitivity, and fast response

of the sensors for various analytes were achieved. Recent progress
in the delicate engineering of the metal core as well as the organic
ligand shell of MNCs provided numerous opportunities to modu-
late the physicochemical properties of MNCs. Such physicochemi-
cal properties of MNCs are largely correlated to the sensing
performance of MNCs-based luminescent sensors. Therefore, engi-
neering MNCs is beneﬁcial to the acquirement of highly lumines-
cent MNCs with special functionalities, which further promoted
the design of MNCs-based luminescent sensors towards desired
sensing target.
In addition, the strong luminescence of MNCs is the prerequisite
of constructing MNCs-based luminescent sensors. With continuous
research efforts, a series of synthetic strategies such as metal dop-
ing, surface motif engineering, ligand engineering, and structure
modulation, have been developed for the synthesis of highly lumi-
nescent MNCs, which substantially offered good platforms for the
sensor development. On the basis, various MNCs-based lumines-
cent sensors with different working modes were separately
devised. Particularly, the novel MNCs-based luminescent sensors
featuring the luminescence ‘‘on-off-on” and ratiometric sensing
modes are useful complement to the traditional luminescence
‘‘on-off”-featured ones. Moreover, the growing prosperity of the
MNCs-based luminescent sensors also offered plentiful paradigms
for summarizing and understanding their underlying sensing
mechanisms, which could be beneﬁcial to the future design of
luminescent sensors. In general, MNCs-based luminescent sensors
have shown their promising potentials in sensing applications.
However, there are also several issues for MNCs constraining
the practical sensing applications of MNCs-based luminescent sen-
sors. For example, the stability of water-soluble MNCs is still not
good enough, and very few MNCs could sustain their size, struc-
ture, and luminescence for months at room temperature or ele-
vated
temperature.
Moreover,
it
is
documented
that
the
complicated aqueous environment such as different pH, solvent
polarity, and some ions could deteriorate the stability of MNCs,
which also inﬂuences the practical application of MNCs-based
luminescent sensors. In addition to the stability, the luminescence
of MNCs is still relatively low, and most of water-soluble MNCs
have QYs of <15%. Although such strategies discussed in section
3 have been corroborated to be efﬁcient in enhancing the lumines-
cence of MNCs (e.g., augmenting the luminescence from 0.1% to
15%), the luminescence of water-soluble MNCs is incomparable
to that of other phosphors such as quantum dots and organic dyes
which usually possess QYs of 30%. In the future, it is critically
important to develop extremely stable and highly luminescent
MNCs for sensor construction, which may lead to the breakthrough
in the practical applications as well as the commercialization of
MNCs-based luminescent sensors. With extremely stable and
highly luminescent MNCs in hands, it is anticipated that the devel-
opment of MNCs-based luminescent sensors will step into com-
mercial era that is alike to the success of the commercially
available Au nanoparticles-based colorimetric sensors.
On top of the challenges of the MNCs-based sensors mentioned
above, many major opportunities are also envisioned for this class
of optical sensors. For example, by summarizing the reported
MNCs-based luminescent sensors, one may ﬁnd that all the sensors
were operated based on the variation of their UV–visible (300–
700 nm) or NIR-I (700–1000 nm) luminescence, whereas no sensor
was developed through using the NIR-II luminescence (1000–
1700 nm) of MNCs. Recent studies have demonstrated that some
MNCs could show strong NIR-II luminescence which has deeper
tissue penetration and high resolution in biospecimens [73,78].
On the basis, constructing biosensors using NIR-II luminescent
MNCs could be a very promising branch for in vivo detections con-
sidering their efﬁcient renal clearance, better interaction with bio-
logical systems, and excellent tissue penetration, which could
further advance MNCs-based luminescent sensors for in vivo appli-
cations. However, prior to comprehensive investigation of in vivo
sensing applications, potential interferences from biological inter-
actions to the in vivo sensing performance of MNCs-based lumines-
cent sensors should be considered [242,243], which requires future
research efforts to evaluate such interferences in biological sys-
tems. For example, protein adsorption as well as cell interaction
may weaken the sensitivity/selectivity of the MNCs-based lumi-
nescent sensors.
Moreover, comparing to the prosperous situation in sensing
hydrophilic analytes in aqueous environments by MNCs-based
luminescent sensors, the reports in the design of MNCs-based sen-
sors for detecting gaseous analytes (e.g., H2S, CO, Cl2, and CH4) are
very rare [162,214]. The reason for this issue may be partially due
to the relatively complicated requirements in evaluating the sens-
ing performance for gaseous analytes. However, as well known,
sensing of gaseous species is comparably important to that of the
hydrophilic
analytes
in
industrial
ﬁeld
[244,245].
Therefore,
increasing research efforts should be devoted to the design of
MNCs-based luminescent sensors for the detection of various gas-
eous analytes in the future, which could be an important applica-
tion direction differentiated from the hydrophilic analyte detection.
Currently, external ﬁelds (e.g., magnetic ﬁeld) have been
applied to assist the improvement in the sensing performance of
MNCs-based luminescent sensors [198], which may inspire the
design of other MNCs-based luminescent sensors performing in
the assistance of external ﬁelds. For example, MNCs have been con-
ﬁrmed to be a class of high-efﬁciency photocatalysts [67], which
may be utilized to design solar light-mediated sensing platforms
using photocatalytic MNCs as luminescent probes (i.e., the partici-
pation of light ﬁeld). It is believed that external ﬁeld-mediated
sensing applications of MNCs-based luminescent sensors will be
increasingly explored in the future.
In addition to the photoluminescence, MNCs’ electrochemilu-
minescence (ECL) sensing is also very attractive in sensing pollu-
tants and biomarkers due to its advantages such as low cost,
high sensitivity, simple integration, and chipability [246–248]. Dif-
ferent from the photoluminescence, the ECL of MNCs is a radiative
process arising from the high-energy electron transfer reaction
between electrogenerated species. In recent years, a rapid progress
has been gained in the investigation of MNCs’ ECL [249–253]. For
example, Hesari et al. remarked on the strong and sustainable

times higher than that of the tris(bipyridine)-ruthenium(II) com-

developed for high-performance sensing of analytes such as dopa-
mine, biomarkers, RNA, etc. [246–248,255,256]. In the future,
MNCs-based ECL assays could be an important supplement for
MNCs-based photoluminescent sensors, which will deﬁnitely push
the development and applications of MNCs-based sensors forward.
In addition, it is anticipated that the combination of the lumines-
cence and ECL of MNCs will further improve the sensing perfor-
mance in the accuracy, selectivity, and sensitivity.
To sum up, MNCs-based luminescent sensor with abundant
unique advantages have shown great potential in diversiﬁed sens-
ing applications. It is believed that the collective research efforts
could help scientists not only to address the existing problems
and challenges of MNCs, but also to seize those aforementioned
opportunities of MNCs-based optical sensors, which may promote
their development in sensor commercialization and further expand
their sensing applications in many ﬁelds.

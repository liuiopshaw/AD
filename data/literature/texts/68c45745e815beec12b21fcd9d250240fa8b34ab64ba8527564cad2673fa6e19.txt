Designing the homogeneous assembly of the bio–nano interface to fine-tune the interactions between
the nanoprobes and biological systems is of prime importance to improve the antimicrobial efficiency of
nanomedicines. In this work, highly luminescent silver nanoclusters with the homogeneous conjugation
of an antimicrobial peptide (referred to as Dpep-Ag NCs) were achieved via the reduction–decomposition–
reduction process as a single package. The as-designed Dpep-Ag NCs inherited the two distinctive features
of bactericides from the Ag+ species and the antimicrobial peptide of Dpep, and exhibited enhanced
bacterial killing efficiency compared with other control groups including BSA-capped Ag NCs and the
original antimicrobial peptide bactenecin (Opep)-protected Ag nanoparticles (Opep-Ag NPs). The ultrasmall
size feature of Dpep-Ag NCs combined with the positively charged bactericidal tail allow a better interface
and interaction with the cell membrane owing to the selective targeting of lipopolysaccharides in the
Gram-negative bacteria and electrostatic interaction, facilitating the membrane permeability. Dpep-Ag
NCs restrained the E. coli growth visibly and outperformed commercial Ag NPs (30 nm) with reduced
(ca. 100-fold) minimal inhibitory concentration. The analysis of infected wound sizes and tissues treated with
Dpep-Ag NCs in a murine model reveal obvious differences in the healing effect compared with the other
counterparts, demonstrating its antibacterial efficiency in practical application.
Introduction
The threat of bacterial resistance and tolerance1–3 motivates the
urgent need for developing novel antibiotic agents4–10 to combat
the pathogenic bacteria. Currently, due to their broad spectrum
activity and low propensity for resistance, antimicrobial peptides
(AMPs) have attracted great attention.11–13 The mechanism of
AMPs for bacterial killing is probably induced via a membrane-
lytic effect,14 which endows them an efficient and characteristic
capacity from that of conventional antibiotics. Moreover, cationic
amphipathic AMPs are easy to bind with negatively charged
biofilms than the mammalian cells and achieve an accumulation
effect on the surface of the bacteria. Despite so many attributes,
AMPs suffer from the high cost of peptide extraction from natural
organisms and safety concerns in the human body, which limit
their clinical and commercial applications.
Metallic silver (Ag)-based materials as eﬃcient bactericides
to combat bacteria have a long history.15–18 With the develop-
ment of nanotechnology, silver’s efficiency to fight against
bacterial infections has been explored in the form of Ag-based
nanomaterials.19,20 Of note, Xiu et al.21 disclosed that the
antibacterial activity of the Ag nanoparticles (Ag NPs) was
associated with the release of Ag+ ions from the NPs. While
the direct usage of Ag+ can lead to bio-toxicity due to high
affinity to the biomolecules,17 the controllable release of
Ag+ without damage to human cells in the form of different
Ag-based nanoreservoirs has been suggested and developed.22
Given the fact that with the decreased size of the Ag NPs the
potential to release Ag atoms and ions will increase due to the
high surface to volume ratio,23 ultrasmall Ag nanoclusters (NCs)
have been developed as highly efficient candidates for bacterial
killing.24,25 To further improve the antimicrobial efficiency
of Ag NCs, integrating Ag NCs with distinctive bactericides to
engineer the bio–nano interface has been developed as
an effective strategy to tune the interactions between the
Ag NCs and the biological systems.26 However, recent works
have focused on subsequent chemical modifications of the
Ag NCs with antibiotic agents.27 Studies using antibacterial
reagents as capping reagents for the direct synthesis of Ag NCs

Inspired by the unique antibacterial properties of the AMPs

feature and well-designed bactericidal tail, the fluorescent

reduced the amount of AMP required and provided an eﬀective
method to alleviate the high expense of extraction from bovine
neutrophils via a complex process. Its antibacterial ability was
further unambiguously delivered by healing the mice wounds
exposed to the resistant bacteria.
Experimental
Synthesis of Opep-Ag NPs and Dpep-Ag NCs
The synthesis of Opep-Ag NPs was carried out by referring to
a
method
that
was
previously
reported
with
moderate
modification.28 Briefly, NaBH4 (50 mL, 10 mM) was added into
the bactenecin solution (1 mL, 2 mg mL1). The odor of –SH
can be detected after the action, then AgNO3 (50 mL, 10 mM)
was introduced and stirred for 1 h. To synthesize Dpep-Ag NCs,
AgNO3 (50 mL, 10 mM) was mixed with Dpep (1 mL, 2 mg mL1)
and incubated overnight, and then the reduction reagent,
NaBH4 (10 mM), was introduced to produce Ag NC intermediates.
After the decomposition of Ag NC intermediates to colorless
products, additional NaBH4 (10 mM) was introduced to the above
mixture and light-brown Ag NCs with strong pink emission under
UV illumination were obtained after 3–4 h; the final amount of
NaBH4 (10 mM) usage was B50 mL. The ratio between Ag and –SH
was found to be 1:2. It should be noted that Opep and Dpep
showed an obviously different stabilizing effect towards Ag.
Opep tended to form larger sized NPs while much smaller NPs
were obtained with Dpep. BSA-Ag NCs were synthesized according
to a method previously reported with NaBH4 (50 mL, 10 mM)
added.29 The as-synthesized Ag NPs and clusters were separated
and purified to cut off the free peptide and ions by an ultra-
filtration tube with a molecular weight cut-off of 3 kDa (60 kDa for
BSA-Ag NCs) for triple centrifugation at a speed of the 8000 rpm.
The Ag NCs remaining on the filter can be readily re-suspended in
water for further use. To get an equivalent peptide in the three
samples, 1 kD dialysis bags were used to cut off the ions existing
in the solution.
Bacterial culture
Three kinds of bacterial strains including two Gram-negative
ones (E. coli and S. oneidensis MR-1) and a Gram-positive one
(S. aureus) were grown in sterile Mueller Hinton (MH) broth
(preparation according the instruction). Before the experiment,
a single colony of the two strains was picked from MH agar
plates and incubated in the MH broth medium overnight in a
shaking incubator at 37 1C. The bacterial cell suspensions were
diluted by fresh MH broth medium before use.
Ag NCs uptake determination
The uptake amount of Ag was measured by ICP-MS. Before
measurement, the bacteria were treated with Ag NPs (the
concentration of Ag was noted as Ag0), bearing diﬀerent surface
ligands for 2 h. Following this, the bacteria were washed and
the concentration of Ag left in the solution (Ag1) after treatment
was determined. The uptake amount of Ag was calculated by

MIC determination
MIC was defined as the minimum drug concentration, under
which the bacteria could not grow in 16 h.31 The MIC towards
S. aureus, E. coli, and S. oneidensis MR-1 was determined by the
broth micro-dilution method, which was performed in 96-well
microtiter plates and added with MH broth solutions (100 mL).
The 96-well microtiter plates were arranged in two-fold dilution
from left to right and in triplicate. They were incubated at 37 1C
for 16 h. The results were measured by the Spark multimode
microplate reader (TECAN, Switzerland) at 600 nm. The initial
concentration of the bacterial solution was diluted 1000 folds
from OD600 = 0.5.

and randomly divided into 5 groups (three mice per group).

Results and discussion
To describe the synergism from two distinctive bactericides
(Ag+ species and Dpep), Dpep (CCLLLLRRRRRR) was employed
as a capping ligand to protect Ag NCs. Here, the design of two
cysteine residues was based on the consideration of the capture
of Ag+,33 and abundant arginine and leucine residues were
involved due to the verified antibacterial activity.34,35 The
preparation of Ag NCs was based on the reduction–decomposition–
reduction process (details seen in the experimental section).36
Briefly, Ag(I)–ligand complex was formed firstly and then the
reducing reagent NaBH4 was introduced to produce Ag NC
intermediates. After the decomposition of Ag NC intermediates
to colorless products, additional NaBH4 was introduced into
the above mixture and light-brown Ag NCs with strong pink
emission under the UV illumination was obtained (Fig. 1). The
maximum emission was centered at 635 nm when excited at
480 nm. The presence of the absorption peak at 480 nm also
indicated the formation of the size-focusing Ag NCs.36 Transmis-
sion electron microscopy (TEM) analysis and size distribution
histograms revealed that Dpep-capped Ag NCs were spherical in
shape and had average diameters of 1.25 nm (Fig. 1b). Using
electrospray ionization (ESI) high definition mass spectrometry
(HDMS) to further determine the chemical composition, we
found that [Ag19Dpep1] NCs were predominantly responsible for
the peaks observed in the positive mode (Fig. S1b and c, ESI†).
The [Ag19Dpep1] NCs carried 8, 7, 6 positive charges and
three sets of peaks at 458.04 m/z, 523.19 m/z, and 610.40 m/z
were present, respectively. Fig. 1c shows the position with eight
positively charged [Ag19Dpep1-4H] NCs accounting for the main
constituent, where the simulated molecular formula agreed
well with the experimental results. Other species observed in
the ESI† (e.g., Ag3Dpep1 and Ag20Dpep1) may be from the
fragmentation or the recombination of the Ag NCs during
ionization.37 Relative to the pure Dpep (Fig. S1a, ESI†), which
primarily has triply and quadruply positive charged constituents,
the possible more positive charges from [Ag19Dpep1] NCs were
also beneficial for interaction with the negatively charged bacterial
biofilms.
The antibacterial eﬃciency of Dpep-Ag NCs was firstly
demonstrated by comparing with the individual AMPs. We
screened the antibacterial capacity by choosing Gram-negative
bacteria
(E.
coli)
and
Gram-positive
bacteria
(S.
aureus)
as models. As shown in Fig. 2, Dpep exhibited similar anti-
bacterial eﬃciency as that using Opep38,39 (Fig. S2, ESI†)
towards Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli). The better anti-
bacterial capacity towards E. coli compared with that towards
Gram-positive bacteria stemmed from the target recognition
feature of the AMP due to the diﬀerent contents of LPS in the
two kinds of bacteria.40 Specifically, Gram-negative bacteria
with the presence of negatively charged LPS on the outer
membrane can be easily bonded with the AMP via electrostatic
interactions,
which
induces
perturbations
in
the
inner
membrane of live cells.14 As expected, the integration of Ag
NCs with Dpep in one entity showed enhanced bacterial growth
inhibition compared to the control groups due to the synergis-
tic eﬀect between AMP and Ag,41,42 especially towards E. coli.

The antibacterial performance of Dpep-Ag NCs with other Ag
containing counterparts was then compared. To have an overall
view of their antibacterial performance, we firstly compared the
antibacterial eﬃciency of the as-prepared Dpep-Ag NCs with
other ligand-capped Ag NCs since the capping agents have been
proved as an important factor that aﬀects the antibacterial
eﬃciency.30,43 Due to the shared size distribution and similar
light emission, BSA-Ag NCs (emission at 620 nm, Fig. S3 for
characterizations, ESI†) were employed as a ref. 29. As shown in
Fig. 3a, exhibiting totally diﬀerent optical density at 600 nm
(OD600), the BSA-Ag NCs treated group behaved in a similar
trend as that using the blank group while the Dpep-Ag
NC-treated group greatly repressed the growth of the bacteria
under the same Ag concentration. The agar plate assay (Fig. 3b)
also indicated that the antibacterial activity of Dpep-Ag NCs was
higher than that of BSA-Ag NCs. The results exemplified the
importance of the surface ligands assembled on the small-sized
NCs in improving the antimicrobial activity. In contrast to the
negatively charged BSA, the positively charged character of the
AMPs on the surface of Dpep-Ag NCs endowed them with
increased adhesion to the outer bacterial membrane, enhanced
permeability, and reduced drug eﬄux. Moreover, the superior
target recognition from Dpep was also responsible for the
enhanced antibacterial performance in comparison with that
of BSA-Ag NCs.
It
has
been
previously
reported41,44–46
that
with
the
decreased size of the Ag NPs, increased antibacterial activity
was observed in the size range of 5–100 nm. However, the
antibacterial efficiency comparison between Ag NCs (o2 nm)
and Ag NPs (B5 nm) was seldom compared and discussed.
To demonstrate the effect of the size-dependent antibacterial
performance, Opep with comparable antibacterial performance
was chosen for the preparation of Ag NPs (Opep-Ag NPs).
The average size of the as-prepared Opep-Ag NPs was ca. 5 nm
(Fig. 4a), which was a little larger than that of Ag NCs. As shown in
Fig. 3, Dpep-Ag NCs showed better antibacterial activity than that
of Opep-Ag NPs. It is known that extra-small particles have a
significantly enhanced tendency of permeating into the micro-
organism. Moreover, we suspected the surface charge and
the predominant Ag+ species also contributed to the enhanced
activity. To further reveal the underlying mechanism, we first
checked their z potentials. As shown in Fig. 4b, Opep, as a typical
kind of cationic AMP, possessed a strong positively charged
feature in water. Compared with the individual AMP, the charge
levels of the Opep-Ag NPs and Dpep-Ag NCs were both improved,
especially the latter. We assumed that the increased level of
positive charges stemmed from the abundance of Ag+ species.

Opep-Ag NPs and Dpep-Ag NCs. As shown in Fig. 4c, the Ag 3d3/2

the combination levels of the Dpep-Ag NCs with the bacteria

high local concentration of Ag+ for killing the bacteria efficiently.
To understand the mechanism of the antimicrobial ability,
the level of intracellular ROS in the Dpep-Ag NC-treated samples
were measured. The ROS kit was used to evaluate the ROS level
generated in the bacteria after incubation with different kinds of
Ag NPs. It was found that Dpep-Ag NCs induced more than
8-fold increase in the ROS level when compared with that of
the BSA-Ag NC-treated group. The trend of ROS production by
Dpep-Ag NCs, BSA-Ag NCs, and Opep-Ag NPs was in accordance
with their bacterial killing efficiency. It was noticed that ROS
generation contributed a lot in killing the bacteria.
To further aﬃrm the eﬃcacy of Dpep-Ag NCs, two kinds of
the Gram-negative strains including E. coli and Shewanella
oneidensis MR-1 (S. oneidensis MR-1) and one kind of Gram-
positive strain (S. aureus) were selected as the models to register
MIC through the standard broth dilution method. As demon-
strated in Table 1, the Dpep-Ag NCs had a strikingly improved
antibacterial eﬃciency against E. coli, S. aureus, and S. oneidensis
MR-1 with MIC of 6.5, 13.8, and 0.65 mM, respectively. The MIC
was much lower than that using BSA-Ag NCs due to the surface
ligand eﬀect. Integrating the ultra-small size with the unique
antibacterial performance from the surface ligands, the MIC of
Dpep-Ag NCs decreased by about 2 orders of magnitudes than
those of the commercial Ag NPs (30 nm) against Gram-negative
strains. The lower MIC against S. oneidensis MR-1 may be due to
its innately slower growth rate, leading to its sensitive reaction to
all of the antiseptics.47 The MIC of chloramphenicol (Cm) against
E. coli with resistance was as high as 900 mM, which was in
accordance with the previous results.48

Based on the satisfactory results we got above, the anti-
bacterial eﬃciency and the practical applications of Dpep-

rats before treatment were cut with wounds (B0.25 cm2) on the
dorsal skin and infected with an equivalent amount of
Cm-resistant E. coli. After a day of proliferation, the wounds
were treated with water (blank), Cm (0.5 mM), BSA-Ag NCs,
Opep-Ag NPs, and Dpep-Ag NCs with the same content of Ag
(200 mM and 100 mL), respectively. The amount of usage is given
in Table 1 and Table S1 (ESI†). As shown in Fig. 5a, the wounds
can be healed as time is prolonged. Among these alternatives,
the Cm (500 mM) treated group showed no obvious better
healing eﬀect than the control group due to the resistance.
On the other hand, Opep-Ag NPs and Dpep-Ag NCs obviously
accelerated the wound closure of mice with the wounds healed
by 80% and 91% at 5 d, respectively (Fig. 5b). In the meantime,
the tissue slices of the wounds (Fig. 5c) after diﬀerent disposals
are represented. Compared with the wound treated with water
after one day, the surface at the 5 d wound became flatter but
abundant inflammatory cells existed. Opep-Ag NP- and Dpep-Ag
NC-treated groups showed less inflammatory cells. Furthermore,
as the arrows indicated, appendage-like blood vessels were
regenerated in the Dpep-Ag NC-treated group. On the other hand,
in the BSA-Ag NC- and Cm-treated groups, no obvious cutaneous
appendage regeneration was observed. This behavior further
verified our speculation and was in accordance with the wound
size change results. Dpep-Ag NCs displayed the fast healing eﬀect
with a significantly reduced cost, showing its potential as an
alternative to antibiotics.
Conclusions
A synergistic antibacterial platform utilizing two distinctive
bactericides of Ag+ species and well-designed AMP of Dpep
was constructed. Dpep was employed as the capping ligand to
synthesize Ag NCs to ensure that both Ag+ species and AMP are
delivered as a single package. The as-prepared Dpep-Ag NCs
exhibited much higher antibacterial activity relative to the other
NCs, which can, in part, be attributed to their stronger target
binding to LPS in Gram-negative bacteria based on the capping
agent eﬀect and improved positive charges. The small size-
induced enhanced antibacterial activity was revealed by com-
paring with commercial Ag NPs (30 nm) and Opep-Ag NPs with
B5 nm size distribution. Mechanistic investigations demon-
strated that improved Ag+ uptake and ROS generation may be
the factors involved in the enhanced bacterial-killing process.
The in vivo wound healing model further showed its safety and
reliability with significantly accelerated wound closure eﬀect.
This present study provided insights and inspirations for the
rational engineering of target-guided extra-small functionalized
NCs for biological applications.

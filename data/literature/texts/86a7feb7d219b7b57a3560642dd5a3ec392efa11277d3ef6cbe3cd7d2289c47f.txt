A B S T R A C T   
Obesity is widely considered a chronic inflammatory disease and is recognized as one of the major challenges to 
global public health, but there is no successful anti-inflammatory therapy for obesity so far. In this study, 
atomically precise Au22 nanoclusters are introduced as reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging clusterzymes, 
with the aim of achieving anti-inflammation in high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity. Au22 clusterzymes display 
high catalase (CAT)-like and superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activities, excellent antioxidant capacity. We 
demonstrate that Au22 clusterzymes treatment can effectively scavenge ROS, relieve the antioxidant system 
disorder, and restrain the inflammation in HFD-induced obese mice, with good biocompatibility. After 30 days of 
Au22 clusterzymes treatment, high therapeutic efficacy in anti-obesity is observed in HFD-induced obese mice, 
including decreased body weight by 13%, reduced serum levels of key inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, and 
TNF-α) in the obese mice by 23 – 48%, improved glucose tolerance and lipid metabolism, and increased 
browning of white adipose tissue (WAT). Overall, Au22 clusterzymes have good performance on ROS-scavenging 
and anti-inflammation in vivo, offering a promising approach to combat obesity and related metabolic diseases.   
1. Introduction 
Obesity is a pathological state characterized by excessive accumu­
lation of fat in the body, resulting in abnormal weight gain. It is 
considered as one of the major challenges to global public health due to 
its association with various diseases such as type 2 diabetes, cardio­
vascular disease, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [1]. The main 
factors contributing to obesity include genetics, excessive calorie intake, 
reduced physical activity, use of drugs with side effects of increasing 
body weight, inadequate sleep, and other factors [2,3]. According to the 
latest report from the World Obesity Federation, it is estimated that by 
2035, more than 4 billion (or 51%) people over five years old will be 
overweight or obese, leading to a reduction in global GDP by 2.9% (US$ 
4.32 trillion) [4]. Common treatments for obesity include lifestyle 
changes, medication, and bariatric surgery. However, these in­
terventions face challenges such as low efficiency, side effects, and high 
risk. Weight loss drugs, also known as anti-obesity medications, are 
commonly used to achieve body weight loss by reducing energy ab­
sorption and suppressing appetite. Unfortunately, these drugs are 
associated with various side effects and their safety in clinical practice is 
questionable [5]. For instances, orlistat has been reported to cause 
gastrointestinal symptoms and liver injury; liraglutide is associated with 
diarrhoea, constipation, pancreatitis, and gallstones. Therefore, there is 
a need for scientists to explore new anti-obesity methods that offer both 
efficacy and higher safety. 
The nature of obesity should be deciphered in order to explore effi­
cient and safe anti-obesity methods. Indeed, obesity is a widely recog­
nized chronic inflammatory disease, featuring the occurrence of a 
chronic inflammatory state in adipose tissue [6]. This chronic inflam­
mation is induced by obesity through factors such as macrophage 
infiltration in adipose tissue [7], mitochondrial dysfunction [8], and 
endoplasmic reticulum stress [9]. Subsequently, the chronic inflamma­
tion impairs the functions of adipose tissue, including glucose uptake 
and lipid storage, leading to aggravating insulin resistance and lip­
otoxicity. Consequently, this creates a positive feedback loop by 
enhancing inflammation in adipose tissue [10]. Numerous studies have 
extensively reported inflammation as a driving force behind obesity- 
related metabolic diseases, but unfortunately, there have not been

anti-inflammatory therapies by reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scav­

acute liver/kidney/lung injury [12–14], accelerating spinal cord injury 
recovery [15], alleviating intestinal inflammation [16], managing Par­

metallic nanomaterials known as atomically precise metal nanoclusters 
(NCs) has garnered attention in the biomedical field [19]. For instance, 
Zhang et al. employed single-atom engineering to develop clusterzymes 
Au24Cd1 NCs with high catalytic activity and selectivity, demonstrating 
their effectiveness in modulating neuroinflammation in injured brain 
[20]. Subsequently, they used catalytically active Au NCs for noninva­

study is the hypothesis that the development of biocompatible yet effi­
cient clusterzymes could lead to successful anti-inflammatory therapy in 
obesity. 
Herein we report the application of atomically precise Au22(SG)18 
NCs (here SG denotes tripeptide glutathione) as ROS-scavenging clus­
terzymes, termed as Au22 clusterzymes, for anti-inflammation-mediated 
therapy of high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity (Fig. 1). The antioxidant 
capacity of the Au22 clusterzymes, which exhibits combinational high 
catalase (CAT)-like and superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activities, en­
ables it to efficiently scavenge ROS. This ROS-scavenging property, 
along with the superior biocompatibility of the Au22 clusterzymes, re­
lieves the antioxidant system disorder and results in satisfactory thera­
peutic efficacy in anti-obesity. Treatment of HFD-induced obese mice 
with the Au22 clusterzymes for 30 days leads to a remarkable body 
weight loss of 13%, and improves glucose tolerance and lipid meta­
bolism. Additionally, the Au22 clusterzymes promote the browning of 
white adipose tissue (WAT), further contributing to its high therapeutic 
efficacy in treating HFD-induced obesity. This study provides a para­
digm for anti-inflammation-mediated obesity therapy through the use of 
novel metal clusterzymes. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials 
All chemicals are commercially available and used without further 
purification unless otherwise stated. Gold (III) chloride tetrahydrate was 
purchased from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. Reduced glutathione (GSH), 
sodium borohydride (NaBH4), xanthine (X), and bovine serum albumin 
(BSA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Xanthine oxidase (XO), 30% 
gelatinizing solution (29:1), SDS-PAGE separating gel buffer (4×) and 
SDS-PAGE spacer gel buffer (4×) were purchased from Solarbio. 
Endogenous peroxidase blocking solution (3% H2O2) was purchased 
from Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Dihydroethidium (DHE, 
superoxide anion fluorescent probe), and p-phthalic acid (PTA) were 
purchased from Macklin. Antifade mounting medium with 4′,6-dia­
midino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI), Triton X-100 and PMSF 
(100 mM) were purchased from Beyotime. Glucose injection (50%) was 
purchased from China Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. DifcoTM skim 
milk was purchased from BD. Omni-ECLTM femto light chem­
iluminescence kit were purchased from Epizyme. 
2.2. Synthesis of Au22 clusterzymes 
Au22 clusterzymes (Au22(SG)18 NCs) were synthesized by the previ­
ously reported method [22], with minor modification. In a typical syn­
thesis, 3.0 mL HAuCl4⋅4H2O (20 mM), 1.8 mL GSH (50 mM), and 55.2 
mL ultrapure water were added into a three-necked flask and then 
vigorously stirred, followed by adjusting pH to 11.0 with NaOH (1 M). 
After that 2.52 mL NaBH4 (1 mM) was added dropwise and then mixed 
adequately by stirring, followed by adjusting pH to 2.5 with HCl (1 M). 
After gentle stirring for 6 h, the crude products were obtained, and then 
purified with an ultrafiltration centrifuge tube (3 kDa) under centrifu­
gation (4000 rpm) for 30 min. 
2.3. Characterization 
UV–vis absorption spectra and photoluminescence spectra were 
recorded by a Persee TU-1901 UV–Vis spectrometer and Horiba 
FluoroMax-4 spectrofluorometer, respectively. Transmission electron 
microscope (TEM) images were obtained on a Jem-2100F transmission 
electron microscope. Electrospray ionization mass spectra (ESI-MS) 
were obtained on a Bruker Impact II ESI-MS system in negative ion 
mode. Luminescence lifetime measurement was carried out on an 
Edinburgh FLS 1000 photoluminescence spectrometer.

2.4. Sod-like and CAT-like activities evaluation 
The SOD-like and CAT-like activities of Au22 clusterzymes were 
evaluated by the elimination efficiency of O2
•−and H2O2 respectively, 
according to our previous report [23]. In brief, for SOD-like activity, X 
and XO produced O2
•−; Au22 clusterzymes were added to eliminate O2
•−; 
DHE reacted with the residual O2
•−resulting in the fluorescent product 
ethidium. Accordingly, the SOD-like of Au22 clusterzymes can be 
quantified by the fluorescence signal from ethidium, and a low fluo­
rescence intensity means a high SOD-like activity. As for CAT-like ac­
tivity, H2O2 reacted with PTA resulting in the fluorescent product HTA. 
Au22 clusterzymes can decompose H2O2, resulting in a decreased HTA. 
Accordingly, the CAT-like of Au22 clusterzymes can be quantified by the 
fluorescence signal from HTA, and a low fluorescence intensity means a 
high CAT-like activity. The fluorescence signal was recorded by a 
Lengguang F96Pro spectrofluorometer. 
2.5. Total antioxidant capacity evaluation 
The total antioxidant capacity of Au22 clusterzymes or trolox was 
determined by a rapid 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline 6-sulfo­
nate) (ABTS) method using a total antioxidant capacity assay kit 
(Beyotime). The antioxidant capacity was ultimately evaluated by the 
ABTS•+ intensity, which was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 
414 nm using a TECAN Infinite M200 PRO microplate reader. 
2.6. Animals 
All animal experiments were performed under the approval and 
guidance of the Ethics Committee of Laboratory Animals of Wenzhou 
Institute, University of Chinese Academy of Sciences (WIU­
CAS21110808). Male C57BL/6J mice (6 weeks) were purchased from 
Zhejiang Laboratory Animal Center (Hangzhou, China). Mice were kept 
at 21 ± 1 ◦C under a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle with free access to 
food and water. 
2.7. Biodistribution of Au22 clusterzymes in mice 
Male C57BL/6J mice (6 weeks) were fed with HFD (60% Kcal fat, 
Research Diets) for 12 weeks. Then the HFD-induced obese mice 
received a single intraperitoneal injection (i.p.) of Au22 clusterzymes (1 
mg in 600 μL 0.9% NaCl solution). At 6, 12, and 24 h after injection 
respectively, the mice were sacrificed and inguinal WAT (iWAT), peri­
renal WAT (pWAT), dorsal subcutaneous WAT (dsWAT), heart, liver, 
spleen, lungs, and kidneys were harvested. The fluorescent images of 
these tissues and organs were obtained on an in vivo imaging system 
(IVIS) (Lumina Series III, PerkinElmer). Meanwhile, Au element in WAT 
was determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP- 
MS) (Agilent 7700). 
2.8. Cytotoxicity assay 
The cytotoxicity of Au22 clusterzymes on white adipocytes was 
evaluated using 3-(4,5 dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium 
bromide (MTT) assay. 3T3-L1 preadipocytes (ZQXZBIO) were differen­
tiated into white adipocytes as described in our previous study [24]. The 
white adipocytes were incubated with a complete medium containing 
varying concentrations of Au22 clusterzymes (2.5, 5, 25, 50, or 125 μg/ 
mL) for 48 h, following by adding MTT solution. After further incubation 
for 4 h, DMSO was added. Finally, the cell viability was quantified by the 
spectrophotometrical absorbance using a TECAN Infinite M200 PRO 
microplate reader. 
2.9. Hemolysis assay 
Fresh blood from the HFD-induced obese mice was centrifuged to 
collect red blood cells (RBCs). RBCs were washed until the upper layer 
appeared colorless, and then were diluted to 2% RBCs suspension. 
Subsequently, 100 μL RBCs suspension was mixed thoroughly with 1 mL 
Au22 clusterzymes solution (50, 100, 200, 400, or 800 μg/mL). Mean­
while, 0.9% NaCl solution and 1% Triton X-100 solution, instead of Au22 
clusterzymes solution, were used as negative and positive control, 
respectively. After incubation at 37 ◦C for 3 h, the mixture was centri­
fuged, and then hemolysis was observed and imaged. The absorbance of 
the supernatant at 540 nm was quantified by a TECAN Infinite M200 
PRO microplate plate reader. Hemolysis rate can be obtained by the 
formula: Hemolysis rate (%) = (Asample −
Aneg)/(Apos −
Aneg) × 100%, 
where Asample, Aneg, and Apos were the absorbance values of Au22 clus­
terzymes solution, negative control, and positive control, respectively. 
2.10. In vivo Au22 clusterzymes treatment 
There were three groups in this experiment, i.e., Normal group 
(standard chow feed mice), HFD group (HFD-induced obese mice), and 
HFD+Au22 group (HFD-induced obese mice treated with Au22 clus­
terzymes). Prior to treatment, male C57BL/6J mice (6 weeks) in HFD 
group and HFD+Au22 group were fed with HFD, while those in Normal 
group were fed with standard chow for 12 weeks. Then the mice in 
HFD+Au22 group received 15 rounds injections of Au22 clusterzymes (1 
mg in 600 μL 0.9% NaCl solution for each injection, once two days, i.p.), 
while those in Normal group and HFD group received injections of 0.9% 
NaCl solution. The feeding conditions during the treatment were the 
same as before. After that the mice were sacrificed, and iWAT, pWAT, 
dsWAT, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were harvested, mean­
while blood was collected. 
2.11. Body weight, Lee’s index and organ coefficient 
The body weight was measured two days after each treatment. The 
body length (straight-line distance from the tip of the nose to the anus) 
was measured at the last weighing. Lee’s index was obtained by the 
formula: Lee’s index = (Body weight (g) × 1000)1/3/Body length (cm). 
Meanwhile, organ coefficient was obtained by the formula: Organ co­
efficient = Total organs mass (g)/Body weight (g), in which the total 
organs mass included heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys. 
2.12. Glucose tolerance test (GTT) 
The mice underwent overnight fasting before GTT. The initial blood 
glucose concentration (0 min) was determined using the blood from the 
tail of the mice. Then mice received glucose injection (3 g/kg, i.p.), and 
the blood glucose concentration was determined at 15, 30, 60, 90, and 
120 min after injection, respectively. 
2.13. Detection of antioxidant enzymes, inflammatory factors, 
biochemical markers, and lipid metabolism indicators in serum 
Antioxidant enzymes (CAT and SOD), as well as GSH and oxidized 
glutathione (GSSG), were determined using corresponding assay kits 
(Beyotime). Inflammatory factors IL-1β and IL-6 were determined using 
IL-1β ELISA kit (Boyun) and IL-6 ELISA kit (Boyun) respectively, while 
TNF-α was determined using TNF-α ELISA kit (MultiSciences). 
Biochemical markers (alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate trans­
aminase (AST), albumin (ALB), total protein (TP), creatinine (CRE), uric 
acid (UA), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN)) and lipid metabolism in­
dicators (Triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipo­
protein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL- 
C)) were detected using corresponding assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng 
Bioengineering Institute).

2.14. H&E staining 
The lobes of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys)

Frozen sections of liver and iWAT were prepared by a Leica CM1950

30 min protected from light, and then washed with PBS, followed by 
DAPI incubation. The sections were imaged by a KEYENCE BZ-X800 
automated high-resolution microscope. 
2.16. Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) 
The total RNA was extracted by RNAiso Plus (Takara) and then was 
quantified by a Thermo NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, followed by 
reverse transcription using TOROIVD®qRT Master Mix 2.0 (TOROIVD). 
TOROGreen®qPCR Master Mix (TOROIVD) was used in qPCR. The 
relative mRNA expression was quantified by the delta-delta threshold 
cycle (Ct) method. The primer sequences for qPCR are listed in Sup­
plementary Table S1. 
2.17. Western blotting analysis 
Total proteins from liver and iWAT were extracted using RIPA buffer 
(Beyotime), supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime). 
The concentration of protein was determined using a BCA protein assay 
kit (GLPBIO). Subsequently, the protein was separated in 12% SDS- 
PAGE gel and then transferred to PVDF membranes (Cytiva). The 
membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and then incubated with 
primary antibodies overnight at 4 ◦C, followed by incubation with sec­
ondary antibodies. Finally, the bands were visualized and qualified by a 
Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system. The antibodies for Western 
blotting analysis are listed in Supplementary Table S2. 
2.18. Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence 
The preparation of paraffin sections was the same as that in H&E 
staining experiment. The sections were firstly blocked by 5% BSA and 
incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ◦C. For immunohis­
tochemistry, the sections were then treated using a rabbit two-step 
immunohistochemistry kit (ZSGB-BIO). Finally, the sections were 
counterstained with hematoxylin and then were imaged by a KFBIO KF- 
PRO-005 digital pathology slide scanner. For immunofluorescence, the 
sections were then washed and incubated with the secondary antibodies 
for 1 h, followed by staining nucleus with DAPI. The sections were 
imaged by a KEYENCE BZ-X800 automated high-resolution microscope. 
2.19. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number 
Total DNA was extracted from frozen iWAT using TIANamp Genomic 
DNA Kit (TIANGEN). Mitochondrial encoded cytochrome oxidase III 
(mtCo3) and TOROGreen®qPCR Master Mix (TOROIVD) were used in 
qPCR. The amount of cytosolic mtDNA was normalized to 18S. The 
primer sequences for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S1. 
2.20. Data analysis 
All statistical analyses were performed by GraphPad Prism software. 
Statistical methods: Student’s t-test was used for comparison of two 
samples; one-way ANOVA was used for comparison of multiple samples. 
A p-value less than 0.05 is considered statistically significant, presented 
as * (p < 0.05), ** (p < 0.01), or *** (p < 0.001). Errors bars plotted on 
graphs are presented as the mean ± SD. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Characteristics and SOD/CAT-like activity of Au22 clusterzymes 
In this study, atomically precise Au22(SG)18 NCs are chosen as a 
clusterzyme model since the following advantages: 1) the bioinert na­
ture of gold and the biocompatible attribute of tri-peptide SG guarantee 
its excellent biocompatibility; 2) SG contains a thiol group and possesses 
antioxidant properties, ensuring good protection of the Au kernel and 
possibly enhanced ROS-scavenging capability; 3) the bright photo­
luminescence allows for easy investigation of fundamental issues such as 
biodistributions and tissue uptake. 
As shown in Fig. 2A, Au22(SG)18 contains a bitetrahedron Au7 kernel 
protected by a [Au6(SR)6] motif and three Au3(SR)4 staples [25]. Au22 
clusterzymes obtained in this study are firstly characterized by TEM and 
ESI-MS. The TEM image displays that Au22 clusterzymes are highly 
monodisperse with kernel sizes < 2 nm (Figure S1, Supporting Infor­
mation), indicating the successful formation of ultrasmall NCs. 
Regarding the atomically precise size analysis, the ESI-MS displays three 
sets of dominant peaks at m/z = ~1640, ~1968, and ~2460 in the range 
of 1500 – 3000 m/z (left panel of Fig. 2B), which can be clearly assigned 
to Au22(SG)18
−
carrying −6, −5, and −4 charges, respectively. This result 
is corroborated by the well match between the experimentally obtained 
isotope pattern of [Au22(SG)18
−
+ 3Na – 7H]5−
and the theoretically 
simulated one (middle and right panels in Fig. 2B). 
The optical properties of Au22 clusterzymes are investigated using 
UV–vis absorption spectrometer and luminescence spectrometer. The 
UV–vis spectrum shows an absorption peak at 520 nm, meanwhile the 
maximum wavelengths for photoexcitation and photoemission are 
respectively 520 nm and 665 nm (Fig. 2C), consistent with the previ­
ously reported Au22(SG)18 NCs [26]. Moreover, the luminescence decay 
profile reveals an average lifetime of 0.97 μs (Figure S2, Supporting 
Information). It is observed that Au22 clusterzymes in water emit bright 
red luminescence under UV light (Fig. 2D). The large Stokes shift (>140 
nm), bright red luminescence, and long luminescence lifetime suggest 
that Au22 clusterzymes have the aggregation-induced emission feature 
[27], making fundamental studies such as biodistributions and tissue 
uptake of Au22 possible. 
The SOD-like activity of Au22 clusterzymes is evaluated by the 
elimination efficiency of O2
•−, while the CAT-like activity is evaluated by 
the elimination efficiency of H2O2. With the increase of concentration of 
Au22 clusterzymes from 1 μg/mL to 10 μg/mL, the SOD-like activity 
increases from 34% to 77% while the CAT-like activity increases from 
23% to 70% (Fig. 2E; Figure S3, Supporting Information). Previous 
studies have demonstrated that Au NCs have high ROS-scavenging ca­
pacity and antioxidant activity, by mimicking natural enzymes espe­
cially SOD and CAT [21,28,29]. More interestingly, the Au22 
clusterzymes exhibit superior SOD-like and CAT-like activities, which 
endow them capable of scavenging ROS for obesity treatment. Excessive 
ROS will lead to oxidative damage to intracellular lipids, proteins and 
genetic substances, resulting in a variety of diseases including obesity, 
rheumatoid arthritis, insulin resistance, liver damage, and tumors [30]. 
The SOD-like activity of Au22 clusterzymes can convert O2
•−to H2O2, 
while the CAT-like activity can further convert H2O2 to less reactive O2. 
Therefore, Au22 clusterzymes can be considered as tandem artificial 
enzymes in scavenging ROS. The above results distinctly indicate that 
Au22 clusterzymes can effectively scavenge ROS by mimicking SOD and 
CAT simultaneously. Obviously, the combined SOD-like and CAT-like 
activities endow Au22 clusterzymes with high efficiency in scavenging 
ROS. Meanwhile, the total antioxidant capacity of Au22 clusterzymes is 
determined to be 30 times higher than that of the natural antioxidant

Dietary ROS scavengers have received great attention, due to their 
natural occurrence and diverse biological functions. Recently, dietary 
ROS scavenger-based nanomaterials have been widely developed for 
prevention and treatment of a variety of ROS-related chronic diseases 
[31]. Fortunately, Au22 clusterzymes in this study exhibit comparable or 
even superior ROS-scavenging capability (i.e. SOD-like and CAT-like

example, the SOD-like activity (elimination efficiency of O2

[33]. The CAT-like activity (elimination efficiency of H2O2) for Au22 
clusterzymes (10 μg/mL) is 70%, while approximately 60% for Au/ 
CeO2@hyaluronic acid nanozymes (100 μg/mL) [34]. 
3.2. Biocompatibility and biodistribution of Au22 clusterzymes in HFD- 
induced obese mice 
The above results have demonstrated the ROS-scavenging and anti­
oxidant capability of Au22 clusterzymes, thus providing a potential 
approach to combat obesity through anti-inflammation. Therefore, Au22 
clusterzymes are applied for treating HFD-induced obese mice in this 
study (Fig. 3A). To evaluate the biocompatibility of Au22 clusterzymes, 
the cytotoxicity is firstly determined by MTT assay. It can be known that, 
in the range of 2.5 – 125 μg/mL, Au22 clusterzymes show no significant 
effect on the cell viability of white adipocytes (Figure S4, Supporting 
Information). In vitro hemolysis test displays, in the range of 50 – 800 
μg/mL, the hemolysis rates are all below 3% (Figure S5, Supporting 
Information), lower than the standard safe limit (5%) [35]. Meanwhile, 
H&E staining images show that, after treatment with Au22 clusterzymes, 
no discernible pathological alterations are observed in important his­
tological parameters, including tissue architecture, interstitial spacing, 
and nuclear morphology, across vital organs such as the heart, liver, 
spleen, lungs, and kidneys (Fig. 3B). 
Furthermore, a series of important biochemical markers in serum are 
detected. It can be known that the levels of ALT, AST, ALB, TP, CRE, UA, 
and BUN for Normal group, HFD group, and HFD+Au22 group are 
generally in the normal range (Fig. 3C). It is noteworthy that HFD- 
induced obesity results in varying degrees of elevation in these critical 
serum biochemical markers. However, Au22 clusterzymes treatment 
effectively mitigates this upward trend. These findings collectively 
suggest that Au22 clusterzymes have a high degree of biocompatibility 
and no deleterious impact on murine health. 
The biodistribution of Au22 clusterzymes can be traced without 
modification benefiting from the luminescence property as mentioned 
above. It is observed that, at 6, 12, and 24 h after Au22 clusterzymes 
injection, Au22 clusterzymes are mainly accumulated in liver, lungs, 
kidneys, and WAT (Fig. 3D). Previous studies have showed that liver, 
lungs, and kidneys were the targeted organs of Au22 NCs [36]. Espe­
cially, we demonstrate that WAT is also a dominant organ for Au22 
clusterzymes accumulation in this study. Besides, ICP-MS verifies the 
accumulation of Au in WAT (Fig. 3E). In a word, our results confirm the 
existence of Au22 clusterzymes in WAT, which is a necessary precondi­
tion for Au22 clusterzymes to scavenge local ROS in WAT.

Glucose tolerance reflects the body’s ability to regulate blood 
glucose. Obesity is usually accompanied with insulin resistance, result­
ing in decreased ability in regulation of blood glucose and reduced 
glucose tolerance. GTT result displays that, the blood glucose concen­
tration in HFD group increases rapidly after glucose injection, much 
higher than that in Normal group (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, the blood 
glucose concentration in HFD group comes down very slowly and is not 
able to reach the normal level two hours after the injection of glucose, 
indicating that glucose tolerance is impaired in HFD-induced obese 
mice. As for HFD+Au22 group, the curve of blood glucose concentration 
is similar with that for Normal group, rather than HFD group. Mean­
while, the area under the curve (AUC) in HFD+Au22 group is nearly 3/5 
of that in HFD group (Fig. 5E). These results reveal that Au22 clus­
terzymes treatment can remarkedly recover the impairment of glucose 
tolerance in HFD-induced obesity. 
The occurrence and development of obesity is closely related to the 
disorders of lipid metabolism. As expected, the mice in HFD group have 
higher levels of TG, TC, LDL-C but lower level of HDL-C in serum 
comparing with Normal group (Fig. 5F). After treatment with Au22 
clusterzymes, the levels of TG, TC, and LDL-C decrease while the level of 
HDL-C increases significantly, revealing that Au22 clusterzymes treat­
ment can evidently improve the lipid metabolism in mice thus combat 
HFD-induced obesity. 
WAT browning can increase energy expenditure and thermogenesis, 
as well as improve glucose and lipid homeostasis, thus is considered as a 
promising therapeutic target to prevent and treat obesity [37]. WAT 
browning refers to the development of beige adipocytes in WAT in 
response to specific stimuli [38]. UCP1 plays a central role in non­
shivering thermogenesis [39], and is considered as the most important 
biomarker of WAT browning. In addition, PPARγ and PGC-1α are key 
transcription factors in regulation of WAT browning [40]. 
Au22 clusterzymes treatment significantly up-regulates the mRNA 
expressions of Ucp1, Pparg, and Pgc-1α (Fig. 6A). Meanwhile, Western 
blotting analysis shows that the protein expressions of UCP1, PPARγ, 
and PGC-1α in HFD+Au22 group are much higher than those in HFD 
group (Fig. 6B), which is further confirmed by immunohistochemistry 
and immunofluorescence experiments (Fig. 6C, D). The images of 
immunohistochemistry show that the size of adipocytes for iWAT in 
HFD+Au22 group is much smaller than that in HFD group. This is 
possibly due to the formation of multilocular and small lipid droplets in 
white adipocytes, which is a typical characteristic of WAT browning 
[41]. Furthermore, abundance of mitochondria in iWAT is quantified by 
mtDNA copy number. The mtDNA copy number in HFD+Au22 group is 
much higher than that in HFD group (Fig. 6E), indicating an increased 
thermogenesis level after Au22 clusterzymes treatment. These results 
reveal that Au22 clusterzymes treatment can effectively promote WAT 
browning and thermogenesis, thus curb HFD-induced obesity. 
As an essential endocrine organ, WAT secretes numerous adipokines 
that play important roles in regulating whole-body metabolism [42]. 
Moreover, inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) have sig­
nificant impacts on the capability of WAT browning [43,44]. Our results 
show that Au22 clusterzymes can scavenge ROS in vivo and inhibit 
inflammation in HFD-induced obese mice, meanwhile promote WAT 
browning. Unfortunately, the relationship between the decreased 
inflammation and increased WAT browning needs further exploration, 
which is beneficial to understand the potential anti-obesity mechanism 
of Au22 clusterzymes in HFD-induced obesity. 
4. Conclusion 
In summary, we have reported atomically precise Au22 NCs as ROS- 
scavenging clusterzymes for anti-inflammatory therapy in the treatment 
of HFD-induced obesity. The combined SOD-like and CAT-like activities 
endow Au22 clusterzymes excellent antioxidant capacity. In vivo 
experiments show that Au22 clusterzymes have high performance on 
scavenging ROS, relieving the antioxidant system disorder, and anti- 
inflammation in the HFD-induced obese mice, with good biocompati­
bility. After treatment with Au22 clusterzymes for 30 days, the obese 
mice acquire body weight loss of 13%, meanwhile glucose tolerance and 
lipid metabolism are remarkably improved. Besides anti-inflammation, 
the promotion effect of Au22 clusterzymes on WAT browning may also 
contribute to the high therapeutic efficacy in treatment of HFD-induced 
obesity. This study demonstrates that Au22 clusterzymes can effectively 
scavenge ROS and restrain inflammation, providing a new strategy to 
combat obesity and related metabolic diseases.

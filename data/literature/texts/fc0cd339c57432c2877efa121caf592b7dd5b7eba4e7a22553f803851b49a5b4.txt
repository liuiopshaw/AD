ABSTRACT: It is well established that the dynamic hydration
shell plays a vital role in macromolecular functions such as
protein−ligand, protein−protein, protein−DNA, and protein−lipid
interactions. Here we investigate how the water modality affects
conformational changes, solubility, and motion of fibrillar proteins.
The hypothesis is that the introduction of a poly hydroxyl amino
acid would increase solvation of the fibril forming peptides,
preventing their misfolding and aggregation. For the amyloid β
(Aβ) peptide, which is considered to be connected with nervous
system diseases, including dementia and cognitive decline in
Alzheimer’s disease, the formation of β-sheet fibrils always occurs
with a conformational change and a decrease in the dynamic
hydration shell around Aβ(1−42). We present novel cyclic D-
amino acid peptides that effectively inhibit fibrillation through affecting the dynamic hydration shell of Aβ(1−42) in vitro. Using de
novo design within the software Molecular Operating Environment (MOE), five different peptides that recognize Alzheimer’s fibrils
were designed and synthesized. Three of them were cyclic all-D-amino acid peptides incorporating the same polyhydroxy building
block derived from D-glucosaminic acid (GA). One peptide was the parent cyclic all D-amino acid inhibitor with no GA incorporated,
and another was an all L-amino acid linear fibrillation inhibitor. The GA-containing peptides were found to show significantly
improved inhibition of Aβ(1−42) aggregation. The inhibition was dramatically improved by the synergistic application of two GA
peptides targeting each end of the growing fibril. The present study may facilitate future developments of intervention strategies for
Alzheimer’s disease and similar neurodegenerative diseases.
■INTRODUCTION
Amyloid β (Aβ) peptide is generated by proteolysis of the
neuroprotective and multifunctional amyloid precursor protein
(APP).1 It is excised by β-secretase 1 [an aspartyl protease, β-
APP-cleaving enzyme-1(BACE1)] at the ectodomain site and
γ-secretase [an enzymatic complex of catalytically active
presenilin (PS1/PS2), nicastrin, anterior pharynx-defective 1
and presenilin enhancer 2] at an intramembranous cleavage
site.2,3 Particularly, while the human Aβ sequence is highly
conserved across vertebrate taxa since around the Devonian
period, it has frequently been characterized as a functionless
metabolite.4,5 The structurally labile Aβ monomers, having a
dual structural preference toward both α-helix6 and β-strand,7
interact with itself, initially by dimerizing through the
nucleation sequence, KLVFFAE, followed by oligomerization.
This results in the formation of several different globular
oligomeric structures. Subsequently, primary amyloid nuclea-
tion leads to spherical oligomers and formation of larger donut
shaped protofibrils, closely followed by construction of fibrils
and precipitation in plaques.3,8,9 Thus, an efficient catalytic
cycle of fibrillation is established. A link between Alzheimer’s
Disease (AD), an incurable and chronic neurodegenerative
disorder, and the Aβ pathway was first proposed in 1991 with
the amyloid-cascade hypothesis.10−12 A homeostasis has been
suggested between Aβ(1−40) and Aβ(1−42), produced by
sequential cleavage by BACE1 and γ-secretase, in which
Aβ(1−42) is believed to be the main cause of the neurotoxic
effects.13,14 Other reports indicate toxicity to be mainly due to
predominant Aβ(1−40) probably through NCAM2 degrada-
tion and other Aβ-associated proteolytic activities.15,16
Missense mutations of APP, PS1, and PS2 genes, and
imbalanced APP processing can offset the homeostasis and
increase the ratio of Aβ(1−42)/Aβ(1−40). This in turn results
in erroneous downstream events such as Aβ(1−42) oligome-
rization and protein deposition, eventually leading to cognitive
impairment and dementia.14,17 Despite decades of studies,
there is currently no direct proof linking the molecular
mechanism of Aβ aggregation to neurodegeneration, and after

However, based on translational research, from medicinal
experimental zoology to genetic and clinical pathology,
multiple points of evidence indicate an AD pathophysiology
model in which Aβ species trigger tau pathology. The
interaction between misfolded tau and Aβ then synergistically
drives AD progression.18−20 This finally leads to cortical
neurodegeneration.19,21,22 These mechanistic considerations
have tempted numerous research groups to develop inhibitors
for Aβ aggregation in AD including both small molecules and
macromolecules that target soluble Aβ monomers, Aβ
oligomers, β/γ-secretases, etc.23−26 So far, these studies have
encountered various insurmountable challenges. APP is a
multifunctional protein both when intact in the membrane and
when processed into smaller fragments. γ-Secretase is an
essential regulatory pivot in many physiological processes and
its inhibition brings about undesirable side effects.27,28
Discovery of viable medicines for treating AD requires more
studies of the molecular mechanism of Aβ aggregation and
activity in order to develop new methods of intervention,
including the complex interaction of Aβ with its aqueous
cellular environment.
Water, with its unique hydrogen bond (HB) network,
provides a mild operational environment for biomolecular
interactions. It controls cell membrane formation and supports
transport, complex formation and engagement of macro-
molecules in essential inter- and intracellular processes.29 The
hydrogen bond network of bulk water is dramatically affected
by the presence of solutes with hydrogen bond donors,
acceptors, and hydrophobic patches all involved in restriction
of the structure of both water and solutes. The orientation of
water with hydrogens toward hydrophobic parts of the solutes
has both an entropic and an enthalpic component, and
reorientation of this water shell is a driving force in
hydrophobic collapse and aggregation. Water reorientation
also seems to be important to drive specific molecular
interactions and binding.30,31 All events in biology are
preceded by an event of molecular recognition. As two
proteins approach each other, the native hydrogen bond
network of the surrounding water gradually increases to reduce
energy and guide the interaction. Thus, the entropy and HB-
enthalpy of hydration shell around the molecules significantly
facilitate their molecular function.32,33 It has been proposed
that fibrillation and elongation of Aβ fibrils undergo two
essential phases in a “dock−lock” mechanism in which Aβ
initially associates with a fibril template through a weak and
reversible interaction, then this deposited Aβ associates by an
essentially irreversible formation of the fibrillary β-sheets.34−36
From the Aβ monomer to fibrils and plaques, conformational
alterations trigger and promote the docking reaction. During
aggregation and fibril growth, the dynamic hydration shell
drives structural changes.37−39 Klenerman and co-workers
investigated the hydrophobic area variation in α-synuclein
aggregation, by using the solvatochromic dye Nile Red, which
implicated that the gradual reduction of hydrophobicity
correlates with the process of aggregation.40 Furthermore,
2D-IR spectroscopy of Aβ-aggregation indicates that the
pathways of structural polymorphs in the Aβ oligomerization
are potential targets for inhibition of Aβ fibril formation.41
Peptides act as natural inhibitors, as hormones, and as
agonist and antagonist signal molecules. Some possess extreme
potency and selectivity, and peptides target a broad range of
protein classes. They possess tremendous potential as
therapeutic agents due to the unique structural features that
facilitate design of intracellular protein−protein interaction
(PPI) inhibitors.42 PPIs are viewed as “undruggable”, by a
conventional small molecule approach.43 Using peptide like
structures in drug design, their inherent weaknesses, including
lack of membrane permeability,44 catabolism,45,46 excretion
and aqueous solubility,47,48 must be considered. To increase
the peptide solubility, PEGylation has been a viable method.
However, PEGylation also introduces antigenicity, lack of
biodegradability, and peroxide degradation pathways.49,50
Peptide conjugates with nanoparticles are also not biodegrad-
able, and may be toxic due to metal components, and present
generally poor biocompatibility.51
Conformationally well-defined β-hairpin peptides have two
structurally different sides as defined by the preferred direction
of side chains toward or away from the central HB-zipper of
the hairpin, respectively. β-Bodies are β-hairpins in which
residues on the center oriented side are threonines stabilizing
the hairpin structure and providing solubility through the
threonine hydroxyls. The hairpin structure can be further
stabilized by replacing a set of threonines with either a
CuAAC-click or a disulfide bridge.52 This maintains the hairpin
structure, while on the other side of the hairpin, the residues
oriented away from the center may be mutated to fit a protein
surface for antibody-like recognition ideal for PPI inhibitors
Computationally designed β-bodies were therefore developed
through mutational molecular dynamics (MMD) simula-
tions.52 Cyclic β-bodies binding to the growth surface of
Alzheimer fibrils were designed de novo as promising cyclic
peptides with good solubility for inhibition of Aβ(1−42)
aggregation. The peptide solubility and structural stability were
improved by incorporating the unnatural amino acid (UAA),
D-glucosaminic acid (GA), with a poly hydroxyl structure
improving dynamics of the peptide hydration shell. Here, five
different peptides were designed virtually, by MMD, toward an
Aβ(1−42) cryo-EM structure, using either all L- (2) or D-
amino acids (1, 3, 4, and 5). These peptides were investigated
for their inhibiting effects on Aβ(1−42) aggregation.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
MMD by Molecular Operating Environment. The cryo-
EM structure of Aβ(1−42) fibrils by Gremer et al. (5OQV)53
was used as a target for β-body design. The slightly helical
structure has a center-up and a center-down site in the growth
directions, toward which the β-bodies were modeled. A generic
β-hairpin was constructed from L- or D-amino acids comprising
a PG-turn and two strands in which every second residue was a
threonine, click residues for cyclization or a glucosaminic acid
(GA) residue to increase the hydrodynamic shell. Every other
residue starting on either side of the two turn residues was
mutated to provide an initial fit to the cryo-EM structure
presenting the two growth faces of the Aβ(1−42) fibrils. A
cryo-EM structure stack of 4 Aβ peptides was used as target in
all calculations. The complex was soaked in water or saline (0.1
M NaCl) and while MD was performed, the recognition
residues were mutated (MMD) toward optimal fit to the
topology, hydrophobicity, and polarity of either of the two
growth faces of the fibril cryo-EM structure. During the initial
fitting process, Aβ(1−42) was fixed in space. Occasionally,
when mutations showed promise, the fixation was removed for
the terminal Aβ peptide in the cryo-EM structure to allow the
final local induced fit. The stability of the complex in saline was

Thus, selected best β-bodies presenting optimal interactions

the low immunogenicity54 and better protease resistance,55 the
design and synthesis of 1, 3, 4, and 5 was performed with D-

The recognition residues of peptides 1 and 3 are similar but
they deviate at the following points. Two D-threonines were
exchanged for GAs in 3 to make it more water-soluble. The C-
terminal glycine was also omitted in 3, and the positions of pra
and orn(N3) were exchanged to improve the bridge structure.
Compounds 1 and 3 were designed to recognize the center-
up site of the fibril growth surface, while 2, 4, and 5 were
designed to recognize the center-down site of the fibril growth
surface, in order to be able to inhibit growth of the fibrils from
both sides and affect precipitation at various stages of fibril
formation.
GA-Building Block and Peptide Synthesis. In the
present study, three of the synthetic peptides incorporated the
UAA, GA. To utilize the GA building block in the synthesis of
peptides through the Fmoc solid phase peptides synthesis
(SPPS) strategy, a suitable protection scheme for the hydroxyls
was required. Preferably, the hydroxyls should remain
protected during Fmoc removal with 20% piperidine/DMF,
while protection should be easily removed by acid during side
chain deprotection with ∼95% TFA/H2O. Importantly, the
protective group should be introduced simultaneously to all
four vicinal hydroxyl groups on GA.
The glucosaminic acid (GA) was converted into a suitable
building block for Fmoc-based SPPS. There are many different
general and useful strategies for both acid and base labile
protections of hydroxyl groups.
Acid labile protections include silicon-ether-based protection
and 1,2- and 1,3-acetal based protection of diols. The
protection of GA may also benefit from the rich protection
group chemistry of carbohydrates. Upon initial screening of a
series of methods and reaction conditions (equivalents,
temperature, and solvents) to introduce either trimethylsilyl
(TMS), t-butyldimethylsilyl (TBDMS) or triisopropylsilyl
(TIPS) groups, acetates, trichloroacetates, allyl carbonates
(Alloc), acetonides, or tetrahydropyranyl ethers (THP), we
selected triethylsilyl trifluoromethanesulfonate (TES triflate)
to be superior to introduce a hydroxyl protection group,
compatible with Fmoc peptide synthesis, in the GA protection
strategy. Full protection was required to avoid lactone
formation during carboxylate activation. All reactions were
monitored by TLC, UHPLC-MS, and NMR. GA was reacted
with Fmoc-OSu to selectively protect the amino group with
Fmoc yielding a waxy detergent like material. This product was
reacted with TES triflate in pyridine to protect all hydroxyl
groups, including that of the carboxyl group. Subsequently, the
TES group on the carboxylate of Fmoc-GA(TES5) was
selectively removed by NaHCO3 to form the target building
block Fmoc-GA(TES4)−OH (Scheme 1). Structures of
intermediates and target building block were confirmed and
characterized by HRMS and NMR (Supporting Information
Synthesis Procedures and Figures S1−S9). Depending on
conditions, several side reactions were observed, including
lactonization, dehydration, and intramolecular ether bond
formations, probably due to reactivity of the flexible vicinal

The peptides 1−5 were synthesized through the Fmoc
strategy by a manual approach in syringe or by automated
synthesis using a custom-made flow synthesizer and 2 h
couplings. After acid deprotection of the peptides, cleavage
from resin, and drying, linear peptides 1, 3, 4, and 5 were
dissolved in water and cyclized by CuAAC reaction. To save
on equivalents and improve the final yield in the automated
synthesis, the coupling times of Fmoc-GA(TES4)−OH were
extended to 12 h. After purification by preparative HPLC, all
peptides were characterized through LCMS and HRMS
(Supporting Information, Figures S10−S19).
ThT Fluorescence Assays. Inhibition of Aβ(1−42) and
Aβ(1−40) fibrillation by peptides 1−5 was performed using a
Thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence assay (Supporting Informa-
tion, Fibrillization Assay), based on the increase of
fluorescence induced by ThT binding to amyloid fibrils.56,57
A modification of a previously described method was used.58,59
Peptide 3, mixtures of 1 + 2, and 2 + 3 in PBS or Aβ(1−42)
in PBS were used as negative and positive controls,
respectively, performed in triplicates (Figure 2). The same
peptide 1−3 mixtures in PBS buffer were assayed in
combination with Aβ(1−42) in triplicates. The control
group showed that peptides 1−3 do not possess any fibrillation
properties of their own as compared with the positive control
of Aβ(1−42). The curves for the peptide controls presented
zero fluorescence intensity (FI), while positive control of
Aβ(1−42) showed significant FI due to fibrillation. Next,
Aβ(1−42) fibrillation was investigated in combination with a
mixture of 1 + 2, 3 alone, and a mixture of 2 + 3 (B).
Combination of 1 + 2 contained no GA in the structure
reduced the FI to half, compared to the positive control,
showing some inhibition for Aβ(1−42) fibrillation. When 3
alone was tested with Aβ(1−42), it showed a further decrease
of FI compared to the combination 1 + 2 with Aβ(1−42). This
indicated that the increased hydrodynamic shell of 3 was
important for the inhibition of Aβ(1−42) fibrillation. The
most efficient combination for inhibiting fibril formation was
the mixture of peptides 2 + 3 as FI was close to zero
throughout the assay, demonstrating also the importance of
blocking both ends of the growing fibril. The results indicated
that for the best inhibition of fibrillation, two GA-containing
peptides were required to bind efficiently to both the center up
and center down ends of the growing spiral shaped β-sheet
found in the cryo-EM structure of the fibril.

The promising results with peptide 3 in the initial assays
called for blocking both fibril surfaces with GA containing D-
peptides. This led us to design and synthesize the two GA
peptides, 4 and 5, to be assayed together with 3 and compared
with the activity of 2.
All fibrillation inhibition experiments (Figure 4) were
performed using Milli-Q water since fibrillation of both
Aβ(1−40) and Aβ(1−42) controls was excellent in that
medium at 20 μM concentrations. Unexpectedly, at higher
concentrations in Milli-Q water, both peptides 3 and 5 showed
some fibrillation in the absence of Aβ-peptides, while peptides
1, 2, and 4 did not aggregate.
Moreover, when 3 and 5 were mixed at the assay
concentration, self-fibrillation diminished and was not
observed.
Fibrillation experiments for Aβ(1−42) (Figure 5A) showed
that all assayed peptides had a significant inhibitory effect on
the fibrillation process when compared with the positive
control. In all assays, the effective concentration of β-bodies
was 20 μM, interacting with Aβ(1−42) or Aβ(1−40) also at
20 μM. Of the assayed peptide combinations, the mixtures of 3
and 5 were the most promising, showing almost no fibrillation.
Fibrillation of Aβ(1−40) (B) was performed in 5% DMSO in
Milli-Q water or in Milli-Q water as indicated. It should be
noted that all the peptides had been designed toward the cryo-
EM structure of Aβ(1−42) fibrils, which is somewhat different
from that of Aβ(1−40). Therefore, the inhibitory effect on
Aβ(1−40) fibrillation was less pronounced, as can be seen with
peptide 4, which actually increased the fibrillation of Aβ(1−
40). The mixture of 3 + 4 did inhibit fibrillation, probably due
to the strong inhibitory effect of peptide 3. However, inhibition
was much better for other combinations. Peptide 5 showed
excellent inhibition by itself and as for Aβ(1−42), the mixture
of peptide 3 + 5 completely inhibited fibrillation of Aβ(1−40)
both in DMSO in Milli-Q water and in Milli-Q water alone.

■CONCLUSIONS
We have developed a new building block for solid phase
peptide synthesis from D-glucosaminic acid that simulate the
structural function of D-threonine, while upon deprotection
provide synthesized peptides with a significantly improved
hydration shell in water. We optimized the hydroxyl protection
condition and found the triethyl silyl group to have the optimal
steric and stability properties including stability to base and
acid lability for peptide deprotection with TFA. We
investigated the effect of the hydration shell on interaction
with Aβ(1−40) and Aβ(1−42) fibrillation, associated with
neurodegenerative Alzheimer’s disease, as an example. We
designed five peptides by mutational molecular dynamics
(MMD in the software MOE) that bind to either of the two
growth surfaces of Aβ(1−42). Novel CuAAC click-cyclized β-
body peptides were introduced, which incorporated GA, an
unnatural poly hydroxyl amino acid. The peptides were
efficiently synthesized in high yield, and peptides 1, 3, 4, and
5 were cyclized by CuAAC click chemistry. The effect of the
GA residues on dynamic hydration shell was very significant,
and the best pair of peptides, 3 + 5, could completely prevent
Aβ(1−42) fibrillation in a ThT-fluorescence fibrillation assay.
We established that the increase of the dynamic hydration shell

complexed Aβ peptide with the surrounding water thereby
facilitating the solvation process, which in turn improves
structural mobility and solubility of the Aβ(1−42) peptide.
Here, we provided a novel strategy to effectively prevent Aβ
aggregation and fibril formation. These results may facilitate
future development of therapeutic approaches to Alzheimer’s
and other neurodegenerative diseases.

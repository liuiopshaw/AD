Abstract
Background  Emerging evidence implicates the gut microbiome in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathogenesis, yet the 
underlying mechanisms remain elusive. This study elucidates the bidirectional relationship between gut microbiota 
and AD using fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in a mouse model.
Result  Through meticulous experimentation, we conducted reciprocal FMT between AD (5xFAD) and healthy 
(C57BL/6) mice to unravel the impact of gut microbiome alterations on cognitive function and neuroinflammation. 
FMT from 5xFAD to C57BL/6 mice induced profound memory impairment and cognitive deficits, accompanied by 
elevated inflammatory cytokine levels, oxidative stress markers, and systemic inflammation, as evidenced by increased 
plasma cytokines. Conversely, transplanting healthy microbiota into 5xFAD mice yielded remarkable behavioral 
improvements, including enhanced spatial memory performance in the Morris water maze, directly correlating with 
cognitive recovery. Our findings underscore the pivotal role of the gut microbiome in AD pathogenesis and offer a 
promising therapeutic avenue.
Conclusion  Targeted modulation of the gut microbiome through strategies like FMT may offer potential benefits in 
Alzheimer’s disease by influencing neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and cognitive function. This comprehensive 
study provides novel insights into the gut-brain axis dynamics and paves the way for innovative microbiome-based 
interventions in AD management.

AD patients display distinct intestinal microbial com­
positions compared to healthy individuals, character­
ized by higher levels of Bacteroidetes and lower levels of 
Firmicutes and Bifidobacterium [4]. Modifying the gut 
microbial composition has shown promising results in 
alleviating Alzheimer’s symptoms in mouse models [5]. 
FMT, a procedure involving the transfer of feces from a 
healthy donor to a recipient, has been successful in nor­
malizing the microbial communities in the intestines and 
has been applied to the treatment of various gastrointes­
tinal conditions, including recurrent Clostridium difficile 
infections [6].
Multiple studies have observed altered gut microbiota 
in rodent models of AD and AD patients, indicating a 
potential association between the microbiota and AD 
pathogenesis. Dysbiosis of microbial commensals, such 
as decreased Ruminococcus and increased Blautia and 
Odoribacter, might play a significant role in the disease 
progression. It is essential to determine whether these 
alterations are merely complications of the pathogenic 
pathways [7]. Notably, weight loss has been observed in 
AD patients before the onset of clinical symptoms [8]. 
Although FMT has been effective in ameliorating intes­
tinal dysbiosis in mouse models of Parkinson’s disease 
and treating various neurological conditions [9], the 
exact mechanisms through which the gut microbiota 
influences AD brain pathology remain unclear. Some 
studies have demonstrated improved cognitive behavior 
in AD mouse models through FMT of healthy micro­
biota. Although studies have investigated the effects of 
gut microbiota transplantation from Alzheimer’s disease 
(AD) mouse models—one demonstrating impaired mem­
ory function and neurogenesis in C57BL/6 mice [10] 
and another exploring the microbiota-AD connection 
using short-term antibiotic treatment followed by fecal 
microbiota transplantation [11]—no research has spe­
cifically examined the impact of FMT from transgenic 
(Tg) to WT mice in modeling AD progression. Our study 
remains novel as it establishes a new model to assess gut-
brain axis modulation and the therapeutic potential of 
novel molecules or formulations [12].
Building upon our previous studies that revealed 
changes in the microbiota of 5xFAD, 3xFAD, and 
C57BL/6 mice after 60 days, when disease progression 
was evident based on behavioral data, gene expression, 
and serum inflammatory markers, this study aims to gain 
a clearer understanding of the role of FMT in 5xFAD 
mice. We conducted reverse treatment by transplanting 
gut microbiota-derived from 5xFAD (Tg) and C57BL/6 
(WT) mice, which exhibited differences after 60 days, to 
investigate their effects on cognitive behavior, inflamma­
tory and oxidative parameters, and alterations in the gut 
microbiota associated with AD progression.

Experimental design and FMT
Tg mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 12 
per group): (1) AD model group (Tg) serves as the con­
trol, FMT(Tg) + Tg, and FMT (WT) + Tg. Further, WT 
mice were divided into two groups (n = 12 per group): 
WT as the normal control and FMT (Tg) + WT group. 
The mice of Tg + FMT and WT + FMT groups were intra­
gastrically administered with 0.2 ml fresh fecal solution 
of WT/Tg mice once daily for 8 weeks. For FMT experi­
ments, 200  mg of stool (from WT/Tg mouse pellets) 
were collected daily from Tg/WT mice, resuspended in 
5 ml of PBS mixed with sterile normal saline, and then 
passed through a 20 mm filter to remove large particu­
lates. The filtrate was centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min 
and dissolved in physiological saline with a concentra­
tion of 400  mg/ml for ingestion (Fig.  1). Before FMT 
treatment, mice were intragastrically administered an 
antibiotic cocktail comprising three antibiotics and one 
antifungal (ampicillin (100 mg/kg Sigma Aldrich India), 
vancomycin (50  mg/kg Sigma Aldrich India), metroni­
dazole (100 mg/kg, Sigma Aldrich India), and neomycin 
(100  mg/kg, Sigma Aldrich India) in phosphate buffer 
saline to remove indigenous gut microbes, once a day 
through gavage for 15 days.
Behavioral tests
After day 50 of treatment, the Morris water maze 
(MWM) for the spatial memory test and the Y maze for 
the exploratory behavior test was performed to evaluate 
the cognitive function in mice (Fig. 2).
Morris water maze test
The spatial learning and memory ability were assessed 
using the MWM test, which involved a cylindrical water 
tank filled with water and equipped with a video capture 
system. The tank had a diameter of 120 cm, a height of

time it took to find the platform (escape latency time) 
was recorded. The data collected during the test were 
recorded and analyzed using ANY MAZE software [5].
Y maze test
The performance of spontaneous alternation was exam­
ined using the Asymmetrical Y-maze, 120°from one 
another. Each arm is 40 cm long, 10 cm wide and 12 cm 
high wall. Each mouse was placed in the center of the 
Y-maze and given an 8-min session to navigate the 
maze on day 1 freely. On day 2, the order and the num­
ber of arms the mouse entered were recorded. For an 
arm entry to be considered complete, the mouse’s hind 
paws had to be fully inserted into the arm. The percent­
age alternation was calculated by dividing the number 
of triads with entries in all three arms by the maximum 
alternations = total number of arms entered- 2 × 100. To 
assess whether the animal explored the novel compo­
nent, the animal was left inside the maze, and the num­
ber of entries into the novel arm was counted for the 
entire duration of the session. The data obtained from 
the Y-maze test were recorded and analyzed using ANY 
MAZE software [2, 5].
Biochemical estimation
Following the behavioral performance, the mice were 
anesthetized intraperitoneally with a combination of 
100 mg/kg of ketamine and 10 mg/kg of xylazine hydro­
chloride; blood was then drawn from the retro-orbital 
region into a non-anticoagulant tube. Subsequently, the 
mice were euthanized with carbon dioxide in accor­
dance with the facility’s standard guidelines. The col­
lected blood samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 
15 min at 37 °C, and serum was separated from the blood 
and stored at − 20  °C. The Acetylcholine (ACh), Lipid 
peroxidation (LPO), and Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) lev­
els were measured from the serum of samples as per the 
given manufacturer’s instruction (Immunotag™ G-Bio­
sciences, Missouri, USA) of all groups of mice. The lev­
els of proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines 
in the serum were examined using the Bio-Plex ProTM 
Cytokine Standard kit from Bio-Rad, California, USA. 
The plate was prepared according to the instructions pro­
vided by the manufacturer and read using the Bio-Plex 
200 Systems, also from Bio-Rad, California, USA. The 
data obtained from the plate was analyzed using the Bio-
Plex Manager software for acquisition and analysis. The 
analysis was performed with low PMT (Photomultiplier 
Tube) and RP1 instrument settings [5].
Short-chain fatty acid extraction and analysis
After 8 weeks of treatment, Fecal samples were collected 
from mice and mixed in the deionized water. The butyr­
ate and propionate levels were measured using the pre­
viously described protocol for high-performance liquid 
chromatography (HPLC LC-20AT, Shimadzu) equipped 
with a carbohydrate column (Aminex HPX-87 H column, 
Waters) and a photodiode array detector (PDA, Shi­
madzu) for analysis [5].

Microbiome profiling
After the behavior study, the fecal pellets were col­

Statistical analysis
Data were presented as mean ± standard error for the 
indicated numbers of independently performed experi­
ments. One-way or Two-way ANOVA assessed statistical 
significance with Bonferroni post hoc test. All statistical 
analyses were performed using Graph Pad Prism 9.0 for 
Windows (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).
Results
FMT attenuates memory loss in 5XFAD and contrasts in 
wild type (WT) mice
FMT has surfaced as a captivating avenue of investiga­
tion in the context of Alzheimer’s disease. Recent stud­
ies have provided indications of a gut-brain connection, 
suggesting that the gut microbiota may exert influence 
over neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer’s [13]. 
The potential manipulation of gut microbial composition 
through FMT holds the prospect of impacting neuroin­
flammation, amyloid-beta aggregation, and other fac­
tors implicated in the progression of the disease. Despite 
promising initial inquiries, further robust research is 
imperative to ascertain the viability and efficacy of FMT 
as a therapeutic intervention for Alzheimer’s disease [14]. 
Building upon prior research, we employed an antibi­
otic cocktail to eliminate the existing microbiota across 
all groups of mice. Following a 14-day antibiotic cock­
tail treatment in both 5XFAD and wild-type mice, we 
conducted FMT to investigate its effects on the progres­
sion and prevention of Alzheimer’s. We administered 
FMT from (WT and 5XFAD (Tg) mice to both 5XFAD 
and wild-type mice to assess their impact on cogni­
tive function. Subsequent to FMT, the cognitive perfor­
mance of the 5XFAD + FMT (Tg), WT + FMT (Tg), and 
5XFAD + FMT (WT) groups was evaluated using the 
Morris Water Maze and Y Maze paradigms. The Mor­
ris Water Maze evaluated spatial memory, while the Y 
Maze assessed exploratory behavior. The measuring 
parameter is to find the platform or the distance they 
swim to reach it, along with their swimming speed and 
trajectory, which are commonly measured parameters. 
The Anymaze track software recorded all the set param­
eters with the movement of mice. In Tg mice treated 
with FMT (WT), a notable improvement (p < 0.05) in 
swim speed (Fig.  3a) and a reduction in distance trav­
eled (Fig. 3b) were observed after 7 weeks of treatment, 
comparable to WT mice. Conversely, 5XFAD and WT 
mice treated with FMT (Tg) exhibited a significant 
(p < 0.05) decrease in swim speed compared to WT mice 
and Tg mice. During the probe trial spanning day 1 to 
day 4, the escape latency time was recorded using Any 
Maze software. For Tg mice, escape latency decreased 
from 58.11 ± 1.12 to 54.33 ± 2.28, while for WT mice, it 
decreased from 54.22 ± 1.23 to 18.12 ± 1.75. For FMT 
(Tg) + Tg, escape latency remained at 60 s and decreased 
to 58.17 ± 2.28; for FMT (Tg) + WT, it decreased from 
56.12 ± 1.13 to 50.42 ± 1.40, and for FMT (WT) + Tg, it 
decreased from 52.33 ± 2.62 to 27.83 ± 1.81. Notably, the 
significant enhancement observed during the probe trial 
in FMT (WT) + Tg mice was akin to that seen in WT 
mice (Fig. 3c). In contrast, FMT (Tg) + Tg mice displayed 
worsened performance during the trial compared to Tg 
and WT mice. The visual representation of track pat­
terns recorded via Any Maze software (Fig. 3d) provides 
a clear depiction of the observed conditions. On Day 5 
of the Morris Water Maze test, escape latency in FMT 
(WT) + Tg mice demonstrated a noteworthy improve­
ment (p < 0.05), reflecting enhanced cognitive function 
in locating the concealed platform, akin to WT mice 
(Fig.  3e). Continuing the behavioral investigation, the 
exploratory Y Maze model exhibited a similar trend. The 
percentage alteration, indicative of exploratory behavior, 
demonstrated significant (p < 0.05) similarity between 
FMT (WT) + Tg mice and WT mice. However, FMT 
(Tg) + Tg mice exhibited diminished alteration. Tg mice 
displayed fewer triads during the 8-minute trial period 
(Fig. 3f). Conversely, the number of entries into the novel 
arm was lower in Tg mice, FMT (Tg) + Tg mice, and FMT 
(Tg) + WT mice. A noteworthy number of entries were, 
however, recorded in FMT (WT) + Tg mice and WT mice 
(Fig. 3g).

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) play a pivotal role in 
modulating brain function through their production by 
gut bacteria [20]. These SCFAs, including acetate, propio­
nate, and butyrate, are generated as byproducts of bacte­
rial fermentation of dietary fibers in the colon. Emerging 
research suggests that these SCFAs hold the ability to 
influence neural processes and cognitive functions. They 
can traverse the gut-brain axis, a bidirectional commu­
nication system connecting the gastrointestinal tract 
and the central nervous system, thereby exerting their 
effects on brain health. SCFAs possess anti-inflammatory 
properties and are capable of maintaining the integrity 
of the blood-brain barrier, which is crucial for prevent­
ing the infiltration of harmful substances into the brain. 
Additionally, SCFAs have been linked to promoting the 
production of neurotransmitters and brain biogenic 
amines, which can impact mood regulation and cogni­
tive functions [21]. We collected feces after a behavioral 
study, which were analyzed to assess the level of butyrate. 
Butyrate is also involved in modulating the TCA cycle 
and mitochondrial function through fatty acid metabo­
lism [22].
During behavioral study, we collected fecal samples 
for analysis to determine the level of butyrate. Notably, 
butyrate is also involved in modulating the TCA cycle 
and mitochondrial function through fatty acid metab­

(WT) + Tg mice and WT mice exhibited significantly 
higher levels of butyrate in their feces (p < 0.05), while the

onstrated lower levels comparable to those found in Tg 
mice (Fig. 5a). The study indicated associations between 
alterations in gut microbiota and AD. Recent evidence 
underscores the altered gut microbiota composition in 
AD patients and animal models. Remarkably, the trans­
plantation of fecal samples from AD mice to germ-free 
(GF) mice led to significantly increased brain Aβ levels, 
highlighting the potential role of the gut microbiota in 
influencing Aβ deposition and neuroplasticity processes 
[23, 24].
To comprehensively understand the impact of FMT 
treatment in both Tg and WT mice, we employed iso­

sequencing following behavioral assessments. This 
analysis identified a total of 222 operational taxonomic 
units (OTUs), with major phyla including Bacteroidetes, 
Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Cyanobac­
teria, Verrucomicrobia, TM7, Deferribacteres, and Fuso­

mice exhibited 82% Bacteroidetes, 13% Firmicutes, 
and 2% Proteobacteria, with the remaining percentage 
unclassified. Comparatively, WT mice displayed a simi­
lar composition with 79% Bacteroidetes, 14% Firmicutes, 
3% Proteobacteria, and 3% Unclassified. However, the 
FMT (Tg) mice showcased a reduced Bacteroidetes level 
(54% in FMT (Tg) + Tg and 57% in FMT (Tg) + WT) and 
increased Firmicutes level (40% in FMT (Tg) + Tg and 
35% in FMT (Tg) + WT), resembling the composition 
observed in Tg mice (57% Bacteroidetes and 34% Fir­

revealed significant variations, highlighting the top 20

the association of aerobic Akkermansia muciniphila with

Discussion
The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has granted 
its first-ever approval for a fecal microbiota product, 
Rebyota, signifying a significant milestone in human 
health. This approval specifically greenlights Rebyota for 
preventing the recurrence of Clostridioides difficile infec­
tion (CDI) in individuals aged 18 and older. It is intended 
for use after completing antibiotic treatment for recur­
rent CDI [34].
Ongoing research is expanding our understanding, 
and FMT may assume an increasingly prominent role in 
healthcare in the coming years. Investigations are cur­
rently underway to explore the use of FMT in conditions 
like irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), metabolic disorders, 
autoimmune diseases, and even mental health disor­
ders, with a particular focus on the gut-brain axis [35]. 
The symptoms of Alzheimer’s disease have been dem­
onstrated to pass to a healthy young organism through 
the gut microbiota, affirming the significant role of gut 
microbiota in Alzheimer’s disease. This discovery also 
underscores the importance of hippocampal neurogen­
esis as a central cellular process that governs systemic 
circulatory and gut-mediated factors contributing to 
Alzheimer’s [36]. Numerous clinical studies are currently 
underway following the publication of animal studies. 
The effects of six techniques on the gut microbial pop­
ulation were explored by evaluating fecal microbiota 
transplantation (FMT) from a human donor to antibi­
otic-induced microbial-depleted mice, with experimental 
details highly inconsistent, variable, and/or incomplete. 
Intrarectal FMT at low dosing intervals better preserved 
the donor’s gut bacterial community at the genus level, 
with a lower abundance of several fungal genera observed 
in animals treated with AB + T. AB + T gut decontamina­
tion followed by per os FMT, once every 3 days, affected 
behavioral parameters when compared to other FMT 
techniques, suggesting a possible mediation between 
specific gut fungal genera and behavioral tests [37]. The 
link between fear extinction learning in animal models 
and the gut microbiome has been explored, with memory 
domains investigated according to obesity status. Micro­
biota transplantation from human subjects with obesity 
decreased memory scores in mice, indicating potential 
therapeutic implications for memory impairment [38]. 
It’s important to note that clinical studies often build 
upon insights gained from animal studies, so these inves­
tigations must comprehensively address various aspects 
to provide a complete set of answers.
It’s important to note that while preclinical models 
offer valuable insights, the high variability in methodol­
ogy, host response, and microbiota composition across 
studies introduces significant challenges in drawing 
definitive conclusions. These issues emphasize the need 
for standardized protocols and robust analytical frame­
works in both animal and human FMT research.
In this study, we aimed to investigate the impact of 
FMT on cognitive function, inflammation, oxidative 
stress, and gut microbiota modulation in both wild-type 
(WT) and 5XFAD (Tg) mice, a model of AD. Our results 
provide valuable insights into the potential therapeu­
tic effects of FMT on AD progression and prevention. 
Our cognitive behavioral assessments utilizing the Mor­
ris Water and Y Maze models revealed intriguing find­
ings. Tg mice treated with FMT derived from WT mice 
exhibited significant improvements in spatial memory, 
as evidenced by increased swim speed and decreased 
distance traveled. These improvements were comparable 
to those observed in WT mice. Furthermore, during the 
probe trial, FMT (WT) + Tg mice displayed enhanced 
cognitive performance, resembling the pattern observed 
in WT mice. In contrast, FMT (Tg) + Tg mice demon­
strated impaired cognitive function during the trial. 
These results suggest that FMT from WT mice can posi­
tively impact cognitive function in Tg mice, while FMT 
from Tg mice might have a detrimental effect on cogni­
tive performance. The track image analysis corroborates 
these observations, visually depicting the varying cogni­
tive conditions.
Parallel trends were noted in the Y Maze study, where 
alterations in exploratory behavior were assessed. FMT 
(WT) + Tg mice exhibited similarities to WT mice in 
terms of exploratory behavior alteration. Conversely, 
FMT (Tg) + Tg mice displayed reduced alteration, sug­
gesting a potential negative impact of Tg-derived micro­
biota on exploratory behavior. The number of entries into 
the novel arm also aligned with these findings. Nota­
bly, FMT (WT) + Tg mice and WT mice demonstrated 
higher levels of entries, indicative of improved explor­
atory behavior. To validate the cognitive improvements 
observed after FMT, we evaluated acetylcholine and

serotonin levels in the serum. The increased levels of ace­
tylcholine and serotonin in WT mice and FMT (WT) + Tg 
mice compared to Tg mice and FMT (Tg) + WT mice sup­
port the positive cognitive effects associated with FMT 
from WT mice. Conversely, FMT (Tg) + Tg mice exhib­
ited significantly low levels, in line with their cognitive 
performance. The analysis of inflammatory markers and 
oxidation product LPO levels revealed promising out­
comes. FMT (WT) + Tg mice displayed reduced levels of 
inflammatory markers, indicating a potential attenuation 
of inflammation. Additionally, they exhibited decreased 
LPS levels, suggesting a reduction in gram-negative bac­
terial abundance. Antioxidant activity was increased after 
FMT of WT microbiota in Tg mice, signifying a reduc­
tion in oxidative stress. These results collectively suggest 
that FMT from WT mice may exert anti-inflammatory 
and antioxidant effects. Our investigation into gut micro­
biota modulation following FMT indicated significant 
changes in butyrate levels and microbial composition. 
FMT (WT) + Tg mice and WT mice exhibited elevated 
levels of butyrate, a metabolite associated with cogni­
tive modulation. The microbiota analysis revealed dif­
ferences in phyla abundance, with FMT (WT) + Tg mice 
and WT mice showing a higher abundance of beneficial 
Bacteroidetes. Conversely, FMT (Tg) groups displayed a 
pattern similar to Tg mice, with decreased Bacteroide­
tes and increased Firmicutes. These alterations reflect a 
shift in gut microbial composition that might contribute 
to the observed cognitive effects. The increased presence 
of symbiotic bacteria, such as Ruminococcus gnavus, Lac­
tobacillus helveticus, Lactobacillus vaginalis, Parabacte­
roides distasonis, and Parabacteroides gordonii, has been 
linked to higher levels of short-chain fatty acids, contrib­
uting to the maintenance of the gut epithelium wall. This 
close interaction between the bacteria and the gut epithe­
lium facilitates the colonization and abundance of these 
beneficial symbiotic bacteria.
These findings have potential clinical implications 
for AD management. FMT from healthy individuals 
appears to positively impact cognitive function, poten­
tially through modulation of gut microbiota, reduction 
of inflammation, and improvement in neurotransmitter 
levels. However, caution is warranted when considering 
FMT from individuals with AD-like pathology, as it may 
have adverse effects on cognitive function.
Conclusion
This study highlights the therapeutic potential of FMT 
in alleviating cognitive decline, inflammation, and oxida­
tive stress in an AD mouse model while also introducing 
a novel approach for AD model development. However, 
the translational applicability of these results remains 
uncertain. The interspecies differences in microbiota 
composition, immune response, and brain structure 
present significant challenges when extrapolating find­
ings from mouse models to humans. Further validation 
in larger preclinical models and eventual clinical trials 
is essential to establish the safety, reproducibility, and 
efficacy of FMT as a therapeutic strategy in Alzheimer’s 
disease.
Nevertheless, further research is essential to unravel 
the precise mechanisms responsible for these observed 
effects. Additionally, translating these findings into prac­
tical interventions for AD patients or further refining the 
new AD mouse model will require continued investiga­
tion and exploration.

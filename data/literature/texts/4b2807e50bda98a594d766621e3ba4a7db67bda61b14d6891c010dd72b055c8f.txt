ABSTRACT 
Metal nanoclusters (MNCs), comprising several to hundreds of metal atoms, have attracted significant research interest owing to 
their distinctive physicochemical properties. Reticular frameworks (RFs) with ordered porous structures, including metal–organic 
frameworks (MOFs), covalent organic frameworks (COFs), hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks (HOFs), and supramolecular 
organic frameworks (SOFs), possess a variety of unique properties due to their high crystallinity, high porosity, large surface 
area, and adjustable structure. The integration of MNCs with RFs endows the resulting composites with desirable features (e.g., 
enhanced and tunable optical properties, improved catalytic and photophysical activities, selective molecular recognition), which 
facilitates a broad spectrum of biomedical applications and advancing the development of integrated theranostic nanoplatforms. 
This review summarizes recent advances in the synthesis and biomedical applications of various MNCs/RFs composites. We 
systematically categorize and evaluate key strategies for incorporating MNCs into four types of RFs (MOFs, COFs, HOFs, and 
SOFs) while discussing the advantages and limitations of each approach. The biomedical applications of these composites are 
comprehensively reviewed, encompassing biosensing, bioimaging, antitumor therapy, and antibacterial treatments. Finally, the 
review addresses current challenges and outlines future research directions, with the aim of guiding the rational design of novel 
MNCs/RFs composites, enabling precise control over their structures and functions toward advanced biomedical applications.

Introduction 
Over the past few decades, the rapid advancement of nanoma- 
terials has profoundly impacted human life. Metal nanoclusters 
(MNCs), a class of nanomaterials comprising several to hundreds 
of metal atoms, have garnered considerable research interest 
owing to their distinctive physicochemical properties and highly 
tunable structures [ 1–5 ]. Currently reported MNCs primarily 
include those made of gold, silver, copper, platinum, as well as 
alloy clusters comprising two different metal atoms [ 6–10 ]. With 
dimensions comparable to the Fermi wavelength of electrons, 
MNCs exhibit split energy levels and quantum size effects, which 
confer molecular-like fluorescence characteristics ranging from 
ultraviolet to near-infrared region [ 11–14 ]. Additional advantages, 
including ultrasmall size (1–3 nm), large Stokes shift, favorable 
photostability, and low toxicity, render them suitable for applica- 
tions in fluorescence sensing, bioimaging, and photonic devices 
[ 15–18 ]. In particular, through tailored ligand and structural 
design, MNCs can be engineered to emit in the second near- 
infrared window (NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm), which significantly

minimizes interference from tissue autofluorescence and enables 
their application in deep-tissue and disease imaging [ 19 ]. Fur- 
thermore, their abundant unsaturated active sites and high 
specific surface area enable enzyme-like activity, photodynamic 
effects, and photothermal properties, which are advantageous 
for therapeutic applications [ 20–24 ]. For instance, CuNCs with 
both peroxidase-like activity and catalase-like activity exhibit out- 
standing antioxidant and robust oxygenation effect in mitigating 
the hypoxic microenvironment, which holds enormous promise 
for the treatment of hypoxia-related chronic tissue defects and 
vascular injury in the field of regenerative medicine. The well- 
def ined composition and structure of MNCs also facilitate the 
precise design of integrated theranostic functionalities and mech- 
anistic studies through ligand engineering, establishing them as 
a highly promising class of nanomaterials [ 25–27 ]. 
Reticular frameworks (RFs) are a class of porous crystalline 
materials constructed from molecular building blocks (e.g., metal 
ions, organic molecules and MNCs) through strong directional 
bonds (e.g., coordination bond, covalent bond, hydrogen bond, 
electrostatic interaction, and Van der Waals forces) [ 28–30 ]. RFs 
with various types, including metal organic frameworks (MOFs) 
[ 31–34 ], covalent organic frameworks (COFs) [ 35–37 ], hydrogen- 
bonded organic frameworks (HOFs) [ 38–40 ], and supramolecular 
organic frameworks (SOFs) [ 41–43 ], exhibit a range of unique 
properties and have become a global research focus in recent 
years. Owing to their inherent characteristics including high 
surface area, adjustable pore size, good crystallinity, and modular 
construction, RFs can be rationally designed and optimized for 
specific functionalities and physicochemical properties to be 
applied in gas storage, sensing, catalysis, and so on [ 44, 45 ]. 
The large pore sizes, high specific surface area and abundant 
interaction sites of RFs facilitate the effective incorporation of 
MNCs [ 46–48 ]. The integration of MNCs with RFs effectively 
enhances their performance and brings about some new func- 
tions, endowing MNCs/RFs composites with many outstanding 
advantages in the biomedical field: 
1. The confinement effect from the pores of RFs effectively 
enhances the stability and fluorescence intensity of MNCs 
through promoting electronic transitions of MNCs and sup- 
pressing the non-radiative attenuation of triplet energy [ 49 ]. 
In addition, combined with the pore microenvironment 
shaped by the building units of RFs, the structure of MNCs 
(e.g., surface components and size) can be effectively regu- 
lated, thereby achieving simultaneous control of the emission 
wavelength and quantum yield of MNCs [ 50 ]. Moreover, the 
unique porous properties of RFs can breakthrough traditional 
synthesis methods via host–guest adaptability, thus enabling 
the construction of new structures. 
2. The periodic frameworks (RFs) promote efficient electron 
transfer and provide confined spaces for MNCs to act as 
potent catalytic centers. These effects collectively enhance 
the photodynamic activity, enzyme-mimetic properties, and 
photothermal performance of the composites, which are 
highly beneficial for biomedical applications. In addition, the 
incorporation of MNCs can induce localized surface plasmon 
resonance or modulate the electronic structure of RFs, which 
enhances light absorption and promotes charge separation, 
thereby advancing both catalytic and photothermal processes 
[ 51–53 ]. 
3. The porous and ordered structure of RFs imparts selective 
recognition and adsorption capabilities to the composites. 
Various biomarkers (e.g., small molecules, ions, and radi- 
cals) can be concentrated within the pores, enhancing the 
sensitivity and specificity of biosensing platforms. Moreover, 
the porous architecture enables high drug loading capacity, 
which combined with intelligent controlled release and the 
inherent therapeutic properties of MNCs/RFs, facilitates 
the development of an integrated multimodal therapeutic 
platform [ 54, 55 ]. 
4. Both RFs and MNCs possess well-defined and tunable 
structures, which facilitate the rational design of composite 
materials and enable detailed mechanistic studies that are 
essential for understanding their behavior in biological con- 
texts [ 56–58 ]. Meanwhile, by mimicking key features of living 
systems, the intricate hierarchical architecture of MNCs can 
be employed to engineer MNCs/RFs composites with high 
structural complexity and advanced functionality. 
5. The virtually limitless diversity of building units allows 
for extensive structural variation of both RFs and MNCs. 
This diversity provides a strong foundation for designing 
multifunctional theranostic platforms towards integrated 
diagnosis and treatment [ 59, 60 ]. For instance, the modular 
integration of components with distinct properties, such 
as magnetic nodes for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), 
fluorescent linkers for optical imaging, and catalytically 
active metals for enzyme-mimetic activity, enables the con- 
struction of platforms for multi-modal imaging coupled with 
synergistic therapy. 
In summary, the construction of MNCs/RFs composites not only 
combines the complementary advantages of both components but 
also holds considerable significance for advancing the application 
of nanomaterials in biomedicine. However, it should be noted 
that comprehensive discussions on the synthesis strategies and 
biomedical implementations of MNCs/RFs composites remain 
limited. This review aims to address this gap by systematically 
summarizing the developmental trajectory, fabrication strategies, 
and recent advances in biomedical applications of MNCs/RFs 
composites (Figure 1 ). Specifically, the synthesis methodologies 
for MNCs and various types of RFs are first introduced. Subse- 
quently, the biomedical applications of these hybrid materials, 
including biosensing, bioimaging, cancer therapy, and antibacte- 
rial treatment, are critically reviewed. Finally, the current chal- 
lenges and future research directions are outlined, with emphasis 
placed on achieving controllable synthesis and broadening the 
scope of their biological applications.

significantly broadened the biomedical applications of nanoma- 
terials [ 61–63 ]. Along with advances in this field, several synthetic 
strategies have been developed to construct MNCs/RFs compos- 
ites, including post-assembly, in-situ synthesis, and the direct use

structural control and uniform MNCs distribution, though it

composites comprising MNCs with MOFs, COFs, HOFs, and

2.1 
MNCs/MOFs 
MOFs, as a class of RFs being extensively studied, are typically 
coordination polymers formed through the self-assembly of 
multitopic organic ligands (e.g., carboxyl and imidazole) and 
transition metal ions (e.g., Zn2 + , Zr2 + , and Co2 + ) [ 46, 64 ]. Com- 
bining the rigidity of inorganic components with the flexibility of 
organic building blocks, MOFs exhibit ultrahigh porosity, large 
specific surface area, and highly tunable structures and functions, 
making them promising candidates for applications in adsorption 
and separation, gas storage, catalysis, sensing, and biomedicine 
[ 31, 65, 66 ]. Owing to the precisely tunable coordination envi- 
ronments between metal ions and organic ligands, MOFs are 
uniquely suited for multifunctional integration in biomedical 
applications. For instance, the unsaturated iron-carboxylate sites 
in MOFs can act as catalytic centers for Fenton-like reactions 
within the tumor microenvironment, producing hydroxyl radicals 
for chemodynamic therapy. The coordination structure of Gd3 + 
with aromatic carboxylate ligands imparts strong paramagnetic 
properties, making it an effective high-performance contrast 
agent for MRI [ 67, 68 ]. Integrating MNCs with MOFs establishes 
a synergistic relationship, which not only enhances the intrinsic 
properties of each component but also introduces new optical, 
catalytic, and electronic functionalities to the MOF matrix. For 
instance, integrating the well-ordered conductive frameworks of 
MOFs with the unique electronic properties of MNCs enables effi- 
cient charge transfer. This synergy significantly enhances their 
performance across photo-driven processes (e.g., photocatalysis), 
enzyme-mimetic catalysis, and photothermal conversion, thereby 
enabling superior multifunctional biological applications such as 
multimodal imaging and synergistic therapy [ 69 ]. Furthermore, 
precisely controlling the construction method of MNCs/MOFs 
composites is crucial for regulating their microstructure and 
chemical composition, which in turn determines their final 
functional performance. The synthesis of MNCs/MOFs compos- 
ites can be categorized into several strategies (Figure 2 ): post- 
assembly, single in-situ growth (either growing MNCs within 
pre-formed MOFs or growing MOFs around pre-synthesized 
MNCs), double in-situ synthesis, and the direct use of MNCs as 
structural building units of MOFs. 
2.1.1 
Post-Assembly 
Post-assembly represents the most straightforward and con- 
venient strategy for constructing MNCs/MOFs composites. In 
earlier studies, this typically involved the physical mixing of 
pre-synthesized MNCs with pre-formed MOFs, relying on pore 
size compatibility and intermolecular interactions for MNCs 
immobilization. Since the cavity sizes of most MOFs are smaller 
than the dimensions of MNCs, the incorporated clusters tend

in MOFs [ 73 ]. This covalent bridging establishes efficient charge 
transfer pathways, thereby enhancing both the photocatalytic 
performance and structural stability of the composites. 
2.1.2 
In Situ Synthesis 
Single in-situ growth approach requires only the pre-synthesis of 
either MNCs or MOFs, followed by in situ formation of the other 
component (either within the MOF pores or around pre-formed 
MNCs). The strategy of growing MNCs inside pre-synthesized 
MOFs is often described as the “ship-in-a-bot tle.” Owing to the 
high chemical stability of MOFs, this approach can be applied to 
a wide range of MNCs by infiltrating metal precursors into the 
MOF pores and subsequently reducing them to form clusters. As 
early as 2005, Fischer and coworkers demonstrated this concept 
by growing 1.5 nm PdNCs inside the pores of MOF-5 (coordination 
of Zn2 + with phthalic acid) [ 74 ]. However, a common limitation 
is that MNCs intend to deposit on the external surface of 
MOFs, along with difficulties in achieving atomic precision of 
the yielded clusters. To address these challenges, post-synthetic 
modification of MOFs with stabilizing functional groups (e.g., 
thiols) has been exploited. For example, Kurt and coworkers [ 75 ] 
introduced thiol groups into MOF-808 (coordination of Zr2 + with 
1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid) yielding MOF-808-SH, mixed it 
with the gold precursor and conduct photoreduction to form 
AuNCs within the functionalized framework. The successful 
stabilization of AuNCs by the thiol moieties was confirmed 
through low-energy ion scattering, Fourier-transform infrared 
spectroscopy, and Raman spectroscopy. The resulting composite 
exhibited high activity for photocatalytic hydrogen generation 
from water under solar irradiation. Conversely, the “bottle- 
around-the-ship” method involves growing the MOFs matrix 
around pre-synthesized MNCs. This strategy is not constrained 
by the intrinsic pore size of MOFs, enabling the encapsulation of 
even larger MNCs into frameworks with small pores. Compos- 
ites prepared via this route typically display high stability and 
minimal metal leaching. For instance, Su and coworkers [ 76 ] 
utilized the pre-coordination of Zn2 + ions with surface ligands of 
AuNCs to drive the in situ growth of ZIF-8 around the clusters, 
leading to a uniform distribution of AuNCs within the ZIF-8 
architecture. The resulting materials enabled dynamic control 
over the catalytic reduction of 4-nitrophenol, facilitated by the 
size-selective porosity of ZIF-8. In another study, Zhang and 
coworkers [ 77 ] incubated thiolate-protected AuNCs with Zn2 + 
and 2-methylimidazole in a one-pot aqueous synthesis to obtain 
ZIF-8@AuNCs. The composites exhibited a quantum yield seven 
times higher than that of free AuNCs. Moreover, the fluorescence 
of the encapsulated AuNCs could be selectively quenched via 
coordination between Zn2 + and bilirubin, providing a sensitive 
and efficient platform for visual detection of bilirubin in human 
serum. 
Recently, Li et al. introduced a double in situ approach for 
preparing MNCs/MOFs composites (Figure 3a ) [ 50 ]. This strategy 
involves the simultaneous introduction of both MNCs precur- 
sors and MOFs precursors into a liquid-phase reaction system, 
followed by a one-step reduction to yield the composites. This 
strategy preserves the spatial confinement effect while overcom- 
ing the pore-size limitations inherent in conventional in situ 
synthesis (the pore diameter of ZIF-8 is 1.1 nm). During the 
formation process of the composites, the Zn2 + -imidazole complex 
enhances the interactions between the ligand (GSH) and AuNCs. 
Meanwhile, the imidazole group can also protect AuNCs, thus 
enabling them to remain stable even at a lower ligand density. As a 
result, the AuNCs@ZIF-8 composite with significantly enhanced

2.1.3 
MNCs as Building Units 
Notably, the structural similarity between MNCs and the metal 
nodes typically used to construct MOFs, combined with the 
tunable surface chemistry of MNCs, allows them to be directly 
employed as the building blocks of ordered frameworks via 
functional ligand design or exchange. As a result, this innovative 
synthesis strategy has garnered increasing attention in recent 
studies. For example, Zang and coworkers [ 69 ] successfully 
constructed the first AgNCs-based MOF (Ag12 BPY) by using 4,4- 
bipyridine (BPY) to coordinate with Ag12 NCs (Figure 3b ). In 
this structure, each Ag12 node connects to four adjacent clusters 
through eight BPY ligands by coordination to form an extended 
network with a pore diameter of 0.6 nm. The resulting Ag12 BPY 
showed a bright green photoluminescence that was blue-shifted 
by 113 nm relative to free AgNCs, accompanied by an approxi- 
mately 60-fold enhancement in quantum yield. Compared to free 
AgNCs, the resulting Ag12 BPY framework exhibited markedly 
enhanced luminescence intensity and stability. Owing to its 
phosphorescent properties and porous architecture, the materials 
demonstrated an ultrafast response to O2 under vacuum/air 
conditions. 
Beyond serving as metal nodes, MNCs can also function as 
organic linkers in MOFs. Wang and coworkers [ 78 ] employed 
fluorescent AuNCs protected by 2-pyridyl-diphenylphosphine as 
the linkers for Ag+ nodes, where coordination between Ag+ and 
the ligands on the surface of AuNCs led to the formation of an 
interpenetrated framework with NbO topology and hexagonal 
pores ( ∼ 1.1 nm). These pores allowed reversible solvent exchange, 
which in turn induced detectable luminescence responses, high- 
lighting the potential of such composites in stimuli-responsive 
sensing applications. 
2.2 
MNCs/COFs 
COFs, first reported by Yaghi and coworkers in 2005 [ 37 ], are a 
class of porous crystalline materials constructed through dynamic 
covalent chemistry. These materials exhibit well-defined peri- 
odic architectures, high specific surface areas, and remarkable 
functional versatility, rendering them promising candidates for 
applications in molecular adsorption, catalytic conversion, and 
energy storage [ 35, 79, 80 ]. Composed entirely of light elements,

COFs possess relatively low mass density. The reversible nature of 
the dynamic covalent bonds enables structural self-healing, facili- 
tating the formation of extended crystalline networks. In contrast 
to the predominantly microporous structures of many MOFs, 
COFs often feature mesoporous channels, which contribute 
to their high specific surface areas. Furthermore, by selecting 
monomers with specific geometries and connectivity, the pore 
size, topology, and surface functionality of COFs can be precisely 
tailored to meet targeted performance requirements [ 81 ]. The 
large, ordered mesoporous channels of COFs are ideal for hosting 
and stabilizing ultrasmall MNCs. The covalent nature of COFs 
often provides excellent chemical stability, protecting the embed- 
ded MNCs from harsh environments. The periodic π-conjugated 
structures of COFs can promote efficient electron transfer to 
the confined MNCs, dramatically enhancing their photocatalytic 
efficiency [ 82 ]. Meanwhile, MNCs act as powerful catalytic sites 
within the COF pores, transforming the framework into highly 
efficient and selective heterogeneous catalysts. The presence of 
MNCs can also introduce localized surface plasmon resonance 
or adjust the electronic structure of COFs, improving light 
absorption and charge separation for photocatalysis. The synergy 
between the MNCs’ catalytic activity and the COFs’ precise 
pore environment enables size-selective catalysis and enhances 
the stability against metal sintering [ 83 ]. The integration of 
MNCs with COFs has been achieved through several strategies, 
including post-assembly, deposition techniques, pyrolysis, and 
liquid-phase reduction. 
2.2.1 
Post-Assembly 
Post-assembly remains a convenient strategy for constructing 
MNCs/COFs composites, primarily relying on electrostatic inter- 
actions between MNCs and the framework. For example, Jiang 
and coworkers [ 84 ] fabricated COF-PtAuNCs by assembling 
peptide-protected PtNCs and AuNCs with COFs which were 
prepared by Schiff-base condensation of 2,4,6-trihydroxy-1,3,5- 
benzenetricarbaldehyde with benzidine. The resulting compos- 
ites exhibited distinct ultraviolet-visible light absorption, reso- 
nance Rayleigh scattering (RRS), and surface-enhanced Raman 
scattering (SERS) signals, enabling effective detection of Cd2 + in 
water and rice samples. However, the relatively large pore size 
of COFs often leads to MNCs leakage or aggregation. Although 
coordination or covalent bonding can be introduced to enhance 
the loading efficiency, excessive chemical modification may cause 
structural collapse of COFs or detachment of MNCs’ surface 
ligands, with assembly sites of MNCs remaining difficult to 
control precisely. 
2.2.2 
In Situ Synthesis of MNCs Inside COFs 
In contrast to MNCs/MOFs systems, the excellent thermal and 
chemical stability of COFs makes in situ growth of MNCs within 
the framework a preferred method for composite construction. 
This approach allows precise control over binding sites of MNCs, 
ensures uniform distribution of MNCs throughout the COF 
matrix, and significantly improves the stability of the resulting 
composites. Based on the reduction mechanism employed, this 
process can be categorized into deposition, pyrolysis, and liquid- 
phase reduction. 
The deposition method primarily employs light, vapor depo- 
sition, or electrodeposition to incorporate MNCs into COFs. 
This approach eliminates the need for external reducing agents, 
thereby minimizing impurity introduction and preserving the 
intrinsic porous crystalline structure of COFs. In 2022, Deng 
and coworkers [ 82 ] pioneered the use of photodeposition to 
synthesize PtNCs/PY-DHBD-COF composites, in which COF 
was prepared via a Schiff-base condensation reaction of 1,4- 
dihydroxybenzidine with 1,3,6,8-tetra(4-formylphenyl)pyrene. By 
integrating adjacent hydroxyl groups and imino-N sites into the 
COF building units, they created periodic adsorption sites for Pt 
precursors, which were subsequently reduced in situ to PtNCs 
via photogenerated electrons. The uniformly distributed PtNCs 
in the composites expose abundant active surfaces, significantly 
enhancing the electron transfer efficiency. At a Pt loading rate 
of only 1%, the composites achieved a remarkable photocatalytic 
hydrogen evolution rate of 42,432 µmol g− 1 h− 1 . This work demon- 
strated precise atomic-level control over MNCs’ morphology and 
positioning, offering a strategic direction for designing highly 
efficient MNCs/COFs photocatalysts. Similarly, Ramanathan and 
coworkers [ 85 ] developed a strategy to modulate Cu dispersion in 
COFs, achieving a controlled transition from single atoms to NCs 
by varying the electrodeposition time. Through the electrodeposi- 
tion, CuNCs with diameters of 1–2 nm were uniformly distributed 
within the COF channels, forming an excellent catalytic system 
for Ullmann coupling reactions. 
The pyrolysis method typically involves two steps: first, metal 
precursors are introduced into the COF framework, and then 
the precursor-loaded COF powder is pyrolyzed to reduce the 
metal species into MNCs [ 86 ]. For instance, Jiang and coworkers 
[ 83 ] incorporated Pt2 + into a BPY-based COF (PT-PY-COF) via 
coordination with dipyridine units, obtaining Pt-COF (Figure 4a ). 
Subsequent carbonization at 800◦C yielded Pt-COF800, in which 
PtNCs were uniformly dispersed in the PT-PY-COF matrix. The 
heterogeneous active sites formed by PtNCs exhibit significant 
potential for energy conversion under electrocatalytic processes. 
Compared to commercial Pt catalysts, Pt-COF800 demonstrates 
superior performance with lower Pt usage, exhibiting a half- 
w ave potential of 0.88 V and a diffusion-limiting current density 
of 6.79 mA cm− 2 . This study provides valuable insights into 
the design of MNCs/COFs composites and the development of 
industrial catalytic systems through precise regulation of active 
sites. 
While both deposition and pyrolysis methods enable uniform 
dispersion of MNCs within COF pores, their implementation 
typically requires high-temperature or controlled-atmosphere 
conditions, limiting applicability to many MNCs systems. Con- 
sequently, liquid-phase reduction has emerged as a preferable 
strategy for preparing MNCs/COFs composites. This approach 
involves immersing COFs in a solution containing metal pre- 
cursors, followed by direct reduction using external agents. For 
instance, Shao and coworkers [ 87 ] immersed a ketone-based COF 
(TAPT-2OHTb-COF) in PdCl2 solution and introduced a reducing 
agent to grow PdNCs within the COF pores. The enol structure 
of TAPT-2OHTb-COF effectively confined PdNCs, enhancing the 
composite stability and promoting photocatalytic H2 O2 synthesis 
efficiency. This study presented a new strategy for applying COFs 
in photocatalytic H2 O2 production using both freshwater and sea- 
water. However, the introduction of reductants and other reagents

thereby enhancing the photocatalytic performance and offering a 
promising route for developing highly stable photocatalysts with 
controllable active sites. 
2.3 
MNCs/HOFs 
HOFs are crystalline porous materials constructed from organic 
or metal–organic building units interconnected through hydro- 
gen bonds. In 2011, Chen and coworkers first established the 
definition of HOFs and demonstrated their application as porous 
materials (HOF-1) for ethylene/ethane separation [ 38 ]. In con- 
trast to MOFs (based on coordination bonds) and COFs (based 
on covalent bonds), HOFs exhibit lower bond energy due to 
their hydrogen-bonded networks. These characteristics endow 
HOFs with distinctive advantages, including mild synthesis 
conditions, solution processability, and facile regeneration [ 40 ]. 
Furthermore, by varying solvents and reaction parameters, a wide 
range of HOF isomers can be obtained, significantly enriching 
structural diversity and expanding their potential in adsorption, 
separation, biosensing, and catalysis [ 91 ]. The integration of 
MNCs with HOFs can effectively promote electronic transitions 
of MNCs through intermolecular hydrogen bonds and suppress 
the non-radiative attenuation of triplet energy, thereby enhancing 
the fluorescence intensity of MNCs. In addition, MNCs can 
promote the interlayer ion mobility and the carrier rate of HOFs 
to enhance the enzyme-like activity of the composites [ 92 ]. At 
present, the main construction methods of MNCs/HOFs include 
post-assembly, in-situ preparation of MNCs within HOFs and 
self-assembly of MNCs to form HOFs.

nitrate cluster and hexa(4-sulfonatophenyl)benzenea through 72 
hydrogen bonds to host NCs. This research inspired the method

2.3.2 
In Situ Synthesis of MNCs Inside HOFs 
Advances in the study of HOFs have led to the development 
of structures with enhanced thermal and chemical stability as 
well as permanent porosity, enabling the in situ synthesis of 
MNCs within their matrices. For instance, Liu and coworkers 
[ 94 ] designed a HOF functionalized with 2,6-pyridinedicarboxylic 
acid (PDA) ligands to synthesize PdNCs inside the pores through 
photo-reduction (Figure 5a ). The light absorption of the obtained 
composites at 463 nm was significantly enhanced, while the pho- 
toluminescence showed a blue shift accompanied by a decrease 
in the intensity. The increase in transient photocurrent intensity 
indicated superior charge separation in the composites, which 
led to exceptional performance in photocatalytic CO2 reduction 
under water vapor, achieving a methane production rate of 
44.3 µmol g− 1 h− 1 with a high selectivity. In a recent study, Zhu and 
coworkers [ 95 ] adopted a mechanochemical synthesis strategy to 
grow various MNCs within HOF-101. The resulting Au25 /HOF- 
101 catalyst showed a remarkable 315-fold increase in the activity 
compared to free AuNCs, underscoring the critical role of HOFs 
in enhancing the catalytic performance. This versatile synthesis 
approach allows customized carrier selection to meet specific 
application needs and demonstrates considerable potential for 
the design of multifunctional catalytic systems. 
2.3.3 
Self-Assembly of MNCs Into HOFs 
Furthermore, utilizing ligands capable of forming hydrogen 
bonds to pre-stabilize MNCs offers a viable route for the sub- 
sequent construction of HOFs [ 96 ]. In a 2014 study, Landman 
and coworkers [ 97 ] assembled Ag44 NCs into HOFs through 
hydrogen bonds between their ligands. Under external pressure, 
the hydrogen bonds within HOFs undergo significant twisting, 
which in turn induces chirality. This research provided a guiding 
direction for exploring design principles of MNCs/HOFs at the 
molecular scale. Similarly, Huang and coworkers [ 98 ] assembled 
Zn-doped LnNCs into three-dimensional HOFs with accessible 
channels through hydrogen bonding between the ligands. The 
resulting frameworks retain the unique luminescent properties 
of lanthanide ions and exhibit a significant fluorescence response 
to nitro aromatic compounds, thereby enabling highly selective 
and sensitive detection of 1,4-dinitrobenzene and trinitrotoluene. 
Cao and coworkers [ 99 ] reported the formation of two- 
dimensional hydrogen-bonded mesoporous frameworks (HMFs) 
via the micelle-directed self-assembly of NCs (Figure 5b ). In 
this process, amphiphilic diblock copolymer micelles first guide 
arrangement of NCs into hexagonal building units featuring 
mesopores, which subsequently reassemble into uniform 2D 
superstructures. By varying the length and concentration of the 
micelles, the mesopore size (from 8.5 to 11.6 nm) and the overall 
HMF architecture (from spherical to cylindrical) can be precisely 
tailored.

2.4 
MNCs/SOFs 
SOFs represent an emerging class of RFs, typically constructed 
from functionalized building units (e.g., supramolecular macro- 
cycles, metal–organic coordination complexes, and MNCs) via 
non-covalent interactions including Van der Waals forces, π–
π stacking, and dipole interactions [ 41, 43 ]. SOFs offer several 
advantages, such as pore formation through molecular stacking, 
synthetic diversity, low mass density, facile synthesis, solution 
processability, and ease of functionalization. The dynamic nature 
of SOFs allows for stimulus-responsive behavior, where exter- 
nal triggers (e.g., vapor adsorption, mechanical pressure) can 
induce reversible structural changes in the framework. This in 
turn switches the luminescence of MNCs [ 100 ], while MNCs 
with hierarchical structure complexity can impart higher-order 
architectural complicacy and advanced functional regulation to 
SOFs [ 101 ]. Moreover, MNCs/SOFs composites hold the potential 
to combine with the specific recognition, adsorption, assembly, 
and stimuli-responsive properties inherent to supramolecular 
macrocycles [ 102, 103 ]. As a result, they are increasingly regarded 
as a promising class of materials under active investigation. 
Primary construction strategies include the direct self-assembly 
of MNCs into monolithic SOFs, as well as the co-assembly of 
MNCs with supramolecular macrocycles to form hybrid diblock 
SOF architectures. 
2.4.1 
Self-Assembly of MNCs Into SOFs 
MNCs can self-assemble into single-component SOFs by lever- 
aging supramolecular interactions between their surface ligands, 
thereby extending individual clusters into three-dimensional 
topological networks. Given the relatively weak nature of 
supramolecular forces, the inter-cluster distances in such frame- 
works are typically short. This assembly strategy relies solely on 
the intrinsic crystallinity of MNCs. The single-component nature 
of the system enables the formation of diverse topological archi- 
tectures through controlled crystallization, thereby broadening 
their potential applications. In 2019, Yam and coworkers [ 100 ] 
demonstrated the first assembly of Au10 NCs into SOFs through 
non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding and Van 
der Waals forces. The resulting Au-SOFs undergo reversible struc- 
tural transformations under vacuum conditions, accompanied by 
visible changes in luminescence. This work provides a new model 
and valuable insights for the development of artificial hierarchical 
materials and stimulus-responsive luminescent systems based on 
MNCs/SOFs. 
Inspired by the multiple non-covalent interactions in biomole- 
cules and leveraging the structural complexity of biomimetic 
systems, our research group has constructed a series of metal- 
based supramolecular organic frameworks (MSOF-1 to MSOF- 
6) with exceptional structural adaptability [ 104–106 ]. MSOF-1, 
MSOF-2, and MSOF-3 were obtained by assembly of three differ- 
ent metal–organic coordination compounds Ag(4-Mpy)2 (PPh3)2 
(4-Mpy: 4-mercaptopyridine), [Ag(4-Mpy)2 ]n and Ag(PPh3 )3 I, 
respectively, through the C ─H ⋅⋅⋅S interaction, C ─H ⋅⋅⋅π inter- 
action, C ─H ⋅⋅⋅H hydrogen bond, N ⋅⋅⋅N interaction, and π⋅⋅⋅π
stacking effect between the surface ligands. MSOF-4 and MSOF-5 
are two distinct structures self-assembled from AuNCs [Au11 (4- 
Mpy)3 (PPh3 )7 ] through C ─H ⋅⋅⋅π interaction, featuring cylindrical 
and cage-like pores, respectively. These materials undergo single- 
crystal to single-crystal transformations under various external 
stimuli, including mechanical pressure, solvent exchange, and 
guest inclusion. Among these MSOFs, the electron-rich Ag-based 
materials (MSOF-1, MSOF-2, and MSOF-3) form charge-transfer 
complexes with electron-deficient iodine molecules, exhibiting 
remarkable iodine uptake capacity of up to 3.70 g g− 1 and 
can further be utilized for real-time fluorescence sensing of 
iodine. Meanwhile, the AuNCs-based frameworks (MSOF-4 and 
MSOF-5) demonstrate distinct host–guest adaptation behaviors 
toward 1-BPR and 2-bromopropane (2-BPR) isomers (Figure 6a ). 
Notably, MSOF-4 selectively incorporates 1-BPR vapor, which can 
induce the ligand exchange with AuNCs, triggering significant 
structural reorganization that yields rare double- and quadruple- 
helical assemblies in the resulting MSOF-6 crystal with AuNCs 
[Au11 Br3 (PPh3 )7 ] as the assembly unit. This transformation is 
accompanied by intense near-infrared emission. Importantly, the 
double-helical architecture of MSOF-6 facilitates efficient elec- 
tron transfer in catalytic reactions, exhibiting a 6-fold enhance- 
ment in the catalytic rate compared to MSOF-4. This strategy 
enables the development of higher-order biomimetic materials 
through precisely engineered non-covalent interactions, provid- 
ing a pathway to achieve artificial hierarchical systems with 
biological-level complexity and advanced functionality. 
2.4.2 
Co-Assembly of MNCs With Supramolecular 
Macrocycles Into SOFs 
Supramolecular macrocycles (such as cyclodextrins, cucur- 
bit[n]urils, calixarenes, and pillar[n]arenes) are cyclic organic 
structures with intrinsic cavities that enable selective binding to 
specific guest molecules or ions via non-covalent interactions, 
forming diverse host–guest complexes [ 107–109 ]. Co-assembling 
such macrocycles with MNCs to form binary SOFs allows the 
construction of MNCs/SOFs composites with tunable morpholo- 
gies and enhanced molecular recognition capabilities, facilitat- 
ing the development of multifunctional hybrid materials. For 
instance, Zhou and coworkers [ 110 ] reported a shape-controlled 
supramolecular assembly strategy, in which MNCs were grown 
in situ on boron-cluster oxide frameworks (BOFs), then combined 
with cucurbit[n]uril homologues (CB[5-8]), yielding well-defined 
SOFs architectures (e.g., urchin-like, network, and octahedral 
structures) without requiring chemical modification or additives. 
In another example, Xun and coworkers [ 111 ] developed a 
synergistic assembly approach in which chiral γ-cyclodextrin ( γ- 
CD) organized around luminescent AgNCs to form a crystalline 
Ag6 @ γ-CD framework (Figure 6b ). This configuration enabled 
effective supramolecular chirality transfer from γ-CD to Ag6 
clusters, leading to enhanced circularly polarized luminescence.

3 
Biomedical Applications of MNCs/RFs 
The distinctive optical properties, enzyme-mimetic activities, 
photodynamic behavior, and photothermal performance of 
MNCs endow them with promising potential in biomedical fields 
such as diagnosis and therapeutic applications [ 15–18 ]. RFs, 
owing to their tunable pore dimensions, well-defined active sites, 
and high specific surface area, also exhibit remarkable utility 
in bio-application [ 112, 113 ]. The integration of MNCs with RFs 
not only synergistically enhances their individual functionalities, 
but also imparts novel properties (e.g., responsiveness and var- 
ious types of enzyme-like activities) to the composites, further 
advancing their application in the biomedical domain. This sec- 
tion summarizes key, representative biomedical applications of 
MNCs/RFs composites, including biosensing, bioimaging, cancer 
therapy, and antibacterial. 
3.1 
Biosensing 
Biosensing, a technology which converts interactions between 
biological recognition elements and analytes into quantifiable 
signals, is widely employed in medicine, environmental moni- 
toring, and food safety [ 114–116 ]. It plays a vital role in disease 
diagnosis, biosafety assessment, and drug metabolism studies 
[ 117, 118 ]. MNCs serve as excellent biosensing probes due to 
their unique photoluminescence, enzyme-mimetic activities, and 
electrochemical properties [ 119–122 ]. By constructing MNCs/RFs 
composites, the emission wavelength and quantum yield of 
MNCs can be synchronously modulated through interfacial 
engineering, enabling multi-wavelength luminescence and mul- 
timodal responses toward target analytes, thereby improving the 
sensing performance. Furthermore, the high specific surface area 
and structurally ordered pores of RFs facilitate the selective 
enrichment of analytes, which significantly enhances detection 
selectivity and sensitivity [ 123 ]. Currently, MNCs/RFs composites 
have been successfully employed for sensitive and efficient 
detection of various biomarkers, including small molecules, 
ions, proteins, and cells, using fluorescence, colorimetric, and 
electrochemical methods.

and a linear range of 0–50 µM. Encapsulation within ZIF-

lifetime of AuNCs, while imparting high selectivity against 
common volatile organic compounds. Similarly, Huang and 
coworkers [ 128 ] synthesized CuNCs/ZIF-8 nanocomposites by

Ratiometric fluorescence sensing is an analytical technique that 
monitors local environmental changes by measuring the intensity 
ratio of fluorescence signals at two distinct wavelengths. Com- 
pared to conventional single fluorescence signal, this approach 
offers higher sensitivity and accuracy while minimizing inter- 
ference from external factors [ 146, 147 ]. Both MNCs and RFs 
can be engineered to exhibit multiple emission bands, making 
MNCs/RFs composites particularly well-suited for ratiometric 
sensing applications. Zhou et al. [ 148 ] recently developed a 
ratiometric sensing platform using red-emitting europium-based 
MOFs (Eu-MOF) and blue-emitting AuNCs for ATP detection 
(Figure 7a ). Upon ATP binding to Eu3 + , the Eu-MOF structure 
was decomposed, attenuating its red emission, while the blue flu- 
orescence of AuNCs remained constant. This distinct red-to-blue 
emission color transition enabled the construction of a paper- 
based ratiometric fluorescence sensor for visual ATP detection. 
Coupled with smartphone imaging, this system achieved facile 
visual quantification of ATP in serum samples with a LOD of 
10 µM.

significantly enhancing detection sensitivity and selectivity. Fur-

To improve the sensitivity and accuracy of fluorescence imag- 
ing, strategies that activate luminescence in situ to suppress 
autofluorescence interference have gained increasing attention 
[ 203 ]. Hu and coworkers [ 204 ] developed a peptide-linked con- 
jugate by coupling AuNCs to NH2 -MIL-101(Fe) MOFs loaded 
with the anticancer drug camptothecin (Figure 9 ), enabling 
monitoring of targeted intracellular drug release. In this sys- 
tem, the fluorescence of AuNCs is initially quenched. Upon 
cellular uptake, the AuNC/Cam@MOF nanocomposite targets 
lysosomes, where camptothecin is slowly released. This release 
subsequently induces apoptosis and upregulates caspase-3, which 
cleaves the peptide linker to release the AuNCs. These process 
enables dual-mode monitoring via restored fluorescence and 
inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, allowing real- 
time tracking of the therapeutic process. 
Wu and coworkers [ 205 ] designed a tumor microenvironment 
(TME)-activated fluorescence probe for targeted imaging and 
drug release monitoring in mice. This probe consisted of AuNCs, 
DOX, and Cu2 + -doped ZIF-8. In the acidic TME, the ZIF-8 shell 
gradually degrades, releasing DOX and AuNCs. Simultaneously, 
elevated GSH levels at the tumor site reduce Cu2 + , restoring the 
Cu2 + -quenched fluorescence of AuNCs and enabling effective 
tumor imaging. This work offers an innovative TME-activated 
“turn on” fluorescence imaging strategy. Similarly, Wang and 
coworkers [ 52 ] constructed a composite by co-loading MnO2 
nanoparticles and AuNCs onto ZIF-8, followed by coating with 
4T1 cell membranes to evade immune clearance and enhance 
tumor targeting (Figure 10a ). At the tumor site, overexpressed 
GSH degrades MnO2 , restoring the quenched fluorescence of 
AuNCs and achieving targeted tumor imaging. This design 
combines biocompatibility, targeting capability, and activable 
fluorescence for precise bioimaging applications. 
Beyond fluorescence imaging, MNCs/RFs composites can also 
incorporate additional contrast agents to enable multimodal 
bioimaging. In 2015, Wang and coworkers [ 206 ] pioneered this 
approach by co-assembling Fe3 O4 @PAA nanoparticles, AuNCs, 
and ZIF-8 into a trimodal imaging platform (Fe3 O4 @PAA/ 
AuNCs/ZIF-8) capable of MRI, CT, and fluorescence imaging. 
The composites also exhibited remarkably high doxorubicin 
(DOX) loading capacity (1.54 g g− 1 ) and pH-responsive drug 
release behavior, highlighting their potential for cancer theranos- 
tics. In a separate study, Tan and coworkers [ 207 ] constructed 
IL@MIL-101(Fe)@BSA-Au by integrating BSA-protected AuNCs 
with MIL-101(Fe) MOFs encapsulating ionic liquid as an MRI 
contrast agent (Figure 10b ). This system enabled precise dual- 
modal (MRI/fluorescence) tumor imaging and real-time tracking 
of MIL-101(Fe) distribution in vivo. Fluorescence and MRI signals 
peaked in the liver at 1 h and in tumors at 5 h post-administration. 
Furthermore, the microwave-sensitive ionic liquid enhanced the 
catalytic activity of MIL-101(Fe), promoting rapid ROS generation 
from endogenous H2 O2 in the TME for microwave-enhanced 
dynamic therapy (MEDT). This work demonstrates how combin- 
ing synergistic therapy with multimodal imaging can advance the 
clinical translation of MNCs/RFs platforms.

Antitumor Therapy 
Cancer, a global health challenge that has plagued humanity 
for centuries, exhibits an accelerating incidence rate [ 208–210 ]. 
Preclinical and clinical anticancer research has become one of the 
most significant and demanding fields within modern medicine 
and biotechnology, prompting substantial efforts from scientists 
and clinicians [ 211–213 ]. MNCs hold considerable promise for 
cancer therapy due to their unique physicochemical proper- 
ties. The composition and surface chemistry of MNCs can be 
engineered to achieve excellent light-induced ROS generation 
efficiency and photothermal conversion efficiency, enabling the 
application of MNCs for PDT and photothermal therapy (PTT). 
Furthermore, their exceptional absorption capacity for high- 
energy X-ray or gamma radiation positions MNCs as effective 
radiosensitizers for cancer radiotherapy. Their readily modifi- 
able surface structure also provides a platform for combination 
therapy. However, their ultrasmall size facilitates rapid renal 
clearance, hindering long-term therapeutic efficacy. Loading 
MNCs within RFs addresses this limitation by prolonging blood 
circulation time and enhancing tumor accumulation. RFs also 
serve as versatile loading platforms, facilitating synergistic ther- 
apy by co-loading various therapeutic agents [ 67 ].

of Fe3 O4 /ZIF-8-AuNCs composites for treating subcutaneous 
tumors in a HeLa tumor mouse model. Under the near-infrared

oxidase) [ 219–222 ]. These properties enable AuNCs to generate 
efficient ROS upon light irradiation and catalyze the endogenous 
substances of bacteria, thereby inducing the death of bacteria

91.6% of S. aureus in vitro under visible light. In vivo experiments 
further confirmed that Ag28 Au1 NCs@ZIF-8 effectively cleared 
bacteria at infected sites, alleviated inflammation, and promoted 
regeneration of blood vessels, epithelial tissue, and collagen, 
leading to nearly complete wound healing within 11 days in a S. 
aureus -infected wound model. Guo et al. [ 226 ] recently designed 
a plasmon-enhanced strategy to boost the photodisinfection 
capability of MNCs/RFs by incorporating gold nanorods (AuNRs) 
into ZIF-8@AuNCs to construct AuNRs@ZIF-8@AuNCs. Lever- 
aging the strong localized surface plasmon resonance effect of 
AuNRs, the composites showed enhanced visible-light absorption 
and improved photo-triggered ROS production, which exhibited 
potent photobactericidal activity against both E. coli and S. aureus 
under visible light. Importantly, AuNRs@ZIF-8@AuNCs can 
be readily coated onto mask fibers, outperforming commercial 
masks in antibacterial efficacy ( > 99.0%). This work provides a 
new strategy to enhance the antibacterial property of MNCs/RFs, 
with promising applications in environmental and biomedical 
fields. 
Beyond photodynamic antibacterial effects, MNCs/RFs compos- 
ites have also been integrated with other therapeutic modalities 
(e.g., PTT and drug-based chemotherapy) to enhance antibac- 
terial efficacy. For example, Chen and coworkers [ 227 ] loaded 
AuNCs into zirconium-based porphyrin MOFs (PCN) to obtain 
AuNCs@PCN through in situ growth and then applied for 
synergistic PDT/PTT against bacteria. Under near infra-red 
laser irradiation, AuNCs@PCN raised the temperature in the 
wound area to 56.2◦C and generated ROS, effectively inhibiting 
methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and ampicillin resistant 
Escherichia coli (Ampr E. coli ) at 95.3% and 90.6%, respectively. 
Furthermore, AuNCs@PCN was loaded into the hydrogel and 
applied to the treatment of diabetic wounds in living rats, 
which can provide a moist healing environment for the wound, 
and upregulating vascular and epithelial regeneration markers 
(e.g., CD31, VEGF, EGF, and bFGF). In diabetic rat models, 
the wound area decreased to 2.7% after 21 days treatment. This 
study illustrated a synergistic antibacterial strategy and proposed 
a novel clinical approach for diabetic wound management. In 
recent years, stimulus-responsive drug delivery systems tailored 
to wound microenvironment cues (e.g., pH, temperature, ROS, 
and glucose) have emerged as promising platforms for controlled 
release of nanomedicines. Infected wounds exhibit passive ATP 
leakage due to cellular damage, which acts both as a biomarker of 
tissue injury and an alarmin triggering immune responses. Lever- 
aging this mechanism, Liu and coworkers [ 228 ] incorporated 
AuNCs and vancomycin (Van) into ZIF-8, and then integrated 
into hydrogel microneedle tips for real-time ATP monitoring 
and antibacterial treatment (Figure 12 ). ATP-Zn2 + interactions 
triggered ZIF-8 decomposition, quenching AuNCs’ fluorescence 
and releasing Van, which enabled simultaneous ATP detection 
and targeted antibacterial effect. The released Zn2 + further dam- 
aged bacterial membranes, enhancing the bactericidal efficacy. In 
addition, the positively charged microneedle surface promoted 
bacterial adsorption via electrostatic interactions, fostering a 
localized antibacterial microenvironment. This integrated system 
enables real-time wound diagnosis and adaptive therapy, offering 
a reliable strategy for intelligent wound management.

Conclusions and Outlook 
In recent years, MNCs/RFs composites have attracted consid- 
erable research attention owing to their exceptional optical 
properties, tunable functionality, and selective responsiveness. To 
date, various MNCs (including AuNCs, AgNCs, CuNCs, PtNCs, 
and PdNCs) have been integrated with diverse RFs such as 
MOFs, COFs, HOFs, and SOFs, enabling broad applications in 
biomedicine, catalysis, and sensors. Compared to other theranos- 
tic agents, MNCs/RFs composites offer a unique set of advan- 
tages: (1) The well-defined structures and atomic-level precision 
of MNCs and RFs allow for detailed investigation into the mech- 
anisms behind performance evolution in composite materials, as 
well as into the composite formation process, thereby enabling 
structure-based design for tailored functionalities. (2) The struc- 
tural designability of MNCs/RFs allows for the integration of 
diverse diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities, paving the way 
for advanced applications such as multimodal sensing, imaging, 
and combined therapy. Moreover, the tunable emission, large 
Stokes shift, and excellent photostability of MNCs concurrently 
address the common limitation of traditional organic dyes. (3) 
RFs offer controllable degradation due to their dynamic, non- 
covalent structures, whereas MNCs enable rapid renal clearance 
owing to their ultra-small size. Thus the combination could 
effectively minimize the risks of prolonged in vivo retention. 
This review systematically examined strategies for combining 
MNCs with RFs, covering post-synthetic assembly, in situ growth 
(encompassing both single and double in situ routes), and 
the direct use of MNCs as framework building units. Among 
these, post-assembly offers the greatest convenience and gen- 
erality, though it often results in limited loading capacity and 
composite stability. In contrast, in situ growth and framework

small-sized HOFs and SOFs suitable for drug delivery and 
disease treatment [ 229 ]. We believe this strategy can also 
be effectively adapted to construct biologically functional 
MNCs/HOFs and MNCs/SOFs composites with enhanced 
biomedical applicability. Furthermore, developing new and 
precise synthetic approaches by leveraging machine learning 
and computational screening to predict compatible pairs of 
MNC precursors and RF building blocks can dramatically 
accelerate the rational design of composites with predefined 
structures and functions.

3. Current therapeutic applications of MNCs/RFs composites 
remain largely confined to antitumor and antibacterial treat- 
ments. For their future clinical translation, a thorough inves- 
tigation into their long-term fate in vivo, including biodegra- 
dation, biotransformation, and potential long-term toxicity, 
is highly necessary. In particular, the size of MNCs/RFs 
(typically > 50 nm) presents a double-edged sword: it facili- 
tates enhanced retention in target tissues (e.g., the liver) but 
simultaneously limits metabolic clearance and penetration 
into deep tumor regions, compromising both biosafety and 
therapeutic efficacy. Developing new strategies to resolve 
the challenges of enhanced tissue accumulation and effi- 
cient metabolic clearance is therefore essential. Among 
these, in vivo assembly represents a promising approach 
by enabling the precise, in situ construction of functional 
networks from low-toxicity precursors directly within target 
tissues. This strategy will not only maximize local retention 
and penetration but also ensure controllable degradation 
and timely clearance, thereby simultaneously overcoming 
the long-term biocompatibility issues, off-target effects, and 
limited therapeutic depth of conventional nanomedicine. 
Furthermore, recent years have witnessed the development 
of numerous strategies for treating multifocal, recurrent, and 
metastatic tumors, including targeted radiotherapy, gene- 
based immunotherapy, and the induction of ferroptosis, 
cuproptosis, and pyroptosis. Given the structural tunability 
of MNCs/RFs, implementing the functional requirements 
for these therapeutic modalities appears highly feasible. 
Moreover, various diseases (such as pulmonary fibrosis, 
diabetic wounds, acute and chronic inflammation in multiple 
organs, and cancers with concurrent bacterial infections) 
pose serious threats to human health. Parallel to this, in 
the field of disease treatment, the excellent and controllable 
enzyme-like catalytic properties of MNCs/RFs have not yet 
been efficiently exploited and harnessed. By constructing 
cascade nanozymes, the composites can effectively respond 
to the complex physiological micro-environment of these 
diseases and achieve effective treatment. Moreover, through 
functional design, MNCs/RFs could be developed for in vivo 
treatment of these diseases. By integrating their demon- 
strated capabilities in biosensing and bioimaging, these 
composites could further enable the construction of intelli- 
gent theranostic platforms capable of real-time monitoring 
and treatment. This integrated approach represents a crucial 
future direction for advancing MNCs/RFs in biomedical 
therapy. 
Overall, the marriage of MNCs with RFs has presented great 
potential in the field of biomedical application, even though 
some challenges remain to be resolved. Driven by the high 
design flexibility of both RFs and MNCs, along with a growing 
understanding of their unique interactions, we believe that con- 
tinuing development in this field will enable the precise design 
of MNCs/RFs composites for a broader spectrum of biomedical 
applications.

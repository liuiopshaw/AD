bacteria from the Acetobacter, Gluconobacter, Zygo-
saccharomyces, Saccharomyces, and Schizosaccha-
romyces families (Laavanya et al. 2021), and differs 
from plant cellulose due to its high degree of purity 
and nanoscale dimensions (Lee et al. 2014), as well 
as greater flexibility and higher hydrophilicity (Dima 
et al. 2017).
Bacterial cellulose presents itself as an alternative 
to traditional textiles in the fashion industry due to its 
versatility, aesthetics, mechanical similarity to leather 
and, most importantly, its sustainability in production 
processes (Yim et  al. 2017). Currently, the fashion 
industry is focused on producing clothes and accesso-
ries with cheaper materials and shorter life spans aim-
ing to increase consumption, which results in more 
manufacturing activities, disposal and environmental 
impact (Rathinamoorthy and Kiruba 2022). From the 
traditional textile life cycle, these impacts arise from 
the use of toxic chemicals and large amounts of water 
and energy (Mazzoto et al. 2021).
For these reasons, bacterial cellulose has been the 
subject of studies looking for new ways of producing 
clothes and accessories. Kamiński et al. 2020 studied 
a method to enhance properties of bacterial cellulose 
enabling its application on astronauts’ clothes, for the 
purpose of recycling the used pieces (Gregory et al. 
2021). Fernandes et  al. 2019a did the same for the 
application in shoes, obtaining an appropriate mate-
rial in terms of malleability and breathability. Fashion 
designer Suzanne Lee used bacterial cellulose tex-
tile to produce a jacket with cutting and sewing (da 
Silva et al. 2021), showing it’s possible to manufac-
ture bacterial cellulose clothes through conventional 
processes.
However, the use of bacterial cellulose depends 
on surface modifications that promote suitable per-
formance in fashion products, since its high hydro-
philicity, evidenced by its contact angle lower than 
90.0 º (Betlej et al. 2021), results in challenges related 
to product hygiene and even unsuitability for human 
skin contact (Wood 2019).
It is possible to reduce the hydrophilicity of bacte-
rial cellulose through wet chemistry, such as surface 
functionalization through polymerization, as tested 
by Krishnamurthy et al. (2020); Sulaeva et al. 2019; 
Hettergger et  al. 2015). There is also the possibil-
ity of creating composites with synthetic polymers, 
presented by Fernandes et al. (2019a). However, wet 
chemistry makes the process unsustainable due to 
effluent production, and the association with synthetic 
polymers eliminates one of the key desired character-
istics of bacterial cellulose: biodegradability.
One less polluting alternative is plasma treatment, 
which does not involve effluents, solid or liquid waste. 
Furthermore, surface modification with plasma is 
facilitated because plasma generates excited species 
(electronically and vibrationally) and radicals (espe-
cially through molecular dissociation), making the 
environment much more reactive (Zheng et al. 2010). 
Organic species, once activated by the plasma, tend to 
react with the surface and form thin films (Mourgues 
et al. 2005). The present paper uses a modified plasma 
system, namely cathodic cage plasma (Guinter et al. 
2019; Correa et  al. 2018; Gallo et  al. 2017), which 
is characterized by the plasma being produced at the 
space outside the cage. Since the biofabric sample 
of bacterial cellulose (substrate) is placed inside the 
cage, its surface will not be bombarded by plasma 
energetic ions (generated outside the cage), therefore 
it will not suffer overheating and can be maintained 
close to room temperature. However, reactive species 
produced in the plasma (outside the cage), from the 
precursor gas (e. g. ­C2H2, Ar and ­H2), can reach the 
sample inside the cage. They work as active species 
to produce a polymer film on the substrate surface.
Studies have been published regarding the interac-
tion of plasma with traditional fabrics. Vilaplana and 
Cabanes (2012), Yan et al. (2017) and Sohbatzadeh 
et al. (2019) successfully made the surface of cotton 
and polyester fabrics hydrophobic after plasma treat-
ments using gases composed of molecules containing 
carbon and hydrogen. As to bacterial cellulose, sur-
face modification using plasma has been associated 
with other potentially polluting processes, such as 
silanization (Leal et al. 2020). Silanization is a chemi-
cal surface coating process with silane molecules 
that, when combined with plasma, affects both the 
roughness and surface energy of bacterial cellulose 
to enhance the hydrophobic result, but the disposal of 
the involved chemical materials can lead to environ-
mental issues.
Few studies demonstrate the reduction in hydro-
philicity of bacterial cellulose surface through 
plasma treatment (Aditya et  al. 2022; Kurniawan 
et al. 2012). Furthermore, the outcome of surface 
modifications after plasma treatment is linked to 
the chemical aspects of the gases involved, pecu-
liarities of the treated material, and treatment

Materials and methods
In order to study the hydrophilic nature of bacte-
rial cellulose, samples were produced from kom-
bucha and treated with low-pressure cold plasma. 
The influence of the gas mixture (Ar/acetylene and 
Ar/acetylene/H2), treatment time and duty cycle 
was evaluated. The samples were characterized 
through wetting behavior (contact angle), FTIR 
(Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy), TG and 
dTG (Thermogravimetry–differential thermal anal-
ysis), XPS (X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy) and 
FEG-SEM (Field emission gun—Scanning electron 
microscopy).
Preparation of bacterial cellulose
Bacterial cellulose was produced using the cultiva-
tion technique proposed by designer Suzanne Lee 
and biologist David Hepworth, using kombucha for 
the cultivation of microorganisms in an aerobic and 
aqueous environment (Costa et al. 2017). The culti-
vation medium was prepared with water, granulated 
sugar, green tea, apple cider vinegar, and a SCOBY 
(Symbiotic Culture Of Bacteria and Yeast), accord-
ing to Lima et  al. (2021). The mixture remained at 
room temperature (below 20 °C) and, when bubbles 
appeared on the surface, fermentation began, form-
ing a thin, transparent layer. This layer thickened over 
time and, at the end of the 28-day cultivation period 
(Fig.  1), it was removed from the container and 
washed with cold water and neutral detergent, then 
left to air dry at room temperature. The thickness of 
the obtained bacterial cellulose was measured using 
calipers and subsequently segmented into rectangu-
lar samples measuring 3.0  cm × 2.0  cm. Before the 
plasma treatment, each sample was placed in a glass 
desiccator with silica gel for 30.0 min.
Plasma treatment

plasma system, described in a previous work by Cor-
rea et  al. (2018) and Guinter et  al. (2019). In such

systems, the sample is placed on a substrate holder 
inside the cathodic cage, while the glow discharge 
plasma is kept outside the cage. The cathodic cage 
and the sample holder are placed inside the vacuum 
chamber, so gas composition and pressure can be 
controlled. This arrangement allows a lower energy 
transfer to the sample, avoiding surface damage, espe-
cially in thermally sensitive materials. Figure 2 shows 
a scheme of a Cathodic Cage Plasma setup. The cage 
was built from a stainless steel 18-gauge mesh with 
1 mm openings. Similarly to a Faraday cage, the elec-
tric field is null inside and, consequently, the plasma 
glow discharge occurs only outside, once the anode 
is the chamber’s walls. Ions from the glow discharge 
can be accelerated through the cathode sheath and 
bombard the mesh wires of the cathode, which are 
negatively biased (through pulsed bias) by an exter-
nal power supply. However, considering the param-
eters used in the present paper, the ions do not have 
enough energy to pass through the openings of the 
cage and, therefore, do not reach the inner space. 
As the ions stay outside, there is no ionic bombard-
ment on the substrate, which remains at a low tem-
perature, enabling the condensation and polymeri-
zation of active species from the plasma, growing a 
film. Only the secondary electrons generated by the 
ion bombardment on the surface of the mesh can 
enter the cage which, consequently, generates a high 
floating potential inside, close to the negative voltage 
applied to the cathode by the external power supply, 
in a way that the space inside is nearly equipotential 
and the electrical field is next to zero. However, neu-
tral chemical species activated in the plasma, such 
as excited molecules and atoms, are not affected by 
the electrical field in the cathodic sheath and can 
move randomly. So, the neutral active species gener-
ated in the glow space plasma can pass through the 
grid’s openings, without being affected by the elec-
tric field. Those chemically activated species can dif-
fuse toward the inner space of the cage and react on 
the substrate’s surface, producing a thin film (e. g. 
polymerization), as illustrated in Fig. 2. In the present 
paper, reactive species were generated in the glow 
plasma space from the precursor gas mixture ­C2H2 / 
­H2 /Ar. The argon is used as a dilution gas because it 
facilitates the discharge maintenance and the increase 
in ionization/dissociation rate in ­CxHy/H2/Ar gas mix-

involved in the plasma technology both as a prod-
uct and as a precursor gas. According to Benedikt 
(2010), plasmas are relatively safe and environmen-
tally friendly, the material content is usually very low,

The discharge was maintained using a Pinna-
cle Plus™ pulsed DC power supply from Advanced 
Energy Inc. It operates at a constant average power 
of 50 W and a frequency of 100  kHz. The reverse 
time (time in which the cage is positively biased) 
was variable (2.0  µs and 4.0  µs) to achieve duty 
cycles of 80% and 60%, respectively. Changing the 
duty cycle parameter enables to achieve polymer 
films of different thicknesses (Guinter et  al. 2019). 
The samples were treated for two different periods 
(5.0 min and 30.0 min) and in two different working 
atmospheres (Ar + ­C2H2 and Ar + ­C2H2 + ­H2). Using 
a Pirani gauge, the base pressure was established at 
1.33 × ­10–2 mBar and the working pressure at 2 mBar 
­(N2 equivalent pressure). For the Ar + ­C2H2 mixture, 
the ­N2 equivalent pressure ratio was 1:1 and, for 
Ar + ­C2H2 + ­H2, it was 1:1:1. The 30-min treatment 
was set by Vilaplana and Cabanes (2012) for cotton 
and polyester, so the 5-min treatment was chosen as 
a counterpoint. Regarding the choice of the work-
ing gases, Ar is an inert gas that can promote physi-
cal modifications on the sample surface (Pascu et al. 
2003), while the acetylene molecules can be disso-
ciated in the plasma, and the fragments lead to the 
formation of a polymer film (Vilaplana and Cabanes 
2012). On the other hand, hydrogen can generate 
active sites, facilitating the insertion of functional 
groups (Macedo et al. 2010), which can be beneficial 
to the formation of the polymer film using acetylene 
as precursor gas.
To analyze the influence of treatment parame-
ters on the wettability of the samples, a Taguchi L4 
Orthogonal Arrange ­(23) (Montgomery 2013) with 
three factors (treatment time, working gas, and duty 
cycle) in two levels was developed (Table  1). For 
each experimental condition, a replica was produced.
Characterization
Bacterial cellulose was qualitatively characterized 
in terms of surface appearance using Field Emission 
Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM-FEG) with a 
JSM-6701F JEOL electron microscope, operated at 
1 kV and a working distance of 4.6 mm.
Sample wettability was determined through inter-
nal contact angle measurements conducted on a 
Ramé-hart Instruments 590 Goniometer, using 2 μL 
drops of deionized water. Each sample, 24  h after 
plasma treatment, had one drop deposited at 5 dif-
ferent non-collinear regions of its surface. For each 
drop, 50 static measurements were taken with a 0.01 s 
interval. The obtained values were calculated using 
DROPimage software from Ramé-Hart Instruments, 
based on static measurements through circle fit-
tings (Akbari and Antonini 2021). The contact angle 
result was the average of the 5 tested regions on the 
sample, which were analyzed for mean and standard 
deviation.
In addition to bacterial cellulose analysis, contact 
angle testing was performed on a sample of polyu-
rethane (PU) synthetic leather, taken from a fashion 
product after five years of use, with the intention of 
generating a wettability comparison data set. Statisti-
cal analysis was conducted on the contact angle data 
using Minitab Statistical Software.

Results and discussion
The obtained bacterial cellulose was 2  mm thick 
immediately after cultivation. This result is lower than 
the expected 2  cm according to the procedure out-
lined by Lima et al. (2021). This difference indicates 
a lower amount of water in the obtained tissue, attrib-
uted to the slower action of microorganisms at lower 
room temperatures and, consequently, reduced water 
absorption during the process. According to Jayaba-
lan et al. (2014), the ideal temperature for the action 
of kombucha SCOBY in the cultivation medium 
ranges between 18 °C and 26 °C. The appearance of 
bubbles indicating fermentation was expected after 
5 days of cultivation in the ideal temperature range, 
but in the course of this work, conducted at room 
temperature between 13 °C and 18 °C, bubbles only 
appeared after 10 days. Nevertheless, after drying, the 
expected appearance and characteristics described in 
the literature for bacterial cellulose (Mehrotra et  al. 
2023; Morrow et al. 2023; Costa et al. 2017; Ng and 
Wand 2015) could be achieved: flexible, rough, and 
slightly transparent.
Wettability
As expected, the untreated bacterial cellulose sam-
ple exhibited the lowest contact angle (33.4°), which 
indicates hydrophilic characteristics, as it is less than 
90° (Fig. 3). The synthetic leather sample taken from 
a used fashion product also exhibited hydrophilic 
characteristics, with a contact angle of 78.6°. Bacte-
rial cellulose exhibits hydrophilic characteristics due 
to its high free surface energy, which is associated 
with the abundant availability of free hydroxyl groups 
(Provin et  al. 2021), resulting in hydrogen bonding 
between its nanofibers (Lee et al. 2011). Therefore, it 
displays varying contact angles depending on the cul-
tivation conditions, typically around 40°, but always 
less than 90° (Table 2).
All plasma treatment conditions on bacterial cellu-
lose resulted in an increased contact angle, indicating 
a reduction in hydrophilic characteristics. Depending 
on the plasma processing conditions, some contact 
angle results approached 100°, indicating hydropho-
bic characteristics (Latthe et al. 2014).
The literature points to various methods to reduce 
the hydrophilicity of bacterial cellulose, including 
the application of plasma, as shown in Table 2. This 
property can be diminished through changes in sur-
face roughness or chemical surface modification 
aimed at decreasing its free energy (Mondal et  al. 
2018). Plasma treatments can induce both effects, 
increasing the roughness of bacterial cellulose with 
treatment duration (Aditya et al. 2022) and lowering 
the free surface energy by reorienting free groups 
(Pertile et  al. 2010). In this study, the highly reac-
tive environment of the plasma probably favored the 
reaction between the free hydroxyl groups and the 
functional groups generated in the plasma from the 
acetylene gas, forming a thin film and reducing the 
free surface energy. The treatment conditions (C1 to 
C4) align with results obtained from traditional wet 
chemical methods that increase the contact angle, 
achieving even greater angles when combined with 
the plasma treatment (Table 2).

Chemical characterization
Figure 6 presents the FTIR spectra for the non-treated 
and optimized samples. The Bacterial Cellulose spec-
trum (BC sample) presents the characteristic bands 
from 3495 ­cm−1 to 3245 ­cm−1 corresponding to O–H

Surface characterization
The surface analysis of the untreated bacterial cel-
lulose reveals the characteristic cellulose fibers of 
bacterial cellulose (Fig.  11a). The analysis of the 
plasma treated bacterial cellulose shows a smoothing 
of the surface due to the growth of a polymer coating 
(Fig. 11b). It also shows that the film is homogeneous 
and continuous, with no large defects on the topog-
raphy, despite its thinness. This result shows that the 
plasma process used in present paper, namely the 
Cathodic Cage Plasma, is a good candidate to pro-
duce polymer films with superior homogeneity.

Conclusions
In response to the need for more sustainable fabric 
alternatives for the fashion industry, this study cul-
tivated bacterial cellulose and plasma-treated it to 
reduce its hydrophilicity. The biofabric surface treat-
ment, through ­C2H2/Ar/H2 plasma in a cathodic cage, 
was proven effective, forming a thin hydrocarbon 
film on the surface of the BC. Results show that the 
Cathodic Cage Plasma treatment turned the surface 
of the BC from hydrophilic to hydrophobic, as the 
static water drop contact angle changed from θ ≈ 30° 
to θ > 90°, besides purifying it without degradation. 
Bacterial cellulose can be made hydrophobic by using 
parameters such as a longer treatment time, a combi-
nation of gases without the addition of hydrogen, and

Considering the effectiveness of plasma treatment 
in improving the hydrophobic behavior in bacterial

the fashion industry. This is particularly significant

skin, and the high hydrophilicity of bacterial cellulose 
poses challenges for hygiene and a greater suscepti-

mitigates the common issues stemming from hydro-

maintaining the material’s biodegradability.

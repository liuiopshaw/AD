A B S T R A C T
The development of efficient, integrated anti-corrosion and anti-biofouling technologies is crucial for ensuring
the long-term operational safety of marine equipment. In this study, a TiO2/GaInS3 Z-scheme heterojunction
photoelectrode was constructed by in situ growth of hexagonal GaInS3 with a filamentous network architecture
on TiO2 nanorods via a two-step hydrothermal method. Under simulated solar irradiation, the composite pho-
toelectrode exhibited excellent photoelectrochemical cathodic protection performance for 304 stainless steel
(304 SS), shifting its open-circuit potential negatively by 301 mV. Simultaneously, it demonstrated broad-
spectrum and highly efficient photocatalytic inactivation (>98 % within 90 min) against multiple microorgan-
isms (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Microcystis aeruginosa). Even after four
cycles of photocatalytic inactivation of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a high antibacterial rate of >99 % was retained.
By comparing the performance of single-function systems (anti-corrosion or anti-biofouling) with that of the
coupled system (anti-corrosion + anti-biofouling), this study revealed a competitive relationship between the
two processes for photogenerated electrons for the first time. This work not only presents a high-performance
material for integrated corrosion and biofouling prevention but also provides an insightful understanding of
the underlying interaction mechanisms, offering new perspectives for the rational design of marine anti-
corrosion and anti-biofouling materials.
1. Introduction
Metals and structural materials employed in marine applications are
subjected to severe corrosion induced by the aggressive oceanic envi-
ronment [1,2]. Seawater, acting as a natural electrolyte rich in salts, is
inherently
corrosive
[3].
Moreover,
microbiologically
influenced
corrosion accounts for approximately 20 % of metal failure cases in
marine settings [4]. Notably, various microorganisms form biofilms on
metal surfaces. These include Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacte-
ria, such as Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia
coli, and cyanobacteria like Microcystis aeruginosa [5–7]. This process
leads to biofouling and thereby accelerates metal degradation [8]. This
corrosive attack, in turn, creates additional sites for microbial coloni-
zation, resulting in a mutually reinforcing cycle of biofouling and
corrosion. Hence, the development of integrated strategies that combine
anti-corrosion with anti-biofouling functionalities is essential for
enhancing the service longevity of marine infrastructures [9,10].
Semiconductors, capable of converting light energy into electrical
energy, demonstrate significant potential in both corrosion protection
and anti-biofouling applications [11–13]. Specifically, under photoex-
citation, semiconductors generate photogenerated electrons (e−) and
holes (h+). If the photogenerated electrons lead to a Fermi energy level
below the metal's self-corrosion potential, these photogenerated elec-
trons will migrate to the metal/electrolyte interface and accumulate on

leading to intracellular fluid leakage and ultimately bacterial demise,
thus achieving effective anti-biofouling [16]. For example, a Z-scheme
heterojunction WO3/Bi2MoO6 photocatalyst, developed by Cheng et al.,
retains highly oxidative holes and strongly reductive electrons, which
generate ⋅OH and ⋅O2
−. This system achieved over 99 % sterilization rate
of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus within 60 min,
demonstrating excellent stability [17]. In another study, Qian et al.
fabricated TiO2/CdZnS/ZnS heterojunction photoelectrodes, which not
only provided superior corrosion protection for 316 L stainless steel
(316 L SS), evidenced by a potential drop of 415 mV, but also signifi-
cantly reduced the colony count of Pseudomonas aeruginosa in bacterial
solutions after a 2 h sterilization test. This dual functionality underscores
the
potential
of
such
photoelectrodes
for
simultaneous
metal
anti-corrosion and anti-biofouling applications [18].
The anti-corrosion process primarily utilizes the photogenerated
electrons produced by the semiconductor. The consumption of holes can
promote the separation of photogenerated electrons and holes, thereby
enhancing the transfer of photogenerated electrons to the metal [19].
However, biofouling mitigation necessitates the direct involvement of
both charge carriers and their derived radicals (⋅OH/⋅O2
−). When a single
semiconductor is concurrently employed for both functions, a critical
question arises: what determines the allocation of photogenerated
electrons and holes? Moreover, what is the interplay between the
anti-corrosion and anti-biofouling actions, and what mechanism ex-
plains this interplay?
TiO2, with a wide bandgap of ~3.2 eV, is limited to ultraviolet light
absorption (constituting ~5 % of solar energy). In contrast, the narrower
bandgap of GaInS3 (~2.2–2.4 eV) significantly extends the absorption
range into the visible region, markedly improving solar energy utiliza-
tion efficiency. Furthermore, the CB position of TiO2 is not negative
enough to convert O2 into ⋅O2
−, and the VB of GaInS3 is not positive
enough to convert H2O into ⋅OH. Therefore, utilizing GaInS3-modified
TiO2 holds promise for constructing a Z-scheme heterojunction system
capable of both converting H2O into ⋅OH and transforming O2 into ⋅O2
−.
This study elucidates the interaction mechanism between corrosion
and fouling protection. Using cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide and
other surfactants, hexagonal GaInS3 was grown in situ on TiO2 to form a
Z-scheme TiO2/GaInS3-UC photoelectrodes coated with filamentous
network structures. According to comparative analyses of anti-corrosion
and anti-biofouling properties in coupled systems (steel + bacteria
coexisting) versus single systems, it was noteworthy that: when acting
within coupled systems, the TiO2/GaInS3-UC heterojunction still enables
photogenerated holes to oxidize H2O into ⋅OH radicals, thereby
participating in the sterilization process, whereas photogenerated elec-
trons partly exert direct anti-corrosion effects by providing PECCP
protection to 304 SS, while another portion reacts with dissolved oxygen
to generate ⋅O2
−, thereby re-engaging in the sterilization process. This
manifests as a competitive phenomenon between anti-corrosion and
anti-biofouling mechanisms vying for photogenerated electrons. Radical
trapping experiments further confirm the dominant reactive species,
with the electrode exhibiting universal microbial inactivation (>98 %
within 90 min) and outstanding cycling stability (>99 % after four cy-
cles). The study not only achieves dual corrosion-biofouling protection
but also fundamentally reveals the competitive interaction mechanism
between these processes in Z-scheme heterojunctions, providing critical
insights for designing multifunctional photoelectrodes in marine
environments.

2.2. Preparation of photoelectrode materials
2.2.1. Synthesis of TiO2 photoelectrodes
A precursor solution was prepared by adding 15 mL of hydrochloric
acid to 15 mL of deionized water under magnetic stirring for 5 min.
Then, 0.15 g of KCl and 0.36 mL of C12H28O4Ti were introduced into the
mixture and stirred for another 30 min to ensure homogeneity. Two
pieces of FTO substrates (10 × 30 × 1.6 mm), with the conductive side
facing up, were placed into a 50 mL polytetrafluoroethylene-lined
(PTFE-lined) autoclave containing the precursor solution. The hydro-
thermal synthesis was conducted at 180 ◦C for 6 h. After the reaction, the
samples were rinsed alternately with 95 % ethanol and deionized water
three times. Finally, the samples were annealed at 450 ◦C for 2 h in a
muffle furnace to obtain the final TiO2 photoelectrodes.
2.2.2. Synthesis of TiO2/GaInS3 photoelectrodes
Aqueous solutions for the secondary growth were prepared by dis-
solving GaCl3 (0.1 mmol), InCl3⋅4H2O (0.1 mmol), a sulfur source
(0.3 mmol), and a surfactant (0.1 mmol) in 35 mL of deionized water
(molar ratio = 1:1:3:1). The as-prepared TiO2 photoelectrodes wwere
then immersed in the solution within a PTFE-lined autoclave for a hy-
drothermal reaction at 180 ◦C for 24 h. The final product, denoted as
TiO2/GaInS3-MN (where M and N represent the sulfur source and sur-
factant, respectively), was obtained after thorough rinsing with 95 %
ethanol and deionized water. To investigate the effect of the sulfur
source on the morphology of GaInS3, thiourea (abbreviated U) and
thioacetamide (abbreviated A) were used, while the surfactant was
maintained as CTAB (abbreviated C), yielding the TiO2/GaInS3-UC and
TiO2/GaInS3-AC photoelectrodes. The role of the surfactant was then
probed by fixing the sulfur source as thiourea (U) and selecting CTAB (C)
or PVP (abbreviated P) as the surfactant, resulting in the TiO2/GaInS3-
UP, TiO2/GaInS3-UC, and TiO2/GaInS3-U (no surfactant) photoelectrodes.
The working area is all 10 mm × 10 mm. The specific process flow di-
agram is shown in Fig. S1.

2.4. Electrochemical and photoelectrochemical tests
All electrochemical and photoelectrochemical measurements in this
study were conducted using an electrochemical workstation (VersaSTAT
3F, AMETEK Trading Co., Ltd., China) in a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution. A
standard three-electrode system was employed, with the photoelectrode
material as the working electrode (WE), a platinum plate as the counter
electrode (CE), and an SCE as the reference electrode (RE). Electro-
chemical impedance spectroscopy measurements were performed over a
frequency range from 105 to 10−2 Hz with an AC voltage amplitude of
10 mV. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were carried out at scan rates
ranging from 20 to 100 mV/s, with an increment of 20 mV/s. Mott-
Schottky analysis was conducted at a fixed frequency of 1000 Hz, with
an AC potential amplitude of 20 mV.
The photoelectrochemical tests include: open circuit potential-time
(OCP-t), photocurrent density-time (i-t), and photo-induced voltammo-
gram (LSV) tests. The OCP-t test is conducted using a two-electrode
system, with 304 SS (10 mm × 10 mm) and a series of photo-
electrodes serving as the WE, and SCE as the RE. The open-circuit and
dark current tests are performed at 200 s intervals, with a total duration
of 5 cycles. The i-t test is carried out in the ZRA mode, with the series of
photoelectrodes connected to the WE, and the 304 SS connected to the
RE and ground wire. The LSV test is conducted using the same standard
three-electrode system, with the test range set from −0.75 V to 0.95 V.

3. Results and discussions
Fig. 1a illustrates the proposed reaction pathways of photogenerated
electrons and holes for the concurrent anti-corrosion and anti-biofouling
processes. Upon photoexcitation, the semiconductor generates photo-
generated electrons and holes pairs. The photogenerated electrons can
migrate directly to the metal substrate, inducing cathodic polarization to
provide corrosion protection (Process I: anti-corrosion). Simultaneously,
these photogenerated electrons may react with dissolved oxygen to form
⋅O2
−(Process II: anti-biofouling), while the holes can oxidize water to
produce ⋅OH (Process III: anti-biofouling). These reactive species (e-, h+,
⋅O2
−, and ⋅OH) collectively contribute to anti-biofouling by disrupting
microbial cell membranes, leading to cell death. Theoretically, Process
III is expected to facilitate Process I, whereas Process II may compete
with Process I for photogenerated electrons. Nevertheless, the practical
relationship between anti-corrosion and anti-biofouling performance
remains an unresolved issue and represents a significant research gap in
this field.

and sterilization [5,6,17,29–32] properties of TiO2/GaInS3-UC in this
study with those reported in previous works. It is observed that in both
the anti-corrosion and anti-biofouling fields, TiO2/GaInS3-UC exhibits

Fig. 2a shows the OCP variations of the prepared TiO2 and a series of
TiO2/GaInS3 samples coupled with 304 SS. The photopotential drop of
pure TiO2 was only 100 mV. After growing GaInS3 on its surface using
different surfactants and sulfur sources, the photopotential drop was
enhanced to varying degrees. Without any surfactant, TiO2/GaInS3-U
exhibited a photopotential drop of 173 mV. When CTAB and PVP were
used as surfactants, the photopotential drop of TiO2/GaInS3-UP and
TiO2/GaInS3-UC reached 208 mV and 301 mV, respectively. This in-
dicates that CTAB facilitates the better synthesis of GaInS3, likely due to
its electrostatic stabilization mechanism promoting reaction transport,
thereby yielding a product with higher crystallinity and compositional
uniformity. In contrast, although PVP can prevent agglomeration, its
steric hindrance effect may inhibit crystal growth. Using CTAB as the
surfactant, results with different sulfur sources suggest that thiourea is
more suitable than thioacetamide for supplying sulfur in GaInS3 for-
mation, which may be attributed to the faster release of S2−from thio-
urea. The OCP curve of GaInS3-UC (Fig. S3) shows a minimal
photopotential drop likely due to its narrow bandgap, leading to rapid
recombination of photocarriers. The changes in photocurrent density
shown in Fig. 2b and S4 also confirm that the TiO2/GaInS3-UC prepared
with CTAB as the surfactant and TU as the sulfur source can provide
more photogenerated electrons for 304 SS. The variations in both OCP-t
and i-t demonstrate that coupling with GaInS3 significantly enhances the
anti-corrosion performance of TiO2. This enhancement is attributed to
the formation of a heterojunction between the two materials, which
promotes the effective separation of photocarriers, allowing more pho-
togenerated electrons to participate in the anti-corrosion process.
Metallographic images (Fig. S5) of 304 SS immersed in 3.5 wt% NaCl for
12 h, with and without the PECCP provided by TiO2/GaInS3-UC, reveal
that the presence of the PECCP effectively prevents 304 SS corrosion.
The anti-biofouling performance of the series of photoelectrodes was
evaluated through photocatalytic sterilization experiments against
Pseudomonas aeruginosa under simulated sunlight. The sterilization rates
are presented in Fig. 2c, with corresponding plate images shown in
Fig. S6. After 90 min of illumination, TiO2/GaInS3-UC exhibited superior
sterilization efficiency (100 %) compared to TiO2 (64 %) and GaInS3-AC
(89 %), which can be attributed to its enhanced charge carrier separa-
tion efficiency and broader light absorption range. Control experiments
involving
illumination-only
and
photoelectrode-only
conditions
(Fig. S7) showed no significant reduction in bacterial colonies, ruling
out any substantial antibacterial effect from light or the material alone
[33]. Cyclic sterilization tests conducted with TiO2/GaInS3-UC (Fig. 2d
and S8) demonstrated excellent stability, with the inactivation rate
remaining as high as 99.83 % even after the fourth cycle. To further
assess its universality, the photoelectrodes were tested against Staphy-
lococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Microcystis aeruginosa. As shown in
Fig. 2e and S9, after 90 min of irradiation, the inactivation rates reached
100 % for Staphylococcus aureus and 99.10 % for Escherichia coli, while a
rate of 98.96 % was achieved for Microcystis aeruginosa after 120 min,
confirming its broad-spectrum antimicrobial and algal inhibition char-
acteristics. To identify the primary ROS involved in the photocatalytic
antibacterial process, scavenger experiments were performed using
Na2C2O4, IPA, and TEMPOL to trap holes, ⋅OH and ⋅O2
−, respectively.

Fungal organic compounds, including extracellular organic matter
(EOM) and intracellular organic matter (IOM), not only accelerate
biofouling but also contribute to localized corrosion via microbial
metabolic activities, thereby adversely affecting marine ecosystems.
Changes in the DOM were thus monitored using 3D-EEM spectroscopy
(Fig. S12). In Fig. S12a and b, two peaks located at 270/325 nm (Peak A)
and 220/310 nm (Peak B) were assigned to aromatic protein-like sub-
stances I and certain soluble microbial products, respectively. After
90 min of light irradiation, the intensities of Peaks A and B decreased
significantly, indicating that the TiO2/GaInS3-UC photoelectrode not
only inactivates bacterial cells but also effectively degrades EOM. As
shown in Fig. S12c and d, the EEM results of IOM before illumination
exhibited Peak A (λEx/λEm = 280/310 nm) and Peak B (λEx/λEm = 225/
310 nm), corresponding to aromatic proteins and soluble microbial
byproducts, respectively. After 90 min of irradiation, both peaks showed
a substantial reduction in intensity, likely attributable to the leakage of
intracellular protein-like materials caused by microbial cell death and
loss of membrane integrity.
The microstructures of the prepared photoelectrodes were charac-
terized using SEM and TEM, as summarized in Fig. 3. Fig. 3a clearly
displays the typical nanorod morphology of TiO2. In the absence of a
surfactant, GaInS3-U consists mainly of bulk hexagonal and needle-like
crystals (Fig. 3b). The introduction of PVP or CTAB as surfactants
leads to the formation of a wrinkled network structure for GaInS3-UP
(Fig. 3c) and a fibrous network for GaInS3-UC (Fig. 3d), attributed to
surfactant-induced anisotropic crystal growth via selective adsorption.
By contrast, GaInS3-AC exhibits a burr-like morphology (Fig. 3e). Among
these, the fibrous architecture of GaInS3-UC likely favors enhanced light
absorption by the underlying TiO2 and improved electrolyte accessi-
bility. This facilitates an increase in the reactive surface area, thereby
promoting radical generation. Furthermore, this unique architecture
may behave as a “physical trap” to capture microorganisms. EDS and
elemental mapping of TiO2/GaInS3-UC (Figs. S13 and S14) confirm the
presence and homogeneous distribution of Ti, O, Ga, In, and S. High-
resolution TEM imaging (Fig. 3f) reveals lattice spacings of 0.254 nm
and 0.231 nm in the TiO2 region, corresponding to the (012) and (220)
planes of orthorhombic TiO2, respectively. The lattice fringes of
0.199 nm in region C are assigned to the (110) plane of hexagonal
GaInS3. These HRTEM results confirm intimate contact between the two
components in the TiO2/GaInS3-UC heterostructure, which is expected to
facilitate efficient interfacial charge transfer.

Fig. 6 schematically illustrates the charge transfer mechanism within
the TiO2/GaInS3 heterojunction and its integrated anti-corrosion and
anti-biofouling functionality. The configuration is composed of two
semiconductors, TiO2 and GaInS3, with complementary band structures.
By establishing an energy step between their CB and VB, a Z-scheme-like
electron transfer pathway is constructed. The key to the Z-scheme
structure lies in the efficiency of interface reorganization of the inter-
mediate band and the ability to suppress interface recombination. The
CB electrons of the low-band semiconductor (TiO2) will undergo
recombination and annihilation with the VB holes of the low-band
semiconductor (GaInS3). This process ensures that electrons within the
Z-scheme hierarchical junction remain confined to the CB of GaInS3,
while holes remain confined to the VB of TiO2. In essence, the built-in
electric field at the TiO2/GaInS3 interface, in conjunction with this
distinctive charge migration route, synergistically enhances the sepa-
ration of photogenerated electrons and holes. Furthermore, the Z-
scheme heterojunction preserves the strong redox potential of the

4. Conclusion
This work successfully synthesized a TiO2/GaInS3-UC photoelectrode
with a filamentous network structure and systematically evaluated its
dual functionality in PECCP and photocatalytic sterilization/algaecide.
The TiO2/GaInS3-UC photoelectrode provides 301 mV of cathodic pro-
tection for 304 SS while achieving a sterilization and algae inactivation
efficiency greater than 98 % within 90 min, and maintaining excellent
stability. Comparative experiments in individual and coupled systems
revealed, for the first time, a competitive relationship for photo-
generated electrons between the anti-corrosion and anti-biofouling
processes, resulting in a slight decrease in anti-corrosion and anti-
biofouling performance compared to a single system. The Z-scheme
charge transfer mechanism between TiO2 and GaInS3 not only effec-
tively promotes the separation and migration of photogenerated elec-
trons and holes but also preserves charge carriers with strong redox
potentials. This mechanism is identified as the underlying origin of the
high anti-corrosion and anti-biofouling performance.

A B S T R A C T
Bacterial prostatitis represents a specific form of prostatitis, primarily resulting from bacterial infection and
significantly impairing the life quality of patients. In this paper, we respond to the inability of conventional drugs
to simultaneously address both bacterial infection and oxidative stress in the treatment of prostatitis by designing
a multifunctional nanoparticle, called QM (Cu) NPs, with dual functionality. QM (Cu) NPs have the capacity to
generate reactive oxygen radicals to eradicate bacteria under the influence of laser irradiation. Additionally, they
are capable of rapidly scavenging the surplus free radicals, thereby restoring the intracellular redox homeostasis
in the absence of laser illumination. A comprehensive characterization of QM (Cu) NPs was conducted, followed
by an in-depth analysis of their effects on cells. The therapeutic efficacy of QM (Cu) NPs in multimodal treating
bacterial prostatitis was then demonstrated. Furthermore, the outcomes of transcriptomic and molecular biology
experiments indicated that QM (Cu) NPs markedly regulate the NF-κB p65 and Nrf2-Keap1 signaling pathways,
thereby influencing inflammatory and oxidative stress processes. In conclusion, QM (Cu) NPs simultaneously
addressed the dual challenges of antibacterial and antioxidant properties, thereby underscoring their potential
clinical applications in the treatment of bacterial prostatitis.
1. Introduction
Prostatitis is the most prevalent disease in male urology, with a
global prevalence of approximately 10 %. This condition has a signifi-
cant impact on the health and well-being of adult males [1]. Bacterial
prostatitis (BP) represents a prevalent form of prostatitis, accounting for
approximately 20 % of all cases [2]. BP is primarily caused by bacterial
infections, predominantly of Escherichia coli (E. coli), which can result in
pelvic discomfort, altered urination patterns, sexual dysfunction,
decreased fertility, headaches, depression, and a multitude of other
clinical symptoms that collectively impair the patient’s quality of life
[3]. It has been demonstrated that even long-term untreated BP may be
associated with prostate cancer [4]. Currently, antibiotic therapy is the
gold standard for the clinical treatment of BP, with fluoroquinolones
being the preferred drugs. However, the effectiveness of these antibi-
otics is waning due to the rise of multi-drug resistant bacteria [5].
Therefore, it is imperative to identify novel therapeutic modalities that
can supplant antibiotics in the management of prostatitis. In recent
years, research related to photodynamic therapy has surged, positioning
it as a promising antimicrobial strategy [6–8]. The fundamental prin-
ciple of photodynamic therapy is the irradiation of a photosensitizer
with light, which triggers the production of reactive oxygen radicals

approach is employed in vivo, it is crucial to consider the potential
damage to normal cells resulting from ROS generation. Furthermore,
oxidative stress dysregulation caused by bacterial infection, which in-
duces a significant increase in ROS generation in prostate tissue cells,
can also exacerbate prostatitis [10]. It is therefore essential to develop
methods for the rapid removal of ROS generated by photosensitizers
following the administration of antimicrobials, as well as for the elimi-
nation of ROS generated by cellular damage, to mitigate oxidative stress.
The unique physicochemical characteristics of two-dimensional (2D)
materials have prompted considerable interest and investigation among
researchers across a range of disciplines. The exceptional characteristics
of these materials include a high surface-to-volume ratio, minimal
thickness, and noteworthy mechanical and catalytic properties [11,12].
As a novel two-dimensional nanomaterial, MXene has garnered exten-
sive attention and is recognized as a promising candidate for developing
a new class of antibacterial agents [13]. Some studies have demon-
strated that MXene exerts a pronounced inhibitory effect on the growth
of both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria [14]. It may eradi-
cate bacteria through several mechanisms, including the enhancement
of membrane permeability, the reduction of metabolic activity, and the
disruption of DNA [15]. However, the direct application of MXene for
sterilization
frequently
necessitates
elevated
concentrations
[16],
which, in conjunction with the inadequate dispersion of MXene in
aqueous solutions [17], gives rise to concerns about its biosafety for in
vivo applications. It is fortunate that research has demonstrated that
Ti3C2 [18–20] MXene is capable of functioning as a photosensitizer with
photodynamic effects. Irradiation with near-infrared light at 808 nm
results in the production of ROS, including single-linear oxygen and
hydroxyl radicals, in Ti3C2 MXene, thereby enhancing its antimicrobial
capacity. Furthermore, MXene, as a nano-enzyme, exhibits remarkable
ROS scavenging capabilities in the absence of laser irradiation [21],
effectively mitigating ROS accumulation. However, the photodynamic
effect of MXene remains insufficient, necessitating modification to
enhance electron transfer efficiency and augment its capacity to
generate ROS, thereby achieving an optimal bactericidal outcome. In
recent decades, nano-delivery systems have been developed for a
number of significant advantages, including increased bioavailability,
reduced side effects, enhanced targeting capabilities, and improved drug
performance [22]. There is also growing interest in leveraging nano-
technology for the diagnosis and treatment of inflammatory diseases
[23]. Extensive research has explored on the potential of nanomedicines
for prostate-related treatments, with some nanomedicines having been
tested in clinical trials [24]. It is therefore evident that the loading of
MXene with a nano-delivery system presents a promising strategy to
overcome its limitations while harnessing the inherent advantages of
MXene.
In this article, we present the construction of a multifunctional nano-
delivery system capable of delivering Ti3C2 MXene with two-sided
functionality for the multimodal treatment of BP. Ti3C2 MXene ex-
hibits photodynamic capacity, enabling precise and on-demand anti-
microbial activity. Additionally, it displays antioxidant capacity,
facilitating the scavenging of ROS accumulation and the regulation of
redox. The initial step involved the synthesis of quercetin copper com-
plexes (Que-Cu), which were subsequently grafted onto chondroitin
sulfate (Chs) to form the QC@Chs composite. Then M@C was obtained
through the use of carboxylated chitosan (CCS)-loaded Ti3C2 MXene,
which enhanced the aqueous dispersibility of Ti3C2 MXene. Ultimately,
QC@Chs and M@C were assembled independently to create the QM
(Cu) NPs drug delivery system. In this nanoparticle, Cu2+ was intro-
duced to enhance the antimicrobial capacity [25,26] and improve the
photodynamic properties of Ti3C2 MXene. Quercetin acted as a metal ion
carrier and exerted anti-inflammatory effects [27–29], while Chs, as a
water-soluble macromolecule [30,31], enhanced the water solubility of
the quercetin-copper complexes and enabled the nanoparticles to target
the CD44 receptor [32]. In the design of the multimodal nanoparticles,
an innovative approach was employed that emphasized both the
photodynamic properties and enzyme-like activity of Ti3C2 MXene.
Moreover, a natural compound, Que, was incorporated into the nano-
particles to facilitate the loading of metal ions and enhance electron
transfer efficiency. This resulted in a notable enhancement of Ti3C2
MXene’s capacity to facilitate the generation of ROS when irradiated by
laser. Notably, these dual properties align well with the therapeutic
needs of BP. During treatment, the laser is initially employed to rapidly
generate ROS, to sterilize the bacteria. This is followed by the cessation
of laser exposure, which serves to remove ROS and combat inflamma-
tion in a step-by-step manner. A series of systematic experiments were
conducted
to
demonstrate
the
multifunctional
properties,
multi-
modality, antimicrobial activity, protection of cells from oxidative
damage, and modulation of macrophage and inflammation-related
pathways. These experiments provide compelling evidence that nano-
particles possess multifunctional capabilities and can be employed in
multimodal treatments for BP (see Scheme 1).
2. Results and discussion
1 Synthesis and Characterization of QM (Cu) NPs.
The low water solubility of quercetin and its metal ion complexes
represents a significant obstacle to their utilization, and it is therefore
essential to develop methods to improve their water solubility. The
quercetin-copper complex was first synthesized and grafted onto chon-
droitin sulfate in Fig. 1A, which greatly improved the poor water solu-
bility of quercetin and quercetin-copper complex. As shown in Fig. 1B,
both Que and Que-Cu exhibit low solubility in water, and both manifest
as turbid liquids. Following an extended period of standing, the insol-
uble matter settles to the base of the bottle. However, the excellent water
solubility of QC@Chs ensures that there is no significant change in the
color of the supernat before and after standing. After grafting onto
chondroitin sulfate, the water solubility of the quercetin-copper com-
plex could be improved more than 30-fold in Fig. 1C. Comparing the UV
absorption spectra of quercetin and the complexes in Fig. 1D, the
maximum absorption wavelength of quercetin was red-shifted after
coordination with metal ions, which might be related to the formation of
a six-membered ring after the coordination of metal ions to change the
light-absorbing group of quercetin. Besides the FT-IR spectra validated
the structure of Que, Que-Cu, QC@Chs, and Chs. A red shift of the –OH
band was observed concomitantly after the addition of Cu2+ from 3381
(Que) to 3254 (Que-Cu) cm−1, due to the coordination between Cu2+
and –OH. The absorption peak at 1156 cm−1 (Que-Cu in Fig. 1E) proved
the formation of an ester bond corroborating that Que-Cu was success-
fully grafted onto Chs to some extent. The NMR spectra of QC@Chs and
Chs demonstrated the difference in chemical structure more accurately.
The peaks at 6.7 and 7.8 ppm assigned to the ring of Que weakened
distinctly, and the peak at 1.8 ppm that was attributed to the protons of
–COCH3 was present, which meant the successful cross-linking of Que-
Cu with Chs (Fig. S1). And the grafting rate of Que-Cu was about 25
% calculated by integrating the peak area.
Then the Ti3C2 MXene was loaded onto carboxylated chitosan (CCS),
yielding M@C. The observed shift of the hydroxyl peak in the IR spec-
trum provides evidence for the presence of hydrogen bonding, as shown
in Fig. 1F. After that the complex M@C with QC@Chs was prepared to
form QM (Cu) nanoparticles. The observation of QC@Chs peaks in the
FTIR spectra of QM (Cu) NPs in Fig. 1G, in comparison to M@C, in-
dicates that the synthesis of the nanoparticles was successful. X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was then used to determine the
elemental components in QM (Cu) NPs. As the full scan spectrum dis-
played (Fig. 1H) [33], mainly the elements C, N, O, S, and Cu were
present in QM (Cu) NPs, but due to low Ti content, elemental peaks are
not visible. The Ti 2p fine spectra showed the Ti 2p3/2 and Ti 2p1/2
peaks at 453, 459, and 463 eV (Figs. 1I and S2), which is evidence of the
presence of Ti3C2 MXene in the nanoparticles. Separately, the Cu 2p and

2. ROS/RNS-Scavenging Activities of QM (Cu) NPs.
Oxidative stress and an overactive inflammatory response could
result from the imbalance between the generation and elimination of
ROS/RNS. Given this, the ability of QM (Cu) NPs to be scavengers of
excess reactive oxygen species was first investigated. The RNS scav-
enging capability was evaluated by DPPH and ABTS assay, where the
concentration of nanoparticles was calculated from the concentration of
Ti3C2 MXene. More than 75 % of ABTS, DPPH and ONOO−were scav-
enged by Ti3C2 MXene in Figures S12~14. As shown in Fig. 2A and B
and S15, when the amount of Ti3C2 MXene contained in the QM (Cu)
NPs was 80 μg/mL, it was also possible to eliminate more than 75 % of
the ABTS, DPPH and ONOO−. Apart from RNS, the ROS scavenging
capability was evaluated in Fig. 2C–2E and S16~18. PTIO, H2O2, O2
•−,
and ⋅OH, could be effectively eliminated by Ti3C2 MXene and QM (Cu)
NPs containing the same concentration of Ti3C2 MXene. Subsequently,
the H2O2-induced oxidative stress model in RAW264.7 cells was used to
evaluate the intracellular ROS scavenging capacity of QM (Cu) NPs.
Briefly, RAW264.7 cells were cultured with H2O2 for 12 h before adding
QM (Cu) NPs for another 12 h. Cell survival decreased steadily in the
H2O2 group, while QM (Cu) NPs treatment effectively protected cells
from oxidative damage (Fig. 2H). DCFH-DA fluorescent dye was used as
a sensitive ROS detector to stain intracellular ROS in Fig. 2I. The
strongest fluorescence, indicating the generation of a tremendous
amount of ROS, was observed in the H2O2 group. However, similar to
the control group, extremely weak green fluorescence was found in the
QM (Cu) NPs groups. Similar results were also seen in the live/dead
staining in Fig. 2J. These results suggest that QM (Cu) NPs are effective
in reducing intracellular ROS, attenuating oxidative stress, and thus
providing protection to the cells.
3 The On-demand Controlled Generation of ROS and Antibacterial
Activities of QM (Cu) NPs.
In the preceding section, we demonstrated the capacity of the QM
(Cu) NPs to scavenge free radicals. To ascertain the multifunctional
properties of the QM (Cu) NPs, we evaluated their ability to generate
free radicals in vitro. To confirm that QM (Cu) NPs possessed two
opposing sides in ROS regulation, the capacity of Ti3C2 MXene,
QC@Chs, and nanoparticles to generate ROS under laser irradiation was
evaluated through the use of DBPF as a probe. DPBF is susceptible to
oxidation when exposed to ROS, which subsequently its absorbance will
decrease near 410 nm [35]. We exploited this property by co-incubating
a variety of materials with DPBF in either darkness or light (1.5 W/cm2,
808 nm) and then maintaining them for 10 min. This allowed us to
observe the generation of ROS from the nanoparticles. As illustrated in
Fig. 3B, DPBF does not exhibit a decrease in absorbance under 808 nm
light conditions. Additionally, Ti3C2 MXene and nanoparticles demon-
strate an equivalent lack of absorbance decrease under dark conditions
(Figs. S19 and S20). This evidence substantiates the hypothesis that
neither DPBF nor Ti3C2 MXene and nanoparticles generate spontaneous
free radicals. Similarly, the addition of QC@Chs resulted in a minimal
decrease in the absorbance of DPBF when illuminated with 808 nm light
(Fig. S21), indicating a negligible production of free radicals. However,
the addition of Ti3C2 MXene and QM (Cu) NPs resulted in a notable
decline in the absorbance value of DPBF at 410 nm over time following
light exposure in Fig. 3C and D. This observation suggests that the
decomposition of DPBF occurred during this process. The decrease in
absorbance of DPBF treated with QM (Cu) NPs was greater than that of
Ti3C2 MXene, indicating that QM (Cu) NPs are more effective at pro-
ducing free radicals than Ti3C2 MXene. The aforementioned phenome-
non can be attributed to the fact that Ti3C2 MXene undergoes an internal
electron transfer process in response to light exposure. This transfer
results in the combination of electrons with oxygen, leading to the
generation of a significant number of free radicals. The introduction of
copper ions into the QM (Cu) NPs may further enhance this process by
promoting the electron transfer of Ti3C2 MXene, thereby increasing the
production of free radicals. The generation of ROS following laser
penetration into the tissue was also investigated. As illustrated in
Fig. S22, the QM (Cu) NPs retained the capacity to generate ROS
following laser irradiation across the skin and muscle tissue, a finding
that facilitated subsequent animal experimentation. Furthermore, the
generation of ROS was identified through the utilization of the fluo-
rescent probe DCFH-DA. As shown in Fig. 3E, the production of ROS by
Ti3C2 MXene and QM (Cu) NPs demonstrated a gradual increase in
correlation with the prolongation of 808 nm light exposure. The rate of
ROS generation by QM (Cu) NPs exceeds that observed for Ti3C2 MXene
under these conditions. By conducting experiments in which the light
source was alternately activated and deactivated in Fig. 3F, we discov-
ered that the ROS generated by Ti3C2 MXene and QM (Cu) NPs in the
presence of light for 10 min could be entirely eliminated by both in the
absence of light for the same duration. Furthermore, the rate of ROS
scavenging was observed to exceed the rate of production. The property
of maintaining a stable level of ROS generated by QM (Cu) NPs is ach-
ieved by controlling the light source, thus preventing the accumulation
of ROS and preventing damage to cells. The aforementioned results
substantiated the multifunctional nature of the nanoparticles, namely
that the light source can be utilized to regulate the production or scav-
enging of ROS. This enables the QM (Cu) NPs to scavenge the free
radicals generated by the nanoparticles themselves and by tissue cells
due to oxidative stress. This process facilitates the sterilization of the
free radicals and mitigates the damage inflicted upon the tissue cells.
The capacity to regulate ROS production or removal via the light source
enables the QM (Cu) NPs to more effectively respond to BP. During the
pre-disease phase, bactericidal ROS are generated on demand through
laser irradiation, while the light source is deactivated in the post-disease
phase to facilitate continuous ROS removal.

4. Biocompatibility of QM (Cu) NPs
The biocompatibility of nanoparticles is a key determinant of their
feasibility for in vivo application. Consequently, a comprehensive
investigation into the toxicity of nanoparticles has been conducted. It
should be noted that the combination of QM (Cu) NPs and laser treat-
ment involves the addition of nanoparticles, followed by laser irradia-
tion for 10 min, and then the cessation of the laser and incubation for a
further 24 or 48 h in Fig. 4A. First, after treatment with QM (Cu) NPs +
laser or different concentrations of QM (Cu) NPs, fluorescent staining of
live/dead cells was conducted to verify the cytocompatibility of the QM
(Cu) NPs. The CLSM images showed that both the L929 cells and the
RAW264.7 cells treated with QM (Cu) NPs were fluorescent in green
(live cells) in Figs. 4B and S30. It is noteworthy that nanoparticles +
laser-treated cells demonstrated morphological and numeric character-
istics indistinguishable from those of their unexposed counterparts,
compared to the considerable number of cells that perished following
conventional photodynamic treatment. In addition, the cell viability of
L929 and RAW264.7 cells was assessed by MTT assay. In Figs. 4C and
S31, the cell viability of both L929 and RAW264.7 cells exceeded 80 %
after treatment, indicating that the QM (Cu) NPs were not cytotoxic. The
findings indicate that neither QM (Cu) NPs nor QM (Cu) NPs in com-
bination with light treatment are significantly toxic to cells, and QM
(Cu) NPs have been demonstrated to scavenge free radicals and protect
cells in the absence of light.
Furthermore, the hemocompatibility of QM (Cu) NPs was validated
through hemolysis testing and blood biochemical analysis [39]. In
Fig. 4D, following the administration of QC@Chs, M@C, QM (Cu) NPs,
and QM (Cu) NPs + Laser for 3 h, the hemolysis ratio of the erythrocyte
suspension was found to be below 5 %, which demonstrated that QM
(Cu) NPs can be injected for the treatment of bacterial prostatitis. The
results of the blood biochemistry tests revealed no statistically signifi-
cant discrepancies between the groups in Fig. 4E. To further substantiate
the organ toxicity of QM (Cu) NPs, H&E staining and Masson staining
were conducted on major organs of Sprague-Dawley rats 30 days after
intravenous administration of QM (Cu) NPs. As illustrated in Figs. 4F
and S32, no notable physiological distinctions were observed between
the treatment group and the Control group. In the sections of the
treatment group, cardiomyocytes were well aligned without evidence of
inflammation, hepatic sinusoids were not hypertrophied or congested,
splenic tissues exhibited normal white and red pulp, lung tissues were
free of alveolar dilatation and collapse, and normal tubules and
glomeruli could be observed in renal tissues [40]. The histopathological
examinations (H&E and Masson staining) of major organs (heart, liver,
spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were performed in rats at 30 days
post-treatment with QM (Cu) NPs with laser irradiation. No significant
pathological abnormalities, necrosis, or inflammatory infiltrates were
observed in any organ (Fig. S33). This indicates minimal systemic
toxicity under both therapeutic and long-term conditions. The findings
suggest that QM (Cu) NPs are biocompatible and that QM (Cu) NPs and
their metabolites do not exert a toxic effect on tissues and organs.
5. Targeting Capacity of QM (Cu) NPs
Targeting is a fundamental prerequisite for the effective action of QM
(Cu) NPs. Glycosaminoglycan macromolecules [41,42], such as chon-
droitin sulfate, have been demonstrated to bind specifically to the CD44
receptor, which provided QM (Cu) NPs with the ability to target. As
evidenced by the existing literature [43], it has been demonstrated that
inflammatory macrophages exhibit an overexpression of the CD44 re-
ceptor. In order to model inflammatory macrophages, the RAW264.7
cells were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Moreover, chondroitin
sulfate and hyaluronic acid were employed as competitors for QM (Cu)
NPs that bind to the CD44 receptor. The experimental design is illus-
trated in Figs. 5A and S34. As shown in Fig. 5B and C, the LPS-treated
group exhibited a greater capacity to take up green fluorescence (FIT-
C-labeled nanoparticles) than normal macrophages. Nevertheless, the
green fluorescence was significantly reduced in the chondroitin sulfate
or hyaluronic acid-pretreated group. The results indicate that inflam-
matory macrophages can internalize QM (Cu) NPs via CD44-mediated
endocytosis. The introduction of competing agents can facilitate this
process. This capacity to selectively interact with inflammatory cells
facilitates the targeted aggregation of QM (Cu) NPs at sites of inflam-
mation within living organisms. It is evident that flow cytometry ex-
periments are capable of yielding more precise quantitative results in
Fig. 5D and E. The results of the PCR experiments presented in Fig. S35
demonstrate a significant increase in CD44 expression at the prostate
site under inflammatory conditions. To further investigate the targeting
ability of nanoparticles in vivo, QM (Cu) NPs were labeled by the fluo-
rescent dye Cy7 and injected into rats. The distribution of nanoparticles
in rats was investigated using in vivo imaging experiments. The repre-
sentative fluorescence images of rats and the major rat organs including
the prostate, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney are shown in Fig. 5F.
A rapid aggregation of the QM (Cu) NPs at the prostate site was observed
within 24 h of intravenous injection. The prostates exhibited strong
fluorescence, reaching a peak at the sixth hour. The fluorescence in-
tensity of other organs was weaker than that of the prostate, proving the
targeting effect of QM (Cu) NPs on prostate inflammation. As illustrated
in Fig. S36, the injection of Cy7 into rats revealed a markedly diminished
fluorescence at the prostate in the sixth hour, substantiating the targeted
delivery capability of QM (Cu) NPs. The observed fluorescence increase
in the kidney area may be attributed to the excretion of nanoparticles
through the renal system. Additionally, the content of Ti elements in all
major organs of rats was examined by ICP-MS (Fig. 5G), and it was found
that Ti elements were highly enriched in the prostate. Subsequent
testing of the Ti levels in Fig. S37, conducted 14 days after administra-
tion, revealed a significant reduction in levels across all major organs.
These levels did not differ significantly from those observed in the
control group, suggesting that the nanoparticles had been effectively
eliminated. The findings indicate that QM (Cu) NPs can be directed to
the site of prostate inflammation via chondroitin sulfate-specific binding
to the CD44 receptor, which is highly expressed by inflammatory
macrophages.

7 In Vivo Anti-BP Effects and Underlying Mechanism
Bacterial prostatitis is primarily attributable to an infection with E.
coli. Accordingly, as documented in the literature [44], E. coli was
introduced into the prostate region of rats to establish a bacterial pros-
tatitis model. The therapeutic effects of QM (Cu) NPs were evaluated. As
shown in Fig. 6A, the administration of drugs was initiated one day
following the successful construction of the model, and the adminis-
tration of drugs was conducted via intravenous infusion every two days.
Quinolones are frequently employed in clinical settings to address bac-
terial prostatitis. Therefore, we selected levofloxacin as the positive drug
for bacterial prostatitis. Then the observation of treatment outcomes
seven days after the initiation of treatment and the collection of tissue
samples for subsequent testing were conducted. The images of the
prostate and the organ indices of the various groups were presented in
Fig. 6B–D. Upon opening the abdominal cavity, the prostate in the model
group exhibited severe enlargement and hardening. Additionally, the
prostate index was found to be significantly elevated in the model group.
In contrast, although the QM (Cu) NPs and levofloxacin groups
demonstrated a significant reduction in prostate enlargement, a small
amount of sclerosis remained. In the QM (Cu) NPs + Laser group, the
prostate exhibited a near-normal level of restoration. Furthermore, the
photographic images and organ index of the spleen and thymus are
presented in Figs. S38, 6E, and 6F. Obviously, the model group exhibited
splenomegaly and thymus atrophy, which indicates that a severe in-
flammatory response occurred in the rats. The treatment groups
exhibited a range of recovery outcomes, with the QM (Cu) NPs + Laser
group demonstrating the most notable improvement, with the spleen
and thymus nearly returning to normal levels. The prostate tissues of rats
were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson staining, and
the results are presented in Fig. 6G. The prostate tissues of rats in the
model group exhibited notable inflammatory cell infiltration and
destruction of glandular tissues, whereas the glands of rats in the
administered group demonstrated protection, and the prostate tissues of
rats in the QM (Cu) NPs + Laser group exhibited no discernible abnor-
malities. In Fig. 6H, SEM observations of the prostate tissue of rats
revealed the presence of E. coli in the model group, while no evidence of
this bacterium was found in the control and treatment groups. The
expression levels of inflammatory factors and oxidative stress indicators
were examined using ELISA experiments. Following treatment, there
was a notable increase in the expression of anti-inflammatory factors
(IL-10 and IL-4) in Fig. 6I and J, while pro-inflammatory factors (IL-1β,
TNF-α, and IL-17A) exhibited a contrasting trend in Fig. 6K–M. The
expression of macrophage chemokines was also found to decrease
following treatment in Fig. 6N–S39, and S40, which resulted in a
reduction in the inflammatory response. The findings demonstrate that
the QM (Cu) NPs + Laser treatment effectively attenuates the inflam-
matory response in rats with prostatitis and safeguards prostate tissue
from damage. The results of in vivo antibacterial evaluations demon-
strated significant bacterial load reduction in prostate tissues following
nanoparticle-mediated photodynamic therapy in Fig. S41.
To investigate the underlying mechanisms of QM (Cu) NPs therapy
for prostatitis, we conducted a transcriptomic analysis to examine and
compare differential gene expression among the three groups. Principal
component analysis (PCA) demonstrated that the gene expression pro-

multiple genes encoding cytokines associated with the NF-κB signaling

However, following the treatment, the expression was found to be
decreased. The gene expression of CD44 was also consistent with the
previous description, which indicated a significant upregulation during
the onset of inflammation. This allowed the QM (Cu) NPs to be targeted
to the inflammation. The results of the representative genes in the
clustered heat map in Fig. 7F were also consistent with those in the
volcano map. Subsequently, we conducted an enrichment analysis of the

collectively indicate that QM (Cu) NPs may modulate the inflammatory
response of BP by regulating the expression of inflammatory factors
through the NF-κB signaling pathway. Additionally, they may influence
the redox response and thus the process of BP by acting on the oxidative
stress-related pathway.
To further validate the above conjecture, we conducted molecular
biology experiments to elucidate the therapeutic mechanism. As shown
in Fig. 8B and C, immunofluorescence staining revealed a notable

pathogenesis of inflammation, and the attenuation of oxidative stress
has been shown to promote recovery from inflammatory diseases. It has
been demonstrated that the Nrf2-Keap1 pathway exerts a significant
influence on oxidative stress in prostatitis. Furthermore, the modulation

3. Conclusion
In conclusion, a Ti3C2 MXene-based multifunctional nanoparticle
was designed and fabricated for the multimodal treatment of bacterial
prostatitis. The QM (Cu) NPs demonstrated both ROS-generating
bactericidal properties under laser irradiation and rapid scavenging of
excess ROS and RNS in the absence of laser irradiation. The catalytic
nature of Ti3C2 MXene was identified as the underlying mechanism.
Furthermore,

markedly enhance the ability of Ti3C2 MXene to generate ROS. This dual
functionality enables QM (Cu) NPs to safeguard cells by mitigating
oxidative stress subsequent to the on-demand eradication of bacteria at
the prostate site. In vitro and in vivo toxicity assays have demonstrated
that QM (Cu) NPs exhibit favorable biosafety. It is noteworthy that QM
(Cu) NPs exhibited favorable therapeutic outcomes in bacterial prosta-
titis and were also efficacious in non-bacterial prostatitis. The thera-
peutic mechanism of QM (Cu) NPs was investigated, and it was
determined that the NF-κB p65 and Nrf2-Keap1 signaling pathways play
a significant role in the pathogenesis of bacterial prostatitis. The QM
(Cu) NPs significantly modulated both pathways, thereby alleviating
inflammation and oxidative stress levels. This innovative nano-delivery
system provides an available and promising strategy for the treatment of
bacterial prostatitis.

4.1. Materials
Chondroitin sulfate (Chs), quercetin (Que), copper (II) chloride
(CuCl2), N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochlo-

4.2. Synthesis of QC@Chs
The synthesis of Que-Cu complexes adheres to the established
methodology for the synthesis of flavonoid compounds and metal ion
complexes [45]. A quantity of 0.15 g of quercetin was taken and dis-
solved in 20 mL of anhydrous ethanol. Anhydrous copper chloride
(0.036 g) was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol (5 mL) and added drop-
wise to the quercetin solution. The pH of the reaction system was then
adjusted to 8 with 2 mol/L NaOH solution. A water bath at 60 ◦C was
used to heat and stir the reaction system for 8 h. After filtration, the
precipitate was collected and washed three times with anhydrous
ethanol and ultrapure water, respectively. After drying, Que-Cu was
obtained. The Que-Cu was then grafted onto Chs through an esterifica-
tion reaction. In summary, 30 mg of Que-Cu was accurately weighed and
then dissolved in 25 mL of the DMSO solvent. The reaction system was
agitated for 6 h. The resulting product was then subjected to dialysis in
ultrapure water for three days. Following dialysis, the insoluble solids
were extracted by filtration, and the solution was subsequently
freeze-dried.
4.3. Preparation of QM (Cu) nanoparticle
A solution of 0.1 g of carboxylated chitosan in ultrapure water was
prepared. MXene was added and subjected to ultrasonication for 30 min
to facilitate dispersion. The solution was then stirred at room tempera-
ture for 12 h to ensure complete complexation. Excess MXene was
removed by centrifugation, resulting in the formation of the product
M@C. The product QM (Cu) NPs were obtained by taking 0.1 g of
QC@Chs, which had been previously dissolved in 10 mL of water, and
adding 0.1 g of M@C solution in 10 mL increments under stirring con-
ditions. This process was conducted for 12 h at room temperature.

4.6. The generation of ROS and Antibacterial Activities of QM (Cu) NPs
The method described in the literature was employed to detect the
production of ROS. The indicator 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF)
was selected for use in detecting the capacity of MXene and QM (Cu) NPs
to produce ROS. DPBF exhibits strong absorption at a wavelength of 410
nm, and ROS can oxidize DPBF, thereby reducing its absorption at 410
nm A solution of 50 μg/mL DPBF in DMSO was combined with equal
volumes of MXene, QC@Chs, and QM (Cu) NPs. The resulting mixture
was irradiated with a laser having a wavelength of 808 nm and a power
of 1.5 W/cm2. The absorbance at 410 nm was then measured at 2-min
intervals using a UV–Vis–NIR spectrophotometer. Furthermore, the
absorbance of the solution at 410 nm was determined using a
UV–Vis–NIR spectrophotometer, with the initial reading taken at 0 min
before irradiation. The absorbance was then recorded at 2-min intervals
for a period of 10 min. A control sample was prepared by omitting the
light source, and a blank group was created by mixing 50 μg/mL DPBF in
DMSO with an equal volume of deionized water.
The hypothesis that nanoparticles regulate the generation and
scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in a cyclical manner was
verified by measuring the concentration of ROS using fluorescence
photometry (λex = 488 nm, λem = 525 nm) (LS-55, PerkinElmer) with
2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) as a fluorescent
probe. The DCFH-DA probe was added to the MXene and QM (Cu) NPs
solution, and the stock solution was irradiated with a laser with a
wavelength of 808 nm and a power of 1 W/cm2 for 10 min. Subse-
quently, 100 μL of the solution was separated, and the concentration of
ROS was detected by DCFH-DA. Subsequently, the solution was allowed
to rest at room temperature for 10 min, after which 100 μl was extracted
from the original stock solution. The concentration of ROS present in
this solution was then determined using the DCFH-DA method.
The antimicrobial capacity of QM (Cu) NPs was validated through
the utilization of clone formation experiments. For the time gradient,

4.7. Biocompatibility of QM (Cu) NPs
In order to evaluate the biocompatibility of QM (Cu) NPs, two key
parameters, namely cytocompatibility and hemocompatibility, were
selected for analysis. The initial step involved assessing the cytotoxicity
of QM (Cu) NPs on L929 and RAW264.7 cells through the utilization of
an MTT assay. In brief, L929 and RAW264.7 cells were inoculated in 96-
well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. A series of concentrations
of the drug medium was prepared by dissolving QM (Cu) NPs in DMEM.
Following a 24-h incubation period, the DMEM medium was replaced
with the drug medium, with the cells in the original DMEM serving as a
control. Following a further 24-h period, the medium was replaced with
MTT solution and incubated at 37 ◦C for a period of 4 h. Subsequently,
the MTT solution was excluded from the experimental procedure, and
dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well. The plate was
shaken and transferred to an enzyme labeling instrument with a 492 nm
filter to read the absorbance. Furthermore, L929 and RAW264.7 cells
incubated with the highest concentration of the drug were stained with a
Calcein-AM/PI live/dead cell fluorescence staining kit and observed
under an inverted fluorescence microscope (FV3000, Olympus).
In accordance with our previous research, the blood compatibility of
QM (Cu) NPs solutions was assessed using rat erythrocytes. In summary,
an equal volume of erythrocyte suspension was combined with the QM
(Cu) NPs solution and incubated at 37 ◦C for 3 h. The negative control
was prepared using saline, while the positive control was prepared using
a 1 % Triton X-100 solution. Following centrifugation at 3000 rpm for
10 min, the concentration of hemoglobin in the supernatant was
determined by measuring the absorbance at 540 nm using an enzyme
marker. Furthermore, QM (Cu) NPs solution was administered to rats,
and blood was collected and analyzed using a blood biochemistry
analyzer to observe alterations in various biochemical parameters.
Concurrently, the hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys of the rats
were excised and processed for pathological sectioning, allowing for the
observation of any alterations in the organs.

Following the removal of the LPS-containing medium, the drug-

housed under the controlled conditions: humidity of 50–60 %, temper-

facility (License no. SCXK (Shaanxi) 2015–002). All animal experiments
complied with the ARRIVE guidelines and were carried out in
accordance with the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986.
4.10. Establishment of the rat BP model
A bacterial prostatitis model in rats was constructed using E. coli. The
specific procedure was as follows: Following administration of iso-
flurane for anesthesia, the skin of the lower abdomen of Sprague-Dawley
rats was sterilized with alcohol and incised vertically at approximately 1
cm along the white line. Subsequently, the bladder was meticulously
elevated with forceps in order to facilitate its exposure and that of the
prostate. A 1 mL sterile syringe was used to inject 50 μL of a diluted
E. coli solution (1 × 108 CFU/mL) into each prostate lobe. The bladder
and seminal vesicle were repositioned within the abdominal cavity with
the aid of a sterile swab, and the muscle and skin tissues were then su-
tured in a meticulous, layer-by-layer fashion. Following the suturing
procedure, a small quantity of penicillin was administered subcutane-
ously in the vicinity of the sutures, and iodine was applied for the pur-
pose of sterilizing the sutures. The aseptic technique is employed to
reduce the likelihood of contamination throughout the procedure.
4.11. Macroscopic observation, body weight and organ index
The experiment was divided into four groups: a blank group, a model
group, a QM (Cu) NPs group, and a QM (Cu) NPs + Laser group. Levo-
floxacin, a commonly used antibiotic for the treatment of bacterial
prostatitis, was employed as a positive control drug. The model group,
levofloxacin group, QM (Cu) NPs group, and QM (Cu) NPs + Laser group
were administered an intraprostatic injection of E. coli on day 0 to
establish a bacterial prostatitis model. The blank group was adminis-
tered an equal amount of saline. The administration of levofloxacin, QM
(Cu) NPs, and QM (Cu) NPs + Laser was conducted via tail vein injection
on days 1, 3, and 5, respectively. On day 7, the rats were euthanized, and
the prostate tissue and related immune organs were harvested for
evaluation of the therapeutic effects.
The body weights of the rats in each group were recorded on days 0,
1, 3, 5, and 7, and the resulting data were plotted and analyzed. The rats
were euthanized on the seventh day of the experiment. Subsequently,
the lower abdomen of the rats was examined and the prostate glands
situated beneath the bladder were meticulously dissected and photo-
graphed for comparison. After that, the prostate was excised and its
weight was recorded. The prostate index was then calculated, and the

were visualized using an electrochemiluminescence (ECL) luminous
solution.
4.16. Transcriptomics analysis
The samples were pulverized with liquid nitrogen, disrupted with
SDT (containing NaCl), and treated with DTT, followed by sonication.
Subsequently, the samples were subjected to heat incubation, ice bath
treatment, centrifugation, and iodoacetamide alkylation. The samples
were combined with pre-cooled acetone and incubated at a low tem-
perature. Following this, centrifugation was performed to collect the
precipitate, which was then washed and solubilized with DB buffer. The
protein samples were fixed with DB buffer and subsequently digested
with trypsin and TEAB buffer. On the following day, the trypsin and
calcium chloride were replenished, and the digestion was conducted
overnight. Following pH adjustment and centrifugation, the supernatant
was desalted on a C18 column, and the eluate was collected and
lyophilized. Subsequently, the peptides were subjected to analysis using
a Q Exactive™HF-X mass spectrometer in data-dependent acquisition
(DDA) mode, encompassing full-scan and MS/MS analyses. This was
conducted with the objective of optimizing data collection, utilizing
high-resolution settings and multiple parameters.
4.17. Statistical analysis
All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The sig-
nificance of the results was assessed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5
software with one-way ANOVA, with a significance threshold of p <
0.05. In cases where the data did not follow a normal distribution,
Fisher’s exact test was used to calculate the results.

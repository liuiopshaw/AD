Parkinson’s disease (PD) is one of the most prevalent neurodegenerative diseases. It is usually accompa-
nied by motor and non-motor symptoms that seriously threaten the health and the quality of life. Novel
medications are urgently needed because current pharmaceuticals can relieve symptoms but cannot stop
disease progression. The microbiota-gut-brain axis (MGBA) is closely associated with the occurrence and
development of PD and is an effective therapeutic target. Tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs)
can modulate the microbiome and immune regulation. However, such nucleic acid nanostructures are
very sensitive to acids which hinder this promising approach. Therefore, we prepared exosome-like
nanovesicles (Exo@tac) from ginger that are acid resistant and equipped with tFNAs modiﬁed by antimi-
crobial peptides (AMP). We veriﬁed that Exo@tac regulates intestinal bacteria associated with the
microbial-gut-brain axis in vitro and signiﬁcantly improves PD symptoms in vivo when administered
orally. Microbiota proﬁling conﬁrmed that Exo@tac normalizes the intestinal ﬂora composition of mouse
models of PD. Our ﬁndings present a novel strategy for the development of PD drugs and the innovative
delivery of nucleic acid nanomedicines.

1. Introduction
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most prevalent neurode-
generative disorder and severely threatens human health, espe-
cially in the aged population [1]. PD is characterized by both
non-motor symptoms (mainly somnipathy, constipation, dyspha-
gia, weight loss, and gastrointestinal dysfunctions) and motor def-
icits (rigidity, resting tremor, and bradykinesia) [2,3]. While
therapies do exist to improve symptoms, these cannot stop disease
progression [4]. Patients with PD will experience loss of indepen-
dence, disability, and eventually, death. The primary pathological
changes that lead to PD are the formation of Lewy bodies and
the death of nigrostriatal dopamine neurons [5–7]. Interaction
between the brain and the gut has received substantial attention
[8]. The gut might inﬂuence brain function by producing hormones
or neurotransmitters, or via the immune system [9–11]. Intestinal
ﬂora such as Escherichia coli (E. coli), segmentomycetes, and Akker-
mansia muciniphila, play important roles in the occurrence and
development of PD. They can regulate immune cells by activating
inﬂammatory factors and even directly affect host brain activity
through their metabolites. In particular, E. coli produces a protein
named ‘‘curli”, which promotes a-synuclein (a-syn) misfolding.
These misfolded proteins are transmitted to the brain via the vagus
nerve and accumulate, inducing the symptoms of PD [12,13].
Importantly, antibiotic intervention can improve the symptoms
of PD. For example, microvesicles derived from mesenchymal stem
cells ameliorate neurotoxicity by repairing an abnormal gut micro-
biota composition induced by 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahy
dropyridine (MPTP) that is associated with PD [14–16]. Thus, the
gut microbiota and the microbiota-gut-brain axis (MGBA) could
be novel targets for treating PD.
Nanostructures based on DNA have recently emerged as a hot-
spot in the ﬁeld of nanomedicine because they are editable, stable,
biocompatible, and bioavailable [17–24]. Among the various types
of DNA nanostructures, tetrahedral framework nucleic acids
(tFNAs) that include four speciﬁc single-stranded DNAs (ssDNAs)

tFNAs loaded with antisense oligonucleotides targeting the ftsZ

conﬁguration, tFNAs could be designed to target multiple genes
by modiﬁcation with antisense oligonucleotide sequences to inhi-
bit extracellular polysaccharide synthesis, resulting in thinner bac-
terial bioﬁlms [31]. As a drug delivery vehicle, tFNAs signiﬁcantly
increase the uptake efﬁciency of antibiotic drugs. For example,
they have been shown to increase the transportation efﬁciency of
Histatin-5 into fungal cells [32]. Similarly, Methicillin-Resistant

methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and E. coli in vitro.
Moreover, it could alleviate peritonitis and local infections in vivo
[35]. tFNAs can also be modiﬁed with antimicrobial peptides
(AMP) and targeting chains, which may allow them to play a role
in regulating intestinal ﬂora for the treatment of PD. However,
the gastric acid intolerance of tFNAs limits its application in the
gastrointestinal tract.
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanosized lipid bilayer particles
that usually range from 50 to 1,000 nm and are released by many
cell types into the extracellular space where they participate in
intercellular communication [36–39]. The speciﬁc function of EVs
depends on their origin and physiological conditions. They trans-
port proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids that exert dynamic biologi-
cal effects [36]. Extracellular vesicles are natural vehicles for
encapsulating drugs [40]. Compared with artiﬁcial vehicles, EVs
are non-immunogenic, minimally toxic, have a wider biodistribu-
tion [41], and might reliably deliver functional nucleic acids [42].
However, a major limitation is the immense cost of large-scale
EV production from mammalian cells. Furthermore, several poten-
tial
biohazards
can
arise
from
parental
cells.
Plant-derived
nanoparticles have been investigated in an effort to overcome
these problems as some are edible, and thus suitable for drug
delivery to the gut [43]. Nanovectors isolated from ginger have
been loaded with doxorubicin to treat colon cancer, and nanovesi-
cles derived from grapefruit regulate the gut mucosal immune sys-
tem due to selective uptake by intestinal macrophages [44–46].
Thus, nanovesicles derived from edible plants could be promising
vehicles for tFNA delivery.
Here, we aimed to establish a novel platform for the oral deliv-
ery of tFNA derivatives. A new type of nanocomplex was synthe-
sized from ginger and characterized using tFNA derivatives and
nanovesicles. These nanocomplexes were stable in acidic environ-
ments. TFNAs and their derivatives signiﬁcantly altered the micro-
bial composition of the gut and improved motor symptoms in
mouse
models
of
PD
in
vivo.
Ginger-derived
exosome-like
nanovesicles loaded with antimicrobial tFNAs should be a promis-
ing strategy for treating PD by modulating gut microbiota.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Synthesis and veriﬁcation of tFNA-AMP-CPG complexes (tac)
We synthesized tFNAs essentially as described. Table S1 (on-
line) shows that equal proportions of four constructed ssDNAs
(S1–S4) in 10 mmol/L Tris-HCl and 50 mmol/L MgCl2 (pH 8.0;
TM buffer) were transferred to a thermal cycler for synthesis
(95 ℃for 10 min and 4 ℃for 20 min). AMP was chemically linked
to the 30 end of the S1 chain, to form S1-AMP, and the antisense
chain of the targeted sequence CPG was extended to the 30 end
of the S3 chain, to form S3-CPG. The four chains (S1-AMP, S2, S3-
CPG, and S4) were then synthesized into tac in the thermal cycler
as described above. The morphology and size of the tFNAs were
determined using tac transmission electron microscopy (TEM),
atomic force microscopy (AFM), and polyacrylamide gel elec-
trophoresis (PAGE).
2.2. Veriﬁcation of the targeting of tac
We cultured groups of E. coli at the logarithmic growth stage in
equal volumes of LB medium (control), S1-AMP-Cy5, and tFNA-
AMP-Cy5 for 2 h. We then quantiﬁed emitted Cy5 ﬂuorescence
intensity using ﬂow cytometry.
2.3. Exosome-like nanovesicles isolation
Zingiber ofﬁcinale (ginger) purchased from a local market
(500 g) was peeled and ground to extract the juice, which was
brought to 500 mL using double-distilled H2O. The suspension
was sequentially ultracentrifuged for 20, 40, and 120 min at
3000, 1000, and 150,000  g, respectively. Nanovesicles were puri-
ﬁed using 8%, 30%, 45% and 60% sucrose. Bands between 30% and
45% were harvested according to a target product density of
1.13–1.21 g/mL. The extracted exosome-like nanovesicles were
veriﬁed using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and TEM.
2.4. Synthesis, characterization and stability detection of exosome-like
nanovesicles equipped with tFNAs modiﬁed by AMP (Exo@tac)
Tac was electrotransfected into exosome like nanovesicles using
Gene Pulser Xcell (Bio-Rad, California, USA) at 20–25 ℃(0.7 KV,
350 ls, pulsed 20 times). The mixture was left for 30 min at
37 ℃to allow for Exo@tac formation. TEM was used to observe
the morphology and size of Exo@tac. NTA was used to record the
concentration, zeta potential, and particle size of Exo@tac. Subse-
quently, Exo@tac was added to the gastric acid simulants for 2, 4,
6, or 8 h, and NTA was used to observe and record changes in
the particle size and zeta potential.
2.5. Bactericidal effect detection
E. coli in the logarithmic growth stage were divided into ﬁve
groups: control, tFNAs, AMP, tac, and Exo@tac. The control group
was cultured in LB medium. The other four groups were treated
with either tFNAs, AMP, tac, and Exo@tac (250 nmol/L, 12 h),
pre-treated with gastric acid simulants for 6 h. The bacteria were
then centrifuged and re-suspended with PBS. The supernatant
obtained from centrifuged bacteria was collected to prepare for
subsequent cell experiments. A600nm was then used to detect the
proportions of live and dead bacteria. Scanning electron micro-
scope (SEM) and TEM were used to observe the morphological
changes in the bacteria.
2.6. Establishment of a mouse PD model
All animal procedures of the present study were approved by
the Ethics Committee of West China College of Stomatology,
Sichuan University (WCHSIRB-D-2023–643). Fifty C57BL/6 male
mice weighing of 25–30 g at 10 weeks of age underwent adaptive
training 7 d in advance of the experiments to remove mice with
motor incoordination. The mice were divided into ﬁve groups: 1
control, 2 MPTP, 3 transplanted bacteria, 4 MPTP + Exo@tac, and
5 transplanted bacteria + Exo@tac. The MPTP and MPTP + Exo@tac
groups were injected intraperitoneally with MPTP at the weight of
20–30 mg/kg for 15 d to establish PD. The transplanted bacteria

2.7. Behavioral detection
Mice motor behavior was assessed using an open ﬁeld experi-
ment. The open ﬁeld was 20 cm high, 40 cm long, and 40 cm wide
and was divided into 16 compartments measuring 10 cm  10 cm.
Mice were randomly placed in one of the four vertex grids of the
open ﬁeld. The accumulation of physical displacement generated
by the mice within 10 min, the physical displacement generated
per unit time, and the number of trips to the central region were
recorded. Paralysis-tremor experiments were also conducted. Mice
were placed in a transparent observation chamber with a length,
width, and height of 30 cm for 30 min daily during the experimen-
tal period. The paralytic tremors of the mice during the experimen-
tal period were recorded using video evaluated according to
scoring criteria. Finally, rotating-rod experiments were carried
out. Mice were placed on a rotating stick to be assessed. When test-
ing for the ﬁrst time, one usually starts at a low speed and then
gradually increases it. The behavior of mice on a rotating rod was
observed. The number of times the mice dropped or lost their bal-
ance was recorded. Multiple tests were performed for 5 min each
to determine the endurance and exercise ability of the mice.
2.8. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
According to manufacturer’s protocol, the concentration of
interleukin 6 (IL-6), 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), lipopolysaccha-
ride (LPS) and tumor necrosis factor-a (TNF-a) were assessed by
ELISA (Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc, Waltham, USA). The absorbance
was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc).

2.10. Western blotting
Brieﬂy, the samples were lysed in RIPA buffer and then dena-
tured by boiling at 95 ℃for 5 min. The protein was then resolved
by sodium dodecyl sulfate-PAGE. Separated proteins were trans-
ferred to a polyvinylidene ﬂuoride (PVDF) membrane and blocked
with 5% fat-free milk. The membrane was then incubated with pri-
mary antibodies at 4 ℃overnight followed by horseradish
peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room tem-
perature. Bands were detected using an enhanced chemilumines-
cence system (Bio-Rad).
2.11. Gut microbiota proﬁling and bioinformatic analysis
Fecal microbial genomic DNA was extracted using a FastDNA
Spin Kit (Mobio, Carlsbad, USA), and the V4 regions of the 16S rRNA
were ampliﬁed using speciﬁc primers under the following PCR con-
ditions: 95 C for 3 min; then 30 cycles of 95 C for 30 s, 55 C for
30 s, 72 C for 30 s; then a ﬁnal extension of 72 C for 5 min. The
PCR products were puriﬁed using an AxyPrep DNA kit (Corning,
New York, USA) and quantiﬁed using a QuantiFlour ST (Promega,
Madison, USA). Libraries were prepared using a TruSeq DNA LT
Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA). An Illumina
HiSeq 2500 was used to sequence the DNA to generate paired
end 250 bp reads. Raw sequencing data were ﬁltered according
to the Phred scores, and the reads were trimmed if the average
Phred score in the window (5 bp in size, 1 bp step length)
was < 20. Processed reads shorter than 150 bp were excluded from
analysis. After trimming, the reads were assembled using the Flash
software, and the reads that could not be assembled were dis-
carded. Sequences that contained ambiguous bases, >1 mismatch
in their 50 primers, had a homopolymer over 8, or a sequence
length shorter than 200 bp were removed using QIIME. Only
high-quality sequences without chimeras were used for further
analysis.
Uclust
in
Qiime
was
used
to
cluster
high-quality
sequences with 97% similarity to obtain operational taxonomic
units (OTUs). Representative OTU sequences were annotated by
comparison with the GreeGenes database (13.5). Weighted princi-
pal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed using UniFrac
analysis to represent the similarities among the microbial commu-
nities. Heatmap and PCoA were performed using R (version 3.0.3)
and Venn diagrams were created using Venn Diagram software.
Sequencing and bioinformatic analysis services were provided by
Shanghai OE Biotech, Inc. (Shanghai, China).
2.12. Histology
Tissues were ﬁxed in paraformaldehyde, embedded in parafﬁn,
and sectioned for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Nissl, and
immunoﬂuorescence staining. After antigen repair, sections were
blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin. The sections were then
incubated with primary antibody at 4 C overnight. After washing,
corresponding ﬂuorescent secondary antibodies were added and
sections were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The slides
were then washed and photographed using a ﬂuorescence micro-
scope (Leica, Witzler, Germany).

Exo@tac

electro-transfection, and plant-derived nanovesicles were then
obtained by ultracentrifugation (Fig. 1a). TEM and AFM showed
that the tac had stable morphology, uniform size, and uniform dis-

3.3. Exo@tac has regulatory effect on MGBA in vitro
Pathogenic metabolites of intestinal microorganisms affect the
brain mainly through the neuroendocrine, vagus, and immune
pathways, and in this way may contribute to the occurrence and
development of PD [47–49]. The neuroendocrine pathway refers
to the regulation of emotion and behavior by metabolites from
gut microbes due to their inﬂuence on the secretion of the neuro-
transmitter 5-HT by intestinal endocrine cells [50]. The vagus path-
way involves stimulation of the production of a-syn by LPS via
promotion of apoptosis of intestinal neurons, resulting in abnormal
accumulation of a-syn in the brain due to vagus nerve stimulation.
The immune pathway involves LPS produced by bacteria acting on
local immune cells. The inﬂammatory factors produced enter the
blood and then enter the brain where they act on microglia, caus-
ing inﬂammation of the central nervous system. Therefore, we
explored the effect of Exo@tac on E. coli (that is associated with
PD) and its metabolites in vitro and the potential mechanism of
the MGBA theory (Fig. 2a).
Bacteria were divided into ﬁve groups: 1 Control, 2 tFNAs, 3
AMP, 4 tac, and 5 Exo@tac. In Fig. 2b, the uptake of tFNA-AMP
(30.677%) by bacteria was higher than that of single-chain S1-
AMP (10.733%). The addition of the CPG antisense sequence
resulted in a signiﬁcantly higher uptake of tac (93.537%) than that
of tFNA-AMP (10.733%). Compared with group 1, the bacterial mor-
phology of groups 2, 3, and 4 showed no signiﬁcant changes,
whereas the cell wall of bacteria in group 5 was signiﬁcantly dam-
aged, and the ﬂagella disappeared (Fig. 2c). The survival rate of the
bacteria in group 5 was approximately 20% (Fig. 2d), which was
signiﬁcantly lower than in the other groups. (*: vs. 1, &: vs. 5).
Immune cells (RAW264.7 and BV2), nerve cells (SHSY-5Y) and
enteroendocrine cells (RIN-14B) were divided into the same ﬁve
treatment groups as the bacteria. The supernatant obtained by cen-
trifugation of the above ﬁve groups of bacteria was added to
groups 2, 3, 4, and 5, and an equal volume of cell medium was
added to group 1 for 24 h. The results in Fig. 2d and Fig. S2k (on-
line) revealed that compared with groups 2, 3, and 4, the expres-
sion of 5-HT in RIN-14B cells in group 5 was signiﬁcantly
increased and was close to that in the group 1. Western blotting
(Fig. 2e and Fig. S2a–j online) showed that Exo@tac ultimately
inhibited the apoptosis of BV2 cells and RAW264.7 (compared to
groups 2, 3, and 4, the expression of Bcl2 in group 5 was increased
and the expression of Bax was decreased). The expression of
nuclear factor-r-gene binding (NF-rB) and the inﬂammatory
cytokines IL-6 and TNF-a were decreased. This may be closely
related to the inhibition of the activation of NF-rB signaling path-
way. For SHSY-5Y cells, the expression of Bax decreased and that of
Bcl2 increased in group 5, and the apoptosis of SHSY-5Y cells was
inhibited. In addition, the expression of a-syn in group 5 was sig-
niﬁcantly decreased compared with groups 2, 3, and 4 (Fig. S2l–n
online).

4. Discussion and conclusion
We proposed an innovative, oral drug delivery system called
Exo@tac comprising vesicles coated with antibacterial nucleic
acids to regulate MGBA and improve PD. Compared with other
nucleic acid nanostructures, vesicle-coated Exo@tac has better
resistance to gastric acid (Fig. S1e online). This is a new therapeutic
strategy in which nucleic acids and their derivatives are applied to
MGBA-related diseases and diseases associated with intestinal
ﬂora disorders. Compared with current clinical treatment for PD
such as deep brain stimulation, oral Exo@tac is non-invasive, efﬁ-
cient, and easy to administer, and has a wide range of applications,
with high patient acceptance and no fear of surgical indications.
Based on the above advantages, Exo@tac has considerable clinical
translational value. Our study had some limitations, such as
uncertainty about the effects of long-term use of Exo@tac on gut
probiotics. We plan to explore whether long-term administration
of Exo@tac adversely affects gut probiotics while regulating PD-
associated pathogenic bacteria, and how to balance probiotics
and harmful ﬂora in humans.
Exo@tac is composed of tFNAs in exosome-like nanovesicles
derived from ginger to achieve oral delivery of completely framed
nucleic acids (Fig. 6). Exo@tac has strong stability in the acidic
stomach environment and can be retained in the gastrointestinal
tract for a longer time than other nanomedicine structures.
In addition, it can regulate PD-related intestinal bacteria and sub-
sequently affect MGBA-related macrophages, neuromicroglia, and
intestinal endocrine cells, thereby reducing inﬂammation and
apoptosis in the central nervous system, promoting the production
of neurotransmitters 5-HT and the dopamine precursor, and reduc-
ing deposition of the PD signature protein a-syn. Further, in vivo
experiments revealed that the motor symptoms and pathological
characteristics of PD mice improved. We believe that the successful
synthesis of Exo@tac and the manner in which it is administered
orally have signiﬁcant implications for the development of addi-
tional central nervous system therapies, including PD.

Streamlining Bacterial Gene Regulation via Nucleic Acid
Delivery with Gold Nanoclusters
Qingsong Zhang, Xinyi Leng, Lin Peng, Hong Lin, Guanhua Xuan,* Weiwei Zhang,
Hideyuki Mitomo, Kuniharu Ijiro, and Guoqing Wang*
Delivery of exogenous nucleic acids (NAs) for gene regulation in bacteria,
bypassing the barrier of the cell wall, is essential for advancing fundamental
microbiology and genetic engineering, and the treatment of bacterial diseases.
However, current methods that rely on electrical or chemical interventions are
limited by their complexity, specialized expertise, and laboratory-speciﬁc
instrumentation. This study explores the capability of gold nanoclusters
(AuNCs) as carriers for delivering small-interfering RNA and antisense
oligonucleotides into bacteria for targeted gene regulation while shielding
them from degradation during transport. By enhancing the cytoplasmic
membrane permeability, the AuNCs enable eﬃcient internalization of NAs
into both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria while exerting negligible
inﬂuence on bacterial activity. It is demonstrated that the rationally designed
NAs can be released from the AuNCs within bacteria, enabling ˜70%
knockdown of mecA in Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA).
This signiﬁcantly reduces MRSA’s antibiotic resistance and enhances oxacillin
treatment eﬃcacy. Furthermore, the successful silencing of ligA in Escherichia
coli and pilQ in Pseudomonas aeruginosa highlights the broad adaptability of
the approach across diverse bacterial species. The AuNCs-based
next-generation NA delivery system has the potential to transform bacterial
gene regulation—previously restricted to laboratory settings—into a versatile
and scalable solution for real-world application.

Furthermore, NAs, particularly siRNA, are inherently vulnera-
ble to rapid degradation by nucleases present in diverse environ-
ments, compromising their stability for use across numerous bi-
ological processes.[15,16] Consequently, the persistent challenge of
developing convenient, safe, and eﬃcient NA-delivery systems
for bacteria represents a critical barrier to progress in microbi-
ology, biochemistry, and related disciplines, stymieing advance-
ments that could otherwise drive innovation in gene regulation
and therapeutic applications.
Nanostructures have recently emerged as crucial materials for
interacting with bacterial cells in ways that traditional molecules
(e.g., NAs) cannot, because of their high surface area and cus-
tomizable surface chemistry.[17–20] Although the delivery of NAs
using nanostructures into eukaryotic cells has become a popular
concept for gene regulation and disease therapy,[21] indeed, inves-
tigations on carrying NAs into bacteria have been rare. On the one
hand, there have been few results on the evidence of nanoparticle
internalization into bacteria, despite numerous studies on their
antibacterial activities.[22,23] As a sedimental work, it has been re-
ported by Xie and coworkers that nanostructures such as gold
nanoparticles (≤5 nm), can enter into bacteria in a fashion and ef-
ﬁcacy depending on size and surface properties.[24] On the other
hand, a number of works suggest that the bacteria-killing eﬀect of
nanostructures is directly connected to their internalization that
causes membrane disruption.[25,26] Taken together, there has re-
mained an open challenge for materials scientists and microbi-
ologists to develop a nanoparticle-based delivery system for NAs
using nanostructures.
This study aims to leverage the unique size and surface proper-
ties of gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) to develop the next-generation
delivery platform for NAs in bacteria for targeted gene regula-
tion (Figure 1). The polyethyleneimine (PEI)-modiﬁed AuNCs
(PAuNCs) with positive surface charge allow for eﬀective load-
ing of NAs on the PAuNCs, regardless of the use of siRNA or
ASO sequence. Due to the enhanced cytoplasmic membrane per-
meability, the complex of the NA/PAuNCs can further be rapidly
internalized into both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacte-
ria while not inﬂuencing bacterial growth activity, as evidenced
by ﬂuorescence microscopy, ﬂow cytometry, and elemental map-
ping. We also demonstrate that gene silencing eﬃciencies of up

2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Synthesis and Characterization of the PAuNCs
The AuNCs were prepared by reducing Au3+ using glutathione
(GSH) as the reducing agent (Figure 2a). Characterizations by
transmission electron microscope (TEM) reveal a narrow size dis-
tribution (Figure 2b), with statistical analysis estimating an av-
erage particle size of approximately1.6 nm (Figure 2c). Fourier
transform infrared (FTIR) spectra show that, despite the N─H
and C═O vibrational bands, and the N─H deformation band in
common, the S─H stretching vibrational band at 2525 cm−1 van-
ishes for the AuNCs in comparison with GSH (Figure 2d).[27] The
disappearance of the S─H band can be ascribed to the reaction of
GSH with Au and thereby the formation of the Au─S bond.[28,29]
The surface charge of the AuNCs was measured to be
−16.1 mV, a consequence of the surface capping by negatively
charged GSH. To improve the loading eﬃciency for NA, the
AuNCs were subjected to modiﬁcation with positively charged
PEI (Figure 2e). Subsequently, the surface charge of the resultant
PAuNCs turns to +16.8 mV (Figure 2f). TEM characterizations
show that the PAuNCs remain well dispersed. The average diam-
eter of the complexes was enlarged to 10.1 nm (Figure S1, Sup-
porting Information), which was accompanied by a slight shift of
the absorption proﬁle (Figure S2, Supporting Information).
Interestingly, we observed the enhanced permeability of
MRSA’s cytoplasmic membranes in the presence of the PAuNCs.
Upon co-culture of the bacterial with the PAuNCs for diﬀer-
ent times, 2-nitrophenyl-𝛽-D-galactopyranoside was introduced
to probe the permeability change, the hydrolysis of which can
be catalyzed by released 𝛽-galactosidase from the bacteria to pro-
duce yellow o-nitrophenol.[30] The change in optical density at
420 nm indicates the increase in the permeability of the cy-
toplasmic membrane with increasing amounts of the PAuNCs
(Figure 2g), which are attributable to the complexation of the
membrane with the PAuNCs that alters the ﬂuidity of the lipid
bilayer.[31]
Subsequently, the potential of the PAuNCs in penetrating
bacteria cells was examined. MRSA was co-cultured with the
PAuNCs for 1 h, and then treated with 0.1% Triton X-100 to re-
move the PAuNCs from the bacterial surface before proceeding
with acid digestion and inductively coupled plasma-mass spec-
trometry quantiﬁcation of the internalized PAuNCs. As a con-
trol result, no change in MRSA’s cytoplasmic membrane per-
meability is observed with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Figure S3, Sup-
porting Information). The results reveal that the PAuNCs can
penetrate the bacteria (Figure 2h; Figure S4, Supporting Infor-
mation) and that the internalization undergoes a biphasic trend
with increasing concentration of the PAuNCs in the range of

2.2. NA Loading and Delivery with the PAuNCs
Agarose gel electrophoresis was used to evaluate the loading ca-
pacity of the PAuNCs for ASO and siRNA1 against the mecA
gene in MRSA upon their incubation for 20 min (Figures 3a-i
and S1, Supporting Information). When there are fewer or no
PAuNCs, a distinct band can be obtained due to the presence
of free ASO (Lane 1, 2, Figure 3a-ii). As the amount of the
PAuNCs increased (lanes 3), the corresponding band intensity
of free ASO decreased signiﬁcantly. This is accompanied by the
occurrence of a band close to the gel well, attributable to the
accumulation of the NA/PAuNCs. When the mass ratio of the
ASO to the PAuNCs was 1:1.4 or smaller, no free ASO was de-
tected (lanes 4–7), indicating the complete loading of the ASO
on the PAuNCs. Similarly, the siRNA can be fully loaded by the
PAuNCs at a mass ratio of 1:2.1 (lane 4, Figure 3a-iii). Fortunately,
the PAuNCs can further protect the susceptible siRNA against
degradation. The image shown in Figure S6 (Supporting Infor-
mation) reveals a high stability of the ASO against DNase degra-
dation even at high DNase concentrations, consistent with the
results reported elsewhere.[33] In contrast, RNase A was found to
be capable of readily degrading the siRNA (Figure S7, Supporting
Information),[34] while this is not the case for siRNA1/PAuNCs
(Figure S8, Supporting Information).
The PAuNCs’ capability of delivering NA into MRSA was in-
vestigated using ﬂuorescence microscopy. Upon incubation of
the NA/PAuNCs with MRSA for 1 h, Hoechst 33342 was used to
stain bacterial cells, resulting in blue emission (Figure 3b-i).[35]
MRSA emitted red ﬂuorescence upon treatment with the
ASO/PAuNCs (Figure 3b-ii,iii), which can be attributed to the
emission of the Cy3-labeled ASO.[36] In contrast, no red ﬂuo-
rescence could be detected from the bacteria treated with the
Cy3-labeled ASO alone, highlighting the role of the PAuNCs
in the internalization of the ASO into bacteria. It is worth not-
ing that, to ensure the occurrence of the red ﬂuorescence didn’t
result from the adsorption of the ASO/PAuNCs on the bacte-
ria, the ASO/PAuNCs were washed oﬀthe MRSA surface with
0.1% Triton X-100 before ﬂuorescence imaging.[37] Similar re-
sults were also observed for the siRNA1/PAuNCs-treated MRSA
(Figure 3b-iv,v). TEM and elemental mapping were employed to
cross-sectioned ﬁlm of MRSA embedded in epoxy resin, to di-
rectly visualize the proﬁle of the PAuNCs internalized in MRSA
(Figure 3c; Figure S9, Supporting Information). The distinct pro-
ﬁle of the PAuNCs-internalized MRSA once again highlights the
NA-carrying role of the PAuNCs. In contrast to the nano-delivery
of NA to plant cells where nanoparticle uptake is not necessary,[38]
we elucidate for the ﬁrst time the internalization of NA/PAuNCs
into bacteria, suggesting a unique mechanism of interaction
between nanoparticle-loaded NA and bacteria cell wall and
membrane.
The concentration of the PAuNC carriers is an essential
factor that aﬀects the eﬃciency of NA-delivery into MRSA
(Figure 3d-i), which was evaluated using ﬂow cytometry. As
shown in Figure 3d-ii, the ﬂuorescence intensity of the Cy3-
labeled ASO underwent a biphasic trend as the PAuNCs concen-
tration increased. Without the use of the PAuNCs, ﬂuorescence
was hardly observed from the corresponding MRSA, and the in-
ternalization of ASO into MRSA was negligible (Figure 3d-ii).
With increasing concentration of the PAuNCs, the ﬂuorescence
of the Cy3-labeled ASO becomes stronger and undergoes a mono-
tonic increase until 25 μg mL−1 of the PAuNCs is applied. The
ratio of MRSA internalized with the ASO/PAuNCs is as high as
54.1% but tends to decline with a further increase in the concen-
tration of the PAuNCs (Figure 3d-iii). The reversion may also be
ascribed to the inhibited activity of MRSA in the presence of con-
centrated PAuNCs (50 and 100 μg mL−1) (Figure S5, Supporting
Information). By following the identical procedure, we obtained
similar results for the dependence of siRNA1 internalization
on PAuNC concentration. The ratio of the siRNA-internalized
MRSA becomes the highest with the use of the 25 μg mL−1 of
the PAuNCs, which was yet 77.8% (Figure 3d-iv,v).
The incubation time was optimized for the internalization of
the NA/PAuNCs into MRSA (Figure 3e-i). Similarly, we observed
that the ﬂuorescence intensity of the Cy3-labeled ASO shows a
biphasic trend with the increase in incubation time (Figure 3e-ii).
After incubation for 30 min, the ratio of the ASO/PAuNCs-
internalized MRSA is 37.2% (Figure 3e-iii). The ﬂuorescence
intensity of MRSA becomes the strongest after 1 h of incuba-
tion, which is consistent with the highest ratio (54.1%) of the
ASO/PAuNCs-internalized MRSA. As the incubation time in-
creases further to 2 h and then 4 h, however, the internaliza-
tion ratio gradually decreases. The results may be rationalized
by the ﬁxed amount of the ASO/PAuNCs vs the rapidly grow-
ing number of MRSA in the system. Indeed, the PAuNCs at the
test concentration (25 μg mL−1) exert a negligible eﬀect on the
growth of MRSA (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Compa-
rably, the optimal incubation time and the PAuNC concentration
for siRNA1 delivery are identical to that for the ASO, which are
1 h and 25 μg mL−1, respectively (Figure 3e-iv,v). In particular,
the PAuNCs demonstrated higher eﬃciency (77.8%) for siRNA1
delivery.

The phenotype of MRSA was assessed by measuring changes
in the MIC of oxacillin upon mecA gene regulation with the
NA/PAuNCs (Figure 4b-i). The MIC of oxacillin against MRSA
was found to be reduced to 8 μg mL−1 when treated with the
ASO/PAuNCs, half of the MIC (16 μg mL−1) observed for the
control group (Figure 4b-ii). The ﬁndings highlight the eﬀec-
tive knockdown of the mecA gene by the ASO/PAuNCs, which
boosts the susceptibility of MRSA to oxacillin. When treated
with siRNA1/PAuNCs, interestingly, the MIC of oxacillin against
MRSA was decreased to 4 μg mL−1 (Figure 4b-iii), consistent
with the enhanced knockdown eﬃciency of siRNA1 compared
to the ASO. To further evaluate the gene regulatory eﬀects of
the NA/PAuNCs, the spread plate method was employed, al-
lowing for visual inspection of MRSA growth. As shown in
Figure 4c and Figure S10 (Supporting Information), MRSA
growth in the group treated with a mixture of oxacillin and the
ASO/PAuNCs was signiﬁcantly inhibited compared with control
group (treated with water), and the group treated with oxacillin,
the PAuNCs, the mixture of oxacillin and the PAuNCs, or a
mixture of oxacillin and the ASO (Figure 4c-i,ii; Figure S10i–iii,
Supporting Information). Therefore, the strong inhibition of
MRSA growth shown in Figure 4c-iii can be ascribed to the sup-
pression of the mecA gene in MRSA by the PAuNCs-delivered
ASO. Drastic growth inhibition was also observed for the group
treated with a mixture of oxacillin and the siRNA1/PAuNCs
(Figure 4c-iv,v; Figure S10iv, Supporting Information), which
agrees well with the measurements shown in Figure 4b-iii.
These results further support the strong gene regulatory ca-
pacity of NA/PAuNCs in enhancing antibiotic susceptibility in
MRSA.
2.4. Extension of the Gene Silencing Approach to Gram-Negative
Bacteria
The eﬃcient gene silencing in MRSA further encouraged us to
extend the application of the present approach to Gram-negative
bacteria, and E. coli was used to serve as the model. We ob-
served that in the presence of the PAuNCs, the permeability of
the E. coli cytoplasmic membrane also underwent an increase
(Figure S11, Supporting Information). The measurements of in-
ternalized PAuNCs and the corresponding bacterial activity con-
ﬁrmed the successful uptake of PAuNCs by E. coli cells, without
impairing bacterial viability (Figures S12,S13, Supporting Infor-
mation). The gene of ligA encoding DNA ligase was chosen for
testing gene silencing in E. coli. Due to its essential role in the cat-
alytic formation of the phosphodiester bonds in DNA, the tran-
scriptional level of ligA can be directly reﬂected by phenotypic
change with respect to bacterial growth and reproduction.[40] The
siRNA sequence (siRNA2) designed for silencing the ligA gene
is shown in Table S1 (Supporting Information). Electrophoretic
results conﬁrm the eﬃcient loading of siRNA2 by the PAuNCs
(Figure S14, Supporting Information); while ﬂuorescence mi-
croscopy reveals the internalization of siRNA2/PAuNCs into E.
coli (Figure 5a). Elemental mapping images directly validate
the capability of the PAuNCs in carrying siRNA2 to the E. coli
(Figure 5b; Figure S15, Supporting Information).
Flow cytometry measurements reveal the optimized concen-
tration of the PAuNCs for siRNA2 and internalization time to
be 10 μg mL−1 and 2 h, respectively (Figure 5c,d). The re-
sults of experiments conducted under the conditions show that
the transcript level in the ligA gene of the E. coli treated with
siRNA2/PAuNCs decreases by 65.5% compared with the control
group, as assessed using RT-qPCR (Figure 5e). To characterize
the phenotype of the treated E. coli, PI was introduced to label
dead bacterial cells for ﬂow cytometry analysis (Figure 5f-i).[41]
The considerable mortality ratio (42.9%) veriﬁes the eﬃcient si-
lencing of the ligA gene (Figure 5f-ii).
To visualize gene regulation by the present approach using the
naked eye, we used the pilQ gene of P. aeruginosa as the target for
gene silencing, which encodes a protein implicated in type IV
pili formation that promotes twitching motility.[42] Similarly, the
transcription level of the pilQ gene in P. aeruginosa treated with
siRNA3/PAuNCs is noticeably low compared with that in the con-

Inspired by the eﬃcient gene knockdown, we became in-
terested in the mechanism of gene suppression in the bac-
teria by the siRNA/PAuNCs. Cy3-siRNA1/PAuNCs and Cy3-
siRNA2/PAuNCs were subjected to react with MRSA and the E.
coli lysates, respectively. We could observe the release of the NA
from the PAuNCs upon reaction with the bacterial lysates, irre-
spective of whether the bacteria were Gram-positive or Gram-
negative (Figure S16, Supporting Information). The discharge
of the cargo is attributable to the replacement of the nucleic
acids by thiolates (e.g., glutathione) on the PAuNCs in the bacte-
ria, which is distinct from the non-internalization cargo delivery
mode for mature plant cells.[38] The results also rationalize the
subsequent pairing of the siRNA with the target mRNA inside
the bacteria after being carried into the bacteria by the PAuNCs,
which is followed by the cleavage of the mRNA under the action
of nuclease and thereby inhibiting the translation of the mRNA
(Figure 5i).[44,45] Combined with the high biocompatibility of the
PAuNCs (Figure S17, Supporting Information), this approach pi-
oneers a next-generation delivery system for microorganisms,
with the potential to transform bacterial gene regulation into a
versatile and scalable solution for real-world disease prevention
and therapy.

means to deliver NAs to bacteria for gene regulation, the an-

nanostructures as NA carriers. In this work, we have successfully
demonstrated the potential of PAuNCs as the next-generation

Synthesis and PEI Modiﬁcation of the AuNCs:
The AuNCs were pre-
pared using a simple hydrothermal method.[49] First, 1.5 mL of a GSH
(100 mM) was added to the mixture of a HAuCl4 solution (20 mM, 5.0 mL)
and 43.5 mL of water, accompanied by a color change of the mixture from
light yellow to colorless. The system was then subjected to a water bath at
80 °C under stirring (500 rpm), and the color of the mixture changed to
dark brown in minutes. The mixture was continuously stirred at 80°C and
500 rpm in the dark for 24 h, resulting in a yellow solution. Then, the yellow
solution was dialyzed for 48 h using a dialysis bag (3.5 KD) to remove the
unreacted species. Finally, the ﬁltrate in the dialysis bag was lyophilized
to earn powder of the AuNCs. The AuNCs were stored at 4°C in the dark.
For PEI modiﬁcation, the AuNCs (10 mg) were dissolved in 10 mL of wa-
ter, followed by the addition of PEI (30 mg mL−1, 2 mL). The mixture was
stirred for 5 h at room temperature. The resultant PAuNCs were stored in
the dark in a 4°C refrigerator for future use.

Real-Time Quantitative Reverse Transcription-PCR (RT-qPCR):
After in-
cubation of the ASO (25 μL, 20 μM) with the PAuNCs (25 μL, 1.0 mg mL−1)
for 20 min, the mixture was added to an MRSA suspension (950 μL,
106 CFU mL−1). In the system (1 mL), the concentrations of the ASO
and the PAuNCs were 500 nM and 25 μg mL−1, respectively. The mixture
was incubated at 37°C for 1 h. In parallel, water (50 μL), the ASO (50 μL,
10 μM), the PAuNCs (50 μL, 0.5 mg mL−1), and the cDNA loaded by the
PAuNCs (c-DNA/PAuNCs, 50 μL) were separately incubated with MRSA
suspensions for at 37°C 1 h, which were used to serve as the controls.
Subsequently, each of the systems was centrifuged (5000 rpm, 5 min) and
washed with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, and ﬁnally resuspended in
PBS (50 μL). To the bacterial suspension, lysozyme (50 μL, 30 mg mL−1)
was added, followed by incubation for 1 h at 37°C. Total RNA was extracted
using a Trizol reagent (1.0 mL). HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Per-
fect for qPCR was then employed to reversely transcribe the total RNA
to cDNA. Finally, the upstream and downstream primers were added, and
ampliﬁcation was carried out using ChamQ SYBR Colour qPCR Master Mix
(Low ROX Premixed). The primers used in this study are listed in Table S1
(Supporting Information). The values of Ct were measured using qPCR.
By using 16S rRNA1 (Table S1, Supporting Information) as the internal
reference gene, the relative expression of the target gene was calculated
using the 2−ΔΔCt method.[50] The identical procedure was applied to the
experiments for assessing gene expression regulated by siRNA1/PAuNCs.
The assessments of target gene expression for E. coli and P. aeruginosa
followed the identical procedure, except for the use of 10 μg mL−1 of the
PAuNCs for incubation with the E. coli and the P. aeruginosa for 2 h.
Phenotype Assessment for the Gene Suppression Bacteria by NA/PAuNCs:
The silencing eﬀect of NA/PAuNCs on the mecA gene was explored by
measuring the changes in the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)
of antibiotics for MRSA treated with NA/PAuNCs. A log-phase MRSA sus-
pension (90 μL, 106 CFU mL−1) was mixed with the ASO/PAuNCs (10 μL)
in a 96-well plate. Then, an oxacillin solution (100 μL) at a speciﬁc concen-
tration was added and incubated in a shaker (37°C, 220 rpm) for 10 h. In
the system (200 μL), the ﬁnal concentrations of the ASO and the PAuNCs
were 500 nM and 25 μg mL−1, respectively. The ﬁnal concentration of
oxacillin was varied at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128 μg mL−1. Fi-
nally, the optical density of each of the mixtures at 600 nm was measured.
At each of the oxacillin concentrations, a mixture of MRSA suspension and
antibiotics without the addition of the ASO/PAuNCs was used to serve as
the control for comparison. The same procedure was also applied to the
experiments with siRNA1.
The antibiotic resistance of MRSA treated with NA/PAuNCs was fur-
ther assessed using the spread plate method. MRSA suspensions (900 μL,
106 CFU mL−1) were mixed well with water (100 μL), the PAuNCs (100 μL,
250 μg mL−1), oxacillin (100 μL, 80 μg mL−1), the mixture of the PAuNCs
and oxacillin (100 μL, 250 μg mL−1 of the PAuNCs, 40 μg mL−1 of oxacillin),
the mixture of the ASO and oxacillin (100 μL, 5000 μg mL−1 of the ASO,
80 μg mL−1 of oxacillin), or the mixture of the ASO/PAuNCs and oxacillin
(100 μL, 5000 nM of the ASO, 250 μg mL−1 of the PAuNCs, 80 μg mL−1 of
oxacillin) and incubated in a shaker (37°C, 220 rpm) for 2 h. Subsequently,
100 μL of aliquot from each mixture was spread onto LB agar plates and
cultured at 37°C for 24 h. Colony growth was observed and photographed.
The same procedure was also followed for the experiments with siRNA1,
with oxacillin at a concentration of 40 μg mL−1.
The silencing eﬀect of siRNA2/PAuNCs on the ligA gene was analyzed
by measuring the mortality of the E. coli treated with siRNA2/PAuNCs. The
siRNA2/PAuNCs (100 μL) were mixed with a log-phase E. coli suspension
(900 μL, 106 CFU mL−1) in a centrifuge tube and incubated in a shaker
(37°C, 220 rpm) for 2 h. In the system (1.0 mL), the ﬁnal concentrations
of siRNA2 and the PAuNCs were 500 nM and 10 μg mL−1, respectively.
In parallel, water (100 μL), siRNA2 (100 μL, 5 μM), and c-siRNA loading
with the PAuNCs (c-siRNA/PAuNCs, 100 μL) were separately incubated
with the E. coli suspensions at 37°C for 2 h, which were used to serve as
the controls. After incubation, the bacteria were centrifuged (5000 rpm,
5 min) and washed three times with PBS. Afterward, propidium iodide
(100 μL, 9 μ) was added to the bacteria precipitate, followed by incubation
in a shaker (37°C, 220 rpm) for 25 min in the dark. Finally, the bacteria
were washed three times with PBS and resuspended in PBS. The mortality
of the E. coli was assessed using ﬂow cytometry.
The silencing eﬀect of siRNA3/PAuNCs on the pilQ gene was investi-
gated by assaying the twitching motility of the P. aeruginosa treated with
siRNA3/PAuNCs. The siRNA3/PAuNCs (20 μL) were mixed with a log-
phase P. aeruginosa suspension (180 μL, 106 CFU mL−1) in a 96-well plate
and incubated in a shaker (37°C, 220 rpm) for 2 h. In the system (200 μL),
the ﬁnal concentrations of siRNA3 and the PAuNCs were 500 nM and
10 μg mL−1, respectively, with a siRNA3-to-PAuNCs mass ratio of 1: 3.8.
In parallel, water (20 μL), siRNA3 (20 μL, 5 μM), and c-siRNA loaded by
the PAuNCs (c-siRNA/PAuNCs, 20 μL) were separately incubated with the
P. aeruginosa suspensions for at 37°C 2 h, which were used to serve as
the controls. After incubation, the bacterial suspension (5 μL) was aspi-
rated using a pipette, and the pipette tip was vertically stabbed into the
bottom center of the LB agar plate for inoculation. The P. aeruginosa was
then incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Afterward, the LB agar medium was re-
moved and the bacteria at the bottom of the plates were stained with crys-
tal violet solution (2%) for 15 min. Finally, the staining solution was re-
moved and washed three times with PBS. After drying, the diameter of
the motions was measured with a vernier caliper and photographed for
recording.

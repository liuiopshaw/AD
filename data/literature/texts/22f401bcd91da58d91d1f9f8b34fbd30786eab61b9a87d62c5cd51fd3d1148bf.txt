High performance textiles such as Kevlar® ﬁnd large use in many applications including aerospace ﬁeld, thus re-
quire antimicrobial characteristics to avoid the risk of microbial contamination on board of spacecraft and orbital
station during prolonged space exploration missions. In this study, Kevlar® fabric has been coated with antibac-
terial silver nanocluster/silica composite layer with different thicknesses, by means of radio frequency co-
sputtering technique. The ﬁbers morphology and composition has been analyzed by means of ﬁeld emission
scanning election microscopy (FESEM), energy dispersion spectrometry (EDS), x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS). Thermal properties have been evaluated through thermogravimetric experiments (TGA) and differential
scanning calorimetry (DSC). The ﬁbers result homogenously coated with the antibacterial layer composed of
silica matrix embedding silver nanoclusters. A controlled and gradual release of silver was observed in silver
leaching tests in water and artiﬁcial sweat. The antimicrobial behavior is veriﬁed through inhibition halo and
broth dilution tests against different bacterial and fungal species. The in vitro skin penetration test shows that
the Ag amount absorbed through the skin is mainly stored in epidermis. Tensile and tear properties were main-
tained and not degraded by the coating deposition. The reduction of perforation resistance after coating deposi-
tion could be considered negligible whereas abrasion test demonstrated an increased resistance of coated
sample.

1. Introduction
High performance textile materials are highly attractive for industri-
al and advanced applications including sport goods, personal protective
apparels as well as structural application, combining excellent mechan-
ical and chemical properties with light-weight characteristic [1]. The
commercial Kevlar® ﬁbers by Du Pont, are one of these high perfor-
mance textile ﬁbers. Kevlar® is an aramid ﬁbre characterized by high
ﬂame and chemical resistance, excellent thermal behavior and high
tensile properties and modulus [2]. The main applications of the aramid
ﬁbers include (but are not limited to) ballistic protection equipment,
tire reinforcements, sports goods [3], ropes, cables, cut resistant gloves
and composites for structural applications [1]. Recently, the use of tex-
tile support, including Kevlar®, is widely increased also in the aerospace
ﬁeld, including structural parts of the orbital modulus, environment
portion or containers for objects. The concept of inhabited inﬂatable
modules can be entirely implemented with high-performance polymers
and fabrics. As an example, Kevlar® textile was used in the aerospace
inﬂatable modulus for the realization of the structural restraint and
the internal barrier to protect the air containment bladder from the
accidentally induced aggression (i.e. perforation, ﬂame contact) by
crewmembers [4].
In addition to the structural integrity of these inﬂatable habitat mod-
ules, reducing the risk of microbial contaminations is of high importance
to guarantee good health, wellness and safety to the astronauts especially
during longer space missions [5,6]. In fact, the particular conditions of
humidity and temperature, the presence of humans and the reduced
hygiene in the space structures enhance the risk of the microorganism
colonization [5,7]. Bacteria and fungi are able to form a microbial bioﬁlm
which can undermine the astronauts health with pathogenic effects as al-
lergies or infections [6] as well as can potentially degrade internal habitat
surfaces, potable water and air system [8,9]. Microbial contamination in
space stations is kept under implemented thresholds by continuous mon-
itoring and disinfections methods, however, occasional contamination re-
ports signify further improvements and development of new prevention
methods [10].

Antimicrobial treatment of materials to be used in space stations, for
example ﬁbers and fabrics used in inﬂatable habitat module, can be an
effective preventive measure to provide healthy environment to the
astronauts. Most commonly used antimicrobial agents for textile
functionalization include N-halamines [11], chitosan [12], triclosan
and its derivatives [13], quaternary ammonium compounds [14] and
metal [15] and metal oxide [16] nanoparticles. Among various organic
and inorganic antimicrobial agents, silver nano particles (NPs) are
being studied extensively due to their effective biocidal action against
wide variety of microorganisms and their toxicity towards human
cells is perceived to be low [17]. Silver nano particles have also been
used in combination with other antimicrobial agents to obtain synergy
in antimicrobial performance [18].
Silver nanoparticles can be synthesized and applied to textiles using
various chemical and physical methods. Chemical synthesis involves
using a silver precursor, a reducing agent as well as a stabilizing agent
to avoid their aggregation [19]. However, these reducing and stabilizing
agents may not always be environment friendly, therefore, the focus of
research has gradually shifted towards more ecofriendly synthesis and
application of silver nano particles to textiles [20]. Recent approaches
to synthesize and apply silver NPs include pad-dry-cure method, for
an already synthesized silver NPs suspension [21], solution dipping in
an appropriate silver precursor solution and subsequent reduction [15,
22] and Layer by layer (LbL) technique to apply polymeric multilayers
to host and in situ reduce silver nano particles using UV radiation [23]
just to mention a few. It has also been shown recently that silver nano
particles can be synthesized in situ by exploiting inherent reducing ca-
pabilities of some natural ﬁbers like jute [19]. However, these chemical
synthesis routes may not be simple for synthetic ﬁbers having highly
inert surface like Kevlar®. Physical vapor deposition (PVD), for example
sputtering, is another approach to coat textiles with silver nanoparticles
[24,25]. PVD offers the advantage of being ecofriendly as it is a single
step dry process and does not involve the use of any reducing and/or
stabilization agent to synthesize and apply silver nanoparticles to
textiles.
The aim of this work is the deposition and characterization of silver
nanocluster/silica composite coating on Kevlar® fabric by means of
radio frequency (RF) co-sputtering technique, well described in previ-
ous works and applied to glasses [26–28], metals [29] and polymers
[30,31]. The thermal, chemical and mechanical stability of silica com-
bined with the antibacterial properties of silver improves the quality
of the deposited layer compared with other coatings. The silver
nanoclusters result well embedded into the silica matrix tuning silver
concentration and coating thickness [27,28]. It was demonstrated that
the coating was able retain antibacterial properties up to 450 °C [26].
One-step sputtering process avoids the use of solvents or toxic
chemicals and the direct nanoparticles manipulation. Recently, the
silver nanocluster/silica composite coating has been also applied and
studied by authors to different textile materials as nylon, fabric with
activated carbon and a mixture of synthetic ﬁbers [32] and natural
cotton [33].
2. Materials and methods
Kevlar® yarns (supplied by DuPont) were converted into plain
woven fabric by Iniziative Industriali (16 threads/cm resulting in a fabric
with weight per square meter of 250 ± 5% g/m2). The silver
nanocluster/silica composite coating was deposited by means of Radio
Frequency (RF) co-sputtering technique (Microcoat MS450) [26,27].
One-inch silver target (Sigma-Aldrich 99.99% purity) and six-inches
silica target (Franco Corradi S.r.l. 99.9% purity) were used for co-
deposition, applying a power of 200 W in RF mode for silica, 1 W in
DC for silver and operating pressure of 5.5 dPa. Several depositions
with durations of 15, 40 and 80 min were performed considering the
effect of a thin (60 nm), medium (150 nm) and thick (300 nm) coating,
respectively. The parameters were selected and optimized according to
previous depositions described in [30]. The uncoated Kevlar® textile
will be labelled as “Kev” in the text, adding the deposition time to the
“Kev” acronym for the coated samples.
2.1. Morphology, chemical and compositional analysis
The silver nanoclusters/silica composite coatings were observed by
means of ﬁeld emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM,
QUANTA INSPECT 200, Zeiss SUPRATM 40) equipped with electron ener-
gy dispersion spectrometry (EDS, EDAX PV 9900) for the compositional
analysis. A comparison with the uncoated textile was performed. X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS-Phi 5000 Versa Probe) was performed
on the coated and uncoated samples in order to discover the chemical
composition of the textile surface.
2.2. Thermal analysis
Thermogravimetric experiments (TGA) were performed on Kev and
Kev_40 samples (10–20 mg of material) at a rate of 30 °C/min from 20
°C to 470 °C with a SDT Q600 device (TA instrument). Kev and Kev_40
sample (5–7 mg of material) were analyzed by differential scanning
calorimetry (DSC) using DSC Q20 (TA instrument) from −70 °C to
180 °C at a rate of 20 °C/min. A pure nitrogen ﬂow was used.
2.3. Leaching test
The silver release from coated textiles was evaluated by means of
leaching test into two solutions: water and artiﬁcial sweat. In the ﬁrst
test, samples (1 × 1 cm2) were dipped into 25 ml of distilled water
(milli Q) at room temperature (R.T.) with coated side faced up, for 3,
24 and 72 h. “Silver photometer” from “Hanna Instruments” was used
for reading the amount of silver released in the water.
In the second test, Kev_40 samples (1 × 1 cm2) were immersed into
30 ml of artiﬁcial sweat at 37 °C for simulating the body temperature.
The artiﬁcial sweat was prepared by dissolving sodium chloride (0.5%
mass), lactic acid (0.1%mass) and urea (0.1% mass) in distilled water
[34]. One milliliter of solution was picked up after 3, 24 and 72 h. The
spilled solutions were analyzed by means of graphite furnace equipped
with inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS).
2.4. Antimicrobial tests
The antimicrobial behavior of the silver nanoclusters/silica composite
coating was tested by means of inhibition halo test (Kirby Bauer test) [35]
towards different Gram + and Gram-bacterial strains (Staphylococcus
aureus and Escherichia coli) and fungal strain (Candida albicans). Broth
dilution test was also performed against Staphylococcus aureus [36]. The
ﬁrst test is a semi-quantitative method where samples (1 × 1 cm2)
were put in direct contact with a suspension containing bacteria or
fungi colonies, spread on Mueller Hinton agar plates. After 24 h of incuba-
tion at 35 °C, the inhibition halo formation (i.e. a zone in which bacteria
did not proliferate) was observed and measured. Each specimen was
tested in duplicate. The broth dilution test allows the count of colony
forming units (CFU) of bacteria adhered on sample surface and proliferat-
ed in the broth. The samples were put in the bacterial broths containing
standard amount of bacteria, as described in [37]. For this purpose, 3 coat-
ed and 3 uncoated samples (dimension 0.5 × 2 cm2) were immersed in 6
Mueller Hinton broth tubes containing approximately 5 × 105 CFU ml−1
and incubated at 35 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the McFarland index of
each tube, indicating the solution turbidity, was measured by an optical
instrument (Phoenix Spec BD McFarland). The difference in the
McFarland index of broth tubes containing uncoated and coated speci-
mens is a semi-quantitative evaluation of the antibacterial activity of
coated specimens regarding bacterial proliferation. The number of CFU
both adhered on samples' surface and proliferated in the broths was
measured after a series of dilutions of the above incubated bacterial

2.5. Percutaneous penetration
Percutaneous penetration was studied by means of Franz static dif-
fusion cell method [38]. The skin, obtained from the back and arms of
two donors, was cryopreserved immediately after surgical removal.
Each skin piece was clamped between the donor and the receptor com-
partment of the diffusion cell; the exposed skin area was approximately
3.5 cm2 and the skin thickness was approximately 0.5 mm. Skin
integrity was assessed using electrical conductibility before and after
the experiment: cells with a resistance b3.95 ± 0.27 kΩ cm−2 were
considered to be damaged and rejected [38]. Two coated Kevlar® sam-
ple with an area of about 4 cm2 were soaked into each donor chamber,
containing 4 ml of synthetic sweat (0.5% sodium chloride, 0.1% urea, and
0.1% lactic acid, in Milli-Q water, with ammonium hydroxide to reach a
pH of 4.5). Other two cells were added as blank ﬁlled with synthetic
sweat only.
The receptor compartment (with a volume of 14.0 ml) was main-
tained at +32 °C by the circulation of thermostat water in the jacket
that surrounds the cell. The physiological solution (2.38 g of Na2HPO4,
0.19 g of KH2PO4, and 9 g of NaCl dissolved in 1 l of Milli-Q water)
with a ﬁnal pH of 7.35 was used as the receptor ﬂuid and was continu-
ously stirred using a Teﬂon-coated magnetic stirrer. The concentration
of the salt in the receptor ﬂuid was approximately the same as that pres-
ent in blood. 1.5 ml of the receptor solutions were collected for silver
concentration analysis at selected times (4, 8, 16, 20, and 24 h). Each
sample was immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh
physiological solution. The experiment was repeated twice for a total
of 4 independent cells. After 24 h, the donor phases were removed
from the donor chambers and coated Kevlar® sample was separated
from the synthetic sweat. Then, the skin samples were removed from
the diffusion cells and washed three times with 2 ml of Milli-Q water
to remove any excess silver from the surface. Each piece of exposed
skin was divided into two parts. The ﬁrst was used for quantifying the
total silver amount penetrated into the skin, separating dermis and
epidermis by immersion in +60 °C water for 1 min. Subsequently,
they were placed into a drying oven for 24 h and put into glass tubes
with 1 ml of HNO3 69% v/v for digestion. The resulting solutions were di-
luted with Milli-Q water for ICP-MS analysis. The second skin portion
was observed by means of SEM. Before analysis, it was ﬁxed with 10%
glutaraldehyde, washed with ethanol–water solutions with increasing
concentrations of ethanol, and then stored in ethanol at 98%.
The total silver concentration in the receptor and donor ﬂuids and in
the solutions derived from the skin mineralization was determined by
an ICP-MS NEXION 350D (Perkin Elmer; Waltham, MA, USA) with an in-
tegrated autosampler. The selected ion mass was 107 amu. The limit of
silver detection was 0.01 μg/l (standard solutions: 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0, 10.0
μg/l; RSD% for the analysis was always b5%). The accuracy was deter-
mined by analyzing certiﬁed reference material 1640a (trace elements
in natural water) of National Institute of Standards and Technology
(NIST); the analyzed results are within the range of the certiﬁed value
(8.081 ± 0.046 μg/l).
2.6. Mechanical test
A series of mechanical tests were carried out on the optimized coat-
ing in order to control the effect of the co-sputtering deposition on the
properties of the Kevlar® fabric. Tensile, tear, perforation and abrasion
tests were performed according to the respective UNI 8279-4:1984,
UNI 9073-4:1999, UNI 5421:1983 and UNI 12947-2:2000 standards
[39–42]. As described in the standards, the tensile and tear tests were
performed on the two directions of the textile material (along the longi-
tudinal direction of the weaving and along the transversal direction of
the weaving). Due to small dimension of sputtering chamber, samples
were subjected to tensile test with reduced dimensions with respect
to those referred in the standard (100 × 80 mm instead of 100
× 150 mm).
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Morphological, chemical and structural analysis
The silver nanoclusters/silica composite coating has been successful-
ly deposited on the Kevlar® textile. Longer deposition time increased
the coating thickness signiﬁcantly affecting the color of the fabric, as re-
ported in Fig. 1. The original yellow color of the uncoated textile (Kev)
changed from light (Kev_15) to dark brown (Kev_80). The brown
color mainly depends on the presence of silver. In fact the small
nanoclusters promote a scattering effect, with consequent surface
dark color, prevalent with respect to the surface plasmon resonance
effect as discussed in [26].
FESEM images and relative EDS analysis, with the calculated atomic
ratio Ag/(Ag + Si), of Kevlar® textiles before and after coating deposi-
tion at different process times, are reported in Fig. 2. The increment of
the deposition time and so the coating thickness is well visible on the ﬁ-
bers surface. Uncoated Kev (Fig. 2a) ﬁbers appear smooth and uniform
with some scratches on the surface. The surface of the Kev_15
(Fig. 2b) is similar to the uncoated one but silver nanoclusters are visible
as bright particles and well distributed. More the process time increases
more the morphology of the ﬁbers changes showing a porous and

3.2. Thermal analysis
Thermal properties of the Kevlar® were not signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced
by the deposition process of silver nanoclusters/silica composite coating.
Both uncoated and coated Kevlar result thermally stable up to 300 °C.
Fig. 5 shows the thermal behavior of the Kev and Kev_40 samples ana-
lyzed by means of TGA and DSC. Observing TGA curves (Fig. 5a), the ini-
tial weight loss (about 4%) is assigned to the removal of atmospheric
moisture adsorbed on the ﬁbers and a slight weight loss (1–1.5%) is no-
ticed in the range from 150 to 400 °C as reported in [46], thermal events
and the beginning of ﬁbers decomposition occur in the range 460–500
°C (out of this measurement range). DSC curves (Fig. 5b) result similar
for both samples showing an endothermic peak at low temperatures
in a range of about 25–150 °C. This energy could be attributed to possible
relaxation of polymeric chains in the Kevlar ﬁbers [46]. Both TGA and
DSC curves show that the thermal stability of Kevlar fabric was not
compromised after coating deposition.

3.3. Leaching tests
Even if the silver is in the metallic state, as detected by means of XPS
analysis (Fig. 4), it is well known that the Ag nanoparticles usually are
oxidized and can release Ag ions in contact with liquid solution. The
graph in Fig. 6 shows the cumulative amount of ionic silver released in
distilled water, comparing the curves relative to all the coated samples
as a function of immersion time. For all the three depositions, the
curve trend results quite steady for the ﬁrst 24 h whereas an increment
in the release occurs after 72 h, reaching the maximum Ag ion release
values between 0.12 and 0.45 μg/cm2. The total silver concentration,
as is reported later in “Skin permeation test”, has been found to be
123 ± 16 ppm (123 μg of silver per gram of the fabric), for 40 min depo-
sition (Kev_40). The ionic silver released from Kev_40 after 3, 24 and
72 h correspond to approximately 1.9%, 1.9% and 5.5% respectively of
the total deposited silver. Since the individual curves in Fig. 6 show sim-
ilar release trend, the same percentage of the total deposited silver is
also expected to be released for Kev_15 and Kev_80.
Sweat is a body ﬂuid that may come in direct contact with surfaces
containing metallic nanoparticles. Therefore, silver release behavior
from the coating was evaluated in synthetic sweat and results are
shown in Fig. 7. The kinetic curve of silver release in Fig. 7 shows a
time dependent release of silver in synthetic sweat with concentration
comparatively higher than that was released in water. However, for
both the liquid media, a gradual release of silver was observed which
could be a beneﬁcial aspect for a long term antibacterial activity.
Embedding silver nanoclusters in silica matrix may play an important
role in the regulation of silver ions release.
The ﬁnal application of the coated Kevlar®, considered in this study, is
a portion of aerospace inﬂatable modulus, which is not in direct contact
with liquid medium. However it must be considered that the textile ma-
terial is critically exposed to the peculiar conditions of the inﬂatable hab-
itat, as elevate humid degree (up to 70%) and human presence in a limited
and conﬁned environment. The tests were performed to evaluate the ion
release in more severe conditions (continuous immersion in water and
artiﬁcial sweat), a reduced exposure is expected during working situa-
tions which will further reduce the toxicity issues for the astronauts.

nanoclusters/silica composite layer was demonstrated via inhibition

also grew within the inhibition zone (but not under the sample) making

in the case of S. aureus. This indicates that probably the E. coli strain is
more resistant to the silver nanoclusters/silica composite coating in
comparison to S. aureus. Similar effect can be observed against Candida
albicans as up to 1 mm inhibition zone can be observed around the
sample. One corner of the sample was not well adhered to the agar

3.5. Skin permeation test
Silver nanoparticles are most commonly used nanomaterial found in
various consumer products. Therefore, it is important to evaluate the
safety and health risks associated with the potential dermal absorption
of these particles from topical use products as well as surfaces that may
come in direct contact with the body. However, there is still lack of sig-
niﬁcant experimental data about dermal penetration of silver nano par-
ticles from silver containing consumer products [38,49]. The potential of
permeation of the silver nanoclusters through the human skin was eval-
uated by immersing the coated Kevlar® (Kev_40) in the synthetic sweat
and analyzing its permeation in the receptor ﬂuid through the skin. The
cryopreserved human skin is considered to be quite similar to the fresh
skin, simplifying the performance of in vitro experiments for evaluating
silver permeation after skin exposure to AgNPs [50]. During the ﬁrst
24 h, the level of silver, released in synthetic sweat, was 5.6 ± 0.2% of
the original concentration of silver in the textile material (123 ±
16 ppm), and it corresponds to the silver dose in contact with the skin
surface of 0.44 ± 0.05 μg/cm2. Fig. 11 provides the graph relative to
the silver permeation proﬁle. At 24 h, the total amount permeated in
the acceptor compartment was 2.57 ± 0.89 ng/cm2. The medium ﬂux
calculated in the 24 h was 0.18 ± 0.08 ng/cm2 h and the lag time was
8.4 ± 0.4 h.
The amount of silver penetrated in the epidermis (0.073 ± 0.007
μg/cm2) was higher than in the dermis (0.014 ± 0.002 μg/cm2). The
amount of silver penetrated into and across the skin can be considered
low and silver is known for its non-toxicity to human cells at low con-
centrations [17]. However, several experimental variables, for example
very low exposed skin area compared with total body area [51] and
no complete adhesion of the textile to the skin, may lead to under
estimation of the real absorption potential. Furthermore, there is lack
of reference for exposure limits to silver nanomaterials [38], which
makes it further difﬁcult to assess full risk potential.
3.6. Mechanical test
Ensuring structural integrity of the parts made of high performance
ﬁbers and fabrics is very important for various aerospace and other ap-
plications. Finishing treatments may affect negatively the mechanical
properties of the ﬁbers and hence compromising the strength of the ma-
terial at the expense of functional performance. Therefore, mechanical
properties of the Kevlar fabric, before and after coating, were analyzed
in detail. Table 1 reports data from mechanical tests. The sputtering of
silver nanoclusters/silica composite coating did not signiﬁcantly affect
tensile and tear properties. Negligible differences were noticed in ten-
sile strength and tear strength of the fabric, both in longitudinal and
transvers direction of the fabric, after coating deposition. The puncture
resistance slightly decreased whereas an improvement in the abrasion
behavior can be observed for the coated fabric which can be attributed
to the composite coating offering resistance to abrasion. Increase in
abrasion resistance can be useful feature for enhanced wear and tear
resistance of fabric in inﬂatable modulus.
The unchanged or improved mechanical properties could be a bene-
ﬁcial feature for aerospace applications where Kevlar® is used in struc-
tural parts subjected to mechanical stresses (cycles of deployment and
employment of the inﬂatable modulus) and acts as protection barrier
of the air containment bladder.
4. Conclusions
Kevlar® fabric, intended to be used in the ﬂexible and lightweight
part of the aerospace inﬂatable modulus, has been coated with antibac-
terial silver nanoclusters/silica composite coating via co-sputtering
technique. The coating is composed of silver nanoclusters well embed-
ded into a porous silica matrix. The silver nanoclusters in the metallic
state were conﬁrmed by XPS analysis. Thermal properties of the textile

Intranasal Delivery of Pure Nanodrug Loaded Liposomes for
Alzheimer’s Disease Treatment by Eﬃciently Regulating
Microglial Polarization
Qianhua Feng, Xueli Zhang, Xiaowen Zhao, Jia Liu, Qing Wang, Yuqi Yao, Huifang Xiao,
Yucui Zhu, Wenwen Zhang, and Lei Wang*
The activated M1-like microglia induced neuroinﬂammation is the
critical pathogenic event in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Microglial polarization
from pro-inﬂammatory M1 toward anti-inﬂammatory M2 phenotype is a
promising strategy. To eﬃciently accomplish this, amyloid-𝜷(A𝜷) aggregates
as the culprit of M1 microglia activation should be uprooted. Interestingly,
this study ﬁnds out that the self-reassembly of curcumin molecules into
carrier-free curcumin nanoparticles (CNPs) exhibits multivalent binding with
A𝜷to achieve higher inhibitory eﬀect on A𝜷aggregation, compared to free
curcumin with monovalent eﬀect. Based on this, the CNPs loaded cardiolipin
liposomes are developed for eﬃcient microglial polarization. After intranasal
administration, the liposomes decompose to release CNPs and cardiolipin
in response to AD oxidative microenvironment. The CNPs inhibit A𝜷
aggregation and promote A𝜷phagocytosis/clearance in microglia, removing
roadblock to microglial polarization. Subsequently, CNPs are endocytosed
by microglia and inhibit TLR4/NF-𝜿B pathway for microglia polarization
(M1→M2). Meanwhile, cardiolipin is identiﬁed as signaling molecule to
normalize microglial dysfunction to prevent pro-inﬂammatory factors release.
In AD transgenic mice, neuroinﬂammation, A𝜷burden, and memory deﬁcits
are relieved after treatment. Through combined attack by extracellularly
eradicating roadblock of A𝜷aggregation and intracellularly inhibiting
inﬂammation-related pathways, this nanotechnology assisted delivery system
polarizes microglia eﬃciently, providing a reliable strategy in AD treatment.

1. Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most in-
tractable and common neurodegenerative
disease, which becomes an economic and
social burden.[1] Microglia activation in-
duced neuroinﬂammation is considered as
one of the characteristic pathological hall-
marks of AD.[2,3] Regulating microglial po-
larization from pro-inﬂammatory M1 to-
ward anti-inﬂammatory M2 phenotype is
a promising strategy for AD treatment.[4–7]
However, only intervening inﬂammation-
related pathways of microglia is far from
enough for eﬃcient polarization, because
there is abnormal crosstalk between dys-
functional microglia and amyloid-𝛽(A𝛽)
protein.[8,9] Speciﬁcally, A𝛽aggregate (e.g.,
ﬁbril) is not only an inducer of M1-
like microglia but also a roadblock to
M2 polarization of microglia, which re-
sults in low polarization eﬃciency of M2-
like microglia in clinical studies.[10,11] In
this sense, the importance of A𝛽can-
not be ignored, it may be a good idea
to polarize microglia through combined
attack by extracellularly eradicating road-
block of A𝛽aggregation and intracellularly
inhibiting inﬂammation-related pathways.
Microglia as the main neuroimmune cells of central ner-
vous system, are responsible for neuroinﬂammation and A𝛽
phagocytosis.[12,13] In response to diﬀerent stimuli, the resting
microglia are activated into two phenotypes, which may aggra-
vate or antagonize AD progression. In detail, toxic A𝛽aggregates
activate the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor kappa-B
(NF-𝜅B) pathway to induce pro-inﬂammatory M1 microglia in
the early stage of AD, which induces A𝛽phagocytosis disorder
as well as promotes pro-inﬂammatory factors release to dam-
age neurons and accelerate AD progression.[14,15] In contrast,
anti-inﬂammatory M2 microglia secrete anti-inﬂammatory fac-
tors and brain derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) for the pro-
tection, repair, and regeneration of neurons.[16,17] Growing ev-
idences suggest that natural compound curcumin (Cur) could
not only bind A𝛽via noncovalent interaction (e.g., hydrophobic
interaction, hydrogen bonding interaction) to restrict A𝛽-A𝛽in-
teraction to prevent A𝛽monomer from aggregating into toxic

application of Cur presents formidable challenges due to the fol-

owing to monovalent interaction (interaction involving single lig-
and and receptor) between free Cur and A𝛽; insuﬃcient thera-

with the development of nanotechnology, nanoparticle forms of
Cur molecules with enhanced performance under in vitro and in
vivo conditions may tackle the above issues.
The inspiration that nature brings us exists everywhere.
Compared with monovalent interaction, multivalent interac-
tion (simultaneous interactions involving multiple ligands and
receptors) exhibits higher aﬃnity for ligands, which widely
presents in biological processes such as recognition and adhe-
sion processes.[22,23] Inspired by natural multivalency eﬀect, self-
reassembly of Cur molecules into carrier-free pure Cur nanopar-
ticles (CNPs) may be a possible selection to meet these chal-
lenges. In this work, the surface of CNPs exposes and pro-
vides multiple A𝛽-binding sites for multivalency eﬀect to achieve
stronger A𝛽-binding event than free Cur or carrier-assistant nan-
odrug, showing excellent inhibitory eﬀect on A𝛽aggregation.
What is more, self-reassembled CNPs as pure nanodrugs possess
other advantages such as high nanoparticle formation rate, avoid-
ing the toxic side-eﬀects of additional inert nanocarrier.[24–26]
With the help of nanotechnology, CNPs in this study are found to
inhibit A𝛽aggregation eﬃciently and further modulate microglia
polarization from M1 toward M2. After that, A𝛽phagocytosis dis-
orders of microglia is also alleviated to remove A𝛽.
For avoiding the interaction between CNPs and nontarget
protein before reaching AD lesion, CNPs are loaded into re-
active oxygen species (ROS) responsive cardiolipin liposomes
(RCLs) to obtain RCLs@CNPs (Scheme 1). To solve the dilemma
of poor blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration of oral or par-
enteral administration, intranasal delivery is applied to transport
RCLs@CNPs into brain through olfactory and trigeminal nerves
bypassing BBB. What’s more, its several advantages including
ease of self-use, noninvasiveness, rapid onset of action, and high
cerebral bioavailability make it become an alternative for admin-
istration route of central nervous system disorders.[27–31] In re-
sponse to oxidative stress in AD microenvironment, the RCLs
disintegrate to release CNPs and cardiolipin. Compared to free
Cur, CNPs exhibit higher inhibitory eﬀect on A𝛽aggregation,
thus removing roadblock to M2 microglial polarization. Subse-
quently, beneﬁting from the intrinsic phagocytic properties of
microglia, CNPs are taken up by microglia and further inhibit
TLR4/NF-𝜅B pathway for microglia polarization from M1 toward
M2. Moreover, cardiolipin, originally a mitochondrial phospho-
lipid, is identiﬁed as a signaling molecule when released outside
the cell, which normalizes microglial immunologic dysfunction
to prevent pro-inﬂammatory factors release.[32,33] Results showed
that the RCLs@CNPs reduced A𝛽burden, alleviated neuroin-
ﬂammation, and rescued memory deﬁcits in AD transgenic mice.
Inspired by nature, through combined attack by extracellularly
eradicating roadblock of A𝛽aggregation and intracellularly in-
hibiting inﬂammation-related pathways, this simple pure nan-
odrug loaded liposome eﬃciently regulates M2 microglial polar-
ization, providing a new and reliable strategy for AD treatment.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Synthesis and Characterization of RCLs@CNPs
Inspired by natural multivalency eﬀect, the carrier-free pure nan-
odrugs (CNPs) are conductive to providing multivalent binding
eﬀect with A𝛽. Then a simple and green reprecipitation method
was used to synthesize CNPs, as shown in Figure 1a. When free
Cur in ethanol was dropped into water, the hydrophobic Cur
molecules self-reassembled into CNPs via intermolecular inter-
actions (e.g., hydrophobic interactions) during solvent-exchange
process. X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) analysis was carried out to prove
the synthesis (Figure 1b). Although Cur displayed XRD peaks
at 8.9°, 17.3°, 19.5°, 25.6°, and 29.0°, these peaks disappeared
or weakened for CNPs group, which indicated that Cur from
crystalline phase converted into amorphous CNPs. Without ad-
ditional inert nanomaterials, the pure CNPs showed a narrow
size distribution with diameter of ≈53 nm, and a surface charge
of −37.8 mV (Figure S1, Supporting Information). Transmission
electron microscopy (TEM) image showed its uniform morphol-
ogy of spherical structure in Figure 1c. Notably, compared with
water-insoluble Cur, CNPs dispersed well in aqueous solution
thanks to the large surface area of nanoparticles (Figure 1d),
which was expected to improve bioavailability and eﬃcacy.
Next, cardiolipin liposomes (RCLs) were prepared by thin-ﬁlm
dispersion method.[34–36] And RCLs@CNPs were obtained after
multi-step extrusion of RCLs and CNPs (Figure 1a), achieving
high encapsulation eﬃciency of 76.2%. TEM showed that the
size of RCLs@CNPs was ≈70 nm, slightly larger than that of
CNPs (Figure 1c). Furthermore, zeta potential of RCLs@CNPs
was −12.3 mV, close to that of RCLs (Figure 1e). After storage
of up to 7 days at 4 °C, the RCLs@CNPs still remained well dis-
persed without noticeable change in size (Figure 1f), implying ex-
cellent stability and dispersibility. Considering AD microenviron-
ment characterized by high oxidative stress and inﬂammatory,
the behavior of RCLs@CNPs under conditions of oxidative stress
was investigated.[37] When incubating RCLs@CNPs in PBS con-
taining H2O2, TEM showed their morphology and diameter of
nanoparticles were consistent with those of CNPs, revealing the
disintegration of RCLs in response to ROS (Figure 1c). To fur-
ther investigate the ROS responsive disintegration behavior of
RCLs, Cur was loaded into RCLs to obtain RCLs@C, and the
drug release proﬁle of RCLs@C was assessed by dialysis method
(Figure 1g). The Cur release rate reached 80.7% when incuba-
tion in PBS containing H2O2, far higher than that (13.6%) of
RCLs@C without H2O2 group. These results conﬁrmed the ROS
responsive decompose behavior of RCLs, thereby releasing CNPs
and cardiolipin to interfere with A𝛽and microglia.

2.3. Regulation of Microglia Polarization
Then the eﬀect of RCLs@CNPs on cell viability was investi-
gated (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Results showed there
was no signiﬁcant toxicity on neuron-like rat pheochromocy-
toma (PC12) cells and murine microglial cells (BV-2), indicating
good biocompatibility of RCLs@CNPs. M2-polarized microglia
cells were recognized as immune cells for A𝛽phagocytosis and
clearance. When BV-2 microglia were incubated with FITC-A𝛽,
only a dim ﬂuorescence of FITC-A𝛽adhered to the surface of
cells (Figure 3a). These results were assigned to that toxic A𝛽
aggregates activated microglia into pro-inﬂammatory phenotype
with A𝛽phagocytosis disorder. In contrast, Cur, CNPs, RCLs@C,
and RCLs@CNPs groups promoted A𝛽phagocytosis/clearance
of microglia with varying degrees, as substantiated by colocaliza-
tion of A𝛽and lysosomes.

2.4. In Vivo Therapeutic Eﬀect of RCLs@CNPs
Before evaluating the in vivo therapeutic eﬀect of RCLs@CNPs,
the BBB penetration has been a concern. Fluorescence imag-
ing was used to examine the brain targeting eﬃciency of
RCLs@CNPs via intravenous and intranasal administration.
Compared to mice after intravenous administration, ﬂuo-
rescence signal of Cyanine5 (Cy5) labeled RCLs@CNPs in
brain tissue increased 1.6-fold after intranasal administration
(Figure 5a; Figure S8, Supporting Information). These results
implied that intranasal delivery provided good bioavailability of
drugs for brain disorders, which transported drug into brain
through olfactory and trigeminal nerves bypassing BBB. Fur-
thermore, the anti-AD therapeutic eﬀect of RCLs@CNPs was
investigated in vivo. Herein, APP/PS1 transgenic mice were
used as AD model mice, which exhibited AD related clinical
manifestations such as memory and learning deﬁcits. Both of
APP/PS1 transgenic mice and wild-type (WT) mice (7 months
old) were administrated intranasally once a day (Figure 5b). Af-
ter one month of treatment, behavior tests such as Morris wa-
ter maze (MWM) and nesting experiment were carried out. Dur-
ing the training process in MWM, compared to saline group,
RCLs@CNPs shortened the escape latency remarkably, reach-
ing a level similar to WT group (Figure 5d). After removing
escape platform, mice after saline treatment exhibited an aim-
less swimming path, however, other groups showed improved
focused search strategies in varying degrees (Figure 5c). It was
noteworthy that RCLs@CNPs group spent the longest time
in targeted quadrant, and simultaneously exhibited the most
frequent crossings of the escape platform than other groups
(Figure 5e), which rescued memory and learning deﬁcits signif-
icantly. Afterward, experimental nesting data showed APP/PS1
mice failed to build the complete nest due to cognitive im-
pairment (Figure 5f). By contrast, the nest construction per-
formance of Cur, CNPs, RCLs@C, and RCLs@CNPs treated
mice obtained higher nesting scores, respectively. Of note, the
RCLs@CNPs treated mice possessed excellent nesting skills.
The above results corroborated that the pure nanodrug loaded
liposomes (RCLs@CNPs) relieved neuropsychiatric symptoms
remarkably.
After treatments, the eﬀect of RCLs@CNPs on pathological
changes in brain was further examined. Numerous A𝛽plaques
were observed in brain of saline treated mice (Figure 6a), im-
plying the serious A𝛽accumulation. By contrast, RCLs@CNPs
caused reduction of A𝛽deposition, which conﬁrmed the syn-
thesized liposomes inhibited A𝛽aggregation and promoted A𝛽
clearance. In addition, representative immunoﬂuorescence im-
ages (Figure 6b) showed that A𝛽burden (green) recruited ac-
tivated microglia (red), and RCLs@CNPs treatment decreased
plaque area obviously, which were in consistent with the above
results. Compared to saline group, RCLs@CNPs reduced expres-
sions of inﬂammatory factor (TNF-𝛼and iNOS) and ROS, but
increased expression of anti-inﬂammatory Arg-1 in hippocam-
pus (Figure 6c,d). The relieved inﬂammation brain environment
could be assigned to the microglia polarization from M1 to-
ward M2. Then morphology of neurons was detected by Nissl
staining (Figure 6e). It was observed that the outline of neu-
rons was clear in WT group, with nucleoli and a large number
of Nissl bodies presented in the cytoplasm. However, the Nissl
bodies in AD mice underwent chromatolysis signiﬁcantly. For-
tunately, RCLs@CNPs treatment restored the number of Nissl
bodies and improved neuronal integrity. The reduced Nissl score
after RCLs@CNPs treatment indicated their neuroprotection ef-
fect (Figure S9, Supporting Information). In addition, the postsy-
naptic marker (postsynaptic density-95, PSD-95) and presynap-
tic marker (synaptophysin, SYN) in hippocampus were investi-
gated (Figure S10, Supporting Information). Results showed that
a marked reduction in co-clusters of PSD-95 and SYN was found
in APP/PS1 mice, indicating the reduction in synapses. However,
RCLs@CNPs treatment restored the expressions of PSD-95 and
SYN. These results implied that RCLs@CNPs reduced the toxic
eﬀects of A𝛽on synapses, which improved the synaptic plasticity.
Hematoxylin/eosin (HE) staining of tissues showed there was no
obvious histopathological lesion in major tissues after treatment
(Figure S11, Supporting Information), implying the biosafety of
RCLs@CNPs.

liposome (RCLs@CNPs) based on microglia polarization strat-

devised with a focus on eﬃcient microglial polarization, through
combined attack by extracellularly eradicating roadblock of A𝛽
aggregation and intracellularly inhibiting inﬂammation-related
pathways. Notably, with the enhanced performance of nanotech-
nology, the self-reassembly of Cur molecules into carrier-free

4. Experimental Section
Materials:
Curcumin, cholesterol, cardiolipin, 1,2-Dioleoyl-sn-glycero-
3-phosphocholine (DOPC), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine
(DSPC), DSPC-Polyethylene glycol 2000 (DSPC-PEG2000), Thioﬂavin T
(ThT), Cyanine5 (Cy5) were purchased from Aladdin-Reagent Co. Ltd.
A𝛽42 were synthesized by GL Biochem Ltd. Other reagents were standard
Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (Shanghai, China).
Synthesis and Characterization of RCLs@CNPs:
CNPs were synthe-
sized via green reprecipitation method. Brieﬂy, free Cur (2.2 mg) was dis-
solved in ethanol (1 mL), then quickly dropped into water (5 mL) under vig-
orous stirring. After solvent-exchange process, the solution turned orange-
yellow, and CNPs were puriﬁed and concentrated by dialysis and centrifu-
gation. Then RCLs were prepared by by thin-ﬁlm dispersion method. In
a typical run, DSPC (5.0 mg), DSPC-PEG2000 (0.2 mg), DOPC (0.6 mg),
cholesterol (2.4 mg), cardiolipin (0.8 mg) were dissolved in chloroform
(5 mL) and placed in eggplant shaped bottle. The above organic solvent
was evaporated at 40 °C on a rotary evaporator to form a lipophilic thin
ﬁlm. Then the ﬁlm was dissolved in PBS and sonicated for 3 min to ob-
tain RCLs. For CNPs loading, the lipophilic ﬁlm was dissolved in CNPs
solution and sonicated for 3 min. And RCLs@CNPs were obtained after
multi-step extrusion. The encapsulation eﬃciency of RCLs@CNPs was de-
tected by UV–vis spectrometer at 422 nm. The morphology of samples
was characterized by TEM (JEM-1200EX, Tokyo, Japan). Zeta potential
and diameter of nanoparticles were investigated by dynamic light scat-
tering instrument. To investigate the drug release proﬁle of Cur loaded
RCLs (RCLs@C), RCLs@C was placed in dialysis bag and incubated in
PBS containing H2O2 (1 mm) at 37 °C under gentle shaking. At regu-
lar time intervals, the amount of released Cur was quantiﬁed by UV–vis
spectrometer.

A𝛽Aggregation Inhibition Eﬀect of RCLs@CNPs:
To prepare A𝛽
monomer, freeze-dried A𝛽powder (1 mg) was dissolved in pre-cooled
1,1,1,3,3,3-hexaﬂuoro-2-propanol (1 mL). The suspension suﬀered ultra-
sonication in ice bath and further vortex, to decompose the pre-existing A𝛽
aggregates to obtain A𝛽monomer. The solution was allowed to stand and
evaporate, then divided into tubes for storage. Next, Thioﬂavin T (ThT) ﬂu-
orescence assay and morphological characterization were carried out to in-
vestigate A𝛽aggregation inhibition eﬀect of RCLs@CNPs. A𝛽monomers
(50 μm) were incubated with Cur, CNPs, RCLs@C, or RCLs@CNPs (Cur
concentration: 12.5 μm) in PBS containing H2O2 at 37 °C, respectively.
Samples (50 μL) were removed at regular time intervals, and mixed with
150 μL of ThT (20 μm) in PBS and incubated for 15 min in the dark. The
ThT ﬂuorescence intensity was determined by ﬂuorescence spectropho-
tometer (𝜆ex = 440 nm, 𝜆ex = 480 nm). After 7 days of incubation, the
morphology of A𝛽ﬁbrils was characterized by TEM.
Molecular Dynamic Simulation:
Cur/A𝛽interactions and CNPs/A𝛽in-
teractions were analyzed under 10 ns molecular dynamic simulation. The
structure of A𝛽was searched on Protein Data Bank. Gromacs-4.6.7 soft-
ware was used for all simulations. The van der Waals energy, electrostatic
energy and number of hydrogen bonds for molecular dynamic simulation
process were recorded.
Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope (CLSM):
Before in vitro experi-
ments, A𝛽monomers were incubated alone in PBS containing H2O2 at
37 °C for 3 days to form A𝛽ﬁbrils. Moreover, A𝛽monomers were incubated
with Cur, CNPs, RCLs@C, or RCLs@CNPs to inhibit A𝛽aggregation. To
investigate the phagocytosis of microglia on A𝛽aggregates, murine mi-
croglial cells (BV-2) were incubated with medium containing FITC-A𝛽and
formulations (A𝛽:25 μm, Cur:12.5 μm) for 6 h. Then cells were washed with
PBS and incubated with Lyso-Tracker Red for 20 min. Finally, Cells were im-
aged by CLSM (Zeiss Co., Germany). To assess the eﬀect of RCLs@CNPs
on microglia phenotype, BV-2 cells were incubated with medium con-
taining A𝛽and formulations (A𝛽:25 μm, Cur:12.5 μm) for 6 h. Cells were
washed with PBS and ﬁxed with paraformaldehyde (4%), and then incu-
bated with anti-iNOS, anti-Arg-1, anti-TNF-𝛼, and further secondary anti-
bodies. Fluorescence images of cells were acquired by using CLSM. For the
experiment of FITC-A𝛽adhesions on cells, rat pheochromocytoma (PC12)
cells were cultured in 6-well plate overnight, then treated with FITC-A𝛽and
formulations (A𝛽:25 μm, Cur:12.5 μm) for 6 h. After washing with PBS, cells
were stained with DAPI, and observed under CLSM.
Flow Cytometry Analysis:
BV-2 cells were incubated with IL-4 for 24 h to
obtain M2-like microglia. In addition, cells were also treated with A𝛽and
formulations (Cur, CNPs, RCLs@C, or RCLs@CNPs) to evaluate the phe-
notype of microglia. Cells were collected by centrifugation, and incubated
with anti-CD86, anti-CD206, and analyzed by ﬂow cytometer.
Western Blotting Analysis:
BV-2 cells were cultured with A𝛽and formu-
lations (Cur, CNPs, RCLs@C, or RCLs@CNPs). Then cells were treated
with trypsin, collected, and lysed with RIPA buﬀer. The protein concentra-
tion of extracts was quantiﬁed by using BCA protein assay kit according
to the manufacturers’ instructions. Samples were loaded on SDS-PAGE
gels and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane. Then membranes were
incubated with primary antibodies including anti-TLR4, anti-NF-𝜅B, and
appropriate secondary antibodies.
Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA):
BV-2 cells (3 × 105 per
well) were seeded in 6-well plate for 24 h, then washed with PBS and
treated with A𝛽and formulations (Cur, CNPs, RCLs@C, or RCLs@CNPs)
for 24 h. Levels of TNF-𝛼, IL-6, IL-4, and BDNF in supernatants were exam-
ined by using ELISA technology according to the manufacturers’ instruc-
tions.
Cell Viability Assay:
Transwell-based microglia-neuron co-culture
model was used to evaluate the interlocking connection between microglia
and neurons. BV-2 cells were placed in the upper compartment and cul-
tured with A𝛽and diﬀerent preparations, PC12 cells were placed in the
lower compartment. After treatment for 24 h, the PC12 cell viability was
detected by using MTT assay.
Apoptosis Assay:
PC12 cells were incubated with medium containing
FITC-A𝛽and formulations (A𝛽:25 μm, Cur:12.5 μm) for 6 h. Cells were
washed with PBS for three times, and then collected by centrifugation.
Cells were added with Annexin-FITC and Propidium Iodide, cultured for an-
other 15 min under dark. Flow cytometer instrument (BD AccuriC6, Ameri-
can) was used to quantify apoptotic cells, and FlowJo software was applied
for data analysis.
In Vivo Imaging:
Mice were administrated intravenously or in-
tranasally with Cyanine5 (Cy5) labeled RCLs@CNPs. At 24 h post-
injection, mice were sacriﬁced. Then tissues were removed and imaged
by using ﬂuorescence imaging system.
Treatment Regimens In Vivo:
Male APP/PS1 transgenic mice (C57BL/6
J-TgN(APP/PS1)ZLFILAS, 7 months old) were divided into ﬁve groups,
and administrated intranasally with saline, Cur, CNPs, RCLs@C, and
RCLs@CNPs (Cur: 7.36 mg mL−1, 20 μL) once a day, respectively. Wild-
type (WT) mice (7 months old) were administrated with saline as the con-
trol group. After treatment for one month, behavior tests such as Morris
water maze and nesting experiment were carried out. Then mice were sac-
riﬁced, tissues were removed for pathological analysis. All animal handling
procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Insti-
tutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhengzhou University.
Behavior Tests:
For Morris water maze (MWM) test, the circular pool
was divided into four quadrants, and equipped with a escape platform in
the northwest quadrant. During the ﬁve-day training period, mice were al-
lowed to swim for 60 s in the pool until they reached the escape platform.
If mice were unable to ﬁnd the platform within 60 s, the mice would be
guided to the platform to rest for 15 s. The time when mice found escape
platform was recorded as escape latency. On the sixth day, escape platform
was removed. A video-tracking system was used to record the swimming
path of mice within 60 s. The number of crossing escape platform, per-
cent time in targeted quadrant were recorded to evaluate the memory and
learning abilities of mice. For the nesting experiment, mice were individ-
ually housed in cage and adapt the environment for two days. Then ten
pieces of white paper (5 × 5 cm2) were placed on one side of the cage,
and nesting ability of mice was recorded at day 0, 1, 2, and 3. The criteria
for nest score were as follows: 1 point, the papers were not clearly bitten,
and the nest site was not obvious; 2 point, the papers were not clearly bit-
ten, and the nest site could be identiﬁed; 3 point, some papers were clearly
torn, and the nest site could be identiﬁed; 4 point, the papers were clearly
bitten, and the nest site could be clearly identiﬁed.
Pathological Investigation:
After behavior tests, mice were sacriﬁced.
Brains were removed, ﬁxed in formalin, and then embedded in paraﬃn.
Brains were incubated with anti-A𝛽, anti-IBA1, anti-iNOS, anti-Arg-1, and
anti-TNF-𝛼. The pathological changes were imaged by CLSM (Zeiss Co.,
Germany). In addition, tissues were also removed for H&E staining.
Statistical Analysis:
Experimental data was represented by means ±
SD, and the statistical analysis was analyzed and plotted using GraphPad
Prim 8.0 software. The diﬀerences between groups were analyzed using
the One-way ANOVA test. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 could be
considered signiﬁcant.

CONSPECTUS: Atomically precise metal nanoclusters (NCs) are a class of nanomaterials
composed of a specific number of metal atoms stabilized by well-defined organic ligands. These
NCs exhibit molecular-like electronic states and offer exceptional control over their
optoelectronic properties. Recent advancements have extended their photoluminescence
deep into the near-infrared II (NIR-II) window (950−1700 nm), a spectral region that
provides significant advantages for biomedical imaging and photonic applications, including
reduced tissue scattering, minimal autofluorescence, and enhanced penetration depth. In
comparison to conventional quantum dots and larger plasmonic nanoparticles, atomically
precise metal NCs offer unprecedented tunability in terms of emission wavelength, quantum
yield, and photostability, facilitated by the modulation of size, composition, and ligand shell
chemistry.
In this Account, we highlight cutting-edge strategies, including ligand engineering, core−shell
engineering, and alloying, which enable fine-tuning of NIR-II photoluminescence in metal
NCs. We also explore the photophysical mechanisms underlying NIR-II emission, such as core−ligand charge transfer, metal-
centered transitions, and the role of surface electronic states in radiative recombination efficiency. Advanced spectroscopic
techniques, such as time-resolved photoluminescence and transient absorption, are discussed for their ability to probe excited-state
lifetimes and energy transfer processes that control the emission properties. Finally, we critically address the current limitations in
quantum yield enhancement, long-term photostability, and biocompatibility while outlining future directions for developing hybrid
materials and multifunctional NC platforms and advancing NIR-II photonic technologies. Our Account aims to offer molecular-level
insights and guide the rational design of next-generation atomically precise metal NCs as versatile materials for advanced NIR-II
photoluminescence.

1. INTRODUCTION
Atomically precise metal nanoclusters (NCs) are a distinctive
class of nanomaterials composed of a specific number of metal
atoms stabilized by well-defined organic ligands.5,6 Their
molecular-like electronic states arise from their precisely defined
atomic structures, offering unprecedented control over their
optical, electronic, and catalytic properties.7−12 This precision
enables the exploration of fundamental structure−property
relationships, making these NCs highly versatile for a wide range
of applications. In particular, near-infrared (NIR) photo-
luminescence, especially in the second near-infrared window
(NIR-II, 950−1700 nm), has emerged as a promising feature of
metal NCs for biomedical imaging and optoelectronic
applications.13,14 Compared with visible-region emission
(400−700 nm), which suffers from strong tissue scattering,
shallow penetration depth, and significant autofluorescence
interference, NIR-II emission enables deeper light penetration
into biological tissues, reduced scattering, and minimal back-
ground noise. These advantages produce clearer, higher-contrast
images and allow precise, noninvasive visualization of deep-
seated tissues, blood vessels, and tumors.15,16 Unlike conven-
tional emitters such as semiconductor quantum dots or larger
plasmonic nanoparticles, atomically precise metal NCs provide
unique opportunities to tailor NIR-II optical properties through
controlled modulation of NC size, core composition (including
alloying and heteroatom doping), and ligand environment.17−20
Their ultrasmall dimensions, excellent photostability, and
biocompatibility, combined with facile surface functionalization,
make metal NCs an especially promising platform for the
development of next-generation NIR-II probes designed for
translational biomedical imaging.
However, engineering atomically precise NCs with stable,
efficient, and tunable NIR-II emission remains a significant
challenge. The interplay between the electronic structure of the
NC core and the effects of the surrounding ligand shell plays a
crucial role in determining photoluminescence properties.21,22
Quantum confinement effects influence the size-dependent
emission properties of NCs. At the same time, surface chemistry
plays a crucial role in the emission stability and efficiency.
Defects in the ligand shell can act as nonradiative decay centers,
reducing overall performance.23,24 Recent developments have
leveraged strategies such as ligand engineering, core−shell
modulation, and alloying to fine-tune the NIR-II emission of
metal NCs.17−20 These strategies have expanded the chemical
space for NIR-II emitters, improving emission properties and
enhancing stability and efficiency. However, challenges remain
in achieving reproducible synthesis, high quantum efficiency,
and environmental stability, particularly for metal NCs that are
prone to oxidation or surface degradation.
This Account highlights recent progress in the synthesis,
structural characterization, and photophysical mechanisms
governing NIR-II emission in atomically precise metal NCs.
We discuss key advances in synthetic methodologies, the
influence of core and ligand modifications on emission
characteristics, and emerging trends that are poised to overcome
the existing limitations. By providing insights into structure−
property correlations, we aim to guide the rational design of
next-generation NIR-II emissive NCs for various biomedical and
photonic applications.
2. PHOTOLUMINESCENCE IN THE NIR-II REGION
Photoluminescence (PL) in the NIR-II region (950−1700 nm)
is highly valuable for metal NCs due to advantages like reduced
scattering, minimal autofluorescence, and enhanced tissue
penetration, making it ideal for deep-tissue biomedical imaging
and sensing.25,26 The emission in this range is governed by
quantum confinement, where smaller NCs emit in the visible
range and larger ones, with smaller energy gaps, emit in the NIR-
II region, allowing tunable emission wavelengths (Figure 1a).
Surface chemistry plays a critical role; defects in NCs can reduce
quantum yield, but surface passivation with organic molecules or
polymers neutralizes these defects, improving emission stability
and quantum yield, making them suitable for in vivo
applications.27,28 Advancements in ligand engineering allow
fine-tuning of NIR-II emission by modifying ligand concen-
tration and functionality, which controls size and structure,

The PL emission mechanism of metal NCs involves three
main radiative processes: fluorescence, phosphorescence, and
thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) (Figure
1b).29,30 Fluorescence occurs when singlet excitons relax
radiatively from the S1 state to the S0 ground state without
spin change, with lifetimes ranging from nanoseconds and
tunable emission from visible to NIR. Phosphorescence involves
triplet excitons decaying from the T1 state to S0, exhibiting
longer lifetimes (microseconds to milliseconds), enhanced by
spin−orbit coupling in metals such as Cu, Ag, or Au. TADF
occurs when the energy gap between S1 and T1 (ΔEst) is
sufficiently small (<0.2 eV), allowing upconversion of triplets to
singlets via reverse intersystem crossing (RISC), resulting in
delayed fluorescence with lifetimes in the microsecond
range.31,32 These processes are influenced by quantum confine-
ment, where NCs below 2 nm have discrete energy states,
allowing for tunable PL. Thus, NIR-II PL in metal NCs is
governed by quantum confinement, surface chemistry, and
electronic interactions. Advances in size control, ligand
engineering, and alloying have enhanced the performance of
metal NCs for biomedical and optoelectronic applications.
Despite improvements, challenges in synthesis consistency,
quantum yield, and photostability persist, requiring further
advancements in synthesis and mechanistic understanding for
practical use.
3. NIR-II PHOTOLUMINESCENCE PROPERTIES OF
METAL NANOCLUSTERS
Metal NCs exhibit unique PL properties in the NIR-II region,
making them highly effective for deep-tissue biomedical
imaging, therapeutic monitoring, and sensing. Their tunable
emission enhances tissue penetration with minimal scattering
and absorption, offering advantages over conventional fluo-
rophores.33,34 The narrow emission spectra improve the signal-
to-noise ratio for high-resolution imaging in complex biological
environments. Combined with high photostability and bio-
compatibility, these features make NIR-II-emitting metal NCs
ideal for long-term in vivo applications, such as targeted drug
delivery and theranostics.35,36 To optimize NIR-II PL, strategies
such as size control, ligand engineering, surface passivation, and
alloying are employed, which are essential for tuning emission
wavelengths and enhancing quantum yield, photostability, and
efficiency.
3.1. Copper Nanoclusters (Cu NCs)
Cu NCs have garnered significant attention for their potential in
PL applications, particularly in deep-tissue biomedical imaging
and theranostics. These NCs exhibit size-tunable emission in the
NIR-I and NIR-II regions, enhancing tissue penetration and
imaging resolution with minimal scattering.37 Their optical
properties are highly sensitive to surface modifications and
ligand passivation, which stabilize the NCs and reduce
nonradiative decay, improving their efficiency.38 However,
challenges such as oxidation limit their stability, particularly in
biological environments, necessitating further advancements in
surface passivation and protective strategies to enhance their
photostability for long-term in vivo applications.

3.2. Silver Nanoclusters (Ag NCs)
Ag NCs have gained significant attention for their unique NIR-II
PL, making them ideal for biomedical imaging, drug delivery,
and theranostics.40−43 To optimize their NIR-II emission,
strategies such as heteroatom doping, ligand engineering, and
core structural modulation have been employed. Heteroatom
doping, such as incorporating Au or Cu atoms into the Ag NC
core, shifts the emission to the NIR-II region and enhances the
PL quantum yield (PLQY). For example, Ansari et al. (2025)
demonstrated that doping Au onto the surface of
(PPh4)4[Ag44(4-FTP)30] (Ag44) (4-fluorothiophenol = 4-
FTPH) to form an (PPh4)4[Au12Ag32(4-FTP)30] (Au12Ag32)
alloy increased the emission at ∼1340 nm by ten times
compared to the parent Ag44, attributed to improved metal−
ligand charge transfer from Au migration to the surface.44 Ligand
engineering also plays a crucial role in enhancing PLQY and
stabilizing excited states by reducing nonradiative decay and
facilitating charge transfer, leading to red shifting of the
emission. For example, Jiang et al. synthesized [Ag27(bpypz)14]-
(NO3)3 (Ag27) using the 3,5-di(2-pyridyl)pyrazole (Hbpypz)
ligand, achieving NIR-II emission at ∼1087 nm with a
microsecond decay lifetime of 6.10 μs.45 Moreover, Ag27
exhibited TADF, improving its catalytic efficiency and enabling
singlet oxygen generation for oxidative coupling reactions.

3.3. Gold Nanoclusters (Au NCs)
Au NCs are widely studied for their biocompatibility, tunable
optical properties, and high photostability, making them ideal
for biomedical imaging, drug delivery, and theranostics.46,47
These NCs emit in the near-infrared II (NIR-II) region (950−
1700 nm), which can be enhanced by strategies such as metal
atom doping, ligand engineering, and core structural modu-
lation.48−50 These techniques optimize their emission proper-
ties, allowing for precise tuning for applications in both imaging
and therapy. For example, Huang et al. developed a NIR-II NC,
Au24Cd1(MPA)18 (Au24Cd1) (3-mercaptopropionic acid =
MPA), by using a heteroatom doping strategy, incorporating a
single atom Cd to enhance the fluorescence brightness and
stability.51 The Cd doping results in a strong emission centered
around 1020 nm, making this NC highly suitable for long-term
in vivo imaging applications.
Core structural modulation is vital for tuning the optical
properties, PL, PLQY, and emission wavelengths of Au NCs by
altering the arrangement or number of Au atoms. For instance,
Li et al. reported two types of Au NCs: rod-shaped bi-
icosahedral [Au25(PPh3)10(SC2H4Ph)5Cl2]2+ (Au25), which
emits strongly in the NIR-II region around 990 nm with a
PLQY of 8%, and [Au37(PPh3)10(SC2H4Ph)10X2]+ (Au37), a
rod-shaped tri-icosahedral NC, which emits at 1520 nm but has
a much lower PLQY of 0.1%.52 The larger, more complex core in
the Au37 shifts the emission to a longer wavelength but reduces
PL efficiency due to increased nonradiative decay, demonstrat-
ing how core structural changes influence both emission
wavelengths and efficiency.
Ligand engineering plays a crucial role in enhancing the PL
properties of Au NCs, particularly in the NIR-II region. By
modifying the surface ligands, it is possible to stabilize excited
states, reduce nonradiative decay, and facilitate efficient charge
transfer between the metal core and the ligands. For instance,
the Au44MBA26 NC, functionalized with water-soluble 4-
mercaptobenzoic acid (MBA), exhibits strong NIR-II PL, with
an impressive PLQY of approximately 4.5%, featuring two
distinct emission peaks at ∼1080 nm and ∼1280 nm.53 When
conjugated with the immune checkpoint inhibitor 1-cyclohexyl-
2-(5H-imidazo[5,1-a]isoindol-5-yl)ethanol (NLG919) via a
singlet oxygen-cleavable linker, these NCs enabled NIR-II PL
imaging of tumors, photothermal therapy (PTT), photo-
dynamic therapy (PDT), and the
1O2-induced release of
NLG919 for cancer immunotherapy without any significant
change in their emission properties, making them a powerful
theranostic platform for cancer treatment.

(PLQY) of approximately 1.6% using cyanines (Cy7) as a

emission from the S1 state arises from a superatomic P →D
transition in the kernel with an emission energy of 1.07 eV,
slightly underestimating the experimental emission of 1.28 eV
(970 nm), which points to the possibility of phosphorescence as
the emission mechanism. Temperature-dependent PL studies
showed an increase in PL intensity by 1.77 times from 263 to 83
K, with a blue shift of about 10 nm at lower temperatures,
indicating a single electronic transition responsible for the NIR
PL (Figure 5d). Additionally, fs-TA showed a broad ESA signal,
with two short decays (τ1 = 0.328 ps, τ2 = 1.680 ps), followed by
a long-lived relaxation of >7 ns, suggesting internal conversion
from the Sn to S1 state, electron reorganization from the metal
kernel to the outer shell ligand, and slow radiative recombina-

mechanism in Au25a (Figure 5e−g).

triplet-state emission (Figure 6b−h). The fs/ns-TA spectra

indicating intersystem crossing from singlet to triplet states

enabling single/dual-channel NIR phosphorescence and high-
lighting the potential of Au44a as a photosensitizer for singlet
oxygen generation and deep tissue imaging.

biocompatibility with bright NIR-II phosphorescence that is
readily tuned by single-atom doping or alloying. Increasing
nuclearity and constructing icosahedral, bi-icosahedral, or
kernel−shell architectures generally narrows the energy gap
and red-shifts emission, although excessively complex cores can

4. CHALLENGES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS
Despite significant progress in developing NIR-II photo-
luminescent metal NCs, several challenges remain in their
synthesis, stability, and efficiency. One major issue is achieving
reproducible synthesis with consistently high quantum yields.
Variations in reaction conditions, particularly in Cu-based NCs
or those stabilized by complex ligands, often lead to batch-to-
batch inconsistency. Additionally, the stability of these NCs is
compromised, as many, especially Cu-based ones, are prone to
oxidation and ligand dissociation, resulting in rapid lumines-
cence quenching. To address these challenges, further advances
in surface passivation, encapsulation methods, and protective
coatings are needed to enhance their environmental stability and
photochemical resilience. Understanding the photophysical
processes that govern NIR-II emission remains another hurdle.
Key mechanisms, including core−ligand charge transfer and
triplet-state phosphorescence, have been identified but are not
fully understood. The absence of a unified framework linking
these processes to emission wavelength, lifetime, and polar-
ization limits the ability to optimize the materials for practical
applications. Additionally, the structural effects on PL,
particularly in the context of chiroptical activity, are still
underexplored. A deeper understanding of how ligand
modifications and core structuring influence these properties
will be crucial for advancing these materials.
For practical applications, integrating NIR-II emitting NCs
into solid-state matrices or functional platforms, such as organic
light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), sensors, or biomedical imaging
systems, poses additional challenges. Transitioning from
solution to the solid state often causes aggregation or
rearrangement of the ligands, leading to luminescence
quenching. Achieving stable, homogeneous dispersion within
host media that preserves their optical properties is essential.
Furthermore, in biomedical applications, ensuring that NCs
retain strong NIR-II emission while maintaining biocompati-
bility and enabling targeted drug delivery remains a significant
challenge. Looking forward, artificial intelligence (AI) and
machine learning could play a pivotal role in optimizing
synthesis conditions, predicting the best ligands and structural
modifications and accelerating the discovery of new materials.
Hybrid materials combining metal NCs with other nanomateri-
als, such as graphene or quantum dots, could enhance their
optical properties, making them suitable for a wider range of
applications. Advances in supramolecular assemblies and
quantum technologies also promise to improve NIR-II
luminescence and enable new applications in quantum
computing and secure communication. Finally, addressing
sustainability concerns by developing ecofriendly synthesis
methods and minimizing environmental impact will be essential
for the long-term viability and practical use of these advanced
materials.
5. CONCLUSIONS
Atomically precise metal NCs offer exceptional control over
composition, structure, and optical properties, making them
highly valuable for deep-tissue imaging, therapeutic monitoring,
and sensing, especially due to their ability to emit in the NIR-II
window. This emission provides advantages such as reduced
scattering, minimal autofluorescence, and enhanced tissue
penetration, crucial for noninvasive biomedical applications.
Advances in ligand design, core−shell engineering, chiral
modulation, and heteroatom doping have improved our
understanding of their PL properties, particularly in the NIR-
II region.
However, challenges remain, such as reproducibility,
quantum yield, stability, and a mechanistic understanding of
NIR-II emission. Issues such as oxidation and degradation in
biological environments necessitate better surface passivation
and encapsulation techniques to enhance photostability and
longevity. Despite these hurdles, recent innovations in synthetic
strategies, including surface modification and alloying, have
expanded their use in fields such as imaging, photothermal
therapy, and sustainable energy applications. Future progress
will require interdisciplinary research in synthetic chemistry,
spectroscopy, and computational modeling. The continued
development of hybrid materials, multifunctional platforms, and
AI-driven designs will accelerate the application of NIR-II
emitting NCs, positioning them as key tools in next-generation
biomedical and photonic technologies.

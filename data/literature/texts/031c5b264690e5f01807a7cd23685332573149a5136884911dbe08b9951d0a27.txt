ABSTRACT
The urgent need for sustainable agrochemicals motivates intelligent nanoagrochemical development. This study addresses
the challenges in size control of nanomaterials by fabricating size-tunable Cu-GSH nanocapsules (CG) via a cost-effective
co-precipitation method using copper-based nanoparticles and glutathione (GSH). In in vivo experiments on kiwifruit, the
optimized CG achieved 77% therapeutic efficacy and 66% protective efficacy—surpassing conventional copper nanoparticles and
thiodiazole copper (54%)—while reducing toxicity. Morphology dictates function: smaller spheres (e.g., Cu4(OH)6Cl2/GSH, 77%)
enabled plant-internal therapy, while sheet-like structures (e.g., Cu3(PO4)2, 63%) enhanced surface protection. Size refinement
within spherical variants boosted therapeutic activity by 1.19-fold over larger counterparts. Enhanced antibacterial effects arose
from reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated membrane damage via engineered structures. CG’s tunable cavity further allows
pesticide encapsulation or multifunctional composite formation, expanding sustainable disease management applications. This
work demonstrates that morphological/dimensional control critically balances pesticide performance, environmental safety, and
versatility, establishing a size-dependent design framework for high-efficacy, eco-friendly nanopesticides.

Introduction
Plant bacterial diseases represent a major threat to both the
yield and quality of agricultural products. However, traditional
antibiotics and copper-based pesticides have shown diminishing
effectiveness in field applications and are associated with adverse
ecological impacts [1–5]. Notably, some metal-based nanoparti-
cles (e.g., silver nanoparticles, zinc oxide, especially, and copper-
based nanomaterials) have demonstrated considerable potential
in plant protection due to their efficient surface interactions
with pathogens, controlled release of metal ions, and capacity
to induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) [6–13]. Nevertheless,
similar to conventional copper formulations, the performance
of these metal-based nanoparticles in greenhouse or field tri-
als often falls short of laboratory results, and their potential
risks to crops and non-target organisms remain inadequately
understood [14, 15]. Notably, novel copper-based functional mate-
rials developed recently via rational design (e.g., constructing
pesticide-based ligand metal–organic frameworks (AgroMOFs)
or preparing atomically dispersed single-atom materials) have
exhibited excellent disease control efficacy in preliminary field
trials. Simultaneously, they reduce toxicity to crops and non-
target organisms while minimizing metal residues in the soil
[16–18].

Copper-based nanomaterials, typical metal-based nanoparticles,
are widely studied agricultural nano-antimicrobials due to their
cost-effectiveness and well-defined antimicrobial properties [14,
19, 20]. Notably, the size and morphology of copper-based
nanoparticles—key factors for optimizing bioactivity—regulate
their biological effects, as extensively confirmed. From the shape
perspective, copper nanocubes (CuNCs) with sharp edges exhibit
stronger inhibitory activity against Penicillium italicum than
spherical copper nanoparticles (CuNSs) at the same particle
size, attributed to higher specific surface area and anisotropy-
enhanced physical interactions [21]. Regarding size, soil-applied
25 nm copper oxide nanoparticles (nCuO) induce more severe
oxidative stress, reduced photosynthetic pigments, and decreased
soybean (Glycine max) yield/quality compared to 50 and 250 nm
counterparts, linked to superior bioaccumulation and biological
barrier penetration [22]. Additionally, Cu, CuO, and Cu(OH)2
nanomaterials with diverse morphologies (e.g., nanospheres,
nanowires, nanosheets) and sizes (1–1000 nm) have been used
for bacterial/fungal control [24–27], highlighting the dominant
role of size and shape in biological effects. Mechanistically,
particle size affects nanoparticle-pathogen interaction efficiency
and plant absorption: <10 nm particles penetrate the cuticle
directly, while 10–200 nm particles rely on stomatal uptake, with
transport limited by phloem sieve pore size (200 nm–1.5 µm)
[28]. However, bioactivity is also modulated by shape, chemical
composition, and interfacial modifications. For example, 20 nm
MgO nanoparticles show two–fourfold higher bactericidal effi-
ciency than 0.3/0.6 µm particles due to larger specific surface area,
but ultra-small particles (<15 nm) aggregate, reducing effective
concentration [29, 30]. Morphological variations alter modes of
action: selenium nanorod cytotoxicity increases exponentially
with aspect ratio (AR), yet spherical Se particles (AR = 1.0)
exhibit ∼3.1-fold higher toxicity than AR = 22.3 particles, likely
via clathrin-mediated endocytosis [31]; 6 nm gold nanoparticles
achieve 40-fold higher antibacterial efficiency than 2 nm particles
through ordered ligand arrangement-induced “membrane perfo-
ration” [32]. In summary, these findings underscore the critical
role of size and shape in determining nanomaterial biological
performance.
More importantly, copper-based nanomaterials face the dilemma
of “low-dose inefficiency and high-dose toxicity”, namely, high-
dose copper ions can disrupt the redox balance of plant cells,
induce excessive accumulation of ROS, and lead to damage to
lipids, proteins, and DNA [19, 36–38]. Surface modification or
construction of inorganic–organic hybrid composites by combin-
ing with other organic materials (such as flavonoids, tannins,
phenolic compounds, terpenoids, and alkaloids) can significantly
affect the efficacy of copper-based nanomaterials [23]. These
strategies enhance colloidal stability, leaf surface distribution,
and intracellular uptake capacity of copper-based nanoparticles,
thereby improving their bioavailability and biocompatibility.
Moreover, the incorporation of functional molecules not only
enhances antibacterial properties but also enables precise regula-
tion of plant growth behavior [23, 33–35]. Despite these advances,
a systematic strategy for designing multifunctional organic–
inorganic hybrid copper-based nanomaterials remains lacking,
particularly regarding how to effectively balance antibacterial
activity with plant compatibility.
To elucidate the structure–activity relationships among the
morphology, size, assembly behavior, biological effects, and
environmental compatibility of copper‑based nanoparticles, this
study developed a novel synthesis strategy based on biomolec-
ular self‑assembly. Specifically, using spherical, wire‑like, and
sheet‑like copper‑based nanoparticles as building blocks, we pre-
cisely regulated the self‑assembly behavior of glutathione (GSH)
in their aqueous dispersions, successfully constructing a series
of organic–inorganic hybrid nanocapsules with controllable mor-
phology and adjustable size (designated as CG nanocapsules).
Different morphologies of copper‑based nanoparticles endowed
the system with distinct reaction kinetics and assembly rates,
thereby inducing the formation of target products with varying
particle sizes. The optimized Cu(OH)2/GSH, Cu4(OH)6Cl2/GSH,
and Cu3(PO4)2/GSH nanocapsules exhibited average particle
sizes of approximately 280, 260, and 800 nm, respectively, with
corresponding hydrodynamic diameters measured as 352.4, 338.9,
and 1037.3 nm (Figure S5). Systematic performance evaluation
demonstrated that, compared to conventional copper nanopar-
ticles, the CG nanocapsules significantly enhanced antibacterial
activity while effectively reducing phytotoxicity. Importantly,
this study established a clear correlation between morphol-
ogy/size and performance: small‑sized spherical nanocapsules
(260–280 nm) exhibited enhanced antibacterial efficacy, whereas
large‑sized irregular morphologies (>800 nm) showed unique
advantages in plant protection. These findings, together with the
hollow structure capable of loading pesticide molecules, collec-
tively demonstrate that precise control over the morphology and
size of copper‑based nanomaterials via a self‑assembly strategy
is feasible and efficient. Therefore, this work not only provides
a new design paradigm and concrete examples for creating
highly efficient and low‑toxicity copper‑based nanopesticides, but
also illustrates, both in principle and in practice, the promising
prospects of rationally designed nanopesticides with tunable per-
formance and expanded application potential, thereby opening a
new pathway for promoting the innovative development of green
agriculture. (Scheme 1).

Results and Discussion
2.1
Preparation and Characterisation of CG
Nanocapsules

antimicrobial nanomaterials, we present a rationally engineered
system of glutathione (GSH)-functionalized copper nanocom-

of conventional copper nanoparticles. Building upon our ear-

nanosheets (100–400 nm lateral dimensions, 10–15 nm thick-

activity, these architectures exhibited inherent phytotoxic limi-
tations restricting environmental compatibility [44]. To address
this critical barrier, we postulated a chelation-based detoxifi-

Our morphological analysis through transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) revealed a sequential change process from
initial linear nanowires, spherical particles, to sheet-like shapes
to nanocapsules (Figure 2b; Figure S4). During this process, GSH
first undergoes an oxidation-reduction reaction with divalent
copper on the surface of copper-based nanoparticles. The gen-
erated Cu(I) coordinates with GSH to form the Cu(I)–[GSH]2
complex, and resulting in forming the nanocapsules through
complex molecular self-assembly (Figure 3j). Furthermore, X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and energy-dispersive spec-
troscopy (EDS) analyses revealed that all samples contained C,
N, O, S, and Cu elements, which eliminated the interference
from anions of copper-based nanoparticles (Figure 3a–f,h). This
further validated the compositional makeup of the nanocapsules
(Figures S6, S7 and S10). In the XPS analysis of CG, we observed
that the Cu 2p spectrum exhibited the characteristic spin-orbit
splitting characteristics of Cu(I), with the binding energies of
Cu 2p1/2 and Cu 2p3/2 at 932.6 eV and 952.23 eV, respectively.
Compared with paramagnetic Cu(II) compounds, diamagnetic
Cu(I) compounds did not exhibit satellite peaks in the Cu 2p
spectrum, indicating that Cu(II) had been completely converted
to Cu(I) (Figure 3b) [45, 46]. Our S 2p spectrum demonstrated
a peak at 162.5 eV, indicative of Cu–S coordination, while high-
resolution mass spectrometry revealed a molecular mass of
701.49 Da corresponding to the Cu(I)–[GSH]2 complex (Figure
S11) [47].
Our fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) results
also showed that the disappearance of GSH’s characteristic
thiol (S─H) stretching vibration peak at 2525 cm−1 (visible
in the GSH control curve) confirmed covalent conjugation
(Figure 3g; Figure S12) [48]. Meanwhile, we observed GSH-
derived characteristic absorption bands in the conjugate spectra:
1724 cm−1 (C═O stretch of GSH’s carboxyl group) and 1409 cm−1
(C─O stretch), accompanied by a broad 3200–3500 cm−1 signal
(attributed to intermolecular hydrogen bonding between GSH’s
amino/carboxyl groups in the conjugates). Our X-ray diffraction
(XRD) patterns of CG exhibited three broad peaks at 9.36◦, 21.45◦,
and 46.17◦(Figure 3i), consistent with amorphous structures anal-
ogous to plant-derived proteins [49, 50]. These comprehensive
analyses collectively verify our successful nanocapsule assembly
and elucidate the complex coordination chemistry between GSH
and copper nanoparticles.
2.2
Antibacterial Activity and Mechanism of CG
Copper-based formulations remain indispensable in agricul-
tural pathogen control yet pose significant ecotoxicological risks
[51–58]. To address this challenge, we selected Pseudomonas
syringae pv. actinidiae (Psa)—a critically resistant phytopathogen
exhibiting multidrug resistance that invalidates conventional
antibacterial strategies—was selected as a model organism to
evaluate the antibacterial performance of CG nanocomposites
[59–63]. As shown in Figure 4a,b, we found that CG demonstrated
potent antibacterial activity (89.3% inhibition rate) against Psa
at 0.78 µg mL−1 under dark conditions, outperforming both the
positive control (thiodiazole copper) and negative control (GSH),
which were negligible inhibition against Psa.
Although GSH itself did not directly inhibit Psa, we noted that
it has abundant charged functional groups. We hypothesize
that these groups may enhance the adsorption of the drug
onto bacterial cell membranes, thereby increasing the overall
antibacterial activity of CG. Our scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) observations revealed distinct differences between groups:
bacteria in the control group maintained intact structures with
smooth surfaces (Figure 4i), whereas we observed extensive CG
nanocomposite adhesion was observed on bacterial surfaces after
CG treatment. Notably, we found that some CG-treated bacteria
exhibited obvious morphological changes—including surface
damage, pore formation (marked by blue arrows in Figure 4i),
and eventual fragmentation—accompanied by leakage of intra-
cellular contents, which directly contributed to bacterial death.
Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) further confirmed
the presence of CG nanocomposites on bacterial surfaces (Figures
S13–S15).
Our transmission electron microscopy (TEM) provided deeper
insights into the interaction mechanism: CG nanocomposites
showed uniform periplasmic distribution (Figure 4i), in sharp
contrast to the aggregation tendency of conventional metal
nanoparticles like ZnO NPs [64]. We attribute this superior
dispersion and stability of CG facilitated sufficient contact with
bacterial surfaces, enhancing antibacterial efficacy. Additionally,
we observed that the release of copper ions from CG caused
damage to the cell walls and membranes of Psa [14], which
synergistically promoted bacterial inactivation through structural
disruption and functional impairment.
We found that the catalytic capacity of GSH endows CG with
hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-responsive ∙OH generation, a prop-
erty we previously corroborated by 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine
(TMB) chromogenic assays (Figure 4c–h). Our fluorometric anal-
ysis using 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA)
probes revealed that Psa treated with CG exhibited significantly
elevated intracellular ROS levels after 8 h exposure, as evi-
denced by the intensified green fluorescence in Figure 5a–i
relative to controls (CK) and copper nanoparticles (Cu NPs).
Our parallel propidium iodide (PI) staining further demonstrated
dose-dependent membrane permeabilization in CG-treated Psa
(Figure 5j–r), wherein red fluorescence intensity and distribu-
tion correlated with CG dosage—consistent with PI’s speci-
ficity for damaged membranes. Our fluorescence spectroscopy
quantification (Figure 5s–u) corroborated these observations at
the molecular level: characteristic wavelengths (e.g., 541 and
611 nm) exhibited markedly higher fluorescence intensity in
CG-treated groups, aligning with the 8 h exposure timeline
and quantifying ROS accumulation and membrane permeability
alterations.

2.3
In Vivo Efficacy of CG
We found that the size and morphology of nanoparticles critically
influence their protective and therapeutic efficacy. Compared
to conventional copper-based nanoparticles, our GSH-modified
composites generally demonstrated superior antibacterial activ-
ity. We observed that nanoparticles with sheet-like or wire-
like morphologies, owing to their low plant uptake, provided
enhanced surface protection. Conversely, spherical nanoparticles
exhibited greater therapeutic potential, particularly smaller-sized
variants Cu(OH)2/GSH and Cu4(OH)6Cl2/GSH, which readily
entered plant tissues. Reflecting the advantage of the spherical
structure, we found that the therapeutic activity of CG (Com-
posite Nanoparticles) significantly exceeded that of the control
drug thiodiazole copper (54%) and traditional copper-based
nanoparticles (Figure 6a,c; Table S2).
Even among spherical nanoparticles, we observed the size varia-
tions impacted activity. Spherical Cu4(OH)6Cl2 NPs (67%) showed
higher therapeutic activity than sheet-like Cu3(PO4)2 NPs (56%)
and wire-like Cu(OH)2 NPs (44%) (Figure 6; Tables S1 and S2).

terial performance of CG (Figures S16–S18 and Tables S4 and S5).
Due to their unsatisfactory performance, conventional copper-
based nanoparticles were unsuitable for morphology-activity
comparisons in this context. We selected rice seeds for subsequent
safety assessments based on their rapid germination rate.
Based on our research results, during the research and devel-
opment process of nanopesticides, we propose that appropriate
morphologies and sizes should be precisely selected according
to specific prevention and treatment needs. For example, for the
prevention and control of diseases on the plant surface, we rec-
ommend preferentially selecting sheet-like nanopesticides with
larger sizes that are not easily absorbed by plants; while for the
treatment of diseases inside plants, we suggest prioritizing spher-
ical nanopesticides with smaller sizes and higher utilization rates
in plants. Through this targeted selection strategy, we believe that
the prevention and treatment effects of nanopesticides can be
significant.
2.4
In Vivo Accumulation and Physiological
Response of CG
To further clarify the regulatory mechanism of nanopesticide
activity by size and morphology, we supplemented the detection
of the in vivo accumulation characteristics of different CGs and
their corresponding oxidative stress responses. We determined
the copper ion content in plant phloem tissue was determined
(Figure 7a) using a Flame Atomic Absorption Spectrophotome-
ter (FAAS, PerkinElmer PinAAcle 900T) for samples subjected
to digestion, reconstitution, and filtration. We found that the
accumulation levels of small-sized spherical Cu(OH)2/GSH and
Cu4(OH)6Cl2/GSH in the tissue were significantly higher than
that of sheet-like Cu3(PO4)2/GSH — this distribution difference
is highly consistent with the previously reported in vivo efficacy
results.
We further detected the activity changes of antioxidant enzymes
(CAT, SOD, and POD) in plant tissues (Figure 7b–d). We found
that the activities of SOD, POD, and CAT were significantly
upregulated in the treatment groups of Cu(OH)2/GSH and
Cu4(OH)6Cl2/GSH (which had higher in vivo accumulation
levels). This indicates that plants activate the antioxidant system
in response to the accumulation of CGs in tissues; meanwhile,
GSH modification can maintain this oxidative stress within
a physiologically controllable range, which not only confirms
the in vivo transport capacity of small-sized spherical CGs but
also reflects their biocompatibility advantage. In contrast, we
found that the fluctuation amplitude of enzyme activities in the
Cu3(PO4)2/GSH treatment group (with lower accumulation lev-
els) was relatively mild, which is consistent with its characteristic
of less involvement in the internal physiological processes of
plants.
2.5
Safety Evaluation of CG
We explored the safety and potential applications of CG in
agriculture. Due to the slow growth of kiwifruit seedlings,
short-term observations are challenging. Thus, we used rice
seeds and seedlings as models to evaluate the safety of CG. As
shown in Figure 8a, we found that the impacts of copper-based
nanoparticles and copper-based complexes on the germination
rate and bud growth are not significant. We observed that copper-
based nanoparticles and complexes had no significant effects on
germination rate and bud growth, but their effects on root growth
were consistent with antibacterial activity. However, their effects
on root growth are consistent with the antibacterial activity.
Specifically, compared with the sheet-shaped Cu3(PO4)2⋅3H2O
nanoparticles and linear Cu(OH)2 nanoparticles, we found
that the spherical Cu4(OH)6Cl2 nanoparticles almost completely
inhibit root growth. Meanwhile, compared with copper-based
nanoparticles, we observed that the copper-based complexes
generally exhibit significantly reduced toxicity, consistent with
expectations (Figure 8b–d). In seedling trials, we found that
200 µg/mL copper nanoparticle treatments induced leaf wilting
within 48 h and complete desiccation by 120 h, while CG and dd
H2O controls maintained not any wilting or drying throughout
the experiment, indicating that CG had little to no adverse effect
on the growth of rice seedlings at this concentration, further
supporting its safety (Figure 8e).
To gain a deeper mechanistic understanding of the interaction
between CG and plant roots, we selected Cu(OH)2/GSH as
a representative model and characterized its distribution and
adhesion on rice seedling roots via scanning electron microscopy
(SEM). As shown in Figure 8j, we observed that CG was visibly
adhered to and aggregated on the roots of rice seedlings, and
the presence of CG was confirmed by EDX Spectroscopy analysis
(Figures S19 and S20). This observation aligns with our hypothesis
that GSH effectively chelates copper ions within CG (Figure 9),
significantly reducing copper toxicity. These results indicate that
CG not only exhibits excellent antibacterial activity but also
demonstrates good safety for plants, supporting its potential
application in agriculture. Our findings provide a scientific basis
for the broader use of CG in agricultural practices, highlighting
its potential as a safe and effective agrochemical.

In Vivo Activity
The study utilized commercially purchased kiwifruit seedlings

4.10
Method for Activity
4.10.1
Method for Assessing Therapeutic Activity
The branches of the kiwifruit seedling were uniformly sectioned
using a scalpel to create segments of equal length. A wound with
an approximate width of 0.1 cm was carefully made in the middle
of each segment. Subsequently, a treated bacterial suspension
(10 µL) was applied to the wound, then the suspension is gradually
absorbed by the branches in room temperature. These segments
were then placed in a 200 mm diameter glass Petri dish, with
one end covered by cotton to maintain optimal moisture levels.
After incubating for 24 h in a growth chamber, the drug solution
(200 µg/mL) was applied to the wound site using a volume of
10 µL. The same experimental procedure was conducted for both
control and blank groups, and this entire process was repeated
three times.
4.10.2
Method for Assessing Protective Activity
The branches of the kiwifruit seedlings were segmented in a sim-
ilar operation, and wounds with a width of approximately 0.1 cm
were created in the middle of each segment. Subsequently, a 10 µL
aliquot of the drug solution was applied to each wound site, and
then the drug solution is gradually absorbed by the branches in
room temperature. The treated segments were then placed in a
glass Petri dish with a diameter of 200 mm, ensuring that one
side was covered with cotton for moisture retention. Following
this, the segments were incubated in a growth chamber. After an
incubation period of 24 h, a bacterial suspension containing 10 µL
was applied to each wound site using the same procedure as for
the control and blank groups.
Therapeutic and protective effects were observed and measured
after an inoculation period of 14 days. This experiment was
repeated three times.
4.10.3
Evaluation of Treatment and Protection
Effectiveness
The effectiveness of the treatment was calculated using the
following formula:
ProtectionEffect (I) % = (C −T) ∕C × 100%
where C and T represent the average lesion lengths of the negative
control group and the treatment group, respectively.
4.11
Determination of Copper Ion Content in
Plants
The prepared stem segments were immersed in 10 mL of a
200 µg/mL drug solution for 24 h. After immersion, the epidermis
of the stems was peeled off, and 200 mg of phloem tissue was
accurately excised. The tissue samples were digested using a
nitric acid-perchloric acid mixed solution (volume ratio 4:1).
Following digestion, the solvents were evaporated to dryness, and
the residues were redissolved in 10 mL of 1% nitric acid aqueous
solution. After filtration, the copper ion content was quan-
tified via flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry (FAAS,
Thermo Scientific ICE3500), with each experiment conducted in
triplicate.

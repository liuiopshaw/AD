ABSTRACT: Developing bactericidal membranes is of interest to the membrane
industry in seawater desalination and other water puriﬁcation processes. The
pivotal step for the fabrication of a high performance bactericidal membrane is the
delicate design of bactericidal materials, which should feature high eﬃciency, long
lifetime, and low cost. Ultrasmall silver nanoclusters (AgNCs, less than 2 nm, which
could maximize the use of active Ag species) have recently emerged as a promising
class of inorganic antimicrobial agents. Leveraging on the unique properties of
AgNCs, here, we developed an eﬃcient protocol to integrate AgNCs into
polymeric membranes (e.g., forward osmosis (FO) membranes), leading to the
formation of a high performance antimicrobial thin-ﬁlm composite membrane. In
particular, the AgNC-modiﬁed membranes can eﬀectively inhibit the growth of
Escherichia coli, without obvious activity decay over a test period of 1 month. In
addition, the strong thiolate-metal interaction in thiolate-protected AgNCs oﬀers a sustainable and controllable dissolution of
silver ions, acting as active antimicrobial species. More interestingly, the AgNC-decorated membranes show improved FO
performance as compared to the pristine membranes. The delicate design of AgNCs and the fabrication protocol developed in
this study might provide a promising platform to fabricate antimicrobial membranes for various industrial applications.
KEYWORDS: bactericidal membrane, silver nanoclusters, metal nanoclusters, water treatment, sustained release
■INTRODUCTION
Water scarcity is a worldwide problem that would constrain the
future sustainable development. Therefore, there is a pressing
need to explore new technologies for clean water1−4 among
which membrane technology is a promising one.5,6 In the past
decade, the forward osmosis (FO) process has attracted
intensive research attention, as the FO process demonstrates
potentials in various applications, including power generation
and wastewater treatment.7−10 However, similar as other
polymeric membranes, the FO membrane also faces fouling
challenges from many sources, membrane scaling, microbial
growth, and biomacromolecule adsorption, etc. Here, we
introduce a novel antimicrobial fouling strategy to the
polymeric membrane community, as biofouling would reduce
the membrane performance (and lifetime) and increase the
operational cost for large-scale setup.11−13 Several industrial-
ized processes, such as chemical biocide injection and reverse
washing, have been developed to mitigate the eﬀects of
microbial fouling.14−18
More recently, the research eﬀorts have been devoted to the
development of antimicrobial fouling membranes.19−23 For
example, modiﬁcation of the physicochemical properties of
membrane surfaces, such as surface charge and hydrophilicity,
could eﬀectively minimize the bacteria attachment.24−26
However, it has been shown that the membrane is prone to
loss of the microbial resistance even upon a slight surface
alteration. For example, once the ﬁrst layer of microorganisms
is deposited, their exponential growth is expectable, and this
could be developed into severely membrane performance
loss.27 Another strategy to design the antimicrobial fouling
membrane is to couple bactericidal materials into the polymer
matrix or onto the membrane surface.28−31 Among those
newly developed antimicrobial agents, silver-based nanoma-
terials represent one of the most promising ones as they
possess high bactericidal ability and broad-spectrum activities
over bacteria, fungi, and viruses.32−36 The bactericidal
mechanism of silver-based materials is closely related to the
release of silver ions, which could induce multiple biophysical
processes of bacteria, such as blocking phosphate uptake and
exchange, altering proton transmembrane behavior and
binding with enzymes or DNAs.37−40 In general, silver
nanomaterials show a good bactericidal property at the early
implantation stage while the activity would decay signiﬁcantly
over operation due to the uncontrollable silver ion release.
Therefore, regulating the dose of silver ion release could be
crucial for balancing the bactericidal eﬃciency and the lifetime

of a silver-based antibacterial regent.41 In this regard, the
controlled release concept could be a good solution, and this
concept has been well demonstrated in drug delivery
systems.42,43 One eﬃcient strategy for controllable release of
antimicrobial agents is to encapsulate the active species inside a
physical barrier (mostly polymers), which could limit the
diﬀusion kinetics of the active species.44,45 While this strategy
requires tremendous eﬀort to prepare these functional
materials before incorporating them with membrane materials.
Alternatively, Dumée et al. come up with a novel cold spray
process where silver nanoparticles are embedded into the
membrane surface with the help of a supersonic compressed
gas jet.46 In this way, silver particles are encapsulated within
polymeric materials without tedious preparative procedures.
More importantly, the cold spray process could reload the
silver particles easily after the previous loading is fully released.
Therefore, the cold spray technique is promising for practical
usage. At the same time, understanding and then engineering
the interfaces of silver nanomaterials with the protecting outer
layer are an alternative ways for achieving a desired sustained
release feature. Recently, capping the silver nanomaterials with
a stronger host, metal−organic frameworks, has been explored
and applied in a hybrid thin-ﬁlm membrane reactor system.47
Simultaneous ﬁltration of water contaminations and degrada-
tion of toxic chemicals were demonstrated with decent
eﬃciency and lifetime. Therefore, a delicate design of silver-
based antibacterial agents would further help achieve the
desired lifetime during the membrane operation.
To achieve a controllable release, here, we chose ultrasmall
silver nanoclusters (AgNCs) as a model bactericidal regent.
The core size of AgNCs is below 2 nm, and the Ag core is
protected by a layer of thiolate ligands, leading to the
formation of a stable active compound suitable for various
applications in solution. In particular, the strong chemical
bonding between the outer-layer thiol groups and the inner-
layer metal centers is used to regulate the silver ion release in
solution with a sustainable yet controllable manner. Through a
post chemical modiﬁcation of the FO membrane surface,
AgNCs could be chemically bonded to the membrane surface,
as shown in Figure 1. The successful binding of AgNCs to the
FO membrane surface was veriﬁed by X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (XPS). Meanwhile, the membrane surface
structures were investigated by ﬁeld emission scanning electric
microscopy (FESEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM).
The bactericidal activity was evaluated by using Escherichia coli
(E. coli) as the model. In addition, we have also examined the
eﬀects of surface modiﬁcation on the FO membrane
permeability, which suggests that the AgNC-modiﬁed mem-
branes show improved performance as compared to the
pristine FO membrane.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Materials. The deionized (DI) water was used throughout the
experiment (Milli-Q ultrapure water system, Millipore, USA). To
prepare thiolate-protected AgNCs, the following materials were
purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co.: L-glutathione reduced
(GSH, >98.0%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, >97.0%), and silver
nitrate (AgNO3, >99.0%). For the FO membrane fabrication,
polyethersulfone (PES) was purchased from Solvay Co., Ltd. N-
Methyl-2-pyrrolodinone (NMP, >99.5%) and polyethylene glycol 400
(PEG, Mn = 400 g/mol) were obtained from Merck. m-Phenylenedi-
amine (MPD, >99%) and 1,3,5-benzenetricarbonyl trichloride (TMC,
98%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. Hexane,
ethanol, and sodium chloride were purchased from Merck. All
chemicals were used as received.
Synthesis of GSH-Protected AgNCs. The aqueous solutions of
freshly prepared GSH (300 μL, 50 mM) and AgNO3 (250 μL, 20
mM) were mixed under vigorous stirring at room temperature in DI
water (4.45 mL). GSH-Ag(I) complexes were formed, and a white
precipitate was observed immediately. After that, the solution pH was
adjusted to 6.1 by introducing a certain amount of NaOH solution
(180 μL, 0.1 M). The white precipitate was dissolved in a few
seconds, and the reaction solution was turned to clear. The reaction
solution was heated to 175 °C and maintained for about 5 h, followed
by an overnight incubation at 4 °C.48
Fabrication of AgNC-Modiﬁed FO Membranes. First, the
polymer dope was prepared according to the reported protocol, and
the substrate layer of FO membranes was prepared following the
Loeb−Sourirajan wet-phase inversion approach.49,50 The thickness of
the substrate membrane was set at approximately 100 μm. The
selective layer was prepared through an interfacial polymerization
between MPD and TMC on the top surface of the substrate
membrane. The as-obtained FO membrane was then washed with a
large amount of DI water.
To chemically modify the FO membrane surface with GSH-
protected AgNCs, the as-prepared FO membranes were immersed in
an ethylene diamine ethanol solution (40 mM, 50 mL) for about 1 h
in the presence of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-carbodiimide
(EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS). The as-obtained mem-
brane was denoted as EA-TFC. To evaluate the inﬂuence of ethanol
solution soaking, a control experiment was done in a bare ethanol
solution, and the as-obtained membrane was denoted as E-TFC. Last,
the AgNC-modiﬁed membranes were achieved by immersing the EA-
TFC membranes in AgNC aqueous solutions (10 mL) for 12 h at

Determination of the AgNC Loading. The amount of AgNC
loading was analyzed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission
spectrometry (ICP-OES, PerkinElmer Opertima 7300 DV). Gen-

mL of DI water for silver release. Fresh DI water was replaced every
12 h for a time period of 1 month, and the displaced water was
accumulated and acidiﬁed (1% HNO3) for the ICP-OES measure-
ment.
Materials Characterization. UV−vis absorption spectra were
recorded on a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrometer. Transmission
electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained on a JEOL JEM-
2010 microscope operating at 200 kV. The presence of AgNCs on the
membrane surface was examined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy
(XPS). FESEM (JEOL JSM-6700F) was applied to evaluate the
membrane morphology. The roughness of the membrane top surface
was assessed by AFM (Nanoscope IIIA, Digital Instrument, USA).
Several randomly selected areas (in a size of 5 μm × 5 μm) were
examined to reduce the experimental errors. To determine the
hydrophilicity of the membrane top surface, contact angle goniometer
(Rame Hart) was used to measure the water contact angle.
Antibacterial Assessment. The E. coli strain was used to assess
the antibacterial properties of the AgNC-TFC membrane. A control
disk experiment was designed for this purpose. In the experiment, an
aliquot (30 μL) of E. coli solution was spread onto the plate of
Lysogeny broth (LB) agar. After that, the as-fabricated six membranes
(labeled as 1: TFC, 2: E-TFC, 3: EA-TFC, 4: AgNCs1-TFC, 5:
AgNCs2-TFC, and 6: AgNCs3-TFC, with one more AgNCs2-TFC
membrane as 7) were placed onto the agar plate, where the active
layer of the membrane was in contact with E. coli. After that, the plate
was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.
Membrane Performance Assessment. The FO experiments
were performed according to the reported setup.50 The membrane
testing cell consists of two half cells separated by the testing
membrane. Each half cell is 2.0 cm in length and 1.0 cm in width.
Therefore, the eﬀective testing area is 2.0 cm2. No additional spacer or
support was employed to assist the membrane. Draw solution (2.0 M
NaCl, 1.0 L) and feed solution (DI water, 300 mL) were pumped
counter currently through the testing cell and recirculated at the same
rate (0.3 L/min). Both FO (the selective layer facing the feed
solution) and PRO modes (the selective layer facing the draw
solution) were tested at room temperature. The weight change of the
feed water side was monitored by a balance working in the online
mode. Meanwhile, a conductivity/TDS meter was employed to
continuously record the conductivity change of the feed water.

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The as-synthesized GSH-protected AgNCs were ﬁrst charac-
terized by a UV−vis absorption spectrometer. As shown in
Figure 2a, a featureless absorption spectrum was observed,
which is a characteristic absorption for AgNCs below 2 nm.
The particle size of these AgNCs was further measured by
TEM (Figure 2b), showing a typical core size below 2 nm.
These ultrasmall AgNCs show strong red emission under UV
illumination. Therefore, in addition to the bactericidal
properties, ultrasmall ﬂuorescent AgNCs could also be used
as model antimicrobial agents to understand their killing
mechanisms over bacteria.51,52 In this work, we focus our
discussion on the controllable release property of ultrasmall
AgNCs.
The fabrication process of the FO membrane is modiﬁed
from a reported protocol.53 The process for integrating AgNCs
onto the FO membrane surface is illustrated in Figure 1.
Brieﬂy, a molecular linker, ethylene diamine, was ﬁrst
introduced to the FO membrane surface through the
condensation reaction between the carbonyl chloride groups
of unreacted acyl chloride groups on TMC molecules and the
amine groups of ethylene diamine molecules. The as-prepared
AgNCs were then anchored on the membrane surface through
a similar condensation reaction between the carboxylic groups
of thiolate ligands on the AgNC surface and the amine groups
of ethylene diamine on the membrane surface in the presence
of EDC and NHS. To verify the successful attachment of

which might be contributed from both the PES backbone and
the thiolate ligands. It is obvious that the pristine TFC, E-TFC,
and EA-TFC membranes show a very weak sulfate signal at
around 167.6 eV, which is originated from the PES
backbone.56 In comparison, the sulfate signal becomes
prominent after the AgNC modiﬁcation. Meanwhile, the sulfur
originated from the thiolate ligands appears at around 162.2
eV, and this is not the case for those membranes without
AgNC modiﬁcation. This phenomenon could be explained by
the fact that those surface-capped thiol groups were partially
oxidized to sulfate groups, and the assignment of both thiolate
and sulfate XPS bands was supported by previous publica-
tions.57,58 It is not clear at this moment how this oxidation was
initiated, and the eﬀect of the oxidation on the bactericidal
performance will be discussed in the later section. In addition,
the element surveys (C, N, and O) are also included in the
Supporting Information (Figures S3−S5). The quantitative
XPS analysis of diﬀerent elements is summarized in Table S1,
and the AgNCs1-TFC membrane shows the highest Ag
concentration (16.1 at % or 57.0 wt %) probed on the top
surface. The total Ag content determined by the ICP-OES
measurements, however, is merely 0.12 wt %. This stark
diﬀerence suggests that AgNCs are located on the top surfaces
of the TFC membrane.

FESEM to examine the pristine TFC membrane. As shown in

Other than FESEM, AFM is another powerful tool to study
the membrane surface morphology evolution. As shown in
Figure S9, smaller surface particles and roughness values (Ra)
were observed for the post-treated membranes. Again, the
ethanol treatment could be the key factor for the surface
smoothening. In addition to removing nonreacted monomers
and short chains, it has been proved that the ethanol treatment
might also induce the rearrangement and softening of the
polyamide chains due to the swelling during the soaking.59,60 It
is important to point out that both FESEM and AFM
techniques are limited to monitoring the surface morphological
changes with a resolution of ten nanometers. As both EA and
AgNCs are in a nanometer size range, a direct visualization of
the molecular level changes of membrane surfaces by FESEM
or AFM is still challenging (if possible) at this stage, especially
for those rough membrane surfaces.
The bactericidal activity of the as-fabricated AgNC-modiﬁed
TFC membranes was examined by placing them in an agar
plate, which was inoculated with E. coli, followed by an
incubation under 37 °C for 24 h. As shown in Figure 5, a clear
inhibition zone was observed around those AgNC-modiﬁed
TFC membranes. There are no obvious correlations between
the size of the inhibition zone and the loading amount of
AgNCs on the membranes. It is expected that the strong
protection of thiolate ligands on the AgNC surface could
facilitate a sustainable release of Ag ions, and this process
would further regulate the concentration of Ag ions in the
surrounding area at a constant level.42,61 On the contrary,
microorganisms were grown around TFC, E-TFC, and EA-
TFC membranes. It is also worthwhile to mention that some
clear areas were also observed for the control samples like the
EA-TFC membrane. First of all, we can exclude the eﬃcacy of
any silver swapping during membrane preparations as well as
storage stage, since diﬀerent membranes were fabricated and
stored separately. The crossover of release silver ions from one
membrane sample to another can also be excluded as they
were placed as distances larger than 1 cm, while the diﬀusion
length of silver ions was estimated to be within 4 mm
according to the AgNC-modiﬁed membranes. However,
operation factors like the initial spreading of E. coli solution
as well as their redistribution induced when placing the wet
membrane samples into the agar plate would be responsible for
some clear spot observed close to the control samples. Anyway,
the intensive growth of bacteria on the edge of the control
membranes shows that these samples were not antimicrobial
active, which is in stark diﬀerence with the AgNC-modiﬁed
membranes. On the other hand, it was evidenced from the XPS
data that these protecting thiolate ligands were partially
oxidized to sulfate ligands.57 This partial oxidation leads to a
complicated scenario on the AgNC surfaces; as now, the
release of Ag ions is controlled by both strong protecting
ligands and the decreased binding capability of the ligands due
to the oxidation of the thiol groups. Therefore, an investigation
into the thiol oxidation process and its inﬂuence on Ag ion
release would be critical in the future study. In addition, we
cannot completely rule out the bactericidal process contributed
by the AgNCs itself, as it was documented that these quantum-
sized nanoclusters are good photosensitizers that could
generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon excitation
(e.g., O2
−).51 Therefore, the understanding of the bactericidal
mechanisms of the AgNCs would be highly beneﬁcial to
further advance their applications as promising biocides.

morphology of the membranes. The next concern is whether
this change will deteriorate the membrane performance. In

treatment is beneﬁcial for the membrane performance. A
similar improvement of the water ﬂux was also seen in both EA

AgNCs can induce the rearrangement of polyamide micro-
structures to some extent at the surface through physiochem-

the permeability of the selective layer. So far, we do not have
any direct evidence to prove this hypothesis at the molecular
level; thus, the mechanism behind is still a question to answer.
Meanwhile, further structure analysis or examinations at the
polyamide/AgNC interface would give more understandings to
the problem. In this regard, it would be more economical to
load less AgNCs on the surface of the membranes as both the
water and salt ﬂux are approximately doubled, which can be
clearly seen in the AgNCs2-TFC and AgNCs3-TFC
membranes.
To further correlate the membrane activity change with their
surface properties, the water contact angles were measured for
all membranes to understand their surface hydrophilicity.
Surprisingly, the water contact angle for the E-TFC membrane
did not change much when compared to the pristine TFC
membrane. This is because the physicochemical nature
remains the same despite the thinning eﬀect induced by the
ethanol washing. Instead, the water contact angle was
obviously dropped after the surface modiﬁcation with EA,
especially after the modiﬁcation with AgNCs, as shown in
Figure S10. Taken together, the water ﬂux and reverse salt ﬂux
data suggest that the observed FO and PRO performance
change is largely induced by the ethanol treatment, where the
dissolved nonreacted monomers and short chains could open
the molecular channel for water and salt permeation. The
attachment of molecular EA and AgNCs, however, only
changes the surface properties of the membranes. Also, they
are crucial for some surface-related properties such as
hydrophilicity and bactericidal activity.
In addition, we also performed the bactericidal activity
measurement for those membranes being stored in DI water
for about 1 month (by a daily replacement of the DI water).
The AgNC-modiﬁed membranes still maintain the bactericidal
activity, as shown in Figure S11. The presence of AgNCs on
the membrane surfaces has also been veriﬁed by XPS, although
a lower intensity was observed (Figure S12). Both results
suggest that the chemically bonded AgNCs on the membrane
surfaces have reasonable stability over the time (in both
incubation and operation) with only 0.12 wt % total silver
loading. This a signiﬁcant step forward compared to our
previous design with conventional silver nanoparticles, where
an antimicrobial lifetime of 14 days was achieved at a total
silver loading of 2.78 wt %.33 Clearly, the present system owns
the feature of lower cost and longer lifetime, which are key
considerations for commercial usage. Moreover, to prolong the
operational lifetime of the AgNC-modiﬁed membranes, the
controlled release behavior should be investigated in details in
the future as the function of pH, ionic strength, and parameters
related to ﬂuid dynamics, etc. Studies on how to regenerate
AgNC species in situ on the membrane surface after their
depletion are also urgent.
■CONCLUSIONS
We have developed an eﬃcient antibacterial FO membrane by
chemically bonding ultrasmall AgNCs (<2 nm) to the top
polyamide surface of the FO membranes. The strong thiolate-
Ag interaction in the AgNCs would slow down the dissolution
of Ag ions from the AgNCs, leading to a sustainable and
controllable release of the active antimicrobial species during
the membrane operation. The use of ultrasmall AgNCs
together with a low loading amount would largely minimize
the required amount of the Ag active species in the
antimicrobial membranes, which could be crucial for their
further industrial applications. In addition, the as-fabricated
AgNC-modiﬁed TFC membranes also showed enhanced FO
performance as compared to the pristine TFC membrane. This
is another important consideration if one would like to apply
this membrane material for a large scale setup. Although the
detailed bactericidal mechanism of the AgNC-modiﬁed
membrane is not clear at this moment, further development
in the ﬁeld of ultrasmall AgNCs (or other metal nanoclusters,
such as copper nanoclusters) would provide good antimicro-
bial agents for the fabrication of eﬃcient (yet economical)
antimicrobial membranes. We believe the design presented
here would provoke more attentions of using ultrasmall metal
nanoclusters for bactericidal applications.

A B S T R A C T
Cotton fabrics exhibit excellent moisture and thermal management properties due to the hierarchical alignment 
of cellulose fibrils. However, these properties can also retain sweat and sebum, creating an environment 
conducive to microbial growth and potential health risks. The misuse of antibiotics has led to the emergence of 
drug-resistant pathogens, necessitating the development of novel antibacterial materials. In this study, we suc­
cessfully constructed a cutting-edge, multifunctional fabric (Ag@CFs) by seamlessly integrating low-cost silver 
nanoparticles (AgNPs) synthesized from vegetable waste (WV) extract onto the surface of cotton fabrics (CFs). To 
enhance the durability and adherence of these nanoparticles (NPs), we utilized a silicone binder, ensuring a 
robust, long-term stability and their performance. The Ag-treated fabrics (Ag@CFs-15) exhibited remarkable 
antibacterial efficacy against S. aureus, and E. coli with 25.9 ± 2.7 mm and 22.5 ± 2.21 mm demonstrating 
remarkable inhibition zones with sustained Ag+ release respectively. Notably, fabrics also provided excellent UV 
protection, with minimal UV transmission rates of 3.25%, 2.50%, and 1.50% for UV-B and 7.25%, 2.50%, and 
2.0% for UV-A for Ag@CFs-5, Ag@CFs-10, and Ag@CFs-15 samples, respectively. Additionally, AgNPs-treated 
fabric demonstrated improved thermal regulation, reflecting heat effectively and maintaining a lower surface 
temperature of 31.5 ◦C, compared to 36.6 ◦C for the untreated fabric. These findings underscore the vast po­
tential of silver-coated cotton fabrics (Ag@CFs) for diverse applications, from healthcare to sportswear, where 
hygiene and performance are paramount. The convergence of sustainability, nanotechnology, and advanced 
textiles heralds a new era of functional materials.
1. Introduction
Cotton, a natural textile imparts desirable features including softness 
[1], biodegradability, biocompatibility [2], hydrophilicity [3], chemical 
and thermal stability making it a preferred among others for different 
applications [4,5]. However, water absorbency and oxygen exposure 
provide a fertile environment to microbes on fabric surface for prolif­
eration, resulting in an unpleasant odor and fabric deterioration [6]. 
Further, CFs possess limited inherent defense mechanisms against mi­
crobial threats, environmental stressors like ultraviolet (UV) radiation 
[7], and thermal properties which can lead to accelerating fabric dete­
rioration and skin risks [8]. However, moderate UV light exposure is 
essential for vitamin D synthesis, while excessive health risks due to its 
carcinogenic properties. Although the ozone layer reflects a substantial 
portion of UV radiation, a significant amount of UV-B radiation still 
reaches the ground, potentially causing cellular damage. The increasing

Textiles play a pivotal role in clinical and surgical applications,

potent antimicrobial properties [12–15]. These attributes are essential

To mitigate these concerns, AgNPs green synthesis utilizing natural 
resources e.g., plant extracts (Azadirachta indica [21], Aloe vera [22], 
Ocimum sanctum [23], Aconitum leave [24,25] and Mangifera indica [26],

monas, Lactobacillus) [27] avoids the use of toxic chemicals. The VW 
extract revealed a rich source of phytochemicals, including alkaloids, 
flavonoids, terpenoids and steroids. These phytochemicals play a crucial 
role in reduction of Ag+ ions, stabilizing AgNPs by capping their surface, 
which prevents excessive growth and aggregation at nano scale disper­
sion as shown in Fig. 1, step 3. This capping action enables precise 
control over the structural characteristics of the resulting NPs, ensuring 
their stability and functionality [28]. It also promotes low waste, easy to 
handle, sustainable and eco-friendly methods [29]. AgNPs have 
emerged as potent bactericidal agents due to their unique properties, 
including small size, high surface area, and distinct chemical charac­
teristics [30]. These attributes enable AgNPs to effectively target and 
eliminate Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) [31] and Escherichia coli 
(E. coli) [32], commonly found in hospital settings [33] The antimi­
crobial mechanism of AgNPs involves: (a) the release of Ag+ ions; (b) 
which interact with bacterial cells through multiple pathways after 
accumulation on the surface; (c) ultimately diffuse and leading to cell 
membrane disruption; (d) creating oxidative stress and finally cell death 
[34,35].
The association of AgNPs with fabrics has been extensively studied, 
with two primary approaches: in-situ synthesis directly on CFs or 
separate synthesis followed by immobilization [36,37] While direct 
immobilization offers a faster and simpler method, it has limitations, 
including potential leaching and restricted loading capacity, which can 
impact the duration and intensity of the bactericidal effect, UV-blocking 
and thermal properties. Another key challenge is the durability of CFs, 
due to leaching of the Ag+ in multiple washing cycles. To mitigate this in 
current study, we used silicone binder to enhance the adherence effi­
ciency of AgNPs on CFs surface for multiple washing cycles. Green- 
synthesized AgNPs, stabilized by phytochemicals, exhibit nanoscale 
dispersion and surface activity. The CFs are rich in hydroxyl groups, 
pretreated to enhance wettability and reactivity. When immersed in a

Therefore, current study explores sustainability with functionality by

This research pioneers a novel approach by integrating green- 
synthesized AgNPs with CFs and silicone binders, developing a multi­
functional textile with enhanced UV protection, biocidal efficacy, and 
thermal regulation. By utilizing VW extract as reducing and stabilizing 
agent, our sustainable approach supports circular economy principles. 
This innovative textile treatment exhibits excellent UV protection (UPF 
rating ≥50), strong antibacterial performance, and improved thermal 
regulation, making it suitable for various applications, including 
sportswear, outdoor gear, and medical textiles [42,43]. Notably, the 
treated fabrics demonstrate superior durability, retaining efficacy even 
after repeated washing cycles. This work contributes to the development 
of eco-smart textiles, aligning material innovation with sustainability 
imperatives and showcasing the potential of green nanotechnology in 
transforming the textile industry.
2. Experimental
2.1. Materials
Silver nitrate (AgNO3, ≥99% pure), absolute ethanol (C2H5OH, ≥
99% pure), ethylene glycol (CH2OH)2, ≥
99% pure), acetone 
(CH3COCH3, ≥99% pure), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥99% pure), 
nitric acid (HNO3, ≥99.9% pure), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was pur­
chased from Sigma-Aldrich local suppliers in Pakistan. High-purity grey 
cotton fabric (82.5 g/m2) was procured from a local manufacturing unit 
located in the Kasur district of Pakistan, selected for its consistent 
quality and accessibility.
2.2. Methods
2.2.1. Collection, processing and preparation of vegetable waste extract
Vegetable peel waste (400 g), comprising 100 g of each (Solanum 
melongena, Brassica oleracea, Cucurbita moschata, and Phaseolus vulgaris) 
utilized for the green synthesis of AgNPs. To ensure reproducibility, the 
waste components wash thoroughly by distilled water to remove surface 
impurities, followed by chopping into small pieces and shade-drying at 
25 ◦C ± 2 ◦C. Then, dried peels were separately mechanically processed 
into fine powder, using a stainless-steel grinder and mixed in equal 
proportions (1:1:1:1 w/w) using a vortex mixer for 5 min to create a 
uniform blend. Then, 25 g of the blend powder was added to 150 mL of 
distilled water (1:6 w/v), heated at 85 ◦C ± 2 ◦C for 30 min, with con­
stant stirring (150 rpm). Finally, mixture was cooled to room tempera­
ture, filtered (Whatman No. 1), and centrifuged (4000 rpm; 10 min) to 
remove any suspended particles as shown in step 1, Fig. 1, and stored at 
4 ◦C for subsequent use in the biosynthesis of AgNPs.
2.2.2. Biosynthesis of functionalized AgNPs
AgNPs were synthesized via slowly dropwise addition of 10 mL of 
freshly prepared VW extract to 30 mL of 1 mM AgNO₃ under ambient 
conditions (1 mL min−1). The extract contains various phytochemicals, 
including alkaloids, flavonoids, steroids, and terpenoids; among these, 
flavonoids are the primary reducing and stabilizing agents. Their 
phenolic hydroxyl (-OH) groups facilitate electron transfer, reducing 
Ag+ ions to metallic Ag0, which subsequently nucleate to form NPs, 
additionally capping and stabilizing the NPs surface and prevent ag­
gregation (Fig. 1, steps 2–3). AgNPs formation indicated by a color 
change from pale yellow to dark brown. The reaction mixture was 
subsequently centrifuged (4000 rpm; 15 min), washed (5 times by H2O 
and C2H5OH), calcined (600 ◦C) to remove residual impurities, enhance 
stability and stored under ambient condition for further characterization 
and application.
2.2.3. Pre-treatment and loading of silver nanoparticles on cotton fabrics by 
pad-dry method
Cotton fabrics (CFs, 82.5 g/m2) were subjected to a rigorous three- 
step pretreatment process to enhance surface reactivity. Initially, de- 
sizing was accomplished via enzymatic treatment with amylase (0.5 
g/L) and a wetting agent (0.25 g/L) at 70 ◦C for 2 h, followed by 
scouring in NaOH (2.5 g/L) and bleaching with H₂O₂ (1.5 g/L) at 70 ◦C. 
The CFs were then dried and sterilized.
To facilitate optimal anchoring of AgNPs during the padding process, 
a silicone binder (20 g) was dissolved in distilled water, combined with 
AgNPs (0.179 g in 300 mL deionized water), and ultra-sonicated for 1 h 
to ensure homogeneity. Pre-treated, surface-activated CFs (6 × 6 cm2, 
5.8 g) were immersed in the resulting AgNP solution and subjected to a

2.3. Antibacterial test for AgNPs-coated CFs
The antibacterial efficacy of prepared AgNPs-coated fabric samples 
was evaluated against E. coli and S. aureus sourced from the University 
Department of Microbiology. The bacterial inoculum was prepared by 
suspending colonies in 10 mL of normal saline solution. To determine 
the antibacterial efficacy, a sterile cotton swab was dipped into a freshly 
prepared bacterial culture with a cell density of 105–106 CFU/mL. This 
suspension was then spread in 3 to 4 layers over a nutrient agar plate and 
incubated overnight under sterile conditions. The strips of prepared 
fabric samples were placed, and plates were incubated at 37 ◦C for 24 h, 
and the zone of inhibition was measured to assess the antibacterial 
efficacy.

2.5. Ultraviolet protection assessment
The ultraviolet protection performance of the fabric was evaluated 
by measuring its Ultraviolet protection factor (UPF), along with its 
transmittance of UV-A (320–400 nm) and UV-B (280–320 nm) radiation. 
These measurements were carried out using a solid-state UV–visible 
spectrophotometer (U-3900, Hitachi, Japan), scanning across the 
wavelength range of 200–500 nm. Additionally, a textile anti-UV factor 
tester (UV-2000F, Lab sphere Inc., USA) was used to assess the UPF of 
the samples, following the AATCC 183:2000 standard. This combined 
approach allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of the fabric ability to 
block harmful ultraviolet radiation and its suitability for use in UV- 
protective textile applications.
2.6. Characterization
To verify the synthesis of AgNPs and their successful integration into 
cotton fabrics (CFs), we employed a range of advanced techniques. 
UV–Visible spectroscopy (Shimadzu, UV-1240, Kyoto, Japan) confirmed 
the presence of AgNPs with a characteristic surface plasmon resonance 
(SPR) peak between 420 and 450 nm. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy 
(XPS), X-ray diffraction (XRD, Model 3040/60 X'Pert PRO PANalytical), 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Nova NanoSEM 450 field emis­
sion). Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) further validated the 
coating process by detecting elemental silver. Thermogravimetric 
analysis (TGA, SDT Q600 V20.9) was used to assess the thermal 
behavior of the fabrics before and after AgNP loading. The results 
showed that the AgNP-coated fabrics exhibited improved thermal sta­
bility, likely due to enhanced thermal shielding and reduced degrada­
tion facilitated by the embedded AgNPs.
2.7. Silver loading, Ag+ release and washing durability assessment
The total silver loading content was ascertained through exhaustive 
nitrolysis. A precisely weighed of Ag@CFs-15 (300 mg) was subjected to 
digestion using a mixed acid solution comprising nitric acid (HNO₃) and 
hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in a 5:1 volume ratio. The digestion process 
was continued until a transparent solution was obtained, ensuring the 
complete oxidation of AgNPs to Ag+ ions. The resulting digestate was 
subsequently diluted to a known final volume, and the silver concen­
tration was quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spec­
trometry (ICP-MS). The total Ag loading was calculated using Eq. (2):

To elucidate the Ag+ release profile, Ag@CFs-15 (300 mg) was 
immersed in 10 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). The 
samples were incubated in a shaking incubator operating at 150 rpm 
under isothermal conditions. At pre-determined time (30, 60, 90, 120, 
and 150 min), the entire release medium was harvested and immedi­
ately replenished with an equivalent volume of fresh PBS to maintain 
sink conditions. The collected PBS aliquots were acidified with HNO₃ to 
a final concentration of 1–2% (v/v) to stabilize the released Ag+ ions. 
The silver ion concentration in each solution was quantified using ICP- 
MS, employing an external calibration curve generated from certified 
Ag+ standard solutions. The cumulative Ag+ release was calculated by 
summing the Ag+ amounts measured at each interval of fabric.
The washing durability was assessed by using a modified ISO 105- 
C10: 2006 standard in an aqueous detergent solution (0.5 wt% deter­
gent, liquor ratio: 50:1) at 40 ◦C for 10 min, constituting one washing 
cycle. This process was repeated for a total of fifteen cycles. The residual 
silver content was determined after each three-washing cycle by 
digesting the washed fabric using the HNO₃/H₂O₂ method, followed by 
ICP-MS analysis. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and the 
results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The silver 
retention efficacy following washing was determined by calculating the 
ratio of residual silver content to the initial silver loading content. To 
ensure data accuracy, calibration curves were generated prior to each 
ICP-MS measurement, and reagent blanks were analyzed concomitantly.
2.8. Statistical analysis
All antimicrobial experiments were performed triplicate (n = 3), and 
Zone of inhibition (ZOI) values are presented as mean ± standard de­
viation (SD).

The possible mechanism of green-synthesized AgNPs association on 
fabric surface, stabilized by phytochemical moieties, are dispersed 
within a silicone binder through weak van der Waals and polar in­
teractions. The siloxane backbone (–Si–O–Si–) and pendant silanol 
(–Si–OH) groups work in harmony to create a robust anchoring system. 
When cotton fabrics rich in hydroxyl (–OH) groups are treated with this 
suspension, hydrogen bonding and condensation reactions occur, 
forming a strong bond between the silicone binder and cellulose fibers 
(Fig. 1 step 4). As the fabric undergoes pad-drying, the silicone un­
dergoes partial crosslinking, encapsulating the AgNPs within a thin, 
flexible film that adheres tightly to the cellulose fibers. This innovative 
encapsulation ensures durable NPs immobilization, controlled silver ion 
release for sustained antibacterial activity, thermal regulation, UV 
resistance and surface hydrophobicity [12] due to the outward orien­
tation of –CH₃ groups.
3.2. TGA analysis and stability of silver-coated cotton fabrics
The thermal stability of cotton fabrics before and after surface 
modification with green-synthesized silver nanoparticles was evaluated 
using thermogravimetric (TGA) analysis, as shown in Figs. 6a-b. Cotton 
fabric exhibits typical thermal decomposition behavior, with a primary 
weight loss occurring between 300 and 400 ◦C, corresponding to the 
degradation of cellulose. After AgNPs deposition, the modified fabric 
demonstrated a slight shift in thermal degradation temperature toward 
higher values, indicating improved thermal stability. This enhancement 
could be attributed to the presence of silver nanoparticles, which act as 
thermal barriers and promote char formation at elevated temperatures. 
Similar findings have been reported in prior studies on metal 
nanoparticle-functionalized textiles. In addition, the bioactive com­
pounds present in the vegetable waste extract derived capping agents, 
which remain partially adhered to the fabric surface during green syn­
thesis, may contribute to thermal stabilization. These organic residues 
undergo gradual degradation at lower temperatures (approximately 
200 ◦C to 250 ◦C), consistent with the TGA curve's initial weight loss 
phase. This observation confirms that green synthesis not only func­
tionalizes the fabric surface but also leaves behind a thin organic layer 
that influences thermal response.
Typically, degradation occurs in N2 atmosphere (Fig. 7a) in two main 
stages. At the initial stage, below 100 ◦C corresponds moisture loss, 
while in second stage is more significant around (200–300 ◦C), repre­
sents decomposition of cellulose into volatile products (CO2), water and 
carbonaceous residues. The mass loss near 250 ◦C was 59% for pure 
cotton fibers, while the Ag@CFs-5, Ag@CFs-10, and Ag@CFs-15 sam­
ples showed a significantly reduced mass loss of 25%, 21%, and 18%, 
respectively. This demonstrates that, unlike pure CFs, the incorporation 
of AgNPs substantially slows down the degradation process, improving 
the stability of the silver-coated fabrics. At higher temperatures, 
Ag@CFs-15 exhibited only 52.5% weight loss at 600 ◦C, indicating su­
perior thermal stability relative to pure CFs [51–54].The interaction of 
AgNPs with biomolecules (polymers, ligands, or surfactants) and surface 
coating are often employed to enhance stability and reduce toxicity.

3.3. UV-blocking properties of silver-coated cotton fabrics
Ultraviolet (UV) radiation comprises three distinct regions: UV-A 
(320–400 nm), UV-B (290–320 nm), and UV-C (200–290 nm), each 
with unique characteristics and effects. The PCFs offers limited protec­
tion against UV radiation, allowing a significant amount of UV-A and 
UV-B rays to pass through, with UV-B radiation being more affected due 
to its shorter wavelength [58,59]. Ultraviolet protection factor (UPF), 
ratings evaluate the efficiency of a fabric in blocking solar UV radiation, 
with higher values indicating better protection. It is calculated by 
comparing the average UV irradiance on the skin with and without 
fabric protection, using transmission data for UV-A, and UV-B radiation. 
The fabric samples containing UPF score above 40 are considered to 
offer excellent UV protection. However, treating CFs with green syn­
thesized AgNPs can significantly enhance its UV-blocking capabilities 
[60]. Table 2 represents the UV-blocking performance of pure and green 
synthesized silver coated fabric samples across 280–400 nm. The results 
indicate that Ag@CFs-15 demonstrated the most effective UV-blocking 
capabilities, surpassing Ag@CFs-5 and Ag@CFs-10. Overall, the silver- 
coated fabrics demonstrated excellent UV-blocking performance in the 
UV-A, and UV-B region. The pure CFs had a UPF of 11.50, providing 
minimal UV protection, as fabrics with UPF values below 15 are 
generally considered ineffective at blocking UV radiation. However, the 
addition of nano‑silver coating significantly improved UV protection, 
increasing the UPF of Ag@CFs-5, Ag@CFs-10, and Ag@CFs-15 to 24.50, 
42.50, and 52.75, respectively. The Ag@CFs-15 sample, with a UPF of 
52.75, demonstrated exceptional UV-blocking performance.

growth suppression. As shown in Fig. 8b, Ag@CFs-15 exhibited prom­

against S. aureus and E. coli, respectively. Notably, S. aureus showed 
increased sensitivity, which may be attributed to differences in meta­
bolic processes and interactions with AgNPs [61–64]. The antibacterial 
efficacy of Ag@CFs can be attributed to the release of AgNPs and Ag+
ions from the fabric surface, which interact with the bacterial cell

The Ag+ release and washing stability was systematically performed 
with Ag@CFs-15 to assess their long-term durability and suitability for 
antibacterial textile applications [79]. The total silver content loading of 
Ag@CFs-15, quantified by ICP-MS, was 2250 ± 100 μg g−1, confirming 
the substantial incorporation of AgNPs on fabric surface. The Ag+
release profile from Ag@CFs-15 exhibited accumulative release (30.5 ±
2.3 μg g−1) over 15 washing cycles, and 150 min in PBS (pH 7.4) at 150 
rpm, as quantified by ICP-MS shown in the Fig. 9 and table S1. The 
washing durability and residual Ag+ release content of Ag@CFs-15 was 
quantified after each three washing cycles using ICP-MS illustrated in 
Fig. 9 and table S2. After 15 washing cycle, the Ag+ release was limited 
to 16.4% for Ag@CFs-15 depicted that the 83.6% of the initially 
deposited silver firmly retained to the fabric even after 15 washing cy­
cles, highlighting the excellent coating stability [80].
The high washing durability of Ag@CFs can be primarily attributed 
to the presence of the silicone binder, which plays a critical role in 
immobilizing AgNPs on the cotton fiber surface. During curing, the sil­
icone binder forms a continuous and flexible polymeric film that con­
forms to the fiber morphology and encapsulates the AgNPs. This film 
acts as a mechanical anchor, reduce NPs release caused by hydrody­
namic forces and fabric agitation during washing. Similar improvements 
in NPs retention through polymeric binders have been reported in 
antibacterial textile coatings and composite systems [81].
The sustained release of Ag+ ions from Ag@CFs-15 follows a 
diffusion-controlled surface oxidation mechanism. Upon exposure to 
water, AgNPs undergo slow oxidative dissolution in the presence of 
dissolved oxygen, generating Ag+ ions at the NPs surface. These ions 
subsequently diffuse through the surrounding silicone binder matrix and 
are released into the aqueous environment. Importantly, the silicone 
binder regulates this process in two ways. First, partial encapsulation of 
AgNPs reduces their direct exposure to water, suppressing rapid oxida­
tion and preventing burst Ag+ release. Second, the binder matrix acts as 
a diffusion barrier, slowing Ag+ transport and ensuring a steady, low- 
level release over time. This controlled release behavior is consistent 
with previously reported polymer-regulated antimicrobial systems, 
where diffusion resistance governs ion transport kinetics [80,82]. 
Additionally, interfacial interactions between the cotton cellulose (-OH 
groups), silicone binder, and AgNPs further enhance coating stability. 
These interactions restrict NPs mobility while still allowing gradual Ag+
diffusion, enabling long-term antibacterial functionality without exces­
sive silver loss.
3.5. Thermal regulation of silver-coated cotton fabrics
The incorporation of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) with high infrared 
reflectivity significantly enhances the thermal insulation properties of 
cotton fabrics (CFs) [83]. A forearm covering test was conducted to 
evaluate radiant heating performance, where an electric heater was 
maintained at 37 ◦C and simulated skin temperature was recorded. 
Results showed untreated CFs reached 38.2 ◦C, exceeding normal body 
temperature, while AgNPs@CFs-5, AgNPs@CFs-10, and AgNPs@CFs-15 
reached 34.8 ◦C, 37.8 ◦C, and 34.9 ◦C, respectively as shown in the 
Fig. 10(b-e). Thermal imaging revealed AgNPs@CFs-15 had the lowest 
temperature, 31.5 ◦C which is 5.1 ◦C lower than untreated CFs 
(Fig. 10e). This demonstrates enhanced thermal insulation due to silver 
high infrared reflectivity, reflecting thermal radiation and reducing 
surface temperature. Untreated CFs, with weak infrared reflective 
properties, absorb infrared radiation, increasing surface temperature. In 
contrast, AgNPs act as a reflective layer, reflecting infrared radiation and 
achieving a lower surface temperature as shown in Fig. 10a. The 
AgNPs@CFs-15 exhibited superior thermal insulation, attributed to the 
high infrared reflectivity of silver, which effectively reflects thermal 
radiation and reduces surface temperature. This property makes AgNPs 
ideal for enhancing thermal insulation in textiles. The variation in sur­
face temperature among treated fabrics is attributed to differences in 
AgNP coating thickness and density, achieved through 5–15 pad-drying 
cycles. Thicker coatings (higher bin der concentrations) may create a 
thermal barrier, hindering heat dissipation, while thinner coatings with 
dispersed nanoparticles enhance thermal efficiency. Binder thermal 
properties also influence heat retention [84].
A uniform AgNP coating enhances thermal regulation, improving 
heat dissipation. Increasing AgNP concentration (Ag@CFs-5 to Ag@CFs- 
15) lowers surface temperatures. The AgNP coating's high infrared 
reflectivity and binder thickness contribute to improved thermal

4. Conclusion
In this work, uniformly dispersed oval shape AgNPs (13.40 ± 0.5 
nm) were successfully synthesized by green route. The morphology, 
crystallinity, and synthesis were comprehensively confirmed by TEM, 
XRD, XPS and UV–Visible spectroscopy. Silicone-binder facilitated ho­
mogeneously sonicated AgNPs were coated on pre-treated CFs surface 
via pad-drying process. The developed Ag@CFs-15 demonstrated potent 
antibacterial efficacy against S. aureus (25.9 ± 2.7 mm), E. coli (22.5 ±
2.21 mm). In addition to antimicrobial efficiency, modified Ag@CFs-15 
exhibit enhanced UV-blocking and thermal regulation, approximately 
5 ◦C decrease in temperature as compared to untreated CFs. Importantly, 
durability assessments revealed that approximately 83.6% silver re­
mains on fabric surface after 15 washing cycles. This retention efficiency 
of AgNPs on the CFs surface underscores the critical role of silicone- 
binder in wash stability. Overall, these results revealed a robust, scal­
able and multifunctional Ag@CFs with combined Antimicrobial, UV- 
protection and thermal regulation properties.

Fluorescent Probes
A New Class of NIR-II Gold Nanocluster-Based Protein Biolabels for
In Vivo Tumor-Targeted Imaging
Xiaorong Song, Wei Zhu, Xiaoguang Ge, Renfu Li, Shihua Li, Xian Chen, Jibin Song,
Jianping Xie,* Xueyuan Chen,* and Huanghao Yang*
Abstract: The design of bright NIR-II luminescent nano-
materials that enable efficient labelling of proteins without
disturbing their physiological properties in vivo is challenging.
We developed an efficient strategy to synthesize bright NIR-II
gold nanoclusters (Au NCs) protected by biocompatible
cyclodextrin (CD). Leveraging the ultrasmall size of Au NCs
(< 2 nm) and strong macrocycle-based host–guest chemistry,
the as-synthesized CD-Au NCs can readily label proteins/
antibodies. Moreover, the labelled proteins/antibodies enable
highly efficient in vivo tracking during blood circulation,
without disturbing their biodistribution and tumor targeting
ability, thus leading to a sensitive tumor-targeted imaging. CD-
Au NCs are stable in the harsh biological environment and
show good biocompatibility and high renal clearance efficien-
cy. Therefore, the NIR-II biolabels developed in this study
provide a promising platform to monitor the physiological
behavior of biomolecules in living organisms.
Introduction
Photoluminescence (PL) imaging is an efficient technique
for real-time noninvasive imaging in the living cells, tissues,
and organisms, including humans.[1] Among the PL probes
used in vivo, fluorescent proteins and small organic dyes have
attracted the strongest interest. The past decades also
witnessed the extension of fluorescent proteins palette into
the near-infrared-I region (NIR-I, 650–900 nm).[2] However,
PL imaging in such a region might still face a limited in vivo
signal-to-noise ratio.[3] Recently, PL imaging in the second
near-infrared window (NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm) has propelled
bioimaging into a new realm of spatiotemporal resolution and
sensitivity, due to the diminished autofluorescence and
photon scattering of biological molecules and tissues in this
spectrum.[4] Significant efforts have been devoted to synthe-
size the NIR-II luminescent organic dyes and nanomateri-
als.[5] In particular, NIR-II luminescent nanomaterials are
attractive due to their high physicochemical stability, diverse
modifications, and tunable PL properties.[6] Therefore, they
are promising for the sensitive in vivo deep-tissue imaging,
but it still remains a grand challenge to exert functions like the
fluorescent proteins with long-term biosafety and the specific
and robust biolabelling capacity.
In addition, it is difficult to precisely label the biomole-
cules (e.g., proteins) with NIR-II-emitting nanomaterials as
these materials are typically large in size (e.g., hydrodynamic
diameter above 10 nm), and multiple proteins ( & 5 nm) could
anchor to the surface of large nanomaterials.[7] Such a labelling
process will affect the intrinsic functions and in vivo
biodistribution of the proteins, further constraining the
practical use of NIR-II materials in molecule/protein label-
ling-related biological imaging, such as tumor-targeted imag-
ing.[8] Despite substantial advances in the surface functional-
ization, luminescent nanomaterials may also encounter the
long-term biocompatibility issue because of their nonspecific
accumulation in the major organs (especially in the retic-
uloendothelial system) after intravenous injection.[9] One
possible way to improve the biosafety of nanomaterials is to
exploit ultrasmall nanoparticles with a hydrodynamic diam-
eter below the glomerular filtration cutoff, which can
facilitate the renal clearance pathway of luminescent nano-
materials.[10] Therefore, ultrasmall NIR-II-emitting nanopar-
ticles may provide a solution to the aforementioned labeling
dilemma. However, this has yet to be explored.

the bright NIR-II-emitting ultrasmall gold nanoclusters (Au

Results and Discussion
CD-Au NCs were synthesized by a two-step reaction in
one-pot manner (Figure 1a).[14] Upon mixing mercapto-b-CD
(CD-SH) with the gold precursors, AuI-thiolate complexes
were formed rapidly, which were then subjected to NaBH4
reduction under an alkaline condition. The synthesized
products were purified by dialysis with water to remove
excess reactants, followed by an ultrafiltration (with a molec-
ular weight cut-off (MWCO) of 30 KDa) to remove few large-
sized nanoparticles. Monodisperse CD-Au NCs with a mean
size of 1.85 : 0.24 nm were obtained, as determined by
transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Figure 1b). In sharp
contrast, when using b-CD without a thiol group or conduct-
ing NaBH4 reduction under the acidic or neutral condition,
large-sized spherical Au nanoparticles with size > 5 nm
(featuring plasmonic absorption at & 520 nm) were synthe-
sized rather than ultrasmall Au NCs (Figure 1c and S1). The
existence of surface CD ligands on Au NCs surface was
verified by Fourier transform infrared spectrometry (FTIR,
Figure 1d), which showed the characteristic glycoside C@O/
C@C stretching vibration at 1156 cm@1 and 1078 cm@1, CH2
stretching vibration at 2928 cm@1, and O-H stretching vibra-
tion at 3385 cm@1. The disappearance of weak -SH stretching
vibration at 2543 cm@1 suggests the formation of Au@S bonds.
The weight ratio of b-CD in the CD-Au NCs was determined
to be & 63.3% by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), man-
ifesting a gold-to-b-CD ratio of & 3.4:1 (Figure S2). X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis showed the char-
acteristic binding energy of S 2p3/2 at & 162.8 eV, providing
a supportive evidence for the existence of Au-S bonds
(Figure S3a). In addition, the Au 4f7/2 binding energy in CD-
Au NCs was 84.3 eV, which locates between those of the Au0
(84.0 eV) and AuI (84.5 eV), suggesting coexistence of Au0
and AuI states (Figure 1e and S3b). The AuI content was
measured to be & 67.6%. Such findings suggest a plausible
Au0@AuI structure in CD-Au NCs, similarly to the structures
of reported thiolate-protected Au NCs.[13]
Considering the high tissue penetration depth of NIR
light, we first examined the PL property of CD-Au NCs upon
808 nm excitation. The CD-Au NCs showed a strong NIR-II
PL with an emission peak at & 1050 nm (Figure 2a), which
was not observed in the large-sized CD-protected Au nano-
particles. It should be noted that the NIR-II PL of the as-
synthesized CD-Au NCs was related to the molar ratio of
NaBH4 and CD-SH to gold precursors, which was optimized
to be 3 and 1.5, respectively (Figure 2b). The NIR-II PL
quantum yield (QY) of CD-Au NCs was & 0.11% by using
IR-26 as a reference dye (QY= 0.05% in dimethyl sulfoxide).
This value is comparable to that of the reported glutathione-
protected Au25 NCs upon 808 nm excitation.[15] Through
surveying the absorption spectra, we observed rapid forma-
tion of NIR-II-emitting Au NCs within 1 h after adding
NaBH4 (Figure S4). Inspired by the broad absorption band of
CD-Au NCs ranging from the visible (400–700 nm) to NIR-I
(700–900 nm) region, we investigated PL spectra of CD-Au
NCs excited by different wavelength of Xenon lamp (350–

To further gain insights into the NIR-II PL origin of CD-
Au NCs, we investigated the temperature-dependent NIR-II
spectra and PL decays. As shown in Figure 2d, PL decay
curves showed biexponentiaLns-level lifetime and increased
PL lifetime with decreasing temperature from 317 K to 77 K.
In particular, the average lifetime increased from 32.4 ns at
317 K to 173 ns at 77 K (Table S1). Meanwhile, a 3-fold
enhancement of NIR-II PL and a slight red-shift in PL peak
(10 nm) were observed with the decrease of temperature from
298 K to 77 K (Figure 2e), suggesting valid suppression of the
thermally activated non-radiative transitions by lowering
temperature. Note that previous studies suggested that low-
energy NIR emissions might generally arise from the surface-
excited states.[16] To investigate this, we recorded the NIR-II
PL intensity of CD-Au NCs after reacting with excess NaBH4
or H2O2 to decrease or increase the surface AuI species,
respectively (Figure 2 f). Compared to CD-Au NCs, signifi-
cant decrease of NIR-II PL ( & 80%) was observed in NaBH4-
treated CD-Au NCs, while H2O2-treated CD-Au NCs showed
slight increase in NIR-II PL intensity (1.2-fold). We also
measured the hydrodynamic size of the CD-Au NCs after
such treatments, which revealed negligible size changes
(Figure S5). Thus, the above data suggest that the NIR-II
PL could be largely originated from the AuI complexes and
the oxidation treatment can strengthen charge transfer
between core Au0 and surface AuI.[16,17] The deeper under-
standing on the NIR-II origin might need further structure
determination of Au NCs, which remains difficult to conduct
currently.
Stimulated by the distinct NIR-II PL of ultrasmall CD-Au
NCs, we would like to explore their use in in vivo NIR-II
imaging. We first accessed their physicochemical stability (in
the biological conditions) and cytotoxicity of CD-Au NCs.
Benefiting from the hydroxyl groups of CD, CD-Au NCs have
a zeta potential of @23.5 mVand a hydrodynamic diameter of
& 2.5 nm (Figure 3a,b). With such a negative surface charge
and the macrocyclic ligand protection, CD-Au NCs showed
good colloidal stability under various conditions, including in
the solutions containing high NaCl concentrations (0–
500 mM) and wide pH range (pH 4–10), and in the cell
culture media (Figure 3c,d and S6). Moreover, CD-Au NCs
have negligible cytotoxic effects on various normal and tumor
cells, such as human hepatocyte (L02), hepatoma cell line
(HepG2), and breast cancer cell line (MCF-7), even at a high
concentration of 1 mM (Au atom basis) Au NCs (Figure S7).
Therefore, CD-Au NCs exhibited excellent bio-stability and
cyto-compatibility.

The prominent NIR-II imaging and renal clearance
capabilities of CD-Au NCs were then demonstrated in vivo.
As shown in Figure 3e, aqueous solutions of the CD-Au NCs
showed concentration-dependent PL imaging contrast at
1050 nm, obtained by NIR-II imaging system equipped with
InGaAs camera and 808 nm laser source. Significantly, the
CD-Au NCs solution was detectable by NIR-II imaging at an
extremely low concentration of Au NCs (e.g., 1 mM). To
survey the in vivo NIR-II imaging capability, the CD-Au NCs
were intravenously injected into the normal mice and NIR-II
imaging enabled to monitor their biodistribution in mice. The
flows of CD-Au NCs into the liver, kidney, and bladder
organs, and the renal clearance were clearly visualized
(Figure 4a), which are well supported by the Au content
measurements in the major organs of the mice (Figure 4b). In
particular, an increased accumulation of CD-Au NCs in
kidney was observed at 30 min post injection, followed by
a significant decrease at 12 h post injection. Such a high renal
clearance efficiency is owing to their good aqueous stability
and the small hydrodynamic size (less than the glomerular
filtration cutoff). In addition, no evident damage of major
organs (via the histological examination) was observed in
those mice treated with CD-Au NCs (Figure S8). In view of
renal clearance pathway, we also conducted blood biochem-
istry assay to analyze the kidney function biomarkers,
including blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (CRE).
No significant changes in blood level of such markers were
observed at 2 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 7 d post injection, indicative of
no renal toxicity (Figure 4c). The NIR-II imaging capability
and biocompatibility of CD-Au NCs promise their further
applications in biolabelling, in vivo tracking and target
imaging.
Besides the aforementioned general feature of renal
clearance and biosafety, CD-Au NCs can also serve as
versatile biolabels for protein labelling, without disturbing
proteinsQ in vivo biodistribution and targeting ability, which
has not yet been attempted by traditional large-sized lumi-
nescent nanoparticles. We utilized the bovine serum albumin
(BSA, hydrodynamic size: & 5 nm) as a model protein, and
synthesized CD-Au NCs-labelled BSA (BSA@Au NCs,
indicating that the Au NCs are labelled on the surface of
BSA) by coupling the adamantly-modified BSA (BSA-Ada)
with CD-Au NCs (Figure 5a). The biolabelling was realized
via the strong host-guest complexation between CD and
Ada,[18] and the binding constant of BSA-Ada and CD-Au
NCs was about 1.2 X 105 M@1 (Figure S9). Importantly, ultra-
filtration centrifugation method was able to remove the
excess CD-Au NCs. A successful labelling of Au NCs to BSA
was suggested by the NIR-II PL of BSA@Au NCs (Fig-
ure 5b). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements
indicated only & 3 nm size increase after labeling CD-Au
NCs to BSA (Figure 5c), suggesting individual protein label-
ling rather than the linking of multiple proteins to CD-Au
NCs. Negative-stain TEM images confirmed the protein
labelling with CD-Au NCs (Figure 5d). The labelling ratio of
CD-Au NCs to BSA was estimated to be & 45 mg-Aumg@1-

We then performed in vivo NIR-II imaging by intravenous
injection of BSA@Au NCs and CD-Au NCs upon excitation
of 808 nm laser to examine the biodistribution property and
real-time tracking capability. The NIR-II images acquired at
various time points post injection showed their increased
accumulation in kidney from 10 to 90 min and in bladder from
10 min to 3 h (Figure 6a). Therewith, NIR-II signals in
bladder were weakened gradually, and finally cannot be
visualized at 24 h (Figure 6), indicating an efficient renal
clearance of Au NCs. Note that although the hydrodynamic
diameter of BSA@Au NCs (8 nm) was slightly larger than
that of the glomerular cut-off size (5.5 nm) previously
determined by using the quantum dots,[10a] BSA@Au NCs
still showed high-efficiency in renal clearance, which can be
attributed to the soft material attributes and the ellipsoidal
shape of BSA.
Compared to CD-Au NCs, BSA@Au NCs showed 2-fold
higher accumulation in liver at 24 h post injection (Figure 6),
indicative of BSA-mediated biodistribution behavior in
BSA@Au NCs. To further confirm this, a control group was
performed in parallel via intravenous injection of a dye-
labelled BSA (BSA@Cy5.5), which emits NIR-I PL at 710 nm
upon 640 nm excitation (Figure S10). NIR-I imaging of ex
vivo dissected organs testified the renal clearance of BSA@-
Cy5.5 and its preferential accumulation and enhanced PL in
liver and spleen compared with other organs at 24 h post
injection (Figure S11). Hence, BSA@Au NCs and BSA@-
Cy5.5 showed similar organ targeting and body clearance
behaviors. It was also shown that PL imaging at 710 nm was
not able to clearly visualize the organ regions and the PL
signal changes in liver, kidney, and other major organs in
living mice (Figure S11), suggesting the great advantage of
NIR-II imaging for real-time protein tracking in vivo. The
enhanced penetration depth of the NIR-II imaging was also
verified in vitro by comparing with NIR-I imaging of
BSA@Cy5.5. Specially, NIR-II imaging at 1050 nm enabled
a much increased penetration depth ( & 9 mm) than the NIR-I
imaging ( & 5 mm) (Figure S12). Taken together, CD-Au NCs
enable facile (and strong) protein labelling and high-resolu-
tion in vivo tracking without disturbing the intrinsic biodis-
tribution fate of the labelled proteins.
Facilitated by such a superior protein labelling capability,
we further demonstrated the efficient labelling of tumor-
targeting antibody with CD-Au NCs for highly sensitive
tumor-targeted NIR-II imaging in vivo (Figure 7a). To this
purpose, epithelial cell adhesion molecule (CD326) was
adopted as a marker for tumor targeting, which has been
widely investigated for diagnosis and therapeutics of various
human carcinomas.[19] CD-Au NCs-labelled anti-CD326 anti-
body (Ab@Au NCs) was constructed by utilizing the host-
guest complexation, similar to that of BSA@Au NCs. Hydro-
dynamic diameter measurement and Coomassie blue staining
results verified the successful synthesis of Ab@Au NCs
(Figure S13). Cell uptake experiments validated the labeling
of CD-Au NCs with antibody and their promising cancer cell
targeting ability (Figure S14). Subsequently, subcutaneous
breast tumor-bearing mice (MCF-7 cells) were built and
intravenously injected with Ab@Au NCs and CD-Au NCs, as
target and non-target groups, respectively. Both NCs enabled
urine excretion of about 75% ID (% ID refers to percentage
of the injected dose) at 24 h post injection, indicative of
efficient renal clearance (Figure 7b,c). It can also be observed

Conclusion
In summary, we have developed NIR-II-emitting Au NCs
to serve as a new class of protein biolabels for efficient protein
labelling and high-contrast NIR-II imaging in vivo, without
evidently affecting their intrinsic physiological properties and
targeting ability. The ultrasmall CD-Au NCs feature a core–
shell Au0@AuI structure and strong NIR-II PL at 1050 nm.
The bright PL of CD-Au NCs was broadband-excitable by
visible and NIR-I light. In addition, CD-Au NCs showed
excellent colloidal and optical stability in biological environ-
ments, and they also exhibited high renal clearance efficiency
and desirable biocompatibility. By virtue of the stable macro-
cycle-based host-guest complexation, CD-Au NCs were able
to tightly label the proteins (or antibodies) functionalized
with the guest molecules, offering a unique strategy for
labelling proteins without the need of tedious modification
procedures. Moreover, labelling with CD-Au NCs not only
allowed for high-efficiency NIR-II imaging to track in vivo
physiological behavior of the proteins after injection, but also
realized the sensitive tumor-targeted NIR-II visualization.
Therefore, the exploited CD-Au NCs with the promising
NIR-II attributes and labelling advantages may provide
readily-tailorable PL biolabels for diverse targets and inspire
further research interest in in vivo NIR-II imaging at the
molecular level.

Herein we report a general protocol for 
the synthesis of high-quality metal NCs 
(specifically, Au, Ag, and Cu) protected by 
a monolayer of organic ligands (e.g., sele-
nolate, thiolate, and phosphine) based on 
a miscible-solvent-assisted phase transfer 
between water and organic solution. The 
key features of this method involve the 
use of hydrophobic ligands as protecting 
cum phase transfer agents of metal spe-
cies, and the deployment of a miscible 
solvent 
(e.g., 
N,N-dimethylformamide 
(DMF)) to facilitate the phase transfer of 
ligand-coordinated metal species from 
water to organic phase, and to regulate the 
reduction kinetics of aqueous NaBH4 for 
NC synthesis (see Scheme  1). The mis-
cible-solvent-assisted two-phase synthesis 
method was demonstrated to be facile, fast (≤3 h), scalable 
(gram-scale synthesis), and versatile (both the ligand shell and 
the metal core of NCs could be varied), which led to the crea-
tion of a large library of metal NC species for future applica-
tions. Furthermore, the working principle of the method was 
comprehensively examined in this study, and the roles of main 
components in the two-phase synthetic system were identified. 
Based on the method we developed, two challenging issues in 
the NC field were tackled. i) By simply varying the miscible 
solvent in proportions and types while keeping other condi-
tions unchanged, the size of selenolated and thiolated Au NCs 
could be tuned from Au10 to Au61. ii) The method has several 
intrinsic advantages, which included fast phase separation 
(within several seconds), straight-forward design (no additional 
phase transfer agents such as tetraoctylammonium bromide 
(TOAB) was needed[32–35]), and no purification treatment was 
necessary (i.e., NCs in organic phase could be spontaneously 
separated from interfering species, such as NaOH, reducing 
agent and Cl− ions in water). This allowed for real-time moni-
toring of metal NC growth within the NaBH4 reduction system 
by optical absorption spectroscopy and mass spectrometry. The 
results showed that the size of Au NCs gradually increased with 
increasing valence electron count (N* = n - m - q) by a step-
wise 2x e− hopping mechanism (x = 0–5), i.e., 0 e- → 2 e- → 
4 e- → 8 e- → 18 e- → 22 e- → 32 e-.

Effective yet versatile synthetic strategies for size-tunable metal nanoclusters 
(NCs) are scarce. This has hampered the development of this unique class of 
nanomaterials. Here, a general protocol is reported for the synthesis of high-
quality metal NCs protected by a variety of organic ligands (e.g., selenolate, 
thiolate, and phosphine) based on a miscible-solvent-assisted phase transfer 
between water and organic solution. This method is demonstrated to be 
facile, rapid (≤3 h), scalable (gram-scale), and versatile. The size of the seleno-

the miscible solvent in proportions and types. The advantages of this method, 
such as quick phase separation and no need for purification treatment, 
enable real-time monitoring of metal NC growth within the NaBH4 reduction 
system. The results show that the size of Au NCs gradually increases with 
increasing valence electron count by a stepwise 2x e- hopping mechanism 
(x = 0–5), i.e., 0 e- → 2 e- → 4 e- → 8 e- → 18 e- → 22 e- → 32 e-.
Metallic nanostructured materials (MNMs) stabilized by a 
monolayer of organic ligands are of great interest due to their 
size-dependent properties,[1–4] as reflected by their impact in 
diverse fields including catalysis,[5–7] sensing,[8,9] photochem-
istry,[10] labeling,[11] energy conversion,[12,13] and medicine.[14,15] 
Depending on the size of MNMs, they can be divided into 
two categories: nanocrystals (>2  nm) and nanoclusters (NCs, 
<2 nm).[16–18] Atomically precise metal NCs are usually defined 
as [MnLm]q, where M and L denote the metal and ligand, and 
n, m, and q denote the number of metal atoms, number of 
ligand molecules, and net charges, respectively.[16] While var-
ious wet-chemical protocols have been developed to control 
the size, composition and shape of nanocrystals in polar and 
nonpolar solvents,[19–23] relatively little advance has been made 
toward tailoring NCs.[1,24–28] Despite two decades of research 
efforts, the synthesis of metal NCs with different size and com-
position remains a major challenge due to the lack of deep

aqueous phase and the organic phase. In this study, the two-
phase system was mainly composed of an aqueous solvent (i.e., 
polar water) and an oily solvent (e.g., less-polar toluene), and 
the two solvents were immiscible with each other. The key to 
our approach involved the introduction of a “cohesive” solvent 
(e.g., DMF) that was miscible with both aqueous and oily sol-
vents. Upon addition, the cohesive solvent could partition par-
tially in the aqueous solvent and partially in the oily solvent to 
achieve a solvent partition equilibrium, thereby lowering the 
polarity difference between the aqueous and oily phases. The 
reduced difference in polarity between the aqueous and oily 
phases would lower the mass transfer barrier at the interface, 
thus making the reaction possible between hydrophilic NaBH4 
and hydrophobic Au(I)–selenolate complexes (formed by mixing 
of HAuCl4 with selenolate ligands) at the water–oil interface. 
The working principle of our method was very different from 
that of Brust–Schiffrin synthesis whereby the reduction of Au(I) 
complexes was supposed to occur in reverse micelles of TOAB 
in which hydrophilic NaBH4 was encapsulated.[33,46,47]
Another interesting question is why size-tuning of Au NCs 
could be achieved by simply changing the type of miscible sol-
vent and their proportions in the system. As evidenced by the 
NMR results (Figure  2 and Figure S21, Supporting Informa-
tion), with increasing cohesive solvent, more H2O molecules 
and cohesive solvent molecules could be accommodated in 
the organic phase, raising the polarity of the organic phase 
(Table S3, Supporting Information). Due to the hydropho-
bicity of selenolate ligands, the increasing polarity could lead to 
reduced solubility of selenolate–Au(I) complexes in the organic 
phase. Since Au(I) complexes would tend to aggregate in a 
less soluble solvent,[48,49] the selenolate–Au(I) complexes in the 
organic phase would increase in size with increased DMF (see 
Figure S19 and S20 and Table S2, Supporting Information). 
Of note, Au NCs were formed by controlled reduction of the 
selenolate–Au(I) complexes, and their size and structure were 
crucial in dictating their reduction chemistry.[50,51] In particular, 
larger Au(I) complexes were easier to be reduced than smaller 
Au(I) complexes (e.g., Au(I)4L3 complex),[52] which was also in 
good agreement with the faster depletion of larger selenolate–
Au(I) complexes (e.g., species Au9(SePh)10 and Au11(SePh)12) 
observed in Figure 3. In addition to regulating the size of seleno-
late–Au(I) complexes, the cohesive solvent assisted mass transfer 
could alter the reduction kinetics of NaBH4. This was because 
increasing cohesive solvent could take more H2O molecules into 
the organic phase, which would not only provide more interface 
between organic and aqueous phases to accommodate the reac-
tion of selenolate–Au(I) complexes with hydrophilic NaBH4, 
but also enhance the reducing power of NaBH4 due to easier 
hydrolysis of NaBH4 in the presence of more H2O (see Supple-
mentary Note 1 in the Supporting Information).
In this protocol, NaOH was also significant for the forma-
tion of monodispersed Au NCs. Like NaBH4, NaOH was only 
present in aqueous phase due to its hydrophilicity. It has been 
reported that hydrogen ion plays a vital role in the self-hydrol-
ysis of NaBH4, and in the presence of NaOH, the reduction 
ability of NaBH4 could be largely inhibited.[31] Therefore, NaOH 
could efficiently weaken the reducing power, but prolonged 
the effectiveness of NaBH4 by retarding the self-hydrolysis of 
NaBH4. It has been well-documented that a controllable reduc-
tion process is key to the synthesis of monodispersed metal 
NCs.[31,50] We conducted two control experiments to study the 
functions of NaOH and the organic phase. Without either 
NaOH or the organic phase in the system, monodispersed Au 
NCs could not be achieved (Figures S22 and S23, Supporting 
Information). Therefore, the aqueous phase containing NaBH4 
and NaOH served as a reservoir for the reducing agents in this 
two-phase synthesis system. The organic phase (e.g., toluene) 
kept the selenolate–Au(I) complex and the reduced Au NCs, and 
provided a benign environment for the growth and digestion 
of NCs. Furthermore, it effectively separated Au species from 
the hydrophilic NaBH4 and prevented potentially undesirable 
reactions.
An added advantage of this method was the spontaneous yet 
fast separation (i.e., within several seconds) of Au NC species 
from the hydrophilic interfering species such as NaOH, NaBH4 
and Cl-. By simply removing the aqueous phase, we could stop 
the reduction process at desired time points, collect and analyze 
the size and/or composition of the Au NC samples instantly 
without purification,[52] allowing the growth process of the Au 
NC to be investigated. In this study, the growth processes of 
4 larger Au NC species with good stabilities (i.e., selenolated 
Au23, Au25, Au40, and Au61) were monitored by UV–vis absorp-
tion spectroscopy and ESI-MS.

in Figure 3).
As discussed above, the growth of [Au23(SePh)16]− is driven 
by increasing the valence electron count of Au NCs in stepwise 
2x e− hopping (x = 0, 1 and 2; i.e., 0 e− → 2 e−/4 e− → 8 e−), 
which is different from the previously reported growth mecha-
nism of Au NCs within the CO-mediated reduction system 
whereby only sequential 2 e− hopping has been observed.[52]

One BH4− anion can donate eight electrons and this means 
that one hydrogen species in NaBH4 can donate two electrons. 
Therefore, the minimum reaction unit is always two electrons. 
Because no less than one hydrogen species in NaBH4 can 
simultaneously donate electrons during the reduction reac-
tion, [Aun(SeR)m]q NCs growth follows a stepwise 2x e− hop-
ping mechanism (where x  = number of hydrogen species of 
NaBH4 molecules that react with one [Aun(SeR)m]q species; 
x = 1, 2, 3, …) within the NaBH4 reduction system. This hop-
ping mechanism also highlights the importance of NaOH in 
the synthesis of monodispersed metal NCs. NaOH weakens 
the reducing power of NaBH4, enabling electron donation by 
NaBH4 to proceed mildly and allowing metal NCs to grow via 
controlled 2x e- hopping. Subsequently, the as-obtained metal 
NCs could be easily processed into monodispersed metal NCs 
through an etching treatment. Without NaOH, NaBH4 can 
donate an abundance of electrons simultaneously, resulting 
in uncontrollable growth of metal NCs with a broad size 
distribution.
Interestingly, at stage II of the Au23(SePh)16 NC synthesis, 
[Au25(SePh)18]− emerged at 5 min and had highest abundance 
at 30 min, but subsequently vanished at 90 min (Figure S25, 
Supporting Information). This observation suggested a revers-
ible conversion process between [Au25(SePh)18]− NCs and 
[Au23(SePh)16]− NCs under the 8 e− shell closure during this 
period. Such phenomenon was discussed in more detail in 
Supplementary Note 2 and Figures S26–S29 in the Supporting 
Information. Nevertheless, the growth of [Au23(SePh)16]− NCs 
could be regarded as a size-focusing process (a typical principle 
in the synthesis of monodispersed metal NCs[1]) due to the 
combination of both the conversion of larger [Au25(SePh)18]− 
NCs to smaller [Au23(SePh)16]− NCs and the growth of smaller 
[Au15(SePh)12]− NCs to larger [Au23(SePh)16]− NCs. The 
growth processes of [Au25(SePh)18]− (8 e−), [Au40(SePh)24]2– 
(18 e−), and [Au61(SePh)31]2– (32 e−) NCs were also monitored 
(Figures S26–S27 and S30–S34, Supporting Information). Their 
growth also involved a stepwise 2x e− hopping (x = 0–5), which 
indicated that the stepwise 2x e− hopping mechanism could be 
universal in synthesizing metal NCs using the “NaBH4 reduc-
tion” system (see Supporting Information Note 3 for detailed 
discussion).
In summary, we have developed a miscible-solvent-assisted 
two-phase method for the synthesis of monolayer-ligand-
protected metal NCs with atomic precision. Using this method, 
NC size can be tuned by simply varying the proportion and type 
of miscible solvent in the organic phase. Our method produces 
monodispersed metal NCs protected by either selenolate, thi-
olate, or phosphine ligands, and can be applicable to gold, silver, 
and copper NCs. Furthermore, the protocol does not involve 
any purification steps, requires less than 3 h, and is scalable. 
We have systematically studied the growth mechanism and 
identified the role of the main components of the two-phase 
system. As the method provides for spontaneous phase sepa-
ration and slow reduction kinetics, the growth process of the 
Au NCs involves a stepwise 2x e− hopping mechanism within 
the “NaBH4 reduction” system. Our size-tunable NCs provide 
an ideal system for investigating cluster size effect on cata-
lytic performance, so that highly efficient nanocatalysts can be 
designed.
Experimental Section
Synthesis and Characterization of Metal NCs: Details of metal NC 
synthesis and characterization were provided in the Supporting 
Information. Briefly, a miscible solvent (e.g., ethanol) and an aqueous 
NaOH solution were introduced to the aqueous solution of hydrophilic 
HAuCl4 and organic solution (e.g., toluene) of hydrophobic selenolate 
ligands to facilitate the formation of selenolate–Au(I) complexes. Next, 
an additional toluene solvent and an aqueous NaBH4 solution were 
added separately to the system to initiate the reduction of selenolate–
Au(I) complexes. After 3 h of reaction, high-quality selenolated Au NCs 
could be obtained in the organic phase.

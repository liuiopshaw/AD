A B S T R A C T
Atomically precise noble metal nanoclusters (e.g. gold and silver nanoclusters), consisting of a few to a hundred 
atoms, have emerged as promising materials in the field of nanoscience and life science. They have attracted 
significant interest owing to their discrete energy levels, intriguing molecular-like behaviors, and unique bio- 
related properties such as controllable optical absorption, adjustable photoluminescence, circularly polarized 
luminescence and clusterzyme activity, which can be tailored through tuning the size, composition distribution 
and types of the capping agents. These valuable bio-related properties of noble metal nanoclusters with defined 
structures have ensured them to become an emerging and promising class of artificial theranostic agents in 
biological applications, including bioimaging, modern antibacterial agents, drug delivery systems and cancer 
therapy. This review comprehensively and systematically summarizes recent advances in the bio-application of 
gold and silver nanoclusters for fluorescent bioimaging, antibacterial agents, drug delivery and cancer therapy, 
highlighting the key factors or efficient strategies to tailor these bio-related properties. Moreover, the current 
challenges and the future development directions of noble metal nanoclusters are also discussed.
1. Introduction
The emergence and termination of global pandemics have proven the 
significant role of nano-sized materials/organic molecules in biomedical 
applications [1]. Among these materials, noble metals have garnered 
considerable attention due to their diverse biomedical uses, including in 
dyes, sterilization, antiseptics, drug delivery, enzyme catalysis, medi­
cine, and as sensitizers in cancer therapy [2,3]. Over the past decade, 
large metal nanoparticles have attracted particular attention for their 
potential in early diagnosis and treatment of diseases such as cancer and 
Alzheimer’s. However, these particles are potentially cytotoxic due to 
the formation of large aggregates that are difficult to metabolize out of 
living organism, leading to accumulation in tissues and organs like the 
liver, kidneys, and reproductive systems, which can induce cell 
apoptosis, necrosis, or even genetic mutations [4].
Quantum-sized aggregates of metal atoms with precise structures, 
known as metal “cluster”, were first proposed by Cotton in 1966 [5]. 
These clusters have sparked significant scientific research interest in 
nanochemistry and nanotechnology due to their intriguing physico­
chemical properties. Conventionally, an atomically precise metal 
nanocluster consists of a well-defined metal core (M(0), where M = Au, 
Ag, Cu, etc.) with the size of less than 3 nm and an organic ligand-metal 
shell (M(I)-R, where R = S, P, C, N, etc.). Metal nanocluster features a 
typical core-shell structure, where organic ligands act as capping agents, 
protecting the metal core from etchants and preventing further 
agglomeration [6,7]. Due to their size being comparable to the Fermi 
wavelength of free electrons, atomically precise metal nanoclusters 
display discrete molecular-like properties attributed to size and

composition, metal oxidation state, atomic packing, and types of surface 
ligands [14–18]. Only by achieving the controllable synthesis and a 
thorough determination of the structures of metal nanocluster, the 
fundamental properties of clusters can be better understood and then 
further tuned for potential applications, particularly in biology. A 
breakthrough in the synthesis of thiolated metal nanoclusters was ach­

strategies, including top-down and bottom-up approaches, have been 
developed [20–25], leading to the successful synthesis of numerous 
metal nanoclusters with diverse structures and compositions [26–29].
Recently, metal nanoclusters, especially those made of gold, silver 
and their alloy combinations, have provided exciting new opportunities 
for various applications, which essentially arise from their inherent 
properties that can be regulated by variables such as types of capping 
agent, dose of reducing agent, reaction temperature and time, metal 
atom-ligand ratios and solution pH, etc., [6,11,12,28,30]. Among their 
various characteristics, the bio-related properties of metal nanoclusters 
are particularly attractive, as some of them offer a fire-new avenue for 
fabricating probes for tumor tissues fluorescence/visualizing biomedical 
applications [21,31,32]. In particular, luminescence characteristics, 
including photoluminescence and circularly polarized luminescence 
[33–36], are essential for their use in the biomedical field. Conse­
quently, significant efforts have been made to develop optical platforms 
based on luminescent metal nanoclusters for applications in NIR bio­
imaging, drug delivery, and therapy [36–38]. Compared to organic 
small molecule fluorophores[38b], traditional quantum dots and large 
nanoparticles, luminescent metal nanoclusters offer advantages such as 
high biocompatibility, superior photostability, well-defined structure, 
excellent photoluminescent lifetimes, and inherent biocompatibility in 
vivo or vitro, efficient renal clearance, minimum nonspecific binding, 
negligible accumulation and target-specific treatment [39–41]. Beyond 
their optical behavior, metal nanoclusters exhibit enzyme-mimic cata­
lytic activities similar to those of natural horseradish peroxidase (HRP) 
[42,43], paving the way for their use as enzyme mimetics in tumor tissue 
detection in pathological diagnosis and expanding their biological 
application scope. Unlike natural enzymes, nanoclusterzymes are time- 
saving, cost-effective, and overcome limitations such as low stability and 
sensitivity to environmental conditions [42,43]. Additionally, these bio- 
related properties enable nanoclusters to possess antibacterial activity, 
biodegradation behavior and pharmacokinetics, while enhancing 
bioavailability through improved cellular uptake, biological distribution 
and penetration of biological barriers [36,44–47]. Therefore, it is no 
exaggeration to say that, due to the unique bio-related properties and 
inherent biocompatibility, atomically precise noble metal nanoclusters 
are promising materials for future biological applications at the sub­
nanometer scale.
The past two decades have witnessed the rapid development of 
atomically precise metal nanoclusters. Several excellent reviews have 
summarized the high potential of metal nanoclusters (mainly poly­
dispersed gold cluster without well-defined structure) in biomedical 
applications, focusing on biological analyses, biological imaging, and 
therapeutic application [12,40,41,43,48–51]. Different from these 
excellent reports, this review aims to timely summarize the advances in 
bio-related properties (e.g., size- or composition-dependent optical ab­
sorption, photoluminescence, circularly polarized luminescence and 
nanoclusterzyme) of gold and silver nanoclusters with atomic precision, 
along with their applications in bioimaging, modern antibacterial 
agents, drug delivery systems, and cancer therapy. Importantly, this 
review also discusses the factors that influence their bio-related 
properties and bio-applications based on representative cases. Finally, 
the potential challenges for atomically precise gold and silver nano­
clusters in future bio-applications are presented.
2. Synthesis methods
For metal nanoclusters with atomic accuracy, the synthesis of clus­
ters with well-defined structures and compositions is a prerequisite for 
in-depth understanding of their structure-application relationships. 
After years of continuous innovation in synthesis, various strategies for 
synthesizing hydrophobic and hydrophilic metal nanoclusters have been 
developed, including two-phase Brust method, one-pot method, ligand- 
exchange, CO-reduction method, etching, seed-mediated growth 
method, solvothermal method, galvanic/anti-galvanic reaction, anion 
templated synthesis, and intercluster reaction (Fig. 1). The synthesis 
methods of metal nanoclusters have been thoroughly summarized in 
recent years [8,20,21,24,25,52–54], thus, in this subsection, we only 
briefly retrospect the different synthesis methods and highlight some 
representative nanoclusters prepared using the corresponding above- 
mentioned methods, as shown in the Table 1.
2.1. Two-phase Brust method
Two-phase Brust method is a foundational method of synthesing 
metal nanoclusters, offering flexibility and controlling over the synthesis 
process. In a typical protocol, the phase-transfer agent TOAB (Tetrabu­
tylammonium Bromide) dissolved in organic phase is first added into an 
aqueous solution containing Au(III) precursor, resulting in the transfer 
of Au(III) from aqueous phase to organic phase via the formation of ion 
pair of [Au(III)Cl]−[TOA]+. Afterwards, a thiolate ligand is added into 
the organic phase, forming polymer Au(I)-thiolate complexes. Finally, 
an aqueous solution of a reducing agent (e.g., NaBH4) is introduced into 
the system, leading to the reduction of the complexes into small nano­
clusters [20,69]. This method marked the beginning of an era in the field 
of gold nanoclusters synthesis. With various synthetic methodologies 
being explored and employed by researchers, the standard two-phase 
Brust method has been proven suitable for preparing hydrophobic 
metal nanocluster [25]. However, uncontrolled reaction between the 
reducing agent and complexes can lead to the formation of polydisperse

2.2. One-pot method
This method is notable for its simplicity as well as good scalablity. In 
general, one or more different metal salts and suitable thiolate ligand are 
first mixed together in organic solution to form metal-thiolate com­
plexes. Then, the reducing agent in aqueous phase is further added to the 
system, leading to the formation of metal nanoclusters[70]. However, 
the original products obtained in this reduction process are generally 
polydispersed intermediates, which need to undergo the “size focusing 
process” or multiple recrystallization to achieve a good monodispersity. 
This method offers simple operation and is suitable for the preparation 
of hydrophilic or hydrophobic mono−/bi−/tri-metallic nanoclusters 
[71].
2.3. CO-reduction method
It is well known that NaBH4 is widely used as reducing agent in the 
synthesis process of metal nanoclusters, however, its reduction kinetics 
are difficult to control owing to the strong reducing ability. The Xie 
group found that carbon monoxide (CO), as a promising mild reducing 
agent, could replace the NaBH4 to support a mild reaction environment 
for the well-controlled growth of gold nanoclusters, generating water- 
soluble nanoclusters with well-defined structures and sizes [64,65]. A 
remarkable feature of the CO-reduction method is its ability to quench 
the reaction promptly, as CO gas can be easily removed from the reac­
tion system. A series of gold nanoclusters, such as Au15(SG)13, 
Au25(SG)18, Au15(MPA)13, Au25(MPA)18, and Au25(p-MBA)18 were pre­
pared by this method[65,72].
2.4. Ligand exchange
As the name implies, this method refers to the reaction between an 
atomocally precise nanocluster and another ligand, aiming to replace 
the original ligand on the surface of clusters and endow them with new 
surface properties. Until now, this method has been proven to be another 
important way to prepare both water-soluble and oil-soluble nano­
clusters[54]. The preconditions for ligand exchange reactions to occur 
depend on the following aspects: (i) the affinity of new ligand to metal 
core; and (ii) the ability of new ligand to maintain the structural stability 
of the resulting nanoclusters after ligand exchange[73]. However, pre­
cisely controlling the reaction conditions, including reaction tempera­
ture and time as well as the type and amount of ligands is crucial because 
these factors significantly affect both yield and size distribution of the 
final products.
2.5. Seed-mediated growth method
Seed-mediated growth is also one of the widely used approaches to 
prepare water-soluble gold nanoclusters. This method requires a tem­
plate cluster with atomic precision, serving as a small seed cluster for 
growing larger gold cluster through the addition of a relatively mild 
reducing agent. The seed cluster is pre-obtained via the various synthetic 
methods. In general, the size growth process of seed clusters undergones 
kinetical accumulation, size growth, and dynamically control of size 
focusing processes. This method can clearly map out the detailed size 
evolution patterns of the clusters during the size growth process based 
on the ESI-MS spectrum [67].
2.6. Etching method
Etching method is a prominent top-down approach for the precise 
synthesis of metal nanoclusters[74]. This technique involves the stra­
tegic use of an appropriate etching agent to convert larger metal nano­
clusters or particles into smaller clusters with well-defined structure 
[75]. Based on the choice of reagents, the etching process can be cate­
gorized into two primary types: ligand-mediated etching and solvent- 
assisted etching. Furthermore, etching reactions require specific condi­
tion such as high temperature and specific solvents. Besides, several 
factors exist, such as concentration and type of ligand, pH value of 
system, which affect the etching process[76].
2.7. Galvanic/anti-galvanic reaction
Galvanic/anti-galvanic reaction methods have also been regarded as 
effective, facile and versatile strategies for synthesizing atomically 
precise alloy nanoclusters[53,77]. Although these two methods may 
seem contradictory, they are actually complemently in the synthesis of 
alloy clusters. In principle, a metal nanocluster can be exchanged with 
other metal elements through these two methods. As with the galvanic 
reaction, dopants incorporated into the parent nanocluster via the anti- 
galvanic reaction may not cause structure changes. However, in some 
cases, replacing a foreign metal atom can lead to structural trans­
formation[78].
2.8. Intercluster reaction
The reaction between two different metal nanoclusters with atomic 
precision serves as an effective way to synthesize multimetallic alloy 
nanoclusters. Intercluster reactions are driven by bond breaks resulting 
from metallophilic interactions, non-covalent interactions between li­
gands, and redox reactions[79]. For the reaction between two types of 
metal nanoclusters capped by the different ligand, the total number of 
thiol ligands remains unchanged during the reaction. However, the 
ligand and metal atoms can be exchanged spontaneously between them 
because of the high mobility[80]. Thus, by maintaining the concentra­
tion and types of the metal nanoclusters during the reaction, controlled 
doping can be achieved.
2.9. Anion templated synthesis
Anion templated synthesis is highly suitable for preparing high- 
nuclearity nanoclusters, especially silver nanoclusters [29]. On the 
one hand, anions template can act as stabilizers to adjust the size and 
shape of target clusters by balancing local positive charges. On the other 
hand, specific anions, as multi-functional components, endow the target 
nanocluster with fine-tuned physical properties, demonstrating the 
templating effects[81].

2.11. Synthetic protocols of hydrophilic metal nanoclusters
To realize their vast potential in biological applications, the water 
solubility of metal nanoclusters needs to be considered. In terms of 
solubility, the protective ligands on the surface of a cluster determine 
whether a metal nanocluster will be hydrophobic (oil-soluble) or hy­
drophilic (water-soluble)[84]. Generally, hydrophobic metal nano­
clusters feature atomically precise structures and sizes, which are 
beneficial for understanding and exploring the structure-property cor­
relations at the atomic level [21]. However, hydrophilic metal nano­
clusters are generally less deterministic in their structures and 
compositions (poly-dispersed), but they are very suitable for the bio­
logical application due to their good solubility in water and other 
excellent biological characteristics[38]. Thus, metal nanoclusters that 
combine the complementary advantages of hydrophobic and hydro­
philic clusters are the best candidates for biological applications. 
Currently, a series of hydrophilic metal nanoclusters have been obtained 
through various pathways and have demonstrated their high suitability 
as multifunctional biomaterials for biomedical applications[85].
Direct synthesis: Currently, typical water-soluble molecular ligands 
used to prepare metal nanoclusters include thiols and biomolecules 
(Fig. 2). Unfortunately, some of these biomolecules protected metal 
nanoclusters are poly-dispersed and do not have a well-defined struc­
ture. Among the water-soluble thiolate ligands, glutathione (GSH), 
bovine serum albumin (BSA), captopril (Capt) and 3-mercaptobenzoic 
acid (MBA) have received the most attention[86]. For example, the 
Whetten group first synthesized four types of GSH-protected gold 
nanoclusters, and their molecular weights were determined to be 4.3, 
5.6, 8.2 and 10.4 kDa, respectively[87]. With the help of polyacrylamide 
gel electrophoresis technique, they separated the gold nanocluster with 
a molecular weight of 10.4 kDa, initially formulated as Au28(SG)18 (later 
corrected to Au25(SG)18). This seminal work provided experimental 
guidance for the synthesis of ultrasmall metal nanoclusters using natural 
and custom-made biomolecules as protecting ligands at the atomic level. 
Subsequently, using the same synthetic protocols, Tsukuda and col­
leagues prepared a series of relatively pure gold nanoclusters capped 
with GSH, Aun(SG)m (n, m = 10, 10; 11, 11; 12, 12; 15, 13; 18, 14; 22, 
16; 22, 17; 25, 18; 29, 20; 33, 22; 35, 22; 38, 24 and 39, 24, respectively) 
[88]. Among them, Au25(SG)18 contained the same number of gold 
atoms and ligands as the thiolated Au25(SR)18 [89]. More importantly, 
the GSH-protected Au25 possessed a crystal structure analogous to that 
of the oil-soluble Au25, showing particularly high stability[74]. When 
proteins were used as the protecting ligands to direct the synthesis of 
water-soluble metal nanoclusters, the purity and size of the as-prepared 
nanoclusters could be accurately tailored through adjusting the solution 
pH and the length of functional groups in proteins. Besides, the forma­
tion mechanism of water-soluble metal nanoclusters is related to the 
redox reaction between metal ions and ligands. Taking Au25(BSA)18 as 
an example, during the synthesis process, Au(III) ions from the salt are 
initially isolated by the protein molecules, leading to the formation of an 
Au(I)-protein complex. As the pH of the system increases to 12, Au(I) is 
gradually converteds into Au(0), ultimately generating the target Au25 
clusters with well-defined size and structure[90]. Among atomically 
precise water-soluble metal nanoclusters, the Au25 nanoclusters stabi­
lized by various hydrophilic ligands such as GSH, Cys, BSA, Capt, 11- 
MUA, and m−/p-MBA occupy an important place due to their excel­
lent bio-related properties, well-defined structure, high stability, high 
yield, and ease of synthesis[84].
Phase transfer strategy: phase transfer is an effective strategy to 
transform oil-soluble nanoclusters into water-soluble nanoclusters for 
biological applications. Ligand exchange, host-guest chemistry, and 
micellization strategy are important approaches within the phase 
transfer strategy. For example, Shichibu and coworkers reported that a 
high-yield synthesis of GSH-capped Au25 nanocluster could be achieved 
through the reaction of glutathione ligand with water-indispersible 
Au11(PPh3)7Cl3 cluster[91]. Zhao et al.[92] first prepared a novel ada­
mantane thiolate-capped gold nanocluster in the oil-phase, where its 
formula was determined to be Au40(S-Adm)22. Due to the excellent 
surface compatibility between organic ligand and γ-cyclodextrin (γ-CD), 
a water-soluble component, γ-cyclodextrin-metal-organic framework 
(γ-CD-MOF) was introduced onto surface of the oil-soluble ligand of the 
Au40 nanoclusters via the host-guest chemistry, endowing the nano­
cluster with good water solubility. The resulting Au40(S-Adm)22/γ-CD- 
MOF could be used for enzyme-mimicking catalysis, which was totally 
different from the parent nanocluster. It is well known that the dual- 
ligand-protected Ag29(SSR)12(PPh3)4 (SSR =
1,3-benzene dithiol) 
nanocluster is oil-soluble. Interestingly, Kang and coworkers discovered 
that the Ag29@Na micelles formed by the nanocluster and solvent 
conjoined cations Na(NMP)5 or Na3(DMP)12 exhibited good dissolv­
ability in aqueous solution [60]. The crystal structure of these com­
pounds revealed that the cations were capped by oxygen-containing 
solvent molecules, serving as both counterions of pure silver nano­
clusters and surface cosolvent for the nanocluster-based micelles in the 
aqueous phase. Due to their excellent water solubility and thermal sta­
bility, the formed Ag29@Na compounds could be utilized as novel 
cluster-based biocompatible nanomaterials for general imaging and 
super-resolution imaging.

3.1. Optical absorption
Depending on the sizes and structures (including core and surface), 
the metal nanoclusters have distinct optical properties that are entirely 
different from counterparts such as nanocrystals and nanoparticles 
which display the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) absorption proper­
ties [6,105]. The distinctive features of the tiny metal nanoclusters with 
well-defined structure usually lose bulk-like electronic properties and 
show molecular-like optical properties owing to the particular quasi- 
continuous electron energy level structure and other quantum effects 
[4,47,106,107]. Generally, as one of the most effective tools to charac­
terize the prepared nanoclusters, a UV/vis/NIR spectrophotometer is 
widely used to monitor the formation of the nanoclusters through 
measuring the optical absorption spectra during the synthesis process, 
the size or structure, composition, ligand and purity of nanoclusters 
heavily influence the wavelength (peak position) of light absorption of 
the target nanoclusters[108–113]. In this subsection, we summarize the 
optical absorption of several typical template clusters. 
(i) Au 25.
A typical example of a well-studied case of the optical behaviors in 
atomically precise metal nanoclusters is spherical Au25 due to its special 
properties[89]. The acquisition of crystal structure data of the

an unnoteworthy shoulder peak at 530 nm (Fig. 3c). Except for the 
composition, the type of ligands and charge states also affect the optical 
behaviors of the Au25 clusters[135–141].

(ii) Au38.

Bakr et al. prepared novel Ag nanoclusters using 4-fluoro thiophenol

3.2. Photoluminescence
In 1969, Mooradian et al. first observed the photoluminescence from 
noble metals[198]. More than a decade later, Boyd and coworkers 
discovered that metal films with rough surface emitted strong photo­
luminescence and believed that this phenomenon of strong PL origi­
nated from the local field enhancement[199]. In 2005, Farrer et al. 
reported highly efficient multiphoton-absorption-induced luminescence 
from gold nanoparticles just a few nanometers in size[200]. Like metal 
nanoparticles, the metal nanoclusters capped by hydrophilic or hydro­
phobic ligands can also emit photoluminescence (PL)[6,201–206]. In 
recent years, metal nanoclusters have been extensively investigated for 
photoluminescence properties owing to (i) their higher thermal stabil­
ity; (ii) structure modularity and diversity; (iii) tunability of emission 
behaviors; (iv) superior photo-stability; and (vi) unique advantages of 
wide potential bio-application. Up to now, various transition states have 
also been proposed based on their precise crystal structures, which 
include: LMCT (ligand-to-metal charge transfer), LMMCT (ligand-to- 
metal-metal charge transfer), MLCT (metal-to-ligand charge transfer), 
LLCT (ligand-to-ligand charge transfer) and MC (metal-centered) 
[18,207]. Although metal nanoclusters are an important type of pho­
toluminescence nanomaterials, the reported quantum yield (QY) of 
nanoclusters is very low, and this shortcoming severely restricts their 
utilization in biological application. Generally, compared to the thiol- 
protected metal nanoclusters, the water-soluble ligands-capped metal 
nanoclusters 
exhibit 
relatively 
stronger 
photoluminescence 
[38,39,51,208,209]. From a practical application point of view, the PL 
QY is an essential indicator of the optical characteristics of metal 
nanoclusters. The discovery of the photoluminescence phenomenon 
from metal nanoclusters, primarily precious metal nanoclusters, dates 
back decades[6,8,69]. Still, it has not received much attention due to its 
meager quantum yield and lifetimes with nanosecond or even pico­
second. According to the literature, most works reported that the PL QY 
values range from 10−3 to 10−1, typically not exceeding 1 %, except for 
several cases, including Au6[210], Au20[211], Au24[212], Au22[213], 
Au28[214], Ag6[215], Ag44[172] and so on[216]. However, in recent 
years, many strategies how to increase PL QY values of metal nano­

First one is aggregation-induced emission (AIE) of nanoclusters.

refers to non-luminescent or weakly luminescent materials being

weakly polar solvent or the solid state[242]. The Tang group first

important strategy to enhance the intensity and QY of metal 
nanoclusters[14,34,235,244–246]. With regard to the basic 
mechanism of AIE in the field of clusters, it is generally accepted 
that the inter- or intramolecular rotations and vibrations are 
greatly restrained owing to the structural features of aggregated 
clusters such as multiple noncovalent interactions from the or­
dered assembly of cluster’s staple motifs[247–250]. As a result,

3.3. Circularly polarized luminescence
Apart from chirality, bright luminescence is another key prerequisite 
for fabricating materials with circularly polarized luminescence activity 
[34,290]. Generally, chirality of metal nanoclusters can affect the po­
larization of light and thus some chiral clusters show a circularly polized 
luminescence that can be concretely quantified by the luminescence 
dissymmetry factor (glum for short), bringing new opportunities in optics 
and biology. For example, some types of chiral ligands with bio-effects 
(e.g., glutathione[291], D−/L-penicillamine[292]) can be selected and 
used to protect the atomically precise nanoclusters in order to trigger 
their circularly polized luminescence for a purpose of potential biolog­
ical applications such as biomolecular recognition, biodetection and 
biolabel. Till now, the investigation of metal nanoclusters exhibiting 
circularly polarized luminescence is still in its early stages because of 
some shortcomings such as low CPL quantum yield, dissymmetry factors 
that hinder the detection of CPL and the nonefficient synergy effect 
between the chirality and PL. In spite of this, many effective strategies 
have been explored to synthesize the atomically precise metal nano­
clusters with circularly polarized luminescence over ten years, and 
considerable fruitful results have also been achieved [33,293,294]. In 
this section, we will discuss the recent progress in atomically precise 
metal nanocluster with circularly polarized luminescence at the atomic 
level based on the proposed effective strategies, including ligand engi­
neering and AIE strategies.
(i) Ligand Engineering. As the name implies, ligand engineering 
refers to the strategy to achieve the CPL properties of atomically precise 
metal nanoclusters through the selection, control and modification of 
ligands during the synthesis process. Generally, one of the most smiple 
and effective way to construct metal nanoclusters with highly efficient 
CPL is by using chiral protecting ligand directly, which is well proven by 
several types of typical CPL-active metal nanoclusters protected by 
chiral monodentate N-heterocyclic carbenes (NHC) ligands or other 
chiral ligands, such as R/S-Au13-NHC (glum of 3.0×10−3)[295], Ag7(R/S-

prop-2-yn-1-amine; glum of 1.2×10−3)[297]. Along with the above 
mentional strategy, surface ligand modification is another approach to 
fabricate the CPL-active metal nanocluster with well-defined structure. 
Recently, a series of atomically precise chiral metal nanoclusters with 
circularly polarized luminescence were reported by the Zang group

nanocluster with specific structure. Importantly, in the solid state, the 
former showed better yellow luminescence with high QY (56 %) at room 
temperature, while the latter’s QY could overpass the 95 % and can be 
emissive up to 150 ◦C in air. Owing to the strong solid state lumines­
cence and chirality, the prepared chiral silver nanoclusters displayed 
excellent CPL (Fig. 8). In another case, this group designed a “host- 
guest” system to achieve the CPL on atomically precise silver nano­
cluster. The proposed “host-guest” system belongs to the scope of surface 
ligand modification, in which the luminescence guests are entrapped in 
the chiral ligand host, leading to the CPL activity of the clusters[292]. 
Following this step-by-step modification strategy, a pure Ag24 nano­

dissymmetry factor (|glum|) of the CPL signals, the corresponding values 
were determined to be 5×10−3 and 4×10−3, respectively. Recently, 
starting from the atomically precise oil-soluble Au23(CHT)16 nano­
cluster, the Zhu group obtained amphiphilic nanoclusters Au23(CHT)16-

to the chirality, the amphiphilic Au23(SR)16 showed chiroptical signa­
ture in the whole CD spectrum, which was different from that of the oil- 
soluble Au23(CHT)16 nanocluster. Partial capped ligand recombined 
with the framework of Au23 into a chiral structure with strong CPL ac­
tivity in the wavelength region ranging from 600 to 950 nm, and the 
luminescence dissymmetry factor value was 3.12×10−3, displaying a 
strong and negative Cotton effect.

4. Biological applications
It is well-known that the controllability of atomic structure and bio- 
related properties of metal nanoclusters make them as the promising 
candidates for biological applications related to commercialization and 
technology [10,15,31,32,41,48,86,93,319–327]. Till now, atomic pre­
cision metal nanoclusters have been extensively employed in the new 
biological applications, and theie ultrasmall size and biocompatibility 
are the most important merits in biological applications. In this section, 
we will comprehensively discuss the applications of atomically precise 
metal nanoclusters in fluorescence imaging, modern antibacterial 
agents, drug delivery and cancer therapy, highlighting the key factors or 
efficient strategies to tailor these bio-related properties for applications 
(Fig. 11).

hold great promise in a wide range of bio-imaging and bio-labeling

4.1.1. In vitro imaging
Due to the sub-cellular resolution and high sensitivity of fluorescence 
microscopy, cell imaging in vitro has attracted increasing attention 
[339–345]. Up to now, many metal flourescent nanoclusters stabilized 
with varying types of surface ligands have been used as optical probes 
for cell imaging and labeling[85,336,346–351]. As early as in 2007, the 
first case of silver fluorescence in cells was reported by Dickson et al. 
[352], who produced a new type of peptide-encapsulated silver nano­

fluorescent silver nanoclusters, indicating that nucleolin as well as other 
types of argyrophilic proteins, could be very important guiding signifi­

aptamer hybrids via artificially modified aptamers such as an Msgc8 and 
successfully used as-obtained nanohybrids to mark the nuclei of live 
cells. Li et al.[354] also designed a new type of multi-functional DNA 
scaffold for synthesizing subnanometer-sized silver nanoclusters (Ag18)

silver nanoclusters possessed good fluorescence properties, high pho­

and “spectrum-shift” response to target DNA in vitro. Thus, such DNA

related mRNA in living cells[355]. Based on the self-assembly of tryp­
tophan loaded DNA-AgNCs and bovine serum albumin (BSA) through 
hydrophobic interaction, a new type of stable DNA-AgNCs-Trp@BSA 
with emission maximum located at the near infrared region was pre­
pared by Mu et al.[356] And the near-infrared fluorescence DNA-AgNCs- 
Trp@BSA biolabels were applied in the cellular imaging of HepG-2 cells.
Aside from small biomolecules, the thiols (including hydrophilic and 
hydrophobic ligands) are used as the stabilizer to prepare the fluorescent

4.1.2. In vivo imaging
Generally, in vivo fluorescence imaging has emerged as valuable tool 
in clinical identification and cell biology, offering a noninvasive and 
nonionizing route to visualize and scan tissues or tumors with meticu­
lous morphological details at subcellular resolution in real-time 
[2,336,362–364]. Ideal metal nanoclusters with high fluorescence in­
tensity, suitable excitation and emission wavelength, excellent photo­
stability, ultrasmall size, and good biocompatibility are widely 
employed in fluorescence imaging in vivo[365–372]. However, there 
are two major obstacles to fluorescent imaging in vivo in animals and 
patients: tissue attenuation limitation and autofluorescence. Fortu­
nately, in vivo fluorescence imaging excited from NIR region has many 
merits including weak autofluorescence, minimal photobleaching, deep 
tissue penetration, low light scatter, and less phototoxicity. That is, in 
vivo NIR fluorescence bioimaging can overcome the major limitations of 
fluorescent imaging in vivo[2,373–375]. Thus, in the past few years, 
various ligands on the surface of metal nanoclusters with a special 
structure have been designed to produce effective NIR imaging agents 
with high fluorescent intensity, excellent biocompatibility, good water 
solubility,

used for in vivo imaging[378]. Zheng et al.[379] found that atomically 
precise GS-AuNCs (Au10–11SG10–11, Au15SG13, Au18SG14, and Au25SG18) 
with near-NR emission exhibited similar behavior to small molecules 
like IRDye 800CW that possessed an efficient renal clearable ability and 
excellent stability at the initial stage of tumor targeting. Furthermore, 
compared to IRDye 800CW, GS-AuNCs showed significantly prolonged 
retention time in tumors and faster clearance from nomal tissues. To be 
exact, the tumor retention of GS-AuNCs was found to be 10-fold higher 
than that of the IRDye 800CW, while the clearance of the former from 
normal tissue was 3-fold faster than that of the latter, indicating that GS- 
AuNCs were highly suitable for rapid detection of tumors owing to their 
rapid clearance in normal tissue, prolonged retention in tumors, and 
high specificity for tumor targeting. The result from the whole body in 
vivo NIR fluorescence imaging observed at different times after injection 
of GS-AuNCs and IRDye 800CW into tumor-bearing mice also confirmed 
the renal clearance of these two types of nanomaterials. In 2018, Sancey 
and co-workers[380] successfully synthesized gold nanocluster (core 
size ~1.5 nm; 17 kDa) protected by bidentate zwitterionic ligands 
(molecular weight was abbreviated to be AuZwMe2) and used it for non- 
invasive fluorescence imaging in subcutaneous and orthotopic glio­
blastoma mice models. For the comparison, Au25 NCs coated with 
glutathione (frequently abbreviated as GSH-AuNCs), one of the most 
classic Au25 nanoclusters described in the literature[381,382], were also 
prepared and their molecular weight was determined to be 10.5 kDa by 
MS technique. Systematic investigations of renal clearance, tumor up­
take and retention ability, pharmacokinetics, and passive tumor tar­
geting of these two types of gold nanoclusters (including AuZwMe2 and 
Au25GSH18) in tumor-bearing mice displayed that AuZwMe2 nano­
clusters not only produced a strong fluorescence signal in the s.c. tumor, 
indicating a strong tumor uptake and retention, but also possessed an 
efficient renal clearance compared to the Au25GSH18 nanoclusters. 
Better performance of AuZwMe2 nanoclusters in the fluorescence in­
tensity and tumor uptake and retention indicated that gold nanoclusters 
protected by the zwitterionic molecules were suitable for NIR imaging in 
vivo.
As is known to all, the surface properties of gold nanoclusters, 
including functional groups from the thiolate ligands and charges, may 
affect their photostability, nanotoxicity, retention and circulation in the 
tumor tissues because these important biological properties can influ­
ence the capability of NIR-II imaging in vivo[383]. Building upon this 
concept, He and colleagues[384] accomplished the controllable creation 
of weak anchoring sites on the fluorescent gold nanoclusters. This was 
achieved through the utilization of a specific water-soluble thiolate 
peptide, such as glutathione, which possessed multifunctional anchoring 
groups including -SH, -COOH, and -NH2. Experimental results showed 
that the biological behavior of the gold nanocluster was significantly 
affected by the different anchoring sites (strong (-SH) and weak (-NH2 
and -COOH)) on its surface at both in vitro and in vivo levels, which 
were in good agreement with the experimental design. Relatively, the 
presence of more exposed -NH2 groups on the surface of Au nano­
clusters, such as 11B–Au and 07B–Au nanoclusters, along with 
increased anchoring sites of COO-Au, resulted in prolonged blood cir­
culation and slower tissue distribution compared to the corresponding 
Au nanoclusters, namely, 11 A-Au nanoclusters and 07 A-Au nano­
clusters. Additionally, the weak anchoring sites on the surface of gold 
nanoclusters played a significant role in controlling in vivo biological 
behaviors and NIR-II fluorescence targeting abilities of gold nano­
clusters. This remarkable study has paved the way for future advances in 
the surface functionalization of nanomedicines by shedding light on the 
significance of weak anchoring sites. It offers the potential for a deeper 
understanding of their fundamental importance, enabling comprehen­
sive investigations in clinical analysis and the development of advanced 
theranostics. Alloy nanoclusters, a new type of multicomponent nano­
clusters with novel bio-related properties, can also be used for tumor 
imaging. By taking advantage of atomically precise alloy nanoclusters, 
Yang et al.[385] firstly used a novel stragety to assemble bimetal Ag28Pt 
nanocluster into the hydrophobic inner cavity of diodegradable 
amphiphilic chitosan derivative (ACD) micelles via hydrophobic inter­
action for the in vivo imaging. Studies showed that the positively 
charged and oil-soluble Ag28Pt@ACD retained not only the original 
fluorescence behavior of atomically precise Ag28Pt nanocluster, but 
possessed improved intensity about three times stronger than that of free 
Ag28Pt nanocluster because the aggregation-induced effect in restricted 
space of the ACD micelle inner. Furthermore, the in vivo fluorescence 
images of the tumor exhibited a significant reduction in the bright red 
fluorescence signal after the injection Ag28Pt@ACD assemblies, 
compared to the free ACD micelle. This observation highlights the 
excellent fluorescence imaging capability of the assemblies, demon­
strating their potential for use in this modality. Compared to the ACD 
and free Ag28Pt nanoclusters, the Ag28Pt@ACD assemblies also 
possessed high biocompatibility, selective accumulation, negligible 
toxicity, tumor-specific targeting ability and excellent staining of tumor 
tissue sections. This work has paved the path for the assembly of 
atomically precise alloy nanoclusters, offering immense potential for 
biomedical applications such as in vivo fluorescence imaging and ther­
apy. Additionally, it has established a foundation for future investigation 
on the mechanism of cell and material interactions at the atomic level.

4.2. Modern antibacterial agents
Antibacterial is an old and significant topic[46,394–396]. The first 
case of the antibacterial goes back to 1967 in Australia[397]. At that 
time, penicillin-resistant to Streptococcus pneumonia was reported. 
Historically, certain forms of metals, namely, silver, copper, magnesium, 
and so on, have been applied to treat diseases owning to their antibac­
terial properties[398–401]. Recently, researchers discovered that metal 
nanoclusters (less than 3 nm in size) exhibited high antibacterial activity 
(including broad-spectrum antibacterial properties, robust antibacterial 
efficiency and fast antibacterial rate). More importantly, their antibac­
terial properties are highly sensitive to the size or ligand features of the 
nanoclusters[46,402–405]. In this subsection, we will discuss several 
key factors affecting the antibacterial properties of metal nanoclusters.
(i) Size Selectivity. The size of nanoclusters is an important factor 
affecting the antibacterial performance of the clusters through their 
interaction with cells[406]. The Xie group designed and carried out a 
series of experiments to explore the influence of the cluster size on 
antimicrobial activity[407–409]. For example, Au25 nanocluster pro­
tected by 6-mercaptohexanoic acid (MHA) ligand could kill the Gram- 
positive and Gram-negative bacteria, displaying a good antimicrobial 
ability[407]. In contrast, its counterpart, typical gold nanoparticle also 
capped with 6-mercaptohexanoic acid (MHA), was not a viable anti­
bacterial nanomaterial. In detail, about 95 % of S. aureus was effectively 
killed by the Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters after 2 h, but the large particle 
killed the only approximately 3 % of S. aureus. Similarly, a majority of 
the Gram-negative type Escherichia coli could be killed by the ultrasmall 
gold clusters, and the mortality rate of the bacteria was up to about 96 
%. Still, the value was reduced to 2 % when the large nanoparticle was 
used as the antibacterial agent, suggesting different antimicrobial effi­
cacy between the gold nanoclusters and gold particles. The mechanism 
underlying the antibacterial properties of the Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters 
involved the induction of a metabolic imbalance in bacterial cells upon 
internalization. This disruption led to an elevation in the generation of 
intracellular reactive oxygen species, ultimately resulting in the anti­
bacterial effect. In addition, the different antibacterial efficacy among 
the gold nanomaterials (Au25 nanocluster vs large particle) may be 
related to their different cell uptake profile and the inherent size dif­
ference between the Au25(MHA)18 nanoclusters and gold nanoparticles.

(ii) Ligand Sensitivity. In addition to the size, the ligand types on 
the periphery of the metal nanoclusters were also found to affect their 
antibacterial activity[415]. It’s well known that metal nanoparticles can 
specifically attack prime biological targets when the proper molecules 
are loaded on the surface of particles; that is, surface modification of the 
particles is used for the targeted elimination of bacteria. Similarly, metal 
nanoclusters can be harnessed as an antibacterial agent in the fight 
against bacteria, and their efficiency can be further enhanced through 
surface modification. As early as in 2010, Jiang et al. discovered the 
sensitivity of the antibacterial agents to their external ligands[416]. 
Inspired by the results demonstrated in the previous reports in which 
gold nanoparticles with 2 nm in size were able to utilize their cationic 
surface behaviors to induce bacterial membrane damage, resulting in 
bacteria lysis and apoptosis. To further systematically investigate the 
role of the surface factors in nanocluster antibacterial activity, Zheng 
et al. prepared five types of monolayer protected Au25 nanoclusters and 
used them as the functional gold nanocluster antibecatial agents with a 
better bacterial killing efficiency for S. aureus[417]. Specifically, the 
surface ligands of these five types of Au25 nanoclusters with the same 
core (25 gold atoms) were constructed by individual or combination of 
two ligands, including the 6-mercaptohexanoic acid (MHA) with one 
carboxylic group (-COOH), p-mercaptobenzoic acid (MBA) with one 
carboxylic group (-COOH), cysteine (Cys) with one carboxylic and one 
amine group (-COOH and -NH2), bi-ligands combination of MHA and 
cysteamine (Cystm), and bi-ligands combination of MHA and 2-mercap­
toethanol (MetH). Interestingly, for the Au25 nanoclusters protected by 
the bi-ligands, their composition of surface ligands can be adjusted by 
changing the adding dose of two ligands. Using the Gram-positive bac­
teria, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) as the bacterial model, they sys­
temically investigated the killing efficiency of the five kinds of the Au25 
nanoclusters containing different surface compositions to assess the ef­
fect of the ligand towards their antibacterial ability. The bactericidal 
efficiency of Au25 nanoclusters with MHA ligand against S. aureus was 
approximately 95 %, which was almost the same as that of Au25 nano­
clusters (93 %) capped with the MBA ligand. Whereas the Au25 nano­
clusters containing the Cys ligands possessed a lower bactericidal 
efficiency towards the S. aureus and the value was only 15 %. Besides, 
the number of the -NH2 and -COOH contained in the surface ligands also 
affected the sterilization of the Au25 nanoclusters. That is, the percent­
age of bacterial deaths varied with the number of functional groups 
contained in the surface ligands of the nanoclusters. For example, the 
percentage of bacterial deaths was reduced from about 91 % to 28 % and 
16 % when the number of -NH2 functional groups increased from 0 to 2 
to 1–4 and 6–9, and the number of -COOH functional groups reduced 
from 16 to 18 to 14–17 and 9–12. Those bactericidal efficiencies fit in 
with the intracellular ROS generation that could regulate the meta­
bolism and kill the bacteria. The intracellular ROS generation induced 
by MHA ligand on the surface of the gold nanoclusters was about 2.5 
times as much as the control group. Similarly, the Au25 nanoclusters 
containing the MBA ligand induced about 2 times increase on the ROS 
generation. On the contrary, the Au25 nanoclusters capped with Cys 
ligand, the ROS generation was almost unchanged compared to the 
control group. For the Au25 nanoclusters protected by the bi-ligands, 
including Au25CystmxMHA18-x and Au25MetHxMHA18-x nanoclusters, 
the trend of their induced ROS generation also consisted with their ef­
ficiency of sterilization, indicating that the ROS generation ability was 
related to the antibacterial efficiency of Au25 nanoclusters protected by 
the different ligands. Those results also agreed with their other reports in 
which the killing mechanism of the gold nanoclusters against the bac­
teria was ROS generation to act upon the bacterial membrane and finally 
kill the bacteria (Fig. 14b). In another study, Bürgi and coworker also 
demonstrated that a change in the ligand of the water-soluble Au25 
cluster could lead to complete changes in their biological performance. 
Although the Au25 cluster protected independently by captopril and 
glutathione could be capable of internalization, captopril capped Au25 
cluster displayed remarkable cytotosicity, more specifically on cancer 
cells by inhibiting the initiating the leakage of electrons into the mito­
chondrial lumen, indicating important of the ligand on the periphery of 
well-defined metal nanoclusters for their application in biology[418]. 
Other groups analyzed the influence of surface ligand chemistry on the 
antibacterial activity of metal nanoclusters/nanoparticles, and an 
obvious surface-activity relationship was also discovered[419,420]. 
Similar to atomically precise gold nanoclusters, the antibacterial activity 
of colloidal biomimetic ligands (cysteine Cys and glutathione GSH) 
capped Ag1.00GSH0.03 and Ag1.00 Cys0.09 clusters [421] was found to be 
highly dependent upon ligand of clusters. Among them, the GSH- 
protected nanocluster had the highest stability and exhibited excellent 
antibacterial activity with the MICs values of 180 and 15 μg mL−1 for 
S. aureus and E. coli, respectively.
(iii) Synergistic Effect. In addition to the size and the types of the 
surface ligands of the nanoclusters, the synergistic effect between metal 
nanoclusters and other nanomaterials (including carbonitrides, metallic 
oxide and small biological molecules) also significantly contributes to 
the antibacterial activity of nanoclusters[422–427], while the exact 
mechanism remains elusive, and detailed investigation is necessary to 
unveil the key rule of different components for the enhanced antibac­
terial activity. For example, the Chen and Xie groups reported a syner­
gistic antibacterial agent (short for Ho-GO-Au) that was prepared by 
assembling the paramagnetic holmium ions and gold nanoclusters 
(Au25Cystm14MHA17–14) onto the graphene oxide nanosheets[428]. 
When the Ho-CO-Au nanocomposites were vertically aligned under an 
applied weak magnetic field, the nanocomposites exhibited an enhanced 
antibacterial activity and the killing efficiency achieved up to 99 % and 
the ROS generation level was approximately 9.1 fold, indicating that the 
magnetically oriented of the nanocomposites affected their antibacterial 
activity. Unlike the antibacterial performance of Ho-CO-Au nano­
composites, the Ho-GO and GO-Au showed lower killing efficacy (78 % 
and 36 %, respectively) and ROS levels (5.9- and 4.5- fold, respectively) 
under the same test conditions. A detailed insight into the mechanism 
was studied, which indicated that the sharp edges from the GO nano­
sheets, like the blade of a blender, might be able to efficiently puncture 
the membrane of the bacteria when the nanocomposites were magnet­
ically aligned. Then the conjugated Au25Cystm14MHA17–14 nanoclusters 
were delivered into the bacteria to produce large volumes of ROS, which 
led to the death of the bacteria, including Gram-positive and Gram- 
negative bacteria. In short, the synergistic antibacterial activity of the 
magnetically aligned Ho-CO-Au nanocomposites originated from the 
both chemical (via GO base and Au25Cystm14MHA17–14 nanoclusters) 
and physical (via aligned GO base) mechanisms. A similar synergistic 
antibacterial activity was also observed in titanium carbide (MXene) and

Generally, for the mechanism of the synergistic effect of nanoclusters 
and supports or biological medicines (e.g., amoxicillin), a hypothesis is 
almost reasonable as follows: (i) if one of them had not antibacterial 
activity on bacteria, another would act as an antibacterial agent in a 
different way; (ii) Functional groups from the surface of supports or 
biological medicine molecules (including hydroxy and amido groups) 
were significant. On the one hand, biological medicine themselves used 
those groups to unite each other via der Waals interaction as well as 
other weak bonds. On the other hand, the antibacterial groups were 
formed by combining the nanoclusters core and surrounding biological 
medicine via the chelation process. Thus, once the antibacterial groups 
contacted a certain point on the bacterial surface, the bacterial cells 
were destroyed; (iii) Bacterial cell membranes were hydrophobic owing 
to their special groups (e.g. phospholipids and glycoprotein). So, 
nanoclusters were likely to be in contact with the membrane of the 
target bacterial cells because the biological medicine was hydrophilic, 
but the nanocluster was not, which facilitated the movement of the 
amoxicillin towards the surface of cells; and (iv) nanocluster chelates 
inhibited the DNA from unwinding. As a result, more damages to the 
cells were caused.

including the composition of the nanoclusters, the flux of light, and the

metal nanoclusters is also the most important factor that would make 
difference to its antibacterial activity. Zheng et al.[431] prepared a se­

4.3. Drug delivery systems
Clinically, a well-designed drug delivery system has several merits: 
improving the drug bioavailability, reducing the extracellular degrada­
tion of the drug, increasing the time of drug circulates in the blood, 
prolonging the sustained antibiotic release, enhancing the antibacterial 
activity of the drug, and providing site-specific targeting and so on 
[432–434]. To date, a variety of materials with high biocompatibility, 
including polymer, phospholipid, nanoemulsion, metal nanoparticles 
and nanocluster, can be used as the building block to construct the 
nanosized drug delivery systems to meet the clinical requirements, 
which can provide a nanoscale movable platform for effective trans­
porting antiviral drugs or biological macromolecules and precise 
delivering the specific drugs or molecules to the targeted tissues 
[435–443]. Among them, atomically precise metal nanoclusters are 
highly desirable for drugs or biological macromolecule delivery owing 
to their ultrasmall size, high thermal stability, enhanced drug uptake 
and low toxicity, and the adjustable multiple drug loading and release 
[444–447]. Based on the above advantages, the metal nanoclusters may 
become a powerful candidate for efficient drug delivery. For example, 
Chen et al.[448] designed and fabricated a type of theranostic nano­
composite containing luminescent gold nanoclusters and pH-responsive 
targeting polymeric nanocarriers for attaining three aims: cancer cell 
imaging, controlled drug release and targeted drug delivery. The as- 
prepared L-AuNCs/FA-PDNH polymer nanocomposites exhibited 
different rates of hydrolysis as well as drug release from the nano­
composites under different acid and alkali intensities. They dispalyed 
the best stability in both PBS buffer and blood serum at the physiological 
environment (pH = 7.4). No obvious drug release from the PTX- 
anchored nanocomposites even placed them in both media for two 
weeks, indicating that as-obtained nanocomposite was an ideal platform 
for target drug delivery. Li et al. constructed a new kind of chemically- 
triggered drug delivery system (DDSs) using pentapeptide protected 
gold nanoclusters (Au-SGaa) that exhibitd self-regulated capabilities for 
drug loading and release[449]. Owing to its merits such as an anchor 
domain for binding to the gold nanoclusters and a binding domain for 
loading vancomycin (Van for short), the pentapeptide GSHaa was 
selected as the protecting and drug-loading ligand. In the mechanistic 
study, the GSH was preferentially selected to replace the SGaa in the 
preparation process of gold nanoclusters to remove the recognition 
groups from the SGaa ligand. Although the GSH protected gold nano­
clusters showed a similar maximum emission wavelength to SGaa cap­
ped gold nanoclusters, they exhibited a minor enhancement in emission 
intensity after the addition of Van, suggesting that the recognition 
groups from the SGaa ligand were key structural condition for loading of 
Van on the surface of the gold nanoclusters as well as the formation of 
the drug-cluster nanocomposite carriers. Interestingly, the number of 
vans released by the nanocarriers was discovered to be proportional to 
the number of bacteria present. Overall, gold nanocluster coated with 
pentapetide was the most critical component in the drug delivery 
nanocarriers. Recently, the Lin group[450] demonstrated the use of 
atomically precise Au25(MHA)18 (MHA = 6-mercaptohexanoic acid) 
cluster as delivery vehicles for the polypeptide melittin in targeting 
human cervical cancer Hela cells. They found that the Au25(MHA)18 
cluster could protect the melittin from degradation to improve the anti-

not only crucial for the subsequent construction of cluster-based drug

precise gold nanoclusters (Au25)-based drug delivery, Tiwari et al.[452]

the stable presence of peptide-based drug on the surface of the cluster

hydrogen bonding interactions between the ligand groups (-COOH) of 
Au25 and polar residues of peptides; (ii) anchoring of the N-terminal and 
charged residues side chains to the Au25 surface; and (iii) hydrophobic 
interactions between the aliphatic chains in the ligand and nonpolar 
residues in peptides. Moreover, gold nanoclusters can be used as 
nucleus-targeting nanocarriers for gene delivery (Fig. 15b-d)[453,454].
4.4. Cancer therapy
Various highly-effective cancer therapeutic approaches-based on 
metal nanocluster with well-defined stuctures have been proposed and 
worldwide used, including photodynamic therapy (PDT), photothermal 
therapy (PTT) and radiotherapy[12,41,455]. The reasons for using 
metal nanoclusters in imaging-guided cancer therapy are their apparent 
features, such as ultrasmall size, attractive fluorescence properties, high 
photostability and excellent biocompatibility[50,330,456,457]. This 
subsection will summarize the therapeutic approaches to provide a 
relatively comprehensive overview (Fig. 16).

clusters demonstrated the ability to bypass the endolysosomal pathway

photosensitizer MB for image-guided PDT in HeLa cancer cells. The 
cellular uptake of MB-loaded AuNC-mucin NPs was further analyzed, 
confirming successful delivery of the photosensitizer MB by the nano­
carrier. Upon light irradiation, the cancer cells were effectively 
destroyed through singlet oxygen generation. Notably, the fluorescent 
properties of the MB-loaded AuNC-mucin NPs enabled the tracking of 
MB delivery within the cells, eliminating the need for additional organic 
fluorescence markers. Therefore, in combination with bioimaging, MB- 
loaded biocompatible AuNC-mucin NPs could be employed to achieve 
efficient photodynamic cancer cells therapy. To overcome the disad­
vantages of gold nanoclusters such as short circulation and weak tumor 
retention in PDT, Geng et al.[467] encapsulated the gold nanoclusters 
into the BSA-nanospheres with good biocompatibility, by which the 
BSA-capped gold nanocluster (AuNCs@BSA) probe was designed and 
prepared to enhance the PDT performance. Compared to the single gold 
nanocluster, the AuNCs@BSA not only had higher singlet oxygen yield 
with the value increasing from 34.5 % to 45.5 %, but also showed lower 
cytotoxicity and stronger cellular uptake. Owing to the merits of the 
proposed nanoprobe including the high 1O2 production, excellent 
cellular uptake and good biocompatibility, the cell viability of the probe 
was decreased to 20.6 %, better than 47.5 % of the single gold nano­
cluster, which contained an equivalent gold concentration. Besides, the 
tumor was destroyed after establishing 4 T1 tumor-bearing nude mice, 
and the recurrence and death were not observed, implying that the 
improved PDT performance of AuNCs@BSA possessed high safety and 
reliability. Totally, the theranostic systems constructed by confined 
metal clusters in porous nanostructures possess the following advan­
tages: (i) high photosensitizer metal nanocluster load rate; (ii) no- 
specific photosensitizer release in the circulation process; (iii) 
enhanced cellular uptake efficiency of photosensitizer, resulting in 
enhancing the PDT efficacy; (iv) subcellular characterization of the 
nanoformula according to the fluorescence of photosensizer and plas­
mon fluorescence of supports; and (v) fluorescent imaging-guided PDT. 
In combination with good stability, high water dispersibility and solu­
bility, non-cytotoxicity and good biocompatibility, the multiple func­
tional nanocomposite system based on metal nanoclusters could be 
applied in multi-modal optical CT and photoacoustic imaging-guided 
PDT.
4.4.2. Photothermal therapy
In addition to the above-mentioned photodynamic therapy method, 
photothermal therapy (PTT) has also become a prospective technique 
for the treatment of cancer cells and tumor tissue owing to its spatio­
temporal address ability and deep tissue penetration ability as high 
therapeutic efficacy[468]. Similar to the PDT, a variety of ligands sta­
bilized gold or silver (mono-metallic) nanoclusters with intriguing bio- 
related properties can also be used for PTT[469–471]. For example, 
DNA-templated silver nanoclusters with efficient photothermal con­
version effect (converting optical energy into local heat), producing an 
efficient photothermal therapy, was reported by Wu et al.[472]. In this 
system, the exceptional photothermal characteristics of DNA-templated 
silver nanoclusters, achieved through cell hybridization chain reaction 
(HCR), resulted in the effective eradication of cancer cells. By incorpo­
rating multiple-amine groups of chitosan with excellent photothermal 
and fluorescent properties into silver nanocluster could be used as an 
energy transfer photosensitizer for imaging-traced tumor target PDT 
[473]. Under the light irradiation with the wavelength in the NIR region, 
the agents could efficiently transduce NIR optical energy into heat and 
increase the temperature up to 23 ◦C. Meanwhile, biological tissues 
minimally absorbed the light, resulting in minimal tissue damage, deep 
penetration, and negligible tissue damage. Coupled with the fluores­
cence property, the developed new therapeutic protocols enabled to the 
trace amount of nanophotoabsorbers into the targeted tissue, facilitating 
the application of this dual-response system in photothermal cancer 
therapy. A variety of multifunctional nanoplatforms based on the gold 
nanoclusters was also proven to have great promise for fluorescence- 
guided excellent photothermal therapy. For example, the BSA- 
stabilized gold nanoclusters were firstly conjugated with methionine 
and hydrophilic indocyanine green derivative MPA, giving birth to an 
Au-Met-MPA nanoprobe, which could be used for tumor targeting im­
aging[474].
Based on the “star” gold nanocluster (Au25(SG)18 nanocluster), the Li 
group[475] fabricated and utilized the Au25 nanocluster as a photo­
thermal therapy agent to systemically investigate the PTT of the MDA- 
MB-231 breast cancer cells under different conditions such as laser 
powers and irradiation times. The experimental outcomes demonstrated 
that the agent displayed remarkable photothermal activities, leading to 
complete cell death when irradiated with an 808 nm laser source at a 
power of 10 W/cm2 for 5 min, highlighting the immense potential of 
gold nanoclusters in the field of cancer phototherapy. Similarly, the 
atomically precise glutathione-stabilized Au25 nanoclusters, which were 
conjugated with indocyanine green (ICG) as an FDA-approved near- 
infrared (NIR) fluorescent dye, exhibited molecular-like photothermal

Especially, the original tumors could be completely eradicated with the

combined photodynamic and photothermal therapy.
4.4.3. Synergistic phototherapy
In terms of therapeutic mechanism, photodynamic therapy (PDT) 
and photothermal therapy (PTT) are completely different[477]. In the 
PTT process, the purpose of the destruction and even complete eradi­
cation of cancer cells are achieved through the induction of hyperther­
mia by converting the absorbed light energy into local heat to raise the 
tumor temperature. And the tumor cells are killed via apoptosis or ne­
crosis by denaturing intracellular proteins and destroying them. Besides, 
the excellent photothermal agents possessed a low fluorescence quan­
tum yield, which is a benefit for the optimal conversion of absorbed light 
into local heat because the fluorescence emission is suppressed[478]. 
But for PDT, this therapy process involves the photosensitizer with the 
visible light activation properties. Under light activation, the photo­
sensitizers can produce reactive oxygen species, which induce photo­
toxicity. Besides, the excellent PDT platform is sufficiently small to 
ensure complete coverage of the tumor because the tumor microenvi­
ronment, such as hypoxia and high endogenous concentration of 
hydrogen peroxide, is very important factor affecting the effectiveness of 
PDT[479]. Owing to the different mechanisms in both PDT and PTT, 
phototherapy with excellent performance, including the accuracy and 
effectiveness of tumor diagnosis, may be achieved by combining them 
[480,481]. For example, Li and coworkers[482] designed a 
nanoplatform utilizing gold clusters for confocal laser endomicroscopy- 
guided photothermal therapy/photodynamic therapy (PTT/PDT), spe­
cifically targeting pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). The 
nanoplatforms consisted of four components: a PTT-carrier comprised of 
a gold nanocluster, an active targeting ligand U11 peptide, a Cathepsin E 
(CTSE)-responsive PDT therapy prodrug, and a CTSE-sensitive imaging 
agent (cyanine dye Cy 5.5). In the experiments, the surface plasmon 
resonance peak of the gold clusters could be moved to the NIR region 
owing to the strong coupling among cross-linked gold particles, leading 
to the gold nanoclusters playing a useful role in the effective PTT ther­
apy. Moreover, the uptake of AuS-U11 was improved through the uPAR- 
targeting strategy, resulting in excellent reactive oxygen species (ROS) 
generation and high photothermal conversion efficiency. In studies 
using the human pancreatic tumor cell line PANC1-CSTE and its 
orthotopic tumor model treated with nanoclusters, the three-in-one 
nanoplatforms demonstrated optimal therapeutic efficacy for PDAC 
with minimal side effects because this novel nanoplatform integrated 
active targeting of tumor tissue, enzyme-triggered release of 5-ALA from 
the PDT prodrug, and endomicroscopy-guided photodynamic therapy 
and photothermal therapy. Overall, the nanoplatforms exhibited several 
distinctive features: i) NIR imaging guided therapy; ii) high stability in 
both water and physiological solution; and iii) highly synergistic PDT/ 
PTT treatment efficiency. Therefore, the fabricated nanoplatforms based 
on gold clusters represented a promising imaging-guided PDT/PTT 
therapeutic agent for tumor therapy, coupled with the highly synergistic 
therapeutic effects against tumor and the reduced side effects in normal 
pancreas tissue. Similarly, the gold nanoclusters conjugated with 
indocyanine green (ICG), forming a novel type of well-designed Au NCs- 
ICG nanoprobes, could also be applied for the real-time therapeutic 
monitoring based on the fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) 
[483]. For this system, thanks to the several unique merits, including the 
improved permeability, retention effect, the gp60-mediated secreted 
protein acidic and rich in cysteine combined transport pathway, the 
nanoprobe destromented the enhanced cellular uptake and effective 
tumor targeting, realizing the dual-modal NIRF/PA imaging in vivo and 
in vitro. Compared to the efficiency of PDT or PTT alone, the synergistic 
PDT-PTT of the prepared nanoprobe could effectively kill 95 % of cancer

4.4.4. Radiation therapy
Moreover, radiation therapy is also a promising tool in cancer ther­
apy. This common modality could achieve the main goal of therapy 
using the high-energy X-ray or gamma ray radiation to kill cancer and 
tumor cells. During the therapeutic process, the high energy radiation 
can kill the cancer cells and normal ones, resulting in inevitable and 
serious damage to healthy cells[487–489]. Thus, a major obstacle in 
efficient radiation therapy is to find the balance between the efficiency 
of tumor eradication and minimal side effects, that is, the treatment 
could maximize the killing effect to cancer or tumor cells and minimize 
the damage to the healthy cells surrounding them. The most promising 
approach to tackle this challenge is to use a radiosensitizer that can 
amplify the effect of radiation at relatively low and safe radiation dose, 
thereby preserving healthy tissue. Metal nanoclusters are one of the 
most promising radiosensitizers and have received extensive attention 
owing to their enhancement radiation effects under the radiation beams 
collision at the tumor site[490–492]. For example, as early as 2013, the 
Xie group[493] was the first to demonstrate the enhancement of 
radiotherapy of Au25 nanoclusters, which consisted of 25 gold atoms and 
biocompatible capping agents such as GSH and BSA. The GSH- and BSA- 
capped Au25 nanoclusters showed superior tumor accumulation via the 
improved permeability and retention effect owing to the strong radio­
therapy enhancement from the gold core, leading to excellent perfor­
mance for cancer radiotherapy compared to the much larger gold 
nanoparticles. The enhanced radiotherapeutic performance of atomi­
cally precise Au25 cluster was ascribed to the photoelectric effect and 
Compton scattering for the nanoclusters, where Au25 nanocluster (sta­
bilized by the GSH) as the radiosensitizer displayed a remarkable 
decrease in tumor volume and weight. Meanwhile, the hydrosoluble 
GSH-capped Au25 nanocluster exhibited efficient renal clearance and no 
obvious toxicity. That was in stark contrast to the BSA-stabilized Au25 
nanoclusters, which could not be effectively cleaned by the kidney, 
causing liver damage. In the same year, this group also observed that the 
size of the gold nanoclusters played a key role in the design of the 
theranostic agents for biomedicine[494]. The authors prepared the GSH- 
protected Au10–12 nanoclusters and applied them as a promising radio­
sensitizer for radiotherapeutic therapy. Due to the biocompatibility and 
the enhanced deposition behavior in tumor tissues of the outer shell on 
the surface of the gold nanocluster, the prepared Au10–12 nanocluster 
could increase tumor uptake and the targeting specificity through 
escaping the reticulo-endothelial system (RES) absorption and acti­
vating the transporter on the cell surface, leading to a significant 
enhancement in cancer radiotherapy. The gold nanoclusters with highly 
exposed hydrophilic GSH protecting agent was equipped with the 
properties of an excellent photosensitizer, such as targeting specificity, 
high tumor uptake and efficient renal clearance. These advantages 
promoted them to be ideal photosensitizers that could be widely used in 
the radiotherapy (Fig. 20a-c). In a follow-up study by the same team, the 
GSH-stabilized Au29–43 nanoclusters could also escape the RES absorp­
tion and showed high tumor uptake of ~8.1 % ID/g at 24 h post- 
injection via the improved EPR effect[495]. Thus, the gold nano­
clusters gave rise to a strong enhancement for radiotherapy, coupled 
with negligible damage to healthy tissue. More importantly, the as- 
obtained Au29–43 nanocluster could be efficiently excluded by the kid­
ney after the treatment, avoiding the side-effects or toxicity resulting 
from the accumulation of gold nanoclusters in the body. These excellent 
works indicated that the GSH-protected gold nanoclusters in the sub- 
nanometer regime were a promising rediosensitizer for cancer radio­
therapy. Recently, a type of alkynyl ligand stabilized Au8(C21H27O2)8 
(Au8 NCs) with intensity freen fluorescence (QY = 58.7 %) showed 
excellent radio sensitization effect in the tumor targeting[496]. Form 
the perspective of radiotherapy, the atomically precise Au8 capped with 
alkynyl ligand accorded with the characteristics of excellent radio­
sensitizers: (i) levonorgestrel surface modification that could achieve 
the satisfactory biocompatibility of Au8 nanoclusters; (ii) subnanometer 
sized gold nanoclusters that could be easily accumulated inside tumors 
and focus the passing laser radiation; and (iii) well-defined structure and 
composition that could provide a chance to tailor the radiosensitizer by 
regulating the functional ligands. Mechanistically, the induced produc­
tion of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by laser irradiation is important in 
the radiotherapy process because the ROS could directly attack the co­
valent bond of DNA, leading to the cells death and apoptosis. The un­
derlying mechanism of Au8 nanolcusters-mediated radio sensitization 
was determined to be an increase in intracellular ROS after irradiating 
with X-ray, resulting in cell apoptosis. Besides, no histopathological 
abnormalities in the organs were discovered after the Au8 + 4 Gy were 
uniformly distributed in the tumors. These findings indicated that the 
prepared nanosensitizer achieved two main goals: reducing the X-ray 
dose but achieving high radiation therapeutic efficiency in the cancer 
therapeutic process and decreasing the side effects of radiation in 
healthy tissues (Fig. 20d-g).
5. Conclusion and outlook
In this review, we briefly summarize the basic strategies for syn­
thesizing metal nanoclusters with well-defined structures and compre­
hensively discuss the bio-related properties and biological application of 
atomically precise gold and silver nanoclusters, aiming to provide some 
new insights into gold and silver nanoclusters with bio-related proper­
ties for biological applications. In terms of bio-related properties, we 
discuss diverse properties of atomically precise gold and silver nano­
clusters, which exhibit discrete energy structures resulting from the 
quantum size effects. These characteristics include optical absorption, 
adjustable photoluminescence, circularly polarized luminescence and 
clusterzyme. Regarding biological applications, we summarize several 
uses of metal nanoclusters in the biological field, including bioimaging, 
antibacterial agents, drug delivery systems and cancer therapy, high­
lighting the unique structure/composition-structure relationship

Although significant advances have been achieved in atomically 
precise metal nanoclusters for biological applications, there are still 
many challenges to be addressed: 
(i) Exploring the synthesis mechanism. Numerous atomically precise 
metal nanoclusters have been prepared using various methods, 
however, the detailed structure or size evolution process in the 
solution are not fully understood. Furthermore, the solvent can 
influence the roles of the protecting ligands in the synthesis 
process, thereby affecting the size and structure of the final pro­
tects. However, it is unclear how solvents with different polarity 
affect the role of ligands in the synthesis process. In addition, the 
importance of pH, temperature, the concentrations of precursors 
and reductants and the stirring rate should be studied in depth to 
understand the formation mechanism of metal nanoclusters.
(ii) Obtaining the photoluminescent water-soluble clusters with high 
QY. While the photoluminescence of metal nanoclusters can be 
regulated by ligands, the size and heterogeneous metal dopants, 
the QY and brightness levels remain relatively low, making it 
challenging to meet the requirements of practical applications 
and commercialization. However, it is feasible and meaningful to 
develop highly luminescent metal nanoclusters with precise- 
atomic structures through innovative strategies, such as AIE 
and surface loading of “luminescent gene” - biological small 
molecule. These approaches could potentially lead to significant 
breakthrough in the practical applications.
(iii) Improving the biocompatibility of metal clusters. Biocompati­
bility, as a critical issue in clinical trials, can be enhanced through 
optimizing ligands and constructing reasonable spatial configu­
rations. Compared to conventional biomaterials, thiolated metal 
nanoclusters face a major drawback: they often rely on toxic

We emphasize that significant efforts should be dedicated to the 
guided design of gold and silver nanoclusters for biological ap­

achieve this goal effectively.
(iv) Increasing the variety of cluster types beyond coinage metals. 
Except for the coinage clusters, Pd and Pt clusters were recently 
reported. Generally, Pd and Pt clusters also exhibit strong pho­
toluminescence and good catalytic activity (e.g., production of 
singlet oxygen) with high catalytic efficiency in certain 
biochemical reactions, attributed to their inherent active sites. 
However, these clusters are known for their significant biological 
toxicity, making it a considerable challenge to be utilized for 
biological applications. Therefore, it is highly desirable to 
develop potential strategies, including surface functionalization, 
targeted delivery, and reducing off-target effects, to overcome 
these challenges and achieve possible breakthroughs in specific 
biomedical fields such as precision drug delivery.
(v) Machine learning. Machine learning (ML) is a branch of artificial 
intelligence (AI), which has proven particularly useful for rapidly 
designing materials and predicting their properties. Compared 
with the traditional density functional theory (DFT) approach, 
computational results based on ML are accurate, reliable and, in 
most cases, trustworthy. ML offers significant benefits for atom­
ically precise, water-soluble metal nanoclusters with bio-related 
properties. On the one hand, once an ML model is established, 
it can be used to optimize the atomic structure of water-soluble 
nanoclusters during large-scale screening, thereby enabling 
timely and effective regulation of their bio-related properties. On 
the other hand, ML will be helpful to establish a rational 
structure/composition-properties relationship, facilitate a deeper 
understanding of certain biological mechanisms and provide 
theoretical guidance for designing hydrophilic nanoclusters with 
well-defined structures.

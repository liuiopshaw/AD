Chiral nanoparticle-remodeled gut 
microbiota alleviates neurodegeneration 
via the gut–brain axis

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by amyloid-β accumulation 
in the brain and hyperphosphorylated tau aggregation, as well as 
neuroinflammation. The gut–brain axis has emerged as a therapeutic 
target in neurodegenerative diseases by modulating metabolic activity, 
neuroimmune functions and sensory neuronal signaling. Here we 
investigate interactions between orally ingested chiral Au nanoparticles 
and the gut microbiota in AD mice. Oral administration of chiral Au 
nanoparticles restored cognitive abilities and ameliorated amyloid-β and 
hyperphosphorylated tau pathologies in AD mice via alterations in the gut 
microbiome composition and an increase in the gut metabolite, indole-3-
acetic acid, which was lower in serum and cerebrospinal fluid of patients with 
AD compared with age-matched controls. Oral administration of indole-
3-acetic acid was able to penetrate the blood–brain barrier and alleviated 
cognitive decline and pathology including neuroinflammation in AD mice. 
These findings provide a promising therapeutic target for the amelioration 
of neuroinflammation and treatment of neurodegenerative diseases.
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is an age-related neurodegenerative disorder 
caused by amyloid-β (Aβ) deposits in the brain, hyperphosphorylated 
tau (p-tau) plaques and neuroinflammation1,2. Neurodegenerative dis-
eases are associated with altered gut microbiota3–6, suggesting that the 
gut microbiota may influence brain function and disease progression via 
the gut–brain axis7–10. By converting dietary factors, microbes produce 
metabolites that can affect gut–brain communication11–14. For example, 
certain gut microbes convert dietary tyrosine to 4-ethylphenol, 
which affects oligodendrocyte function and myelin formation in the 
brain, ultimately leading to anxiety-like behaviors15. The host physiol-
ogy is also affected by other gut bacterial metabolites, such as short-
chain fatty acids16, N6-carboxymethyllysine17, indole-3 propionate18, 
indole-3-acetic acid (IAA)19 and nicotinamide riboside20. The long- 
distance interaction between the gut microbiota and neurodegeneration 
may provide a theoretical foundation for treating those diseases21–23. 
Additional interventions, such as dietary adjustment16,24,25, pro­
biotics26,27 and various molecules20,28–31, can further positively alter 
the composition and diversity of the gut microbiota. While such inter-
actions pose considerable challenges, including their efficacy and 
safety, this strategy provides unique insights into ameliorating the 
relevant disease through direct effects on the host. Oral delivery of 
some agents is limited by negative factors, such as acidic conditions 
in the stomach and low permeability of the intestinal epithelium32. 
Therefore, the development of applicable materials that can overcome 
these physiological challenges has become a major area of research.
Several recent studies have reported the fabrication33–35, assem-
bly36–38 and detection39 of plasmonic nanoparticles (NPs) with high 
chiroptical activity40, as well as the biological effects of chiral NPs41,

To investigate whether the chiral l-Au NPs altered the gut micro-
biota, we performed a fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experi-
ment (Fig. 1f). The fecal microbiota of donor mice (APP/PS1 AD mice, 
WT mice or APP/PS1 AD mice orally administered l-Au NPs) was trans-
planted into antibiotic-treated mice (APP/PS1 AD or WT mice). For 
example, l-Au→AD represents fecal microbiota transplanted from 
the donor mice, APP/PS1 AD mice orally administered l-Au NPs, into 
receptor APP/PS1 AD mice. The results showed that the behavior and 
cognition were markedly improved and 79% of Aβ and 75% of p-tau in 
the CSF of APP/PS1 AD mice were reduced in the l-Au NPs→AD group 
compared with the AD→AD group (Fig. 1g–j). Immunohistochemical 
and immunofluorescence analyses confirmed this reduction in p-tau 
and Aβ (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). An increased number of intact neu-
ronal nuclei was also observed in mice in the l-Au→AD group (Extended 
Data Fig. 1c). The time to cognitive improvement in the FMT experiment 
(about 60 days) was shorter than that of the l-Au NPs intervention 
(about 90 days), while the fecal microbiota from APP/PS1 AD mice given 
oral administration of l-Au NPs for 3 months was heat-killed treated 
(HT) and then transplanted into APP/PS1 AD mice (HT of l-Au→AD), 
and there were no pathological or behavioral cognitive improvements 
(Fig. 1g–j). These results suggest that l-Au NPs improve the cognitive 
abilities of APP/PS1 AD mice by remodeling the gut microbiota and ulti-
mately restoring them back to the level of the WT→WT group. However, 
the mice in the WT→AD group did not recover to the same level as the 
WT→WT group after 60 days because the specific gut microbiota of 
the donor mice was insufficient.
Au NPs remodel the gut microbiome and metabolites in AD 
mice
To assess the effects of l-Au NPs on the composition and diversity of the 
gut microbiota, we analyzed the fecal microbiota with high-throughput 
gene sequencing of 16S ribosomal RNA genes. Principal component 
analysis (PCA) showed clear differences between the gut microbiota 
of the AD and WT mice (Fig. 2a). Notably, after oral administration of 
l-Au NPs to APP/PS1 AD mice, the gut microbiota was remodeled and 
became more similar to that of WT mice, but also displayed a distinct 
bacterial community (Fig. 2b). However, the α-diversity (Chao 1, Simp-
son and Shannon indexes) of the gut microbiota remained unchanged 
(Supplementary Fig. 4). The phyla Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobiota 
were increased in the l-Au NPs group, whereas the phyla Proteobacteria 
and Desulfobacterota showed obvious attenuation compared with 
their levels in the AD group, indicating that the composition of the gut 
microbiota was largely remodeled by the Au NPs (Fig. 2c). At the genus 
level, some bacteria in the l-Au NPs group regained and even exceeded 
including immune response42, neural stem cell differentiation43, bacte-
rial suppression44 and enzyme mimicry45,46. It has been reported that 
3.3-nm gold NPs cross the blood–brain barrier following intravenous 
administration and inhibit Aβ aggregation without noticeable toxicity. 
Compared with intravenous administration, oral administration is 
more acceptable because it is noninvasive, simple and convenient. 
Technologies currently being developed for oral drug delivery include 
molecular modifications47 and biomimetic NPs48–51. However, little is 
known about their effects on the complex gut microbiota.
In this article, we investigate the contribution of chiral Au NPs to 
the improvement of AD through their interaction with the gut micro­
biota. We find significant enantiomer-dependent changes in the 
microbiota and its metabolites, and confirm the functions of these 
metabolites with a series of validation experiments. A critical gut 
metabolite, IAA, reduces neuroinflammatory signaling, improves 
cognitive function and attenuates pathological processes by activat-
ing the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) in AD mice. The mechanism 
by which chiral Au NPs remodel the gut microbiota and metabolites 
has been revealed by altering microbial gene expression.
Results
Au NPs improve AD mice by altering gut microbiota
Au enantiomers were synthesized according to our previous protocol, 
with some modifications42 (Supplementary Fig. 1). These NPs have 
symmetric circular dichroic (CD) spectra reaching values of 1,000 
at 590 nm. l-, d- and achiral Au octahedra (denoted as l-Au NPs, d-Au 
NPs and t-Au NPs, respectively) were intragastrically administered to 
mice at a concentration of 10 mg kg−1 per day, and the mice’s behavior 
was monitored using a Morris water maze experiment to assess any 
changes in their cognitive abilities (Fig. 1a). The 10-month-old APP/
PS1 AD mice, which recapitulate the pathological features of AD, were 
used as animal models for 90 days. Notably, the behavior of APP/PS1 
AD mice improved significantly after oral administration of l-Au for 
90 days (Fig. 1b,c). Moreover, the two hallmarks of AD, Aβ and p-tau 
in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), were reduced by more than 70% in mice 
treated with l-Au NPs, with average levels equivalent to those of wild-
type (WT) mice (Fig. 1d,e). These reductions were verified by immu-
nohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses (Supplementary 
Fig. 2). Nissl staining of the hippocampus 90 days after oral adminis-
tration of l-Au NPs showed that the nuclei of the neurons were intact 
and that the number of neurons had increased compared with those 
of APP/PS1 AD mice. The water maze behavior and pathological signs 
of the mice in the l-Au NPs group significantly improved, whereas nei-
ther the mice in the d-Au NPs nor the t-Au NPs group reached the level 
of the WT mice. This indicates that the effect on APP/PS1 AD mice is 
associated with the oral consumption of chiral Au NPs, suggesting a 
possible therapeutic treatment for AD.

showed that the rigid chiral structure of Au NPs made them tolerant 
to gastric acid and proteolysis, and that their structures remained 
largely intact in the gut of mice. Transmission electron microscopy

The abundance of metabolites in mouse feces was also evaluated 
using untargeted metabolomic profiling. A PCA and heat map revealed 
that metabolites were markedly altered in the feces of APP/PS1 AD 
mice after oral administration of l-Au NPs for 90 days, consistent with 
changes in the gut microbiota (Fig. 2f,g). To analyze these transformed 
metabolites between the l-Au NPs and the AD group, pathway enrich-
ment analysis and screening were performed using the MetaboAnalyst 
5.0 online platform and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes 
(KEGG) online database. The results exhibited significant changes in 
the tryptophan (Trp) metabolic pathway (Fig. 2h and Supplementary 
Table 1). Specifically, indole-3-ethanol (IE) and IAA were reduced in the 
APP/PS1 AD mice but markedly increased in the mice treated with oral 
l-Au NPs compared with their levels in the WT mice (Fig. 2i,j). Similarly, 
indole-3-acetamide (IAM), an intermediate in the transition from Trp to 
IAA, was also increased in the mice treated with oral l-Au NPs (Fig. 2k). 
The abundances of Clostridium (especially) and Lactobacillus, which 
generated indole compounds, followed the same trends as indole 
compounds in all groups (Fig. 2d,e). No other indole metabolites were 
significantly altered (Supplementary Fig. 7). Some other metabolites 
of Trp also showed enantiomer-specific changes in l-Au, d-Au and t-Au 
NPs (Supplementary Fig. 8). The metabolites in the l-Au NP group 
were almost the same as those in WT mice, along with the differences 
in cognition abilities and pathologies among groups.
IAA improves cognitive behavior and pathology of AD mice
To evaluate the effects of Trp on AD progression in the l-Au NP-treated 
group, a mixture of Trp (200 mg kg−1 per day) and l-Au NPs (10 mg kg−1 
per day) administered orally for 90 days restored the cognitive ability 
of APP/PS1 AD mice to the level of WT mice, but Trp alone had no effect, 
indicating that l-Au NP-driven conversion of Trp to IAA is essential to the 
therapeutic effects (Fig. 3a–e and Supplementary Fig. 9). Then, a series 
of benchmark experiments were carried out to verify the function of Trp 
metabolites (Supplementary Fig. 10). We administered APP/PS1 AD mice 
with a mixture of IAA (100 mg kg−1 per day), IAM (200 mg kg−1 per day) 
and IE (400 mg kg−1 per day) (indoles group) or a mixture of xanthurenic 
(200 mg kg−1 per day) and 4-(2-aminophenyl)-2,4-dioxobutanoic 
(200 mg kg−1 per day) (kynurenine group), and observed that behavior 
and pathology improved in the indoles group after 45 days, whereas no 
improvement was observed in the kynurenine group mice.
To determine which kind of small molecule is crucial to treat AD, 
APP/PS1 AD mice were given oral doses of IAA (100 mg kg−1 per day), 
IAM (200 mg kg−1 per day) and IE (400 mg kg−1 per day). The experi-
mental results showed that IAA improved cognitive behavior and 
AD-associated pathology, while IAM or IE had no effect (Supplemen-
tary Fig. 11). We also studied the effects of IAA on APP/PS1/Tau trans-
genic (3xTg) AD mice (Fig. 3f). A significant trend for a reduction 
in latency was verified in 3xTg AD mice after treatment with IAA in 
the Morris water maze experiment (Fig. 3g,h). Concurrently, a novel 
object recognition (NOR) test was carried out to examine the cogni-
tive ability of 3xTg AD mice, and the data showed that 3xTg AD mice 
that received an oral administration of IAA spent more time with novel 
object, demonstrating that IAA improved cognition of 3xTg AD mice 
(Fig. 3i,j). The contents of Aβ and p-tau in the CSF of 3xTg AD mice 
were the same as those of the WT mice after treatment with IAA for 
approximately 45 days (Fig. 3k,l), which was also verified by immu-
nohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses (Extended Data 
Fig. 2a–c). In addition, no toxicity was observed in 3xTg AD mice after 
daily administration for 45 days of the safe dose of IAA (100 mg kg−1 
per day) (Supplementary Fig. 12). To investigate the effects of cage or 
the treatment of IAA on microbiota, APP/PS1 AD mice from the oral 
administration of IAA group were co-housed in the same cage with APP/
PS1 AD or WT mice without any treatment for 45 days (Extended Data 
Fig. 3a). The co-housed APP/PS1 AD mice of the oral administration of 
IAA group displayed distinct improvements in cognitive ability and 
pathology, compared with APP/PS1 AD mice, indicating that the effect 
of short-term exposure to microbiota was negligible (Extended Data 
Fig. 3b–j). Taken together, these results provided compelling evidence 
that IAA contributed to the improvement of cognitive behavior and 
disease pathology in AD mice.

and CSF of Au NP-treated mice. The IAA content in the serum and CSF 
of the APP/PS1 AD mice was markedly reduced by 91.1% and 80.5%,

the serum and CSF of APP/PS1 AD mice treated with orally administered 
l-Au NPs was enhanced 13.8- and 5.1-fold, respectively, and was similar 
to that in the WT mice. However, the IAA content in both the serum and 
CSF of APP/PS1 AD mice after oral administration of d-Au NPs was lower 
than that of WT mice, and the t-Au NPs group was the lowest among

IAA reduces neuroinflammation by the NLRP3 inflammasome
Suppressing inflammation in the central nervous system by limiting 
the pathogenic activities of microglia and astrocytes may offer novel 
therapies for AD and other neuroinflammatory diseases52–54. To inves-
tigate the mechanism of the IAA molecule, the inflammatory status of 
mouse brains was evaluated. We found that the contents of the inflam-
matory factors interleukin 6 (IL6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and 
IL1β were higher in the brains of APP/PS1 AD mice than in those of WT 
mice, indicating that neuroinflammatory processes are critical in the 
development and progression of AD2,55. Treatment of APP/PS1 AD mice 
with IAA for 45 days reduced inflammatory factors in the brain to the 
levels of WT mice, demonstrating that IAA ameliorates AD progression 
by inhibiting neuroinflammation (Fig. 5a–c). Similarly, inflammatory 
factors in the brain were reduced in APP/PS1 AD mice by oral adminis-
tration of l-Au NPs and l-Au→AD FMT mice (Extended Data Fig. 5a–f).

mouse brain sections for induction of IBA1 and GFAP, both hallmarks 
of microglia and astrocyte activation, and found no distinct differences 
between APP/PS1 AD mice treated with IAA and their WT counterparts 
(Fig. 5d,e). As activation of microglia and astrocytes can be divided into 
two opposing types, the proinflammatory subtype (M1 and A1) and 
the neuroprotective subtype (M2 and A2), we also characterized the 
phenotypes of IAA-treated microglia cell line BV2 and astrocytes cell 
line MA-c in vitro. Both M1 microglia and A1 astrocytes increased after 
stimulation with monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL), whereas M2 microglia 
and A2 astrocytes increased after treatment with IAA (Extended Data 
Fig. 6a–e). This suggests that microglia and astrocytes tend to enter a 
neuroprotective state after treatment with IAA.
Analysis of NLRP3 in the brain tissue showed that it was obviously 
increased in APP/PS1 AD mice relative to WT mice. In contrast, APP/PS1 
AD mice administered with IAA showed attenuated NLRP3 expression 
and the protein was restored to the level of WT mice (Fig. 5d,e). Western 
blotting and quantitative PCR with reverse transcription (RT–qPCR) 
analyses of brain tissue revealed that IAA inhibited the expression of 
NLRP3, caspase 1, IL18 and IL1β. We also found that NF-κB was reduced 
in the brains of APP/PS1 AD mice treated with IAA (Fig. 5f,g).
It has been reported that regulatory T cells (Treg cells) or microglia 
cells can be controlled by ligand-activated AHR and regulate neuroin-
flammation12,56–58. We therefore speculate that Treg or microglia cells 
are the key targets of IAA while ameliorating the disease progression 
of AD. To explore the role of the cells, we transiently depleted Treg or 
microglia cells in 3xTg AD mice (Extended Data Fig. 7a). After deple-
tion of Treg by anti-CD25, 3xTg AD mice showed improved cognitive 
function and pathology after oral administration of IAA for 45 days, as 
well a decrease in the contents of Aβ and p-tau in CSF, similar effects 
to those of 3xTg AD mice treated with IAA without Treg cell deletion. 
In contrast, the cognitive function, pathological processes and Aβ 
and p-tau concentrations in the CSF of 3xTg AD mice with microglia 
cells depleted by PLX3397 have no any improvements following oral 
administration with IAA for 45 days (Extended Data Fig. 7b–j). Mean-
while, the systemic immune cell population of Treg cells, TH17 cells and 
IL17-producing γδ T (IL17γδT) cells in the colon, periphery, meninges 
and brain parenchyma of 3xTg AD mice orally administrated IAA were 
analyzed by flow cytometry9,59. The results showed an increase in Treg 
cells and a decrease in TH17 and IL17γδT cells in the colon, peripheral 
and meninges of 3xTg AD mice after treatment with IAA for 45 days. 
On the contrary, 3xTg AD mice without IAA treatment had a reduc-
tion in Treg cells and an increase in TH17 and IL17γδT cells in the colon, 
peripheral and meninges (Extended Data Fig. 8a–i). Noticeably, in 
brain parenchyma, these cells were hardly affected (Extended Data 
Fig. 8j–l). Collectively, these results confirmed that the target of IAA 
was microglia and astrocytes cells, rather than Treg cells.
To investigate how the gut metabolite IAA suppresses Nlrp3 tran-
scription and NLRP3 inflammasome activity in microglia and astro-
cytes, the effects of IAA on NLRP3 inflammasomes were evaluated. 
The data showed that the expression of NLRP3, caspase 1, IL18, IL1β and 
NF-κB was significantly attenuated in primary microglia, primary astro-
cyte cells, microglia cell line BV2 and astrocyte cell line MA-c treated 
with IAA, whereas the levels of pro-caspase 1, apoptosis-associated 
speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), pro-IL18 and pro-IL1β 
remained unchanged. Several studies have shown that some metabo-
lites of Trp modulate the immune response by activating AHR60–64. 
When cells were treated in vitro with both IAA and CH-223191, there was 
no significant change in the NLRP3 inflammasome, suggesting that IAA 
acts by activating AHR (Fig. 5h–k and Extended Data Fig. 9a–d). Impor-
tantly, 3xTg AD mice showed that oral administration of IAA failed to 
act on either cognitive abilities (water maze and NOR) or pathologies 
(Aβ and p-tau concentration in CSF, Aβ and p-tau load in hippocampus) 
after the inhibition of AHR receptor, CH-223191 (Fig. 3f–l). Additionally, 
inflammasome activity, the concentration of IL18 and IL1β measured 
by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, was significantly decreased 
after treatment with IAA (Extended Data Fig. 9e–h). The expression of 
NLRP3 in BV2 and MA-c cells did not change after treatment with other 
metabolites, such as melatonin, indomethacin or metformin (Supple-
mentary Fig. 17). Overall, these results indicate that IAA activates the 
AHR in microglial cells and astrocytes both in vivo and in vitro, which 
inhibits NLRP3 expression, caspase 1 activation and subsequent IL1β 
secretion via the transcription factor NF-κB (Fig. 5l). Thus, negative 
regulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome reduced neuroinflammation 
and pathology from AD. These data suggest that IAA can cross the 
blood–brain barrier and ultimately act in the brain once it enters the 
gut through the bloodstream.

Apart from the effect on genes, we also explored the interaction 
between Au NPs and bacteria. PEGylated Au NPs are positively charged, 
which provides a basis for the interaction with negatively charged 
bacteria through electrostatic forces, as confirmed by confocal and 
scanning electron microspcopy in L. Reuteri (Extended Data Fig. 10a–c). 
In addition, the constant of binding affinity, Ka, between l-Au NPs and 
Trp (1.567 × 107 M−1) was 15.2- and 25.2-fold higher than that between 
d-Au NPs and Trp and t-Au NPs and Trp, respectively (Extended Data 
Fig. 10d–f). Collectively, it was shown that l-Au NPs adsorbed higher 
Trp and interacted with bacteria, providing more substrates for Trp 
metabolism in bacteria, confirmed by ultraviolet, CD and fluorescence 
spectra (Extended Data Fig. 10g–i).
The pathway to increase intestinal IAA can be summarized as 
follows: Au NPs interact with the gut microbiota through electro-
static interaction (Extended Data Fig. 10). By binding more Trp to the 
microbiota, l-Au NPs enhance Trp biotransformation while upregulat-
ing the expression of genes that encode enzymes in Trp metabolism 
to increase IAA content (Fig. 6a–i). IAA improves the microenviron-
ment around bacteria in the gut and remodels the gut microbiota 
(Fig. 2a), which resulted in a significant increase in IAA-producing 
bacteria (for example, Clostridium and Lactobacillus) to further 
enhance gut IAA content and create a positive feedback loop.
Discussion
In this study, we demonstrated the crucial effects of chiral Au NPs on 
gut microbiota remodeling. The genera Clostridium and Lactobacillus, 
together with a downstream metabolite of Trp metabolism, IAA, con-
tribute substantially to the improvement of AD. Mechanistic analy-
sis reveals that l-Au NPs upregulate enzyme expression essential for 
IAA synthesis in the microbiota while oral IAA activates AHR in brain 
microglia and astrocytes and inhibits the NLRP3 inflammasome via 
the transcription factor NF-κB, thereby reducing the pathology of AD. 
Upon investigating potential mechanisms of interaction between NPs 
and gut microbiota, it was found that the affinity between chiral NPs 
and Trp resulted in the different therapeutic efficacy.
Noticeably, after deletion of microglia, the cognitive function, 
pathological processes and Aβ and p-tau concentrations in CSF of 
3xTg AD mice diminished any improvements induced by oral admin-
istration with IAA for 1.5 months, supporting the role of microglia 
IAA-induced rescue of behavioral and pathological features exhibited 
by the 3xTg AD mice. However, it remains unclear whether there is 
a direct or indirect effect of IAA on microglia leading to functional 
recovery, and future work using conditional knockout mouse models 
would be required to comprehensively characterize the effect of IAA 
on microglia. Previous studies have proposed targeting Treg-mediated 
systemic immunosuppression for treating AD56,57. Our data demon-
strated that after depletion of Treg by anti-CD25 for 1.5 months, 3xTg 
AD mice treated with IAA showed the gradually improved cogni-
tive function after 15 and 30 days, indicating the Treg cells are not 
the direct target of IAA. However, the increasement in Treg cells and 
the decreases in TH17 and IL17γδT cells in the colon, peripheral and 
meninges of 3xTg AD mice were revealed after treatment with IAA 
for 45 days. Therefore, Treg cells might be involved in the alleviation 
of AD with IAA, as prolonged treatment with allogenic antibodies 
might give rise to neutralizing antibodies in the host that might affect 
treatment efficiency. The limitation of this study is that the causal 
link between the proposed immune cells and AD pathology might 
be further definitively parsed.
In conclusion, this discovery paves the way forward to apply chiral 
nanomaterials for small-molecule exploration via the gut–brain axis 
for a wide range of biomedical demands.
Methods
Human samples
Serum and CSF samples were collected from patients with AD (serum 
n = 33 and CSF n = 38) and healthy humans (serum n = 38 and CSF 
n = 24), aged 50–90 years. No sex and gender analysis was carried 
out, and participants eligible for screening were included by default 
in this study. The study was approved by the institutional review board 
of Beijing Tiantan Hospital of Capital Medical University (KY-2021-
028-01), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. 
All the participants underwent a screening process that included a 
medical history and physical and neurological examinations, and had 
no history of a psychiatric condition or traumatic brain injury that 
could compromise brain function. On the basis of this assessment, we 
excluded samples from patients with the following conditions: those 
taking antibiotics, probiotics, prebiotics, symbiotics and other drugs 
that affect the gut microbiota within 3 months of sampling; those with 
severe heart, brain, liver, kidney or hematopoietic system diseases or 
other serious primary diseases; and those with severe malnutrition, 
drug addiction or alcohol abuse.
Animals
APP/PS1 transgenic AD mice model with overexpression of 
KM670/671NL (Swedish) in human APP, as well as delta E9 mutations 
in human PS1, have been described previously1,2. APP/PS1 AD mice 
(Jac/034829) were obtained from Aniphe Biolaboratory Inc. The 
3xTg AD mice model with Thy-1.2 promoter-driven overexpression of 
KM670/671NL (Swedish) mutations in human APP, M146V mutations in 
human PS1 and P301L mutations in human MAPT, has been described 
previously3. The 3xTg AD mice (zs40056) were obtained from the Zhis-
han Health Medical Research Institute. C57BL/6J mice were obtained 
from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All 
experiments were performed in 10-month-old male mice. All diets 
were obtained from Jiangsu Xietong Pharmaceutical Bio-engineering 
Co., Ltd (1010088). All the mice were maintained in a room at 25 ± 2 °C 
with a strict 12 h/12 h light–dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad 
libitum. Male APP/PS1 AD mice, male 3xTg AD mice and C57BL/6J mice 
were bred in separate cages to avoid microbiota cross-transfer. The AD 
mice were randomly assigned to different treatments.
The mice were treated with l-, d- or t-Au NPs (10 mg kg−1 per day) 
administered by oral gavage once daily for 3 months, based on a behav-
ioral test performed every 1 month. During the experiment, the weight 
of each mouse was recorded every 15 days. For the confirmatory experi-
ments, mice were administered with IAA (100 mg kg−1 per day), IAM 
(200 mg kg−1 per day), IE (400 mg kg−1 per day), Trp (200 mg kg−1 per 
day), xanthurenic acid (200 mg kg−1 per day) and 4-(2-aminophenyl)-
2,4-dioxobutanoic (200 mg kg−1 per day). All metabolites were dis-
solved in vegetable oil and administered via oral gavage every 24 h. 
Control mice received equivalent volumes of vegetable oil via gavage.
APP/PS1 AD mice in the experimental group were labeled and co-
housed with WT/APP/PS1 AD mice of the same age in the control group. 
The APP/PS1 AD mice in the experimental group were gavaged with IAA 
every day and their behavior was monitored during the experiment.
The experimental details for AHR inhibition in vivo follow those 
of a previous study66. The AHR inhibitor (CH-223191) was dissolved in 
dimethylsulfoxide and diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) 
before injection. The 3xTg AD mice received 100 μg CH-223191 intra-
peritoneally 2 days before oral administration of IAA every day. During

Morris water maze test
The cognitive abilities of the mice were assessed with the Morris water 
maze test. The diameter of the maze was about 1.2 m, and the water in the 
maze was drained daily. The water temperature was maintained at about 
21 ± 2 °C. Throughout the period of training, the platform (diameter 
10 cm) was fixed 1 cm below the water surface. To reach the platform 
within the 120 s latency period, the mice were placed at different posi-
tions around the border of the maze in a semi-random order. The test 
was performed once a day for 4 days. If the mouse found the platform 
successfully within 120 s, it was allowed to stay on the platform for 15 s. 
If it failed, it was placed on the hidden platform for 15 s. The motion 
tracks of the mice were recorded and analyzed with a computerized 
video tracking system. At 24 h after the last learning trial, the mice 
were tested to evaluate their memory retention. The trained mice were 
allowed to swim freely for 120 s, from two starting points far away from 
the platform. The time they spent looking for the platform was recorded.
NOR
The NOR test was performed in a 50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm open field box. 
During the adaptation period, mice were placed in the open field box 
without any objects and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. During 
the familiarization period, the mice were placed in the center of the box 
with two identical objects (familiar object) for 10 min. After 1 h, one 
of the objects was replaced with a new object (novel object). The time 
spent exploring the familiar object and the novel object was recorded 
and analyzed. Object exploration was defined as the nose of mice within 
3 cm area of the object edge. The recognition index was calculated as 
follows25: recognition index % = (Tnovel or Tfamiliar)/(Tnovel + Tfamiliar) × 100.
FMT
FMT was performed according to a previous study67. Colonized mice 
(AD or WT mice) were treated with sterile drinking water containing 
antibiotic solution (Abx; 0.5 g l−1 vancomycin hydrochloride, 1 g l−1 
neomycin sulfate, 1 g l−1 ampicillin sulfate and 1 g l−1 metronidazole). The 
water was prepared fresh daily and replaced daily. The mice received 
Abx for 14 days before undergoing FMT. To prepare the samples for 
gavage, the fecal samples from the donor mice of three groups (AD, 
WT or l-Au NPs; six mice per group) were collected. Briefly, the fresh 
fecal samples were resuspended in sterile PBS (15 ml g−1 of feces), vigor-
ously vortexed for 5 min and allowed to stand for 5 min to precipitate 
the particles. The supernatants were then used to colonize the Abx-
treated mice (six mice per group) with oral gavage every day for 1 week 
(200 μl per mouse).
The 16S rRNA microbiome sequencing
The feces of mice were collected in metabolic cages and frozen at 
−80 °C before DNA extraction. The microbial community DNA was 
isolated with the MagPure Fast Stool DNA KF Kit B (Magen), according 
to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA was quantified with the 
Qubit dsDNA BR Assay Kit and a Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen), and its 
quality was checked by running an aliquot on a 1% agarose gel. The vari-
able V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced 
with degenerate PCR primers. The forward and reverse primers (341 F, 
5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′ and 806 R, 5′-GGACTACHVGGGT-
WTCTAAT-3′, respectively) were tagged with Illumina adapter, pad 
and linker sequences. For each sample, duplicate 50 μl PCRs were 
performed, which contained 30 ng of template, fusion PCR primers 
and PCR master mix. The cycling conditions were 94 °C for 3 min, fol-
lowed by 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 56 °C for 45 s and 72 °C for 45 s, and 
a final extension reaction for 10 min at 72 °C. The PCR products were 
purified with Ampure XP Beads and eluted with elution buffer. The 
libraries were qualified with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent). 
The validated libraries were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform 
(BGI, Shenzhen), according to the standard Illumina pipeline, and 2× 
300 bp paired-end reads were generated.
Untargeted metabolomics
Quality control samples were prepared to evaluate the reproducibility 
of the analysis. The samples were analyzed on a system including a 
Waters 2D ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) sys-
tem (Waters) coupled to a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo 
Fisher Scientific) with a heated electrospray ionization (ESI) source 
and controlled by the Xcalibur 2.3 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 
which was equipped with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column 
(2.5 μm, 2.1 mm × 50 mm) maintained at 45 °C for extract separation. 
The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile 
(B) in the positive mode, and of 10 mM ammonium formate (A) and 
acetonitrile (B) in the negative mode. The flow rate was 0.35 ml min−1 
and the injection volume was 5 μl. Separation was performed with the 
following gradient: 2% B (0–1 min), 2–98% B (1–9 min), 98% B (9–12 min), 
98–2% B (12–12.1 min) and 2% B (12.1–15 min). The mass spectrometry 
parameters for the positive/negative ionization modes were set to 
spray voltage 3.8/3.2 kV, sheath gas flow rate 40 arbitrary units (AU), 
auxiliary gas flow rate 10 AU, auxiliary gas heater temperature 350 °C 
and capillary temperature, 320 °C.
The untargeted raw data (raw file) were imported into Compound 
Discoverer 3.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for automatic data process-
ing. The workflow for data processing and analysis included peak 
extraction, retention time correction within and between groups, 
additive ion pooling, missing value filling, background removal and 
metabolite identification. To identify the features detected in the 
samples, the accurate m/z was first derived to match the BGI reference 
library, mzCloud (Thermo Fisher Scientific), ChemSpider, KEGG and 
the Human Metabolome Database. The resulting normalized peak 
intensities were analyzed statistically with the metaX software. The 
multiple steps in metaX processing included data quality assessment, 
missing value imputation, data normalization, and univariate and 
multivariate analyses.
LC–MS/MS method for the analysis of IAA, IAM and IE in serum 
and CSF
For 13C11–Trp tracing, APP/PS1 AD mice were gavaged with 13C11–Trp 
(200 mg kg−1 per day), or 13C11–Trp (200 mg kg−1 per day) and l-Au NPs 
(100 mg kg−1 per day). After 8 h, the sera were collected and frozen at 
−80 °C (refs. 68,69). During this period, the mice were fed as normal.
As in the sample preparation for metabolite extraction, 50 μl of 
each sample was added to methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, vol/vol) solution 
containing 200 ng ml−1 deuterated Trp (Trp-d5). The samples were vor-
texed for 1 min and then sonicated for 10 min. The samples were frozen 
for 1 h at −20 °C and centrifuged at 13,800g for 10 min at 4 °C. All the 
supernatants were dried by vacuum freezing at −40 °C, reconstituted 
with 100 Μl of 20% methanol/water, and vortexed and centrifuged as 
described above. The supernatants were retained for analysis.
The UPLC system was coupled to a tandem mass spectrometer 
(QTRAP 5500 System, AB Sciex) with ESI in positive and negative 
modes. This was equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column 
(2.1 mm × 50 mm, 2.5 μm; Waters) maintained at 40 °C, and the injec-
tion volume was 2 ml. The mobile phase included (A) 0.1% formic acid

Immunofluorescent staining of brain sections
Fixed brain sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated 
with ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed with ethylene diamine 
tetraacetic acid antigen retrieval solution and the sections were blocked 
with bovine serum albumin for 30 min. The diluted primary antibody 
(rabbit anti-NLRP3 antibody (PA5-79740; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 
mouse anti-IBA1 antibody (MA5-27726; Thermo Fisher Scientific) or 
mouse anti-GFAP antibody (E-AB-70205; Elabscience)) was added to the 
sections and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The slides were washed three 
times with PBS and incubated with the secondary antibody (goat anti-
mouse IgG (H + L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa 
Fluor Plus 555 (A32727; Thermo Fisher) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) 
Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 (A11008; Thermo 
Fisher Scientific)) for 50 min at room temperature in the dark. Finally, 
4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole was added to the sections and incubated 
at room temperature for 10 min in the dark. All antibodies were diluted 
according to the product instructions. All antibodies were utilized 
in accordance with the manufacturer’s validated species reactivities 
and established protocols in the laboratories performing the analysis.
The sections were observed under an inverted fluorescence micro-
scope (Leica DMI8).
Quantitative real-time PCR
qPCR was used to determine the relevant gene expression. The total 
RNA concentrations were determined with a NanoDrop spectrophoto­
meter before the RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary 
DNA with the MightyScript First Strand cDNA Synthesis Master Mix 
(Sangon Biotech). Messenger RNA expression was quantified with 
2×SG Fast qPCR Master Mix (High Rox) (Sangon Biotech). The qPCRs 
were performed in 20 μl volumes with the following thermal cycling 
program: 95 °C for 3 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 30 s. 
At the end of the reaction, the amplicons were quantified and a melt-
ing analysis performed according to the instructions for the Applied 
Biosystems 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System.
The primers used for qPCR are presented in Supplementary 
Table 2. The 2−ΔΔCt method was used to calculate the relative expression 
of the target genes.
Flow cytometry
To determine the proportions of neurotoxic subtype M1 microglial 
cells and neuroprotective subtype M2 microglia cells present under

different treatment conditions were collected for flow-cytometric 
analysis. Briefly, the cells were centrifuged at 100g for 10 min, washed

allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated anti-CD86, fluorescein isothiocy-
anate-conjugated anti-CD68, phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated anti-
CD206 or PE-cyanine7 (Cy7)-conjugated anti-CD163 antibody. The cells 
were washed three times with PBS and resuspended in 500 μl of PBS.
To determine the ratio of neurotoxic subtype A1 astrocytes to 
neuroprotective subtype A2 astrocytes generated under different 
culture conditions with a flow-cytometric analysis, the cells were col-
lected, fixed, permeabilized, blocked and then incubated with diluted 
primary antibody (mouse anti-C3 antibody (sc-28294; Santa Cruz Bio-
technology) or rabbit anti-S100A10 antibody (11250-1-AP; Proteintech)) 
for 2 h. Finally, the cells were incubated with a secondary fluorescent 
antibody (goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary

goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa

Single-cell suspensions were isolated from 3xTg AD mice colon, 
periphery, meninges and brain parenchyma. After treatment with

The flow cytometry data were analyzed with the FlowJo 10.3 and 
GraphPad Prism software.

Statistics and reproducibility

methods were used to predetermine sample sizes but our sample sizes 
are similar to those reported in previous publications42. The investi-

or analysis. All samples were analyzed using the reported approach 
without prior knowledge of their levels. No data were excluded from the 
analyses. For each experiment and condition, at least three independent 
technical replicates were performed with similar results. All observa-
tions reported in the manuscript were reproducible. Data distribution 
was assumed to be normal but this was not formally tested. All results 
are shown as mean ± s.d. For all data, one dot or lane represents one 
mouse or one patient. All statistical analyses were performed with

Gold Nanoparticle Transport in the Injured Kidneys with
Elevated Renal Function Biomarkers
Xuhui Ning, Yuncheng Zhong, Qi Cai, Yaohong Wang, Xun Jia, Jer-Tsong Hsieh,
Jie Zheng,* and Mengxiao Yu*
Renal function biomarkers such as serum blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and
creatinine (Cr) serve as key indicators for guiding clinical decisions before
administering kidney-excreted small-molecule agents. With engineered
nanoparticles increasingly designed to be renally clearable to expedite their
clinical translation, understanding the relationship between renal function
biomarkers and nanoparticle transport in diseased kidneys becomes crucial to
their biosafety in future clinical applications. In this study, renal-clearable gold
nanoparticles (AuNPs) are used as X-ray contrast agents to noninvasively
track their transport and retention in cisplatin-injured kidneys with varying
BUN and Cr levels. The ﬁndings reveal that AuNP transport is signiﬁcantly
slowed in the medulla of severely injured kidneys, with BUN and Cr levels
elevated to 10 times normal. In mildly injured kidneys, where BUN and Cr
levels only four to ﬁve times higher than normal, AuNP transport and
retention are not predictable by BUN and Cr levels but correlate strongly with
the degree of tubular injury due to the formation of gold–protein casts in the
Henle’s loop of the medulla. These results underscore the need for caution
when employing renal-clearable nanomedicines in compromised kidneys and
highlight the potential of renal-clearable AuNPs as X-ray probes for assessing
kidney injuries noninvasively.

1. Introduction
Kidney disease aﬀects over 10% of the
global population[1–3] and about 25% of
hospitalized patients worldwide,[4] mak-
ing the measurement of renal function
biomarkers—serum blood urea nitrogen
(BUN) and creatinine (Cr)—crucial for
guiding the choice and dosing of drugs
that are eliminated via the kidneys.[5] For
instance, gadolinium (Gd)-based contrast
agents,[6,7] cisplatin (a chemotherapeutic
drug),[8,9] antibiotics like vancomycin[10]
are excreted
through the kidneys but
could also cause signiﬁcant kidney dam-
age or even lead to kidney failure in
patients with pre-existing renal impair-
ment. Consequently, in clinical settings,
BUN and Cr levels are closely monitored
in
patients
with
potential
or
existing
kidney dysfunction before and after the
administration
of
these
renal-clearable
drugs.[11,12] Elevated BUN and creatinine
levels indicate kidney damage, requiring
dose reductions and early intervention to
minimize the potential risk of sever kidney injury and prevent
kidney failure.[11,12]
Renal-clearable engineered nanoparticles represent a unique
category of nanomaterials distinguished by their ability to be
rapidly eliminated via the kidneys because of their sizes being be-
low the kidney ﬁltration threshold (6–8 nm).[13–15] In the last two
decades, owing to their distinctive transport mechanisms and
interactions within the body, renal-clearable nanoparticles have
been developed as diagnostic imaging agents for cancer[16–23] and
kidney disease,[24–33] exogenous blood or urinary markers for de-
tecting kidney and liver diseases[34–38] or cancer,[38,39] therapeutics
for treating kidney disease[40–44] and iron overload disorders,[45]
and nanocarriers to improve the eﬃcacy of various therapeu-
tic drugs while minimizing their potential toxicities.[46–55] De-
spite being relatively new nanoparticles, some renal-clearable
nanoparticles such as “C” dots (Cornell dots, dye-doped silica
nanoparticles),[18,52] AGuIX nanoparticles (sub-5 nm nanoparti-
cles made of a polysiloxane matrix and gadolinium chelates)[20]
are currently under clinical trial evaluation to tackle clinical chal-
lenges in cancer diagnosis and treatment. Their minimal accu-
mulation in healthy tissues and organs aligns with U.S. Food and
Drug Administration (FDA) guidelines for nanomedicines,[56] re-
sponsible for rapid advance in clinical translation.[13,57] However,

patients with impaired renal function in both preclinical and fu-

to inform clinical decisions in the clinical practices, it is critical
to investigate whether these established biomarkers can reliably
predict the kidney’s ability to eliminate nanoparticles, and there-
fore, guide the use of renal-clearable nanoparticles in patients
with kidney disease.
In addition to the glomerulus, where blood is rapidly ﬁl-
tered, renal tubules have a remarkable ability to gradually con-
centrate ﬁltered engineered nanoparticles as they traverse vari-
ous segments of renal tubules in ﬁve diﬀerent kidney zones (or
regions) because of their eﬃcient water reabsorption capacity
(Figure 1A).[58,59] The proximal tubule (in the cortex) reabsorbs
≈70% of ﬁltered water, and the thin descending limb of the loop
of Henle (in the inner strip of outer medulla, ISOM) reabsorbs
about 20% of ﬁltered water. The collecting duct further adjusts
water reabsorption based on the body’s hydration status before
transforming the ﬁltrate into urine. These processes might also
alter nanoparticle transport and retention in the kidneys, partic-
ularly when renal tubules are injured, which, however, is seldom
investigated in detail.
Cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury is one of the most
common drug-induced kidney diseases.[60] Cisplatin primarily
damages the proximal tubules.[61] In preclinical research, the
cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury model has been widely
used to investigate the interactions of renal-clearable nanopar-
ticles with the diseased kidneys[62] and to evaluate their perfor-
mance in kidney disease detection[32,33,35] and treatment.[41,42]
Herein, we employed renal-clearable gold nanoparticles (AuNPs)
as a model system, coupled with planar X-ray imaging, to nonin-
vasively track their transport in kidneys with cisplatin-induced
tubular injury. This allowed us to observe the AuNP transport
in the kidney in real-time across diﬀerent stages of renal tubu-
lar injury with varying levels of BUN and Cr. In severe cisplatin-
induced acute tubular injury when BUN or Cr levels were ele-
vated to more than tenfold above the normal range, our results
indicate a signiﬁcant slowdown in AuNP transport and prolonged
retention in the ISOM of the kidney. However, in mildly injured
kidneys—where BUN or Cr levels were only four to ﬁve times of
the normal values—the behavior of AuNP transport and reten-
tion in the ISOM was highly heterogeneous. We observed both
rapid elimination through the kidney, similar to that in healthy
kidneys, and prolonged retention in the ISOM, as seen in severely
injured kidneys. Such distinct AuNP transport among the kid-
neys with comparable BUN and Cr levels in mild kidney injury,
can be attributed to formation of AuNP–protein casts in the tubu-
lar lumen of the loop of Henle (Figure 1B,C). Even minor tubu-
lar blockages could signiﬁcantly extend AuNPs’ retention in the
loop of Henle, which, however, cannot be diﬀerentiated through
BUN and Cr levels in the mild injury stage. These ﬁndings high-
light the importance of accurately monitoring kidney transport
of renal-clearable nanomedicines in the presence of potential or
exiting kidney problems, even with slight elevations in BUN and
Cr levels, in future clinical practices. Additionally, these results
suggest that nanoparticle transport could hold great promise
in more accurate staging of the kidney injuries than BUN
and Cr.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Highly Biocompatible Renal-Clearable AuNPs as X-ray
and Fluorescence Imaging Agents
To explore the relationship between renal functional markers and
kidney function in nanoparticle clearance, we utilized near in-
frared (NIR)-emitting, glutathione-coated 2.5 nm AuNPs (GS-
AuNPs) as model for noninvasive imaging of their transport in
the normal and injured kidneys (Figure S1, Supporting Informa-
tion). Among the renal-clearable AuNPs with diﬀerent sizes and
surface chemistries we developed in past decade,[14,57] these GS-
AuNPs were chosen for this study because of their eﬃcient re-
nal clearance,[63] high biocompatibility,[64] and ability to be visu-
alized using both X-ray[26] and NIR ﬂuorescence[17,22,24,25] imag-
ing techniques. They have a core size of 2.5 ± 0.3 nm and a
hydrodynamic diameter (HD) of 3.3 ± 0.6 nm (Figure S1A,B,
Supporting Information).[17,24] The thickness of the glutathione-
coated surface is estimated to be 0.4 nm based on the diﬀer-
ences between the core size and HD [(3.3 – 2.5)/2 = 0.4 nm].
The glutathione coating not only enhances the water solubil-
ity of AuNPs but also makes them resistant to serum protein
adsorption (Figure S2, Supporting Information).[63] As a result,
the HD of GS-AuNPs in the physiological conditions remains
smaller than the 6 nm threshold required for glomerular ﬁltra-
tion, facilitating their rapid elimination via the urinary tract. Over
50% of the administered dose is excreted in the urine within 24
h post intravenous injection,[63] with glutathione remaining the
dominant ligand on the particle surface after excretion into the
urine.[64] Combined with the substantial X-ray attenuation prop-
erties of gold (Figure S1C, Supporting Information), the high re-
nal clearance eﬃciency of these nanoparticles allows suﬃcient
AuNPs to be ﬁltered through the glomeruli and create X-ray con-
trast as they travel through the renal tubules, enabling the dy-
namic tracking of AuNP transport from the renal cortex to the
medulla using planar X-ray imaging.[26] Additionally, these GS-
AuNPs are highly biocompatible and non-nephrotoxic, with a no-
observed-adverse-eﬀect-level (NOAEL) of >1000 mg kg−1 in CD-
1 mice and >250 mg kg−1 in cynomolgus monkeys.[64] This en-
sures that mice tolerate the injection dose of 1000 mg kg−1, which
was found to be the minimum dose required for tracking GS-
AuNP transport in the mouse kidney using our X-ray imaging
system.[26]
Furthermore, the 2.5 nm GS-AuNPs exhibit peak emission
at 810 nm (Figure S1D, Supporting Information), allowing for
the detection of low nanoparticle concentrations using highly
sensitive NIR ﬂuorescence imaging—a valuable adjunct to the
comparatively less sensitive X-ray imaging. The intrinsic NIR
emission of these 2.5 nm GS-AuNPs, without dye conjugation,
is attributed to a signiﬁcant amount of Au atoms in the Au(I)
state, as revealed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Figure S3,
Supporting Information).[63] This emission arises from strong
S–Au(I) and Au(I)–Au(I) interactions, leading to ligand-to-metal
charge transfer (LMCT) and ligand-to-metal-metal charge trans-
fer (LMMCT).[65,66] In the absorption spectrum (Figure S1D, Sup-
porting Information), the absence of a surface plasmon reso-
nance peak at 500 nm is a characteristic feature of 1–3 nm lumi-
nescent AuNPs because a large number of gold atoms are in the
Au(I) state and fail to provide free electrons.[67–70] The emission

2.2. AuNP Transport in the Kidneys with Signiﬁcant Elevation
of BUN and Creatinine
We established the model of cisplatin-induced acute kidney in-
jury model by administering cisplatin (1 mg mL−1) to CD-1 mice
via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection at a high dose of 25 mg kg−1
body weight, referred to as “CP25” in subsequent discussions.
This dosage has been conﬁrmed to induce severe acute kid-
ney injury after 3–4 days, characterized by dramatic increases
in BUN and Cr levels and severe structural damage.[74] Mice
injected with phosphate buﬀered saline (PBS) served as the
healthy control group. Consistent with previous ﬁndings, we ob-
served signiﬁcant elevations in serum BUN and Cr levels in all
cisplatin-injected mice after 3 days. Both BUN and Cr levels in-
creased by 10–15-fold (BUN: 283.0 ± 37.6 mg dL−1; Cr: 2.2 ±
0.5 mg dL−1) compared to the healthy control (BUN: 22.3 ±
5.1 mg dL−1; Cr: 0.16± 0.01) and baseline measurements of the
same group prior to cisplatin injection (BUN: 26.8 ± 2.9 mg dL−1;
Cr: 0.17 ± 0.01 mg dL−1) (Figures 2A and S4, Supporting In-
formation). Morphological examination of the kidneys revealed
that cisplatin injection resulted in proximal tubular injury char-
acterized by tubules with open lumens resulted from brush
border loss, ﬂattened epithelial cells, and focal detachment of
these cells (Figure 2B, CP25).[75] This is dramatically diﬀerent
from healthy proximal tubules, which have high cuboidal epithe-
lial cells with brush borders and less obvious tubular lumens
(Figure 2B, PBS).[75]
Once the cisplatin-induced kidney injury mouse model was
established, we intravenously injected GS-AuNPs at a dose of ap-
proximately1000 mg kg−1 and employed noninvasive X-ray imag-
ing to consecutively monitor the AuNP transport through the kid-
ney for 1 h post-injection and then followed their kidney retention
at 3, 5, 8, and 12 h (N = 4 for both PBS-injected and 25 mg kg−1
cisplatin (CP25) injected mice). After 24 h, the kidneys were har-
vested, cut longitudinally to two halves and subjected to ex vivo
X-ray imaging. Real-time X-ray imaging of the elimination of
AuNPs through the injured kidneys allowed us to unravel how
the injury impacts the particle transport in the diﬀerent kidney
compartments and whether the signiﬁcantly elevated BUN and
Cr levels can reﬂect changes in the kidney’s function in eliminat-
ing AuNPs. In healthy kidneys, the AuNPs were rapidly ﬁltered
through the glomeruli in the cortex, but their movement slowed
down in a speciﬁc area of the medulla (Figure 2C and Figures
S5, S6 and Movie S1, Supporting Information). This selective
AuNP accumulation in the medulla appeared at 16–18 min post-
injection of GS-AuNPs (Figure 2C and Figure S5, Supporting In-
formation), followed by reaching its maximum accumulation at
3 h and then being gradually eliminated out of the kidneys as
shown in the time-dependent contrast dynamics of this medulla
pattern (Figure 2D and Figures S6, S7, Supporting Information).
However, in the mice with severely damaged kidneys (CP25), all
four mice consistently showed a persistent, long-term accumula-
tion of GS-AuNPs in the medulla, as revealed by both in vivo and
ex vivo X-ray imaging. In all four CP25 mice, the accumulation
of the AuNPs in the medulla continuously increased over time
during the 0–12 h post-injection, reaching a peak at 3–8 h post-
injection, and maintained a signiﬁcant amount in the medulla
until 12 h post-injection (Figure 2E,F and Figures S8–S10 and
Movie S2, Supporting Information). This resulted in a similar
trend in the time-dependent curve of the medulla contrast en-
hancement among the mice with severe kidney injury (Figure 2F
and Figure S10, Supporting Information). Statistics analysis of
the medulla pattern, including its detectable time (0.3 h vs 1.7 ±
1.4 h, Figure 2G), time to reach maximum contrast (1.3 ± 1.2 h
vs 5.3 ± 2.1 h, Figure 2H), maximum contrast enhancement per-
centage (17.5 ± 7.4% vs 39.7 ± 15.0%, Figure 2I), and contrast
enhancement percentage at 12 h post-injection (5.7 ± 1.9% vs
29.6 ± 11.8%, Figure 2J), between healthy and severely injured
kidneys, further validated that severe kidney injury signiﬁcantly
slowed down the AuNP transport within the kidneys, particu-
larly increasing AuNP accumulation and retention in the medulla
while delaying the appearance of this medulla accumulation pat-
tern.
At 24 h post-injection, ex vivo X-ray imaging of severely in-
jured kidneys further revealed a more distinct distribution of the
AuNPs in a speciﬁc area of the medulla, identiﬁed as the inner
strip of the outer medulla (ISOM)[76–78] (Figures 3B,C and Figures
S11 and S12, Supporting Information). The kidneys of all four
CP25 mice showed a high-contrast signal in the ISOM area under
X-ray imaging, suggesting substantial GS-AuNPs accumulation
there, further conﬁrmed by intense NIR emission in the same
area (Figure S13, Supporting Information). In contrast, healthy
kidneys did not exhibit a detectable selective distribution under
X-ray (Figure 3A), though NIR imaging showed a cortical pattern
with intensity about 16 times lower than that in severely injured
kidneys (Figures S14 and S15, Supporting Information; this cor-
tical pattern is a typical distribution pattern of renal-clearable
AuNPs at 24 h post-injection, due to the particles being taken up
by the proximal tubules).[62,79] To quantitatively understand the
eﬀect of severe kidney injury on AuNP accumulation, we quan-
tiﬁed the average X-ray density values in the cortex and ISOM
areas across the two groups (Figure 3D–F and Figure S16, Sup-
porting Information). The ISOM area showed marked enhance-
ment with injury severity, with intensity increasing from 0.44 ±
0.02 in normal kidneys to 0.77 ± 0.06 in severe injury stage (P
< 0.05, t test; Figure 3E). The increasing ISOM accumulation of
GS-AuNPs led to an increase in the ratio between ISOM and cor-
tex from 1.17 ± 0.03 for normal status to 1.75 ± 0.23 for severe
stage (P < 0.05, t test; Figure 3F), although cortex intensity also
increased in cisplatin damage kidneys compared to normal mice
due to slower glomerular ﬁltration (P < 0.05, t test; Figure 3D).
On the other hand, cisplatin-damaged proximal tubules exhibited
signiﬁcantly reduced cellular uptake of nanoparticles.[62] These
results further validated that while cisplatin-induced kidney in-
jury slows down the overall AuNP clearance through the kidney,
including both glomerular ﬁltration and movement through the
renal tubules, the ISMO is the key barrier in slowing the move-
ment of ﬁltered AuNPs along the renal tubules in the injured
kidneys.

with BUN of ≈20 mg dL−1, having low accumulation (highest
contrast enhancement percentage of 16.7%) and short retention
in the ISOM (contrast enhancement percentage dropped to 7.0%
at 12 h post-injection, Figure 4C,F and Movie S3, Supporting
Information). In contrast, in the mouse “CP15 m3” with BUN
of 157 mg dL−1, we observed that AuNPs continuously accumu-
lated in the injured kidneys over 12 h post-injection, leading to
delayed appearance of the ISMO pattern (3 h post-injection) and
continuously increased image contrast of ISOM pattern (contrast
enhancement percentage increased from 4.5% at 2 min to 30.9%
at 12 h) (Figure 4D,F and Movie S4, Supporting Information).
This phenomenon was similar to the AuNP transport in the
severely injured kidneys with BUN of 255 mg dL−1 (25 mg kg−1
cisplatin injected mouse “CP25 m1”; Figures 2E,F). In addi-
tion, in mouse “CP15 m2” with BUN of 140 mg dL−1, we also
observed that the AuNPs had high accumulation in the ISOM,
giving the highest contrast enhancement percentage of 32.9% at
3 h post-injection. However, AuNPs had much shorter retention
in the injured kidneys of this mouse, indicated by decreasing
of contrast enhancement percentage of ISOM to 12.0% at 12 h
post-injection (Figure 4E,F and Movie S5, Supporting Informa-
tion). Ex vivo X-ray and ﬂuorescence imaging of the kidneys
obtained at 24 h post-injection further conﬁrmed a gradual
increase in AuNP accumulation in the ISOM and cortex from
mouse “CP15 m1” to “CP15 m2” and “CP15 m3” (Figure S18,
Supporting Information). These ﬁndings indicate that BUN and
Cr levels cannot accurately predict the nanoparticle transport
and retention in the mildly injured kidneys.
To unravel the origin of distinct AuNP transport in these in-
jured kidneys with comparable BUN levels, we conducted patho-
logical evaluation on the kidney tissues. In contrast to normal
proximal tubules, which have high cuboidal epithelial cells with
narrow lumens (Figure 4G, PBS m1), the damaged proximal
tubules in acute kidney injury exhibit open tubular lumens due
to brush border loss, ﬂattened epithelial cells, and detachment
of epithelial cells (Figure 4G, CP15 m2 and m3, marked by
asterisks).[75] Therefore, we analyzed the percentage of open lu-
men area in the renal cortex as a quantitative indicator of acute
kidney injury. We found that these kidneys exhibited distinct de-
grees of injury (Figures 4G,H and S19–S22, Supporting Infor-
mation). The kidney of mouse “CP15 m3,” in which GS-AuNPs
were transported most slowly and exhibited prolonged retention
within the ISOM without signiﬁcant decay, demonstrated the
largest open lumen area in renal cortex (≈21.85%, Figure 4H and
S22, Supporting Information). In contrast, the kidney with AuNP
transport characteristics similar to those in a normal condition
displayed the smallest open lumen area (≈5.62% found in “CP15
m1,” Figures 4H and S20, Supporting Information), which was
close to the baseline value observed in normal kidney (≈4.27%
found in “PBS m1,” Figures 4H and S19, Supporting Informa-
tion), although one cortex area showed high open lumen area
(15.05%) because of patchy feature of cisplatin-induced tubular
injury (area 2 in Figure S20, Supporting Information). The kid-
neys with AuNP transport behaviors intermediate between these
two extremes showed moderate open lumen area (10.20% found
in “CP15 m2,” Figures 4H and S21, Supporting Information). A
strong linear correlation was found between the percentages of
open lumen areas, an indicator of kidney structural changes due
to proximal tubular injury, and the image contrast of ISOM dis-
tribution pattern at 12 h post intravenous injection of GS-AuNPs
(Figure 4H).
2.4. AuNP–Protein Casts in the Loop of Henle
To identify the exact component within the ISOM responsible for
prolonging the AuNP retention, we optically imaged a tissue sec-
tion of the kidney with BUN of 157 mg dL−1 (CP15 m3), where
AuNP accumulation in the ISOM did not show noticeable de-
cay until 12 h post-injection (Figure 4D and 5A). The distinct
yellow color of GS-AuNPs (Figure 5B), coupled with their high
concentration in the ISOM and the high injection dose for X-
ray imaging, allowed for easy identiﬁcation of AuNPs in H&E-
stained kidney sections without the need for silver enhancement
staining: the AuNPs predominantly occupied the extracellular lu-
minal space in over 80% of renal tubules within the ISOM, where
AuNPs were recognized by their yellow color (Figures 5C,D).
The existence of AuNPs in the tubular lumen was further val-
idated by silver enhancement staining (Figure 5E). The AuNPs
ﬁlling the tubular lumen mimic tubular cast formation and ap-
pear like yellow-colored “Gold casts” (Figure 5D), distinct from
the pink-colored proteinaceous casts typically observed in renal
tubules (Figure 5F). These tubules containing AuNPs are the
loops of Henle as they have smaller diameter and relative ﬂat ep-
ithelial cells.[75] In this injured kidney (Figures 5C,J), the loop
of Henle exhibited a notably increased amount of GS-AuNPs in
the lumen compared to normal conditions at 24 h post-injection
(Figure 5G), yet their structural integrity appeared intact under
H&E staining and optical imaging. The enhanced tubular lumen
accumulation of AuNPs in injured kidney was highly speciﬁc to
the loop of Henle while having signiﬁcantly reduced presence
in the lumens of renal tubules (such as proximal tubules, dis-
tal tubules, and collecting ducts) in the cortex, outer strip of the
outer medulla, inner medulla, and papilla, respectively (Figure
S23, Supporting Information). This is likely due to interactions
of the AuNPs with substances in the lumen of the loop of Henle
such as Tam–Horsfall protein, protein casts, and cellular debris,
which are found to frequently locate in the loop of Henle in acute
tubular injury and other kidney disease.[81] Additionally, a reduc-
tion in ﬂuid passing through the renal tubules can further con-
tribute to the long retention of AuNPs in the tubular lumen of
Henle’s loop.
In addition to this kidney with the longest AuNP retention in
CP15 mice (Figures 5A-F,J), we also quantiﬁed the gold casts in
other two injured kidneys with comparable BUNs (Figure 5H,I
and Figures S24–S28, Supporting Information). We found that
there were very few gold casts in the loop of Henle in the kidney,

3. Conclusion
With the increasing development of renal-clearable nanopar-
ticles for enhancing disease diagnosis and treatment, a thor-
ough understanding of their transport within diseased kidneys
is paramount to ensuring their future safety and eﬃcacy in clin-
ical settings. Utilizing renal-clearable AuNPs as X-ray imaging
nanoprobes in a cisplatin-induced kidney injury mouse model,
we systematically examined the relationships between renal func-
tion biomarkers, such as BUN and creatinine (Cr), and AuNP
transport within injured kidneys. Our results reveal that in
severely injured kidneys exhibiting BUN or Cr levels >10 times
higher than normal status, AuNP transport within kidney com-
partments is signiﬁcantly slowed, leading to preferential accu-
mulation in the ISOM with prolonged retention. In contrast, in
mildly injured kidneys, although both BUN and Cr levels were
elevated by four to ﬁve times compared to normal values, they
no longer reliably predict AuNP clearance through the kidney
due to their insensitivity to early-stage kidney injury and inability
to reﬂect nanoparticle interaction with renal tubules. However,
AuNP transport remains highly sensitive to kidney injury, show-
ing a strong correlation with pathological kidney damage. The
underlying reason is likely the interaction of AuNPs with protein
casts and cellular debris formed due to proximal tubular injury
and present in the lumen of Henle’s loop. This interaction im-
pedes AuNP transport through the loop of Henle, resulting in the
prolonged ISOM accumulation pattern of nanoparticles in dis-
eased kidneys. While we focused on the cisplatin model in this
study, this ﬁnding of long-lasting ISOM accumulation pattern
of nanoparticles in diseased kidneys might be applied to other
kidney diseases. Further understanding of this phenomenon is
expected to provide new solutions for kidney disease diagnosis,
which urgently needs innovative methods.[82]
Our ﬁndings suggest that while BUN and Cr are commonly
used renal function biomarkers for guiding the administration
of renal-clearable small molecule drugs, they may not be suitable
for directly guiding renal nanomedicines in patients with mild
kidney injury. Instead, the observation of distinct AuNP trans-
port in mildly injured kidneys, despite comparable BUN and
Cr levels, indicates that nanoparticle transport could serve as a
more sensitive tool for diﬀerentiating injury stages and can be
directly correlated with kidney pathological changes. One chal-
lenge lies in the relatively low sensitivity of planar X-ray imaging,
requiring higher doses of AuNPs. However, with the assistance
of micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), lower doses of gold
nanoparticles may be possible. To address this, we explored the
use of highly sensitive micro-CT, enabling eﬀective monitoring
of nanoparticle transport within healthy mouse kidneys at a re-
duced dose of 500 mg kg−1—half the dose required for planar
X-ray imaging—within the ﬁrst 30 min post-injection. As shown
in Figure 6, three-dimensional (3D) reconstructions of CT im-
ages clearly delineated the ISOM of the kidney, distinguishing it
from the renal cortex and bladder, which also became detectable
due to GS-AuNP excretion through the urinary tract. Therefore,
continuous improvement in micro-CT sensitivity to reduce the
AuNP doses holds promise for advancing the clinical translation
of renal-clearable AuNPs in the coming era.
4. Experimental Section/Methods
Materials and Equipment:
Tetrachloroauric(III) acid (HAuCl4) was
purchased from Fisher Scientiﬁc (Waltham, MA, USA). Cisplatin and all
the other chemicals were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. Absorption
spectra were collected using a Varian 50 Bio UV–Vis spectrophotometer
(Birmingham, NJ, USA). Luminescence spectra were collected by a PTI
QuantaMasterTM 30 Fluorescence Spectrophotometer (Birmingham, NJ,
USA). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of gold nanopar-
ticles were obtained with JEM-2010 transmission electron microscope. X-
ray and Fluorescence images were collected by Bruker In-Vivo Extreme I.
Olympus VS120 virtual slide scanner microscope was used to image the
kidney tissue sections.
Synthesis of 2.5 nm Renal-Clearable NIR-Emitting Glutathione-Coated
Gold Nanoparticles (GS-AuNPs):
The GS-AuNPs were synthesized ac-
cording to the previous work:[17] 1 M HAuCL4 solution was added to
2.5 mM glutathione solution at a molar ratio of 1:0.8 in a 100 mL three-
necked ﬂask. The mixture was then heated at 90°C in an oil bath and
the resulting solution was cooled to room temperature and centrifuged
at 21000g for 1 min to remove the large aggregates, followed by adjusting
pH to 3–4 then adding ethanol (2:1 ethanol to solution) to precipitate the
gold nanoparticles. The PBS solution was centrifuged at 21000g for 1 min.
The precipitate was redissolved in water then puriﬁed by dialysis.
Absorption Measurement and size Characterization of the Synthesized GS-
AuNPs:
Absorption spectra of the synthesized AuNPs were collected by
a Varian Cary 50 Bio UV–vis spectrophotometer using a 1 mm cuvette.
The scanning range was from 900 to 200 nm. The concentration of AuNPs
was adjusted to make sure the maximum absorption of AuNPs at 200 nm
is below 1. Core size of AuNPs was measured using a JEOL 2100F ﬁeld
emission TEM/STEM. AuNPs dissolved in water and deposited on the
TEM grids. The distribution of core size of AuNPs was analyzed using
NanoMeasurer software. Hydrodynamic diameters (HD) of AuNPs were
measured by a Malvern ZS90 particle size analyzer. AuNPs dissolved in
1x PBS buﬀer for HD measurement. The average core size or HD was the
xc ± w/2 obtained by ﬁtting the histogram with a Gaussian distribution in
Origin.
Animals:
The mouse studies were approved by the University of Texas
at Dallas IACUC committee (Protocol number 11-08a) and the Univer-
sity of Texas Southwestern Medical Center IACUC committee (APN 2020-
102888). Male CD-1 mice (8–10 weeks old) were purchased from Envigo
Corporation to establish cisplatin-induced kidney injury model. The ani-
mals were housed in ventilated cages under standard environmental con-
ditions (23 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 5% humidity and a 12/12 h light/dark cycle)
with free access to water and standard laboratory food. A normal male
NOD/SCID mouse was used for micro-CT imaging post intravenous in-
jection of GS-AuNPs.
Establishment of Mouse Model of Cisplatin-Induced Acute Kidney Injury:
The CD-1 mice were equally and randomly divided into three groups. They
received intraperitoneal (IP) injection of either phosphate buﬀer saline
(PBS) or cisplatin (dissolved in PBS at 1 mg mL−1) at doses of 15 or

Noninvasive Planar X-ray Imaging of Nanoparticle Transport in the Kidney
and Urinary System:
X-ray tube potential of In-Vivo Xtreme I was set at 45
kVp, 0.8 nm beam ﬁlter and exposure time was 15 s for both static and con-
tinuous imaging. A CD-1 mouse was anesthetized by 3% isoﬂurane and a
catheter was inserted into the tail vein. The mouse was then transferred
into the imaging chamber and received intravenous injection of 200 μL of
≈150 mg mL−1 GS-AuNPs in PBS, followed by consecutive imaging for 1
h post-injection. After the 1-h dynamic imaging, the mouse was then re-
turned to the cage and X-ray images were taken again at 3, 5, 8 and 12 h
post-GS-AuNP injection. At 24-h post-injection, the mouse was sacriﬁced.
The two kidneys were collected and were cut longitudinally and equally to
two halves, followed by ex-vivo X-ray and ﬂuorescence imaging (excitation
= 470 nm, emission = 830 nm). Finally, the kidney samples were ﬁxed with
10% formalin, processed, and stained for pathology analysis.
Analysis of X-Ray Imaging Data for Quantifying Time-Dependent Intensity:
Using the bundled software of In-Vivo Xtreme I, the original X-ray images
were exported to pictures in TIF format and then analyzed by ImageJ. Mean
intensities of renal cortex and ISOM were carefully traced out and plotted
with time for the time dependent intensity. Contrast enhancement percent-
age is calculated by medulla divided by cortex intensity multiply to 100%.
We then selected several key time points for analysis: 2, 5, 10, 30, and 60
min for the 1-h continuous imaging, and 3, 5, 8, and 12 h for long-term
imaging.
Analysis of Ex Vivo X-ray and Fluorescence Kidney Imaging:
Using the
bundled software of In-Vivo Xtreme I, three Region of Interest (ROI) were
respectively selected from cortex (ROI 1–3) and ISOM (ROI 4–6). The
mean intensity of each ROI was used for comparison.
Slide Scanner Microscopy Imaging of Kidney Tissue Sections:
The kidneys
were ﬁxed in 10% formalin for at least 24 h. The ﬁxed kidneys were then
embedded and sectioned into 4 μm sections that contained entire cross
sections of the kidneys including diﬀerent compartments of the kidneys
(cortex, outer medulla, inner medulla, and papilla). For silver enhance-
ment staining of AuNPs in paraﬃn-embedded kidney sections, the sec-
tions were ﬁrst dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated in a graded series of
ethanol solutions into deionized water. And then 0.1 M silver nitrate aque-
ous solution and 0.2 mg mL−1 hydroquinone aqueous solution were added
onto the kidney section at 1:1 (V/V) ratio to stain the tissue to obtain an
optimal contrast of the AuNPs and minimized nonspeciﬁc staining. The
kidney tissue sections were thoroughly rinsed using deionized water for
several seconds and dried at room temperature, followed by hematoxylin
and eosin (H&E) staining to visualize the physiological structures. The
H&E-stained kidney tissue sections were sealed by cover glass and im-
aged with Olympus VS120 virtual slide scanner microscope.
Micro-CT Imaging:
After intravenously injecting GS-AuNPs at a dose
of 500 mg kg−1, the mouse was imaged using the X-ray cone beam CT ca-
pability on a Smart+ small animal irradiation platform (Precision X-ray Inc,
Madison, CT, USA). During the imaging process the animal was mounted
at the isocenter location around which the gantry holding the X-ray tube
and the detector rotated. A 40 kVp X-ray with 2 mm aluminum ﬁlter was
chosen. The detector pixel size was 200 μm and the reconstruction voxel
size was 100 μm. The acquisition time was one minute for the full rotation.
The reconstructed image was post-processed with segmentation and 3D
rendering on a clinical image processing workstation.
ImageJ Analysis of Open Lumen Area and Gold Cast Counting:
Follow-
ing acquisition of high-resolution H&E images, six areas in the renal cortex
of each mouse were selected for analysis using ImageJ software. The type
of image was converted to RGB Stack, and the threshold for the green
channel was adjusted to highlight the open space of the tubular lumen,
and then the area fraction option was selected in “Set Measurements” for
analysis, allowing for quantitative measurement of the open lumen area
percentage. Additionally, gold casts were manually counted individually,
followed by measuring the total area of the selected region and normaliz-
ing the number of gold casts to a 200 μm × 200 μm area.
Statistical Analysis:
Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation
(s.d.). Diﬀerences between two groups were compared using Student’s t-
test. Signiﬁcance levels are indicated as follows: ns (not signiﬁcant), *P <
0.05. Investigators conducting the experiments were not blinded.

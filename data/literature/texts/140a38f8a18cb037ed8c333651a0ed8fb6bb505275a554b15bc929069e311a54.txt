a b s t r a c t   
Due to the homo-chirality preference of human body to most of chiral species, synthesizing enantiopure 
drugs and investigating the pharmacological activity of each enantiomer would have great meaning. 
However, the influence of chirality at nanoscale on the ability to enhance radiotherapy has not been studied. 
Herein, a pair of enantiomeric alkynyl-protected L/D-Au10(C13H17O5)10 nanoclusters (L/D-AuNCs) with 
strong chirality (g value up to 3.6 × 10-3) and circular polarization luminescence (glum ≈ 3 × 10-3) are pre­
pared and applied as radiosensitizers. As a consequence, D-AuNCs exhibit better radiosensitization effect in 
vitro, which is confirmed by research on mechanisms including reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst 
mediated DNA breakage, cell cycle arrest and up-regulation of apoptotic protein expression. In vivo anti­
tumor studies manifest that tumors treated with D-AuNCs and X-ray show a volume decrease of 51% 
compared with X-ray (4 Gy) only group. This work not only enriches the family of gold-based nano- 
radiosensitizers, but also further proves the significance of chirality in biomedicine.

Introduction 
As one of the most significant geometrical properties in nature, 
chirality plays an indispensable role in various physiological pro­
cesses [1–3]. Chiral components of organism species such as amino 
acids and helical DNA play vital roles to maintain normal metabolic 
activities [4–7]. Besides intrinsic chiral compositions, a series of 
studies have demonstrated that biological systems also display 
miraculous selectivity for exogenous chiral species like molecular 
drugs [8,9]. In most racemic drugs, only one enantiomer demon­
strates pharmacological activity, while the other is ineffective or 
even toxic [10]. For example, L-dopamine could treat parkinsonism, 
whereas D-dopamine displays grave toxicity [11]. 
With the emergence of homochiral nanomaterials and rapid 
progress of nanobiomedical field, chirality-dependent interactions 
between chiral nanomaterials and biological systems (including 
biomolecules and cells) have attracted great interests of scientists 
working in biomaterial and nanomedicine fields [12–15]. A series of 
enantioselective biological catalysts and sensors based on chiral 
nanostructures have been designed and displayed distinct responses 
to enantiomeric substrates and targets [16–21]. Moreover, due to the 
different capture, endocytosis and immunological responses of cells 
to chiral species, some chiral noble metal and metal oxide nano­
particles exhibited chirality-dependent effect during autophagy 
regulation or photoactivated cancer therapy [22–24]. For instance, 
chiral substoichiometric molybdenum oxide (L/D-Cys-MoO3-x) na­
noparticles with chiroptical activity in both visible and NIR regions 
was reported as sensitive photothermal agents for tumor cell abla­
tion [25]. When they were exposed to circularly polarized light (CPL)  
[26,27], the chiral nanomaterials exhibited stronger local heating 
effects than linearly polarized light irradiation. Due to the unique 
optical properties of chiral nanomaterials (dominant absorption of 
left-handed or right-handed circularly polarized light), the use of

As radiotherapy is one of the widely used first-line therapeutic 
strategies for cancers in clinic [30–32], exploiting safe and effective 
radiosensitizers [33,34] is of vital importance. A plethora of gold- 
based nanosensitizers of various shapes and particle sizes have been 
developed [35–38], including some atomic-precise nanoclusters  
[39–41]. However, no work about chirality effect of gold nanos­
tructures on radiotherapy has been reported. In this paper, a detailed 
study was carried out to provide progressive understanding of 
the chirality effect of Au nanomaterials on radiosensitization. 
We designed and fabricated a pair of chiral alkynyl gold nanocluster 
L/D-Au10(C13H17O5)10 (L/D-AuNC) with good biocompatibility for 
enhanced radiotherapy research (Fig. 1). Interestingly, we found that 
D-AuNCs were more stable than L-AuNCs in cell culture medium, 
displaying better dispersivity with average size of about 2 nm. We 
further identified that D-AuNCs possessed more effective sensitiza­
tion ability in vitro, and relevant results of sensitizing mechanisms 
also confirmed these findings.

Syntheses of L-AuNC and D-AuNC 
The precursor of [L-Ag]n or [D-Ag]n was synthesized by adding 
L-ligand or D-ligand (254 mg, 1 mmol) to the ethanol solution of 
AgNO3 (178.5 mg, 1.05 mmol) in the presence of trimethylamine. 
L-AuNC or D-AuNC was synthesized by the reaction of 0.1 mmol 
[L-Ag]n or [D-Ag]n precursor with 0.1 mmol Au(Me)2SCl in 10 mL 
acetonitrile-dichloromethane (v:v = 1:1). The mixture was allowed 
to be stirred in darkness at room temperature for 2 h. Then, the re­
sultant solution was filtered, evaporated and washed with n-hexane 
to obtain yellow-green solid. Yields: 49.3% for L-AuNC and 53.8% for 
D-AuNC based on Au. 
Cell viability test 
HeLa cells were seeded into 96-well plate (3 × 104 cells per well) 
and grown overnight with RPMI-1640 containing with 10% FBS at 
37 °C, 5% CO2. After cell attachment, 100 μL fresh medium supple­
mented with different concentrations of L-ligand, D-ligand, L-AuNC 
or D-AuNC (diluted from stock solution: 250 μM AuNC in ethanol, 2.5 
mM ligand in ethanol), were added to each well, respectively. After 
24 h incubation, fresh culture medium containing CCK-8 was added 
for 1 h incubation according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 
Absorbance of each well at 450 nm was determined on PerkinElmer 
EnSight Multimode Plate Reader. Relative cell viability (%) was cal­
culated as (Atest/Acontrol) × 100. 
Live/dead assay 
HeLa cells were seeded into glass-bottom petri dishes (3.5 cm). 
After the HeLa cell attachment, L-ligand (30 µM), D-ligand (30 µM), 
L-AuNC and D-AuNC (3 µM) were added to each dish for subsequent 
6 h incubation, respectively. Then the cells were treated with dif­
ferent doses of X-ray and further cultured for another 24 h before 
assay. HeLa cells were stained with a LIVE/DEAD cell imaging kit 
according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 
Colony formation 
HeLa cells were cultured in 6-well plate overnight and were 
further treated with different conditions (blank, 2 Gy, 4 Gy, L-ligand + 
0 Gy, L-ligand + 2 Gy, L-ligand + 4 Gy, D-ligand + 0 Gy, D-ligand + 
2 Gy, D-ligand + 4 Gy, L-AuNC + 0 Gy, L-AuNC + 2 Gy, L-AuNC + 4 Gy, 
D-AuNC + 0 Gy, D-AuNC + 2 Gy, D-AuNC + 4 Gy). Two weeks later, 
colonies in each well were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and dyed 
by crystal violet staining solution. Each group of experiments was 
performed in triplicate. Colonies containing more than 50 in­
dividual cells were counted. The cell survival fraction was calculated 
by the ratio of number of colonies formed in the treated groups 
versus number of colonies formed in the control group. 
Detection of intracellular ROS 
HeLa cells were seeded into glass-bottomed petri dishes and 
grown overnight. Then the supernatant was discarded and the cells 
were treated with various conditions (control group, 4 Gy group, 
3 µM L-AuNC group and 3 µM D-AuNC group). After 6 h, X-ray 
treatment was conducted on each group. Finally, the generation 
of intracellular ROS in each group was detected by CellROX Deep 
Red Reagent (a ROS indicator) according to the manufacturer’s 
instructions. 
DNA damage detection 
HeLa cells were seeded into 35 mm glass-bottom petri dishes 
overnight and were further treated with different conditions (blank, 
2 Gy, 4 Gy, L-AuNC + 0 Gy, L-AuNC + 2 Gy, L-AuNC + 4 Gy, D-AuNC + 
0 Gy, D-AuNC + 2 Gy, D-AuNC + 4 Gy). After 24 h incubation, the cells 
were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and permeated 
with 0.25% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Next, the cells were further 
blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. Afterwards, the cells 
were incubated with Phospho-gamma-H2AX (Ser139) Recombinant 
Rabbit Monoclonal Antibody (RM224) overnight at 4 °C. The samples 
were then washed three times with ice-cold DPBS, and Goat anti- 
Rabbit IgG (H+L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody (Alexa 
Fluor 647) was added for 1 h. DAPI was used to stain cell nuclei. 
Animal experiments 
All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the 
Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by 
the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the First 
Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. BALB/c-nude SPF mice 
(5-week-old females) were housed in a controlled environment with 
a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle. 
Results and discussion 
Firstly, 
L/D-ligands 
(1,2-dideoxy-3,4:5,6-di-O-isopropylidene- 
α/β-L/D-arabino-hept-1-yn-3-ulo-3,7-pyranose) were synthesized 
from L/D-fructose following a previous literature [42], as confirmed 
by NMR spectra in Figs. S1–S3. Then, L/D-AuNCs were synthesized 
via a two-step reaction using Me2SAuCl as gold source (Fig. 1A,  
Supporting Information for details). The as-prepared L/D-AuNCs 
both had a core-shell structure. The chemical composition of as- 
synthesized L/D-AuNCs was characterized by positive electro-spray 
ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (ESI-TOF-MS). As shown 
in Fig. 2A and S4–5, the spectra showed a prominent peak at 
m/z = 2273.8940 (L-AuNC) and 2273.8630 (D-AuNC) (calculated: 
2273.8617 for L/D-AuNC), respectively, corresponding to the mole­
cular formulas of [Au10(C13H17O5)10 + 2Na]2+. Additionally, their 
isotopic patterns were both in perfect agreement with their simu­
lated patterns. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of 
L/D-AuNC were obtained to further characterize their chemical 
constitution (Fig. S6). Compared with L/D-ligand, the C^CeH 
stretching vibration band at 3244 cm-1 disappeared and the C^C 
vibrational band showed a slight shift from 2118 cm-1 to 1990 cm-1 in 
L/D-AuNC, corresponding to the deprotonated state of L/D-ligand 
(C11H17O5eC^CeH) due to coordination between Au and C^C bond  
[45–48]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of the as-synthe­
sized L/D-AuNC exhibited excellent monodispersion and homo­
geneous size distribution in ethanol, with an average diameter of 
approximately 2 nm (Fig. 2D and S7).

The as-prepared L/D-AuNC displayed excellent solubility in 
ethanol, suitable for following bioimaging and therapy studies. 
UV–vis spectra of L/D-AuNC showed four optical absorption bands at 
207 (210), 256, 346 and 405 nm, but no surface plasmon resonance 
peak at 520 nm (Fig. 2B), further suggesting the formation of AuNCs. 
With chiral L/D-fructose as precursor, synthesized alkynyl- 
containing L/D-ligand was also chiral (Fig. S8 and S9). The chirality of 
L/D-ligand (L-ligand: [α]34.4
D 
= 78° (c = 0.1, CH2Cl2) and D-ligand: 
[α]34.0 
D = −67° (c = 0.1, CH2Cl2)) was also confirmed by their specific 
optical rotation (Fig. S10). To our delight, CD peaks of the as-ob­
tained L/D-AuNC (Fig. 2E) ranged from 200 to 500 nm (consistent 
with UV–vis absorption spectra) and were mirror images to each 
other, indicating a pair of enantiomers. Apart from the CD features of 
ligands, new features emerged in the visible wavelength region, 
which could be ascribed to the chirality transfer from ligand to 
cluster kernel. The maximum g value reached 3.6 × 10-3 (Fig. S11), 
which is comparable to those of other reported chiral gold clusters  
[49–51]. Interestingly, L/D-AuNCs both exhibited intense yellow- 
green emission around 538 nm (Fig. 2C) and high quantum yields 
(QYL-AuNC = 70%, QYD-AuNC = 69%). And L/D-AuNCs displayed the same 
lifetime (Fig. S12, τL-AuNC = 1.55 µs, τD-AuNC = 1.55 µs). Inspired by the 
outstanding chiral and fluorescent properties of L/D-AuNCs, CPL 
characteristics were then explored. As shown in Fig. 2F, the en­
antiomeric L/D-AuNCs presented remarkable CPL response in the 
same wavelength region as their fluorescence emission band. Here, 
fluorescence anisotropy factor (glum), glum = 2(IL-IR)/(IL+IR), was 
adopted to assess the performance of CPL, where IL and IR are the 
intensities of left- and right-handed CPL, respectively. The glum va­
lues of L/D-AuNC were measured to be around 3 × 10-3 (Fig. S13), 
which are comparable to previously reported chiral gold clusters  
[25,52,53]. Compared with normal fluorescence imaging, CPL could 
provide higher dimensional fluorescence imaging with different in­
tensity of LCP and RCP in target region, exhibiting higher signal to 
noise ratio [14,25]. 
Followed by the successful synthesis of L/D-AuNC, we next 
evaluated their stability in cell culture medium to provide basis for 
studying radiosensitization. Both enantiomers exhibited strong 
fluorescence and normal optical characters in cell culture system 
(Fig. S14–15). Owing to stereospecific interactions between chiral 
AuNCs and proteins (slight binding difference in this study), TEM 
images of L/D-NCs displayed different size distributions in cell 
medium (Fig. S16). Diameters of L-AuNCs ranged from 2 nm to 16 nm 
(averaged 6.39 nm), while D-AuNCs presented narrow size dis­
tribution from 1 nm to 4.5 nm (averaged 2.59 nm). To determine the 
cytotoxicity of L/D-AuNCs after internalization, cell viability experi­
ments were carried out using cervical cancer cells (HeLa) with a 
standard CCK-8 assay after exposure to chiral AuNCs or ligands for 
24 h. As displayed in Fig. 3A, B, both D-ligand and D-AuNCs de­
monstrated better biocompatibility. The IC10 of L-AuNC was 2.9 µm, 
thus 3 µM was chosen as working concentration for the following 
imaging and therapy investigations to guarantee good cell viability. 
In addition, batch-to-batch variation of cytotoxicity analysis was 
evaluated using L/D-AuNCs of three batches (Fig. S5) with inter- 
assay coefficient of variation below 15% (Fig. S17, Table S1). As shown 
in Fig. 3C, HeLa cells incubated with L/D-AuNCs revealed noticeably 
strong fluorescence, which is consistent with high quantum yield 
(QYL = 33%, QYD = 31%) of AuNC in cell medium (Fig. S14-15). Cellular 
uptake of AuNCs was quantified by measuring the amount of Au in 
cells using the inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry 
(ICP-MS) method. L-AuNC showed significantly higher uptake than 
D-AuNC after 6 h of incubation (Fig. S18), which could be assigned to 
stereoselective interactions between chiral nanoclusters and cell

After evaluating biocompatibility and stability of the as-fabri­
cated L/D-AuNCs in cell medium, we next explored their radio­
sensitization effect in vitro. Firstly, cell viabilities of various X-ray 
treated and control groups were assessed by live/dead imaging and 
flow-cytometric analysis. As shown in Fig. 4A, HeLa cells treated 
with 3 μM D-AuNCs and exposed to 4 Gy X-ray presented higher 
death rate than that of L-AuNC group. In contrast, the other groups 
including control and ligand related groups had negligible cyto­
toxicity (Fig. S19), indicating good biocompatibility of alkynyl li­
gands. From the flow cytometry data analysis (Fig. 4C, E and S20), we 
found that the apoptosis rate of D-AuNC + 4 Gy group was the 
highest, which was 1.7 times and 6.7 times those of L-AuNC + 4 Gy 
group and control group, respectively. Moreover, a colony formation 
assay of HeLa cells irradiated by X-ray with a dose of 0, 2, and 4 Gy in 
the absence or presence of L/D-AuNC was performed to further as­
sess their therapeutic effects. As displayed in Fig. 4B, S21–22, the 
colony formation rate of L-AuNC group was lower than that of 
D-AuNC group with the same dose of X-ray irradiation, but L-AuNCs 
was more toxic than D-AuNCs at the same concentration. With in­
creasing X-ray dose, the survival fraction of D-AuNC groups dropped 
more rapidly than L-AuNC groups (Fig. 4D), indicating a higher 
radiosensitizating efficiency of D-AuNC than that of L-AuNC. Notably, 
the survival fraction of D-AuNC at 4 Gy reached 13%. Batch-to-batch 
variation of colony formation assay was also evaluated (Fig. S23), 
displaying satisfied inter-assay coefficient of variation (below 15%,  
Table S2). Therefore, D-AuNC seems to be more suitable as a radio­
sensitizer. 
Since reactive oxygen species (ROS) mediated DNA damage in­
duced apoptosis is one of the main mechanisms of radiotherapy, we 
firstly monitored intracellular ROS levels of HeLa cells using CellROX 
Deep Red probe. After receiving 4 Gy radiation, ROS indicator in 
D-AuNC pretreated group displayed increased fluorescence intensity 
over time and reached its maximum brightness at 8 h (Fig. S24), 
confirming electroactive surface of AuNCs catalyzed ROS burst. From  
Fig. 5A we could see ROS levels in cells with different treatments, the 
average fluorescence intensity of D-AuNC + 4 Gy group was ob­
viously higher than those of the other groups, which was about 4.8 
times of the 4 Gy only group. Afterward, double-strand breaks of 
DNA were monitored by immunofluorescence staining of γ-H2AX to 
appraise the DNA damage efficiency during radiation. As shown in  
Fig. 5B and S25, there were very few fluorescent spots observed in 
cells of control and L/D-AuNC groups without X-ray radiation. 
However, in the presence of AuNC under 4 Gy X-ray, many more red 
fluorescent spots were found inside cell nucleus, indicating that the 
breaks of DNA induced by X-ray radiation was clearly exacerbated by 
the existence of AuNCs. More importantly, compared with L-AuNC, 
D-AuNC group exhibited stronger DNA damage ability during 
radiotherapy. 
In addition, a cell cycle distribution study was applied to further 
evaluate the potential mechanism of AuNCs-induced radio­
sensitizing effects. Compared with the control and AuNC only 
groups, X-ray irradiation mainly induced G2/M phase arrest for HeLa 
cells in this investigation (Fig. 5C, D and S26), preventing cells from 
proliferation. Furthermore, we evaluated the expression of apoptosis 
marker protein via western blotting assay. Data in Fig. 5E revealed 
that combined treatment of AuNC and X-ray irradiation could re­
markably up-regulate the expression of active caspase-3 (apoptosis- 
related protein) [54,55], and down-regulate the expression of anti- 
apoptotic protein Bcl-2 [56,57] at the same time. Similarly, cells in 
group of D-AuNC radiotherapy displayed the highest active caspase- 
3/β-actin expression ratio and lowest Bcl-2/β-actin expression ratio 
(Fig. 5F, G), corroborating with the above mentioned results, all of 
which confirmed D-AuNC as a better radiosensitizer over L-AuNC.

Encouraged by the in vitro performance of D-AuNCs, we further 
explored its radiotherapy effect in vivo. Firstly, biodistribution and 
body clearance of D-AuNCs were studied by inductively coupled 
plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Au content in the tumor and 
major organs at different time points after intraperitoneal injection 
of D-AuNCs could be seen in Fig. S27. At 24 h postinjection, Au 
content in the tumor increased to 9.5% ID/g due to the enhanced 
permeability and retention (EPR) effect [58,59]. Twenty days later, 
Au content in tumor and major organs decreased to less than 1.5% ID/

HeLa tumor-bearing nude mice were exposed to 4 Gy X-ray irra­
diation at 24 h after intraperitoneal injection of DPBS or D-AuNCs.

other day. As shown in Fig. 6A-C and S28, relative tumor volumes 
(V/V0) of control, 4 Gy only and D-AuNC with 4 Gy groups at day 20

significantly lower than the other two groups (Fig. 6E). Meanwhile, 
no obvious impact on the body weight was observed among all 
groups (Fig. 6D), indicating low toxicity of D-AuNCs in vivo. Ad­

Conclusions 
In summary, biocompatible L/D-Au10(C13H17O5)10 clusters with 
strong CD and CPL activities were prepared as radiosensitizers to 
explore the influence of chirality on radiotherapy. In vitro experi­
mental results demonstrated the superiority of D-AuNCs over 
L-AuNCs as radiosensitizer, which may be accounted by the fol­
lowing: (I) L-AuNC was more toxic than D-AuNC due to a higher 
cellular uptake; (II) D-AuNC in biological system exhibited better 
monodispersibility; (III) the radiosensitizating efficiency of D-AuNC 
was higher than L-AuNC. The radioenhancement mechanisms were 
identified to be ROS burst mediated DNA damage, cell cycle arrest 
and up-regulation of apoptotic protein expression upon irradiation. 
Furthermore, in vivo tumor therapy experiment revealed effective 
body clearance and tumor suppression. Compared with X-ray only 
group, tumor volume in mice treated with D-AuNCs and radiation 
decreased ~51% and no pathological tissue damage was observed 
in major organs. The chirality-dependent radiosensitization of 
L/D-AuNC in this study not only promulgated possible role of chir­
ality in radiotherapy, but also provided a new approach for designing 
effective radiosensitizers.

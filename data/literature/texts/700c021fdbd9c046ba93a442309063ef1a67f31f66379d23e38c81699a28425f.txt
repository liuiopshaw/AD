A B S T R A C T
Bacterial infections, particularly drug-resistant intracellular infections, remain a major clinical challenge due to 
antibiotic resistance and immune evasion. Here, we report a vitamin C–functionalized copper-based nanozymes 
(CVs) as multifunctional therapeutic agents capable of simultaneously eradicating intracellular bacteria and 
mitigating hyperinflammation. Benefiting from excellent biocompatibility and acid-responsive activity, CVs 
exhibit potent bactericidal, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging, and immunomodulatory functions. 
Mechanistically, CVs enter bacterial cytoplasm via copper transporters, induce intracellular copper overload, 
disrupt the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and promote lipid peroxides accumulation, thereby triggering a cuproptosis- 
like bacterial death pathway. Moreover, CVs mimic superoxide dismutase activity to efficiently eliminate 
excessive ROS, and regulate immune responses by suppressing macrophage polarization toward the pro- 
inflammatory M1 phenotype while reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production. These combined effects 
attenuate inflammation and promote tissue repair within infection-associated microenvironments. In a 
methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)-induced peritonitis mouse model, CVs achieved robust 
intracellular bacterial clearance and inflammation resolution, underscoring their potential as next-generation 
copper-based nanomaterials for treating refractory intracellular bacterial infections and hyperinflammation- 
related disorders.
1. Introduction
Persistent microbial infections exacerbate inflammation, aggravate 
tissue damage, and markedly delay wound repair, posing a serious 
global health burden. Intracellular bacterial infections are particularly 
difficult to treat due to persist within host cells and their distinct mi­
croenvironments with altered pH, redox potential, and enzymatic ac­
tivity [1,2]. Conventional antibiotics often fail under these conditions, 
exhibiting poor membrane permeability, reduced stability, and insuffi­
cient intracellular concentrations to reach bactericidal thresholds. 
High-dose regimens may disrupt gut microbiota, accelerate resistance, 
and induce systemic complications [3–5], while persistent immune 
suppression converts macrophages into tolerant phenotypes, enabling 
bacterial survival and promoting relapse-prone, chronic infections 
[6–8]. Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during 
chronic infection further amplify inflammation and tissue damage, 
establishing a vicious cycle of oxidative stress and immune dysregula­
tion [9–14].
Nanomaterials provide promising opportunities to address these 
challenges. Their large surface area, tunable surface chemistry, and 
targeted delivery properties confer antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, 
and ROS-scavenging functions [15–17]. Increasing evidence shows that

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid), a natural antioxidant, has attracted 
attention for its ability to modulate inflammation and neutralize ROS in 
infectious microenvironments [28–31]. However, its clinical application 
is limited by poor stability, rapid metabolism, and low tissue accumu­
lation [32]. Integrating copper and vitamin C into a single nanoplatform 
could therefore synergistically combine bactericidal, antioxidative, and 
immunomodulatory effects. Herein, we designed ultrasmall vitamin 
C–functionalized copper-based nanoparticles (CVs), as illustrated in 
Fig. 1. CVs exhibit potent antibacterial activity by inducing intracellular 
copper overload, disrupting the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, pro­
moting lipid peroxidation, and triggering cuproptosis-like bacterial 
death. In parallel, CVs scavenge ROS via superoxide dismutase (SOD)-­
like activity and regulate macrophage polarization by suppressing 
pro-inflammatory M1 while promoting anti-inflammatory M2 pheno­
types, thereby alleviating inflammation and facilitating tissue repair. 
Collectively, CVs represent a multifunctional copper-based nanoplat­
form for the treatment of refractory intracellular infections, offering an 
integrated solution to bacterial persistence, oxidative stress, and im­
mune imbalance.

1,1-Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl radical (DPPH), CuCl2 (≥99%), 
vitamin C, 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulfonate) (ABTS),

1:10). The mixture was stirred continuously at 80 ◦C for 12 h. After

2.3. ROS-scavenging assay (ABTS and DPPH)
Various concentrations (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 10 μg/mL) of CVs were 
evaluated under different pH conditions (pH 7.4, 5.5). In sum, to prepare 
the ABTS•+ stock solution, combine equal volumes of 7.4 mM ABTS 
(812.0 μg in 200 μL) and 2.6 mM K2S2O8 (140.0 μg in 200 μL) in PBS, 
then incubate in the dark at 4 ◦C overnight. The next day, dilute the 
concentrated solution with PBS to obtain a suitable working ABTS•+
solution. After incubated with CVs for 10 min, absorbance was measured 
at 734 nm with a UV–vis spectroscopy. With regard to DPPH assay, the 
method was based on previous research [33]. DPPH•+ was dissolved in 
methanol with working concentration 6 × 10−5 mol/L. CVs were added 
to the DPPH•+ working solution and the decrease in absorbance was 
monitored at 515 nm after incubated for 30 min which the reaction has 
reached a plateau.
2.4. Flow cytometry for cellular experiment
The RAW264.7 cell line was acquired from the Cell Bank of the 
Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These cells were 
maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) enriched 
with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/ 
S), and incubated at 37 ◦C in a humified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. 
Cells were seeded in 6-well plates with 2 × 105 cells/well, to induce M1 
polarization, RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide 
(LPS) at a concentration of 200 ng/mL for 12 h. After cells adhered to the 
culture surface, CVs (2 μg/mL) were added to the culture medium for 12 
h, with three replicates for each time point. Upon completion of treat­
ment, cells were rinsed three times with PBS, then collected and 
analyzed for Cy5.5 mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) via flow 
cytometry.
2.5. Immunofluorescence
RAW264.7 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 1 ×
105 cells/dish. Following treatment, cells were washed three times with 
PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at room tem­
perature, and rinsed again with PBS. CD86 was detected using an anti- 
CD86 primary antibody (Proteintech, China), while nuclei were coun­
terstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Nikon 
A1 confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).
2.6. In vitro cytotoxicity of CVs
The HaCaT cell line was originated from the Cell Bank of the Chinese 
Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). RAW264.7 and HaCaT cells 
were seeded in the 12-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well, 
respectively. After overnight incubation, various concentrations of CVs 
were applied for 24 h, then cell viability assessment was conducted by 
the cell live/dead assay with a staining kit (Beyotime, China). Briefly, 
after the Calcein-AM/PI working solutions were introduced to cells and 
incubated for 30 min at 37 ◦C. Subsequently, after removal of the cell 
culture medium, the cells were rinsed with PBS. Ultimately, the slides 
were examined under the CLSM (Nikon; Tokyo, Japan). To analyze cell 
apoptosis, an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Vazyme, 
China) was utilized. Summarily, adherent cells were gently detached 
and thoroughly washed for three times, then all cells were stained with 
5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL PI at room temperature in the dark for 10 
min. The percentage of apoptotic cells was detected by flow cytometry. 
For CCK-8 assay, RAW264.7 and HaCaT cells were seeded in the 96-well 
plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well, respectively. After overnight 
incubation, cells were treated with CVs with different concentrations, 
then apoptosis rate was evaluated by incubating with Cell Counting Kit- 
8 (Yeasen, China).
2.7. In vitro antibacterial evaluation
The bacterial suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 108 
CFUs/mL and then incubated with saline or CVs (with concentrations of 
1, 2, and 4 μg/mL) for 4 h at 37 ◦C under different pH conditions. For the 
live/dead bacterial assay targeting MRSA, 50 μL of the treated bacterial 
suspensions were mixed with SYTO9 (1 μM) and PI (1 μM), followed by 
15 min incubation. The stained bacteria were then washed and visual­
ized using CLSM (Nikon; Tokyo, Japan), with excitation wavelengths set 
at 488 nm and 561 nm for SYTO9 and PI, respectively. To observe 
bacterial morphology, treated samples were fixed with 2.5 % (w/v) 
glutaraldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and then 
subjected to supercritical drying and gold sputter-coating before scan­
ning electron microscopy (SEM) (Hitachi; Tokyo, Japan) analysis. 
Additionally, 100 μL of the bacterial suspension was cultured on Luria 
Bertani agar plates at 37 ◦C for 24 h. The number of CFU was counted, 
and the viability was calculated to evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of 
different treatment groups.
2.8. Detection of intracellular ROS
The ROS level in RAW264.7 cells was detected with DCFH-DA probe 
(Beyotime, China). Dilute DCFH-DA stock (10 mM) with serum-free 
medium to final 10 μM, discard medium, and wash with PBS gently, 
then add DCFH-DA probe and incubate 37 ◦C for 30 min and nuclear was 
stained with DAPI. The prepared slides were observed using a Nikon A1 
CLSM (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).
2.9. RNA-Seq analysis of RAW264.7
After treated with PBS, LPS, CVs and LPS + CVs, total RNA was 
extracted using the Trizol Reagent (ThermoFisher, USA) according to 
the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing libraries were prepared 
with the NEBNext® Ultra™ II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New 
England Biolabs), then quantified on the Agilent high-sensitivity DNA 
assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Subsequently, libraries were 
sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw data were 
quality-controlled with FastQC [34], aligned to the mouse reference 
genome using HISAT2 [35], and mRNA levels were quantified based on 
the aligned reads with HTSeq [36]. Differential expression analysis (| 
log2Fold-Change| > 1, P-value <0.05) was performed with R statistical 
package software DESeq2 [37]. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment ana­
lyses were performed using DAVID [38]. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes 
and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed by KOBAS 
2.1.1. Protein-protein interactions (PPI) network was analyzed with 
Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) 
algorithm.

2.12. Intracellular MRSA peritonitis model and in vivo antibacterial 
activity
All operative methods and postoperative management of animals 
were carried out in compliance with the Institutional Animal Care and 
Use Committee’s ethical and welfare standards. The Animal Research 
Ethics Board of Zhengzhou University granted approval for the animal 
experiment.
Peritonitis mice model was established through intraperitoneally 
injected 100 μL MRSA bacterial solution (5 × 107 CFUs/mouse) and 
incubated for 24 h. Mice were subsequently randomly assigned to three 
groups (n = 6). Afterward, each group of mice was treated intraperito­
neally with saline, CVs (0.04 mg/kg), CVs (0.08 mg/kg) for 16 h, 
respectively. Mice were then euthanized, and peritoneal lavage fluid 
was collected after intraperitoneal injection of 2 mL cold PBS. The 
peritoneal fluid was treated with lysostaphin (15 μg/mL) to kill extra­
cellular MRSA. After centrifugation, peritoneal macrophages formed a 
pellet. The cells were lysed with PBS containing 0.1% BSA and 0.1% 
Triton X-100 to release intracellular MRSA, followed by plating and CFU 
enumeration.
2.13. Immune responses in intracellular MRSA peritonitis model
After treatment, mouse serum was collected and CRP, IL-6, and TNF-

clotted for 30 min, it was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min, then

cytometry, resuspended the peritoneal macrophages pellet in PBS to 
obtain a single-cell suspension, then the cells were stained with mac­
rophages maker, anti-CD11b-FITC, anti-F4/80-PercpCy5.5, anti-CD86- 
PE, and anti-CD206-APC, all these antibodies were purchased from

2.14. Statistical analysis
The data were expressed as means ± standard deviation (s.d.). Sta­

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Synthesis and characterization of CVs
To establish a synergistic therapeutic nanoplatform, CVs were 
synthesized via a facile, green, one-pot method [39]. Briefly, 10 mM 
CuCl2 was dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water, followed by the 
dropwise addition of 50 mL of 100 mM vitamin C (molar ratio Cu2+: 
vitamin C = 1:10). The mixture was maintained at 80 ◦C under 
continuous stirring for 12 h, yielding a stable copper-based nanoparticle 
suspension. After cooling, the products were purified by dialysis for 48 h 
and concentrated by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 30 min) to afford CVs 
with high yield and reproducibility (Fig. 2A). TEM images revealed that 
CVs were ultrasmall nanoclusters with an uniform size distribution and 
an average diameter of 3.4 ± 0.2 nm (Fig. 2B and C). Powder XRD 
displayed two dominant reflections at 2θ = 42.8◦and 50.0◦, corre­
sponding to the (111) and (200) planes of face-centered cubic metallic 
Cu0 (Fig. 2D). Minor peaks at 36.8◦and 62.3◦were assigned to the (111) 
and (220) planes of Cu2O, indicating partial surface oxidation to Cu+. 
XPS analysis of the Cu 2p region further confirmed the coexistence of 
Cu0 and Cu+ species, with a Cu0:Cu+ ratio of ~3.4 (Fig. 2E and F). Such a 
mixed-valence state is considered beneficial for redox cycling and cat­
alytic activity.
Copper is an essential cofactor for multiple antioxidant enzymes, 
such as SOD, while vitamin C is a potent small-molecule antioxidant 
capable of directly neutralizing free radicals [28,40]. Their integration 
into a single nanoplatform was hypothesized to endow CVs with 
multi-enzymatic ROS-scavenging capacity. In peritonitis-associated 
microenvironments, immune cells generate excessive ROS that aggra­
vate inflammatory injury and delay resolution [41]. Meanwhile, the 
accumulation of immune cells and bacteria disrupts nutrient supply, 
increases glycolysis and lactic acid production, and leads to carbon di­
oxide retention, collectively lowering the local pH to ~5–6 [42]. Under 
such acidic and oxidative stress conditions, the ability to scavenge ROS 
becomes a prerequisite for effective immune restoration and tissue 
repair. Thus, the antioxidant capacity of CVs was first assessed using 
ABTS and DPPH radical assays. At 10 μg/mL, CVs achieved a remarkable 
92.4% ABTS•+ scavenging efficiency, with significantly enhanced ac­
tivity at acidic pH 5.5 (Fig. 2G). A similar trend was observed in the 
DPPH assay (Fig. 2H), confirming robust free-radical neutralization. To 
further delineate the ROS-scavenging spectrum, CVs were tested against 
H2O2, 
O2•- 
and 
•OH 
radicals. 
CVs 
exhibited 
strong 
concentration-dependent and pH-responsive scavenging effects. They 
displayed SOD-like activity with an EC50 below 1 μg/mL (Fig. 2I and J), 
eliminated ~72% of O2•- at only 1 μg/mL (Fig. 2K), and scavenged 
>84% of •OH under acidic conditions (pH 5.5) (Fig. 2L). Importantly, at 
10 μg/mL in acidic medium, CVs removed ~97% of H2O2, catalyzing its 
conversion to H2O and O2. These findings suggest that CVs can mimic 
multiple natural antioxidant enzymes, sequentially transforming 
O2•-and •OH into less harmful products and ultimately decomposing 
H2O2. Compared with other copper-based nanomaterials, such as 
copper-polyphenols [43], copper-tannic acid [44], and copper-MOFs 
[45], CVs exhibited markedly superior ROS-scavenging efficiency, pri­
marily attributed to the incorporation of vitamin C, which endowed the 
nanomaterial with enhanced antioxidant capability. The synthesis and 
characterization results establish that CVs are ultrasmall, mixed-valence 
Cu0/Cu+ nanoclusters with remarkable multi-enzymatic antioxidant 
activity, particularly under acidic conditions. These properties make 
CVs highly promising for mitigating ROS-mediated inflammatory dam­
age in complex infection-associated microenvironments.
3.2. In vitro antibacterial properties of CVs
Intracellular bacterial infections remain a formidable challenge in 
clinical practice. Over the past decade, accumulating evidence has 
overturned the traditional perception of Staphylococcus aureus as a 
strictly extracellular pathogen, instead defining it as an “extrac­
ellular–intracellular dual pathogen.” This pathogen is now recognized to 
establish long-term, metabolically quiescent niches within host cells 
such as macrophages, epithelial cells, and osteoblasts, thereby driving 
chronic, relapse-prone infections and conferring tolerance to

conventional antibiotics [46–48]. Hence, targeting both extracellular 
and intracellular bacterial populations is crucial for effective eradication 
strategies.
To evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of CVs, MRSA (a clinically 
relevant, multidrug-resistant strain) was selected as the model bacte­
rium [49]. A live/dead viability assay was first employed using SYTO 9 
(green, staining all bacteria) and propidium iodide (PI, red, selectively 
entering membrane-compromised bacteria). Confocal laser scanning 
microscopy (CLSM) revealed that CVs treatment induced substantial 
bacterial death, with 4 μg/mL CVs achieving an inhibition rate of 75.32 
± 2.83% under acidic conditions (pH 5.5), markedly outperforming 
neutral pH treatment (Fig. 3A and B). Quantitative colony-forming unit 
(CFU) assays further confirmed the strong bactericidal performance of 
CVs. Increasing concentrations of CVs corresponded to a progressive 
reduction in colony numbers, and acidic microenvironments signifi­
cantly potentiated this activity (Fig. 3C and D). To assess bacterial 
structural integrity, SEM was performed. Untreated MRSA exhibited the 
typical smooth, spherical morphology, whereas CVs-treated bacteria 
showed pronounced morphological disruption, including cell membrane 
shrinkage, surface collapse, and irregular structural deformation, 
indicative of compromised bacterial viability (Fig. 3E). Beyond extra­
cellular bacteria, intracellular infection models were established using 
RAW264.7 macrophages infected with MRSA. Following CVs treatment, 
intracellular bacterial burden was quantified by plate counting. 
Remarkably, CVs achieved an inhibition rate of approximately 99.99% 
compared with PBS-treated controls, effectively eliminating intracel­
lular MRSA (Fig. 3F and G). These results highlight the dual action of

3.3. RNA-Seq analysis of CVs-induced bacterial death mechanism
To gain mechanistic insights into the antibacterial activity of CVs 
against MRSA, prokaryotic transcriptome sequencing was conducted. 
Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed tight clustering within 
each treatment group, indicating high biological reproducibility and 
validating the reliability of the dataset (Fig. 4A). Differential expression 
analysis showed that CVs elicited marked transcriptional reprogram­
ming: under physiological pH 7.4, 325 genes were upregulated and 312 
genes were downregulated compared with control; when the microen­
vironment shifted to acidic pH 5.5, the differentially expressed genes 
(DEGs) profile became more pronounced, with 266 genes upregulated 
and 398 genes downregulated (Fig. 4B and C, Fig. S1). This highlights 
the enhanced antibacterial stress imposed by CVs under infection- 
mimicking acidic conditions. Heatmap visualization further demon­
strated that CVs exposure extensively remodeled MRSA metabolic and 
biosynthetic networks (Fig. 4D). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment 
analysis revealed significant enrichment in categories related to copper 
ion binding, copper ion stress response, ABC transporter activity, 
cellular metabolic regulation, and oxidoreductase activity (Fig. 4E). 
These findings suggest that CVs may enter bacteria through ABC trans­
porter–mediated uptake while simultaneously inducing stress responses 
to intracellular copper overload. KEGG pathway enrichment provided 
further mechanistic evidence. Genes associated with ion acquisition/ 
utilization modules, such as ABC transporters and the sulfur relay sys­
tem, were markedly upregulated. In parallel, key energy metabolism 
pathways, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the tricarboxylic acid 
(TCA) cycle, and glyoxylate/dicarboxylate metabolism, were signifi­
cantly disrupted (Fig. 4F). This indicates that CVs perturb central 
metabolic hubs, restricting bacterial energy production and utilization. 
Moreover, CVs strongly influenced protein synthesis machinery, as 
evidenced by enrichment in ribosome assembly and aminoacyl-tRNA 
biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 4G and H), implying broad translational 
inhibition. Thus, transcriptomic profiling demonstrate CVs trigger 
intracellular copper overload in MRSA, leading to metabolic collapse via 
disruption of the TCA cycle and energy pathways, coupled with 
impaired ribosomal and translational activity. These combined pertur­
bations converge on a cuproptosis-like bacterial death program, char­
acterized by copper dysregulation, metabolic stress, and translational 
arrest. Collectively, these data provide molecular-level evidence that 
CVs can eradicate MRSA by reprogramming its intracellular networks 
toward copper-driven demise.
3.4. Cuproptosis-like death induced by CVs
Given that CVs could release Cu+ and perturb MRSA energy meta­
bolism, particularly the TCA cycle, we hypothesized that they may 
induce a cuproptosis-like bacterial death program, characterized by 
intracellular copper overload, ROS burst, and metabolic collapse [50]. 
To test this, ROS production was evaluated using the DCFH-DA probe. 
CLSM imaging revealed markedly stronger green fluorescence in 
CVs-treated groups compared with controls, with the effect more pro­
nounced under acidic conditions (CVs+, pH 5.5) (Fig. 5A and B). Despite 
unchanged SOD activity across groups, intracellular ROS levels signifi­
cantly increased, indicating that CVs exposure overwhelmed the bac­
terial antioxidant buffering capacity (Fig. 5C). Consistently, BCA assay 
showed elevated protein leakage into supernatants, reflecting mem­
brane disruption and enhanced permeability (Fig. 5D). These findings 
suggest that copper-induced oxidative stress critically contributes to 
bacterial death.
Copper is essential for bacterial physiology but must be tightly 
regulated, as excessive accumulation is toxic [51]. Analogous to 
mammalian cuproptosis, bacterial death appears to result from Cu+
overload, which blocks the TCA cycle and electron transport chain en­
zymes, thereby crippling energy metabolism [52]. Although bacteria 
lack a direct FDX1 homolog, they share the conserved node of 
copper-induced aggregation and inactivation of key metabolic enzymes 
[53]. Supporting this hypothesis, CVs treatment markedly upregulated 
copA and copZ, genes involved in copper transport, thereby accelerating 
intracellular copper accumulation and disrupting homeostasis (Fig. 5E 
and F). A hallmark of cuproptosis is lipid peroxidation. Indeed, malon­
dialdehyde (MDA) levels were significantly elevated after CVs treatment 
(Fig. 5G), while Liperfluo staining confirmed substantial lipid peroxide 
(LPO) accumulation (Fig. 5H and I). Concurrently, oxidative-stress 
response genes ahpC and ahpF were upregulated, reflecting a compen­
satory but insufficient antioxidant response (Fig. 5J and K). Energy 
metabolism was also impaired: glycolytic activity decreased (down­
regulation of gapA2), while genes linked to Fe-S cluster assembly (lipA, 
sufU) and the TCA cycle (pdhA, acnA) were profoundly dysregulated 
(Fig. 5L–P). These metabolic disruptions indicate reduced ATP avail­
ability and compromised electron transport. CVs trigger intracellular 
Cu+ overload, leading to excessive ROS generation, lipid peroxidation, 
Fe-S cluster destabilization, and metabolic collapse. This cascade cul­
minates in bacterial cuproptosis-like cell death (Fig. 5Q and R), 
providing strong evidence that CVs exploit copper’s double-edged na­
ture to eradicate intracellular MRSA.
3.5. ROS-scavenging and anti-inflammatory properties of CVs

metabolic fitness, which supports intracellular survival and immune 
evasion. Targeting central metabolic pathways, such as the TCA cycle, 
therefore represents an effective antibacterial strategy [54]. As 
demonstrated above, CVs disrupt bacterial TCA cycle activity, under­
mining the metabolic foundation of MRSA and simultaneously inter­
fering with bacterial adaptation within host cells. Importantly, such 
metabolic perturbation is likely to coordinate with host immune cell 
reprogramming, particularly macrophages, to enhance clearance of 
intracellular pathogens. Beyond direct antibacterial activity, CVs also 
integrate the well-known antioxidant and immunomodulatory functions 
of vitamin C. Vitamin C can broadly eliminate excessive ROS and sup­
press the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby protecting host 
cells from oxidative stress and chronic inflammation. To evaluate these 
protective effects, we examined whether CVs could attenuate

3.7. In vitro anti-inflammatory mechanisms of CVs
Intracellular bacterial infections often delay resolution of peritonitis 
and drive a sustained inflammatory state within the peritoneal micro­
environment [55]. This persistent inflammation not only aggravates 
tissue injury but also represents a major obstacle in the management of 
chronic peritonitis. Macrophages play a central role in regulating im­
mune responses at the site of infection and thereby directly influence the 
healing [56]. Upon recruitment from circulating monocytes, macro­
phages undergo polarization toward either the pro-inflammatory M1 
phenotype or the anti-inflammatory, tissue-reparative M2 phenotype. 
The balance between these subsets is critical for infection outcomes. 
Excessive or prolonged M1 polarization results in the overproduction of 
ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokines, promoting chronic inflammation 
and impaired resolution. Conversely, timely M2 polarization supports 
immune suppression, tissue repair, and restoration of homeostasis [57].
Given that CVs displayed both ROS-scavenging capacity and pre­
liminary anti-inflammatory activity in vivo, we next investigated 
whether CVs could modulate macrophage polarization and downstream 
inflammatory responses in vitro. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that CVs 
treatment significantly downregulated classical pro-inflammatory cy­
tokines (IL-1β, IL-6) as well as key M1 surface and functional markers, 
including CD80, CXCL10, IFNB1, Arg1, and IRF7, when compared with 
LPS-stimulated macrophages (Fig. 8A–G). These findings suggest that 
CVs effectively attenuate the transcriptional program associated with 
M1 polarization. At the protein level, western blot analysis confirmed 
that CVs markedly reduced expression of inflammatory mediators, such 
as NLRP3, TNF-α, iNOS, and COX-2 (Fig. 8H–L), indicating a broad 
suppression of canonical inflammatory signaling pathways. Comple­
mentary ELISA assays further validated that culture supernatants from 
CVs-treated macrophages contained significantly lower levels of 
secreted pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) compared to LPS- 
only controls (Fig. 8M and N). These results demonstrated that CVs 
reprogram LPS-stimulated macrophages away from a pro-inflammatory 
M1 state, thereby mitigating cytokine release and dampening inflam­
matory signaling cascades. By acting at both the transcriptional and 
protein levels, CVs exert a dual mechanism of action, including scav­
enging excessive ROS while simultaneously downregulating inflamma­
tory mediators.
3.8. In vitro and in vivo biocompatibility of CVs
Biosafety is a prerequisite for the clinical translation of nano­
materials, particularly for those designed to modulate immune re­
sponses or deliver therapeutic metal ions. To systematically evaluate the 
cytocompatibility of CVs, we first examined their effects on human 
keratinocytes (HaCaT) and RAW264.7 macrophages. Live/dead staining 
with PI, which selectively enters cells with compromised membranes, 
showed no obvious increase in red fluorescence across groups, indi­
cating negligible cell death upon CVs exposure (Fig. 9A and B). 
Consistent with these findings, CCK-8 assays demonstrated that cell 
viability remained above 95% at all tested concentrations, with cell 
death below 5% even at the highest dose of 10 μg/mL (Fig. 9C and D). 
Further validation using Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection by flow 
cytometry confirmed the low cytotoxic profile, as apoptosis rates in 
HaCaT cells remained consistently under 5% across tested concentra­
tions (Fig. S4A and B). These outcomes corroborate earlier studies sug­
gesting that copper-based nanomaterials exhibit negligible cytotoxicity 
at relatively low doses, supporting the favorable in vitro safety of CVs 
[58]. Thus, CVs are well tolerated by mammalian cells and possess an 
excellent in vitro biocompatibility profile.
We next assessed the systemic biocompatibility of CVs in vivo. He­
molysis assays were first performed, as a hemolysis rate below 5% is 
generally regarded as a benchmark for intravenous nanomaterial safety 
[59,60]. Compared to the positive control (distilled water, 100% he­
molysis), CVs induced only 1.32% hemolysis at concentrations as high as 
10 μg/mL, which meeting the safety threshold (Fig. 9E and F). To further 
investigate potential organ toxicity, histopathological examinations 
were conducted on major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) 
from mice treated with CVs. No necrosis, congestion, or hemorrhage was 
observed, indicating the absence of overt tissue damage (Fig. 9G). 
Moreover, serum biochemical markers of liver function (alanine 
aminotransferase [ALT] and aspartate aminotransferase [AST]) 
(Fig. 9H) and kidney function (urea and creatinine [CREA]) (Fig. 9I) 
showed no significant differences between control and CVs-treated 
groups. Hematological indices, including white blood cells, red blood 
cells, hemoglobin, and platelets, also remained within normal ranges 
(Fig. 9J), suggesting no hematotoxic effects. These results demonstrate 
that CVs possess both in vitro and in vivo biosafety, displaying minimal 
cytotoxicity, negligible hemolytic activity, and no significant impair­
ment of hepatic, renal, or hematological functions. Such excellent 
biocompatibility strongly supports the translational potential of CVs as a 
multifunctional therapeutic nanoplatform for antibacterial and 
anti-inflammatory applications.
3.9. In vivo antibacterial capability and anti-inflammatory of CVs
The murine peritonitis model provides a reproducible and clinically 
relevant microenvironment where both extracellular and intracellular 
bacterial dynamics can be evaluated, with easy access to peritoneal 
lavage fluid for quantifying bacterial load and immune cell profiling 
[57]. To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of CVs against intracellular MRSA, 
we employed a MRSA-induced peritonitis model (Fig. 10A). Peritonitis 
was established by intraperitoneal injection of MRSA bacterial suspen­
sion (5 × 107 CFU/mouse, 100 μL), followed by 24 h incubation. Mice 
were subsequently treated with graded concentrations of CVs. After 16 
h, peritoneal lavage fluids were collected, macrophages were harvested 
and lysed, and intracellular MRSA colonies were quantified on agar 
plates. CVs treatment significantly reduced bacterial load by approxi­
mately one order of magnitude compared with the untreated group, 
underscoring their robust antibacterial activity in vivo (Fig. 10B and C).

determinant of peritonitis severity. MRSA infection markedly elevated

4. Conclusion
This study reports a novel multifunctional copper-based nanozyme, 
CVs, that provides an effective strategy for simultaneously eradicating 
intracellular bacterial infections and mitigating hyperinflammation- 
related disorders, particularly under acidic conditions. CVs exploit 
bacterial copper-transport systems to induce intracellular copper over­
load, disrupt the TCA cycle, impair energy production, and promote 
lipid peroxide accumulation, ultimately triggering cuproptosis-like 
bacterial death. In parallel, CVs function as potent ROS scavengers 
with SOD-like activity and dynamically reprogram the immune micro­
environment by modulating macrophage polarization, suppressing the 
pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, promoting the anti-inflammatory M2 
phenotype, and reducing the production of pro-inflammatory mediators, 
thereby alleviating tissue damage and systemic inflammation. This 
synergy accelerates intracellular bacterial clearance and promotes tissue 
repair, as demonstrated in a MRSA-induced peritonitis mouse model. 
Furthermore, CVs exhibit excellent biocompatibility and negligible 
toxicity, underscoring their potential as a next-generation copper-based 
nanomaterial for addressing complex bacterial infections, particularly 
those intracellular infections refractory to conventional antibiotic 
therapies.

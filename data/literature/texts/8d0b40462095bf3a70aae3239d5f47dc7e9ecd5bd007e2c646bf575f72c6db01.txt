Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease is a progressive neurodegenerative 
disorder affecting millions worldwide, causing major 
societal and economic burden.1 Neuropathogenic 
changes appear 15-20 years before onset, followed 
by a prodromal mild cognitive impairment phase 
and subsequent progression to Alzheimer’s disease.2 
Patients with amnestic mild cognitive impairment 
(subjects with impaired episodic memory) show 
early memory deficits progressing to widespread 
cognitive, behavioral, and functional decline.3
Alzheimer’s disease involves not only amyloid 
β (Aβ) plaques and tau tangles but also synaptic 
dysfunction, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, 
and metabolic impairment. Until recently, treatment 
focused solely on symptomatic relief by using 
cholinesterase inhibitor drugs and N-methyl-D-
aspartate receptor antagonist drugs. After decades of 
research, the approval of the anti-Aβ drugs lecanemab 
and donanemab marks the first disease modifying 
options for Alzheimer’s disease. This review explores 
current and emerging therapeutic strategies targeting 
Aβ, tau, neuroinflammation, neuromodulation, and 
metabolism, and outlines how new diagnostic criteria 
integrate into anti-Aβ treatment pathways.
Epidemiology
Alzheimer’s disease, the most common form of 
dementia accounting for 60% to 80% of cases,4 
affects over 55 million people worldwide and is the 
seventh leading cause of death.5 6 With global aging, 
cases are projected to triple by 2050, posing a major 
healthcare burden.7 Fewer than 1% of cases stem 
from autosomal dominant mutations in APP (amyloid 
precursor protein), PSEN1 (presenilin 1), or PSEN2, 
while most are sporadic, with age and the APOE ε4 
allele as key risk factors, with women facing over 
twice the risk of men.4 8 The Lancet Commission has 
identified 14 modifiable risk factors for dementia, 
including less education, hypertension, hearing 
impairment, smoking, obesity, depression, physical 
inactivity, diabetes, low social contact, excessive 
alcohol consumption, traumatic brain injury, air 
pollution, untreated vision loss, and high levels of 
low density lipoprotein cholesterol.9
Diagnosis 
remains 
difficult, 
leaving 
many 
undetected cases, especially in underrepresented 
populations, with complex interactions between 
age, sex, genetics, and environment shaping its 
epidemiology.

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease, the leading cause of dementia, is a multifactorial disorder 
involving amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau deposition, impaired glucose metabolism, 
neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, insulin resistance, and progressive 
brain atrophy. Anti-Aβ therapies have shown clinical efficacy and are licensed 
in several countries. Amyloid related imaging abnormalities remain a key safety 
concern, and reimbursement varies across healthcare systems. The mechanisms 
underlying continued cognitive decline after Aβ clearance remain unclear and 
might be independent of amyloid pathology. Because Alzheimer’s disease 
involves multiple pathological processes, effective management will likely require 
combination treatments addressing tau aggregation, neuroinflammation, synaptic 
loss, and metabolic dysfunction. Numerous compounds targeting these mechanisms 
are currently in late stage development for both early and advanced disease. These 
emerging approaches represent a shift toward multimodal, disease modifying 
strategies designed to improve patient outcomes and quality of life. Here, we review 
recent therapeutic advances in Alzheimer’s disease and provide perspectives on 
novel treatment strategies.

Alzheimer’s disease pathophysiology
Alzheimer’s disease is defined by Aβ plaque and 
neurofibrillary tau tangle formation. According to 
the amyloid cascade hypothesis, Aβ—produced 
by β secretase cleavage of APP into Aβ40 and the 
more pathogenic Aβ42—initiates tau pathology and 
neurodegeneration.1  10 The interaction between 
Aβ and tau is considered central to progression of 
the disease.11 The probabilistic model refines this 
view, suggesting that Aβ’s pathogenicity depends 
on genetic and environmental factors,12 with 
deterministic progression in familial cases and 
variable outcomes in sporadic forms.
Beyond Aβ and tau, Alzheimer’s disease involves 
neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, metabolic 
deficits, and insulin resistance (fig 2). Activated 
glial cells lose homeostatic control, releasing 
inflammatory mediators and damaging synapses.13 
Synaptic degeneration appears early and drives 
cognitive 
and 
behavioral 
decline. 
Metabolic 
dysfunction and impaired insulin signaling are 
also early features of Alzheimer’s disease.14 These 
abnormalities worsen cognitive impairment and 
promote Aβ and tau pathology progression.15
The complexity of Alzheimer’s disease has made 
developing effective treatments to modify the disease 
challenging.16 Research is focusing on multi-targeted 
approaches, either by using single drug substances 
that affect multiple pathways or combinations of 
drug substances that target individual pathological 
features. Here, we discuss different drugs for the 
treatment of Alzheimer’s disease and their progress 
in clinical trials.
Diagnosis
Biomarkers
New diagnostic criteria incorporate core biomarkers 
of Alzheimer’s disease neuropathologic change 
and non-specific biomarkers important in the 
disease pathogenesis but also occur in other 
neurodegenerative conditions.17 Core 1 biomarkers 
include Aβ positron emission tomography (PET) 
and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)/plasma markers 
that become abnormal simultaneously, including 
phosphorylated 
and 
secreted 
tau 
(especially 
phosphorylated tau 217), which can help establish 
diagnosis. Core 2 biomarkers include tau-PET and 
other CSF/plasma markers of tau (phosphorylated 
tau 205) that provide prognostic information 
and improve diagnostic confidence. Non-specific 
biomarkers include measures of neural injury, 
dysfunction, or degeneration of neuropil (eg, [18F]
fluorodeoxyglucose-PET and structural magnetic 
resonance imaging (MRI)) and inflammation (glial 
fibrillary acidic protein) (table 1).
Alzheimer’s disease as a clinical-biological 
construct
In contrast to the National Institute on Aging and 
Alzheimer’s Association research framework, which 
defines Alzheimer’s disease as a biological construct 
based on Aβ and tau biomarkers, the International 
Working Group advocates for defining Alzheimer’s 
disease as a clinical-biological construct.19 Although 
it acknowledges the importance of biomarkers, this 
perspective emphasizes the necessity of clinical 
manifestation for a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease. 
The International Working Group defines Alzheimer’s 
disease as a clinical-biological entity requiring 
both the presence of positive pathophysiological 
biomarkers (Aβ and tau) and a specific clinical

Current therapies 
Anti-Aβ therapy
The amyloid cascade hypothesis, eccentric to research 
on Alzheimer’s disease,10  33-35 proposes that Aβ is 
generated from APP cleavage by BACE1 (β-secretase) 
and γ-secretase, producing mainly Aβ40 and the 
aggregation prone Aβ42.36 Aβ monomers form plaques 
by aggregating into oligomers, protofibrils, and 
fibrils.37 Therapeutic strategies target various stages of 
Aβ formation, from BACE1 and γ-secretase inhibition 
to directly targeting Aβ conformations (fig 3).
Between 2004 and 2021, 23 unique anti-Aβ drug 
substances were tested and failed to meet the primary 
endpoint in phase 2 or phase 3 clinical evaluation.38 
Many anti-Aβ trial failures might reflect compound 
non-specificity, Aβ’s physiological roles, timing of 
intervention, and uncertainty over which Aβ forms 
are neurotoxic.
Soluble nonfibrillar Aβ oligomers are considered 
the primary neurotoxic species in early Alzheimer’s 
disease, correlating with symptom severity.39-41 In 
vitro and in vivo studies show that Aβ oligomers 
impair synaptic transmission, inhibit long term 
potentiation, enhance long term depression, and 
reduce dendritic spine density. These effects, 
observed in animal models, are largely attributed 
to Aβ dimers disrupting cell membrane integrity.42 
By contrast, fibrillar Aβ in plaques also causes 
neuronal and axonal loss.43 Overall, disease severity 
aligns more closely with soluble Aβ levels than with 
aggregated plaques.40
Targeting specific Aβ conformations within the 
optimal therapeutic window is crucial, because the 
success of lecanemab and donanemab reinforce 
Aβ’s central role in Alzheimer’s disease, providing 
impetus for future disease modifying therapies.44 
Donanemab binds the pyroglutamate modified Aβ 
present in plaques, facilitating their clearance, 
whereas 
lecanemab 
primarily 
targets 
soluble 
protofibrils. These pharmacodynamic differences 
explain 
their 
distinct 
treatment 
protocols—
donanemab is discontinued once Aβ PET imaging 
becomes negative, while lecanemab is continued 
beyond 18 months to sustain anti-oligomeric activity 
and prevent reaccumulation of toxic soluble Aβ 
species.
Mechanism of targeting amyloid in Alzheimer’s 
disease trajectory
Passive immunotherapy
Recent anti-Aβ therapy efforts have focused on passive 
immunotherapy using intravenously administered 
monoclonal antibodies that target specific Aβ 
conformations. Despite encouraging results from 
some anti-Aβ therapies, several phase 3 trials, 
including Graduate 1 and 2 with gantenerumab, 
failed to show meaningful cognitive improvement 
despite 
substantial 
Aβ 
reduction.45 
Likewise, 
solanezumab, crenezumab, and bapineuzumab 
produced negative outcomes, highlighting that Aβ 
clearance alone might be insufficient to achieve 
noticeable clinical benefit in Alzheimer’s disease.46-48
After many unsuccessful trials, the FDA approved 
lecanemab 
(2023) 
and 
donanemab 
(2024).49 
Aducanumab (2021) received accelerated approval 
but was later discontinued. In November 2024, the 
EMA recommended lecanemab for early Alzheimer’s 
disease in individuals with one or no copy of the 
APOE ε4 allele.50 In July 2025, donanemab was 
recommended for the same indication.51

and Emerge, n=1647) evaluated the efficacy of

International recommendations
Lecanemab is approved for the treatment of early 
Alzheimer’s disease in the US, UK, Europe, South 
Korea, Japan, China, Hong Kong, and the UAE.51 
The US appropriate usage criteria are outlined in 
box 1.56 The recommendations serve as essential 
tools for translating clinical trial data into real world 
practice, but they are not universally applicable.57 
Their relevance depends on each healthcare system’s 
diagnostic infrastructure, biomarker availability, and 
monitoring capabilities. The US recommendations 
have shaped early implementation,56 yet guidelines 
specific to each region have since emerged in Europe 
and South Korea,58 59 which reflect the local medical 
norms and resource constraints.
The risk of intracerebral hemorrhage associated 
with lecanemab is heightened in patients taking 
anticoagulation drugs. Consequently, all major 
recommendations 
advise 
against 
concurrent 
use of vitamin K antagonists or direct oral 
anticoagulants. Low dose antiplatelet therapy is 
generally permissible, if patients are counselled and 
monitored closely. Where anticoagulation becomes 
unavoidable, therapy should be interrupted and re-
evaluated.
A randomized phase 3 trial of 1795 people found 
that lecanemab reduced markers of amyloid in early 
Alzheimer’s disease and resulted in moderately less 
decline on measures of cognition and function than 
placebo at 18 months. However, adverse effects 
were reported (infusion-related reactions in 26.4%, 
amyloid-related imaging abnormalities with edema 
or effusions in 12.6%).60 If lecanemab is prescribed, 
ongoing benefit should be assessed after 18 months.

Amyloid related imaging abnormalities
ARIA was first defined by the Alzheimer’s Association 
Research Roundtable Workgroup in 2011, after 
a phase 1 trial of bapineuzumab in 2010.68 ARIA 
encompasses two distinct forms: ARIA-E (edema) 
and ARIA-H (microhemorrhages).
ARIA-E is characterized by vasogenic edema, 
identifiable 
through 
a 
transient 
increase 
in 
signal intensity on fluid attenuated inversion 
recovery MRI images.69 This edema manifests 
in the leptomeningeal/sulcal space, where the 
hyperintensity is attributed to fluid effusion or 
extravasation from surrounding blood vessels, and 
parenchymal changes indicative of edema.
ARIA-H 
is 
characterized 
by 
hemosiderin 
deposition resulting from the leakage of blood 
products, leading to small superficial siderosis and 
microhemorrhages detectable on T2-weighted MRI 
sequences or susceptibility weighted imaging.69 
Microhemorrhages appear as small focal round 
hypointensities (usually <10 mm) within the 
brain parenchyma. Superficial siderosis is seen as 
curvilinear hypointensities on the brain’s surface 
within the meningeal space.
About 74% of ARIA events are asymptomatic, 
while 6.1% to 39.3% present with the most common 
neurological symptoms, such as headaches, that 
usually resolve after dose adjustment.69  70 Most 
symptoms are transient and diminish by stopping 
treatment or reducing the dose. Severe ARIA events 
are uncommon, occurring in approximately 0.5% to 
3% of treated patients, and may include clinically 
significant brain edema, seizures, or, rarely, death 
(reported in <0.5% of cases). APOE ε4 carriers, older 
patients, and patients with microhemorrhages, 
taking antithrombotic drugs, or previous strokes 
have a higher risk of severe adverse events.71
Emerging treatments 
Active immunotherapy
Active immunotherapy, or vaccination, works by 
introducing Aβ or its fragments to trigger an immune 
response.72 Antigen presenting cells process the 
antigen, activating CD4+ T cells, which then stimulate 
B cells to produce Aβ-specific antibodies.73 Compared 
with passive therapies, active immunization requires 
fewer doses and promotes long term endogenous 
antibody production.72 It is considered a potentially 
more cost effective approach to targeting Aβ.74 
However, early clinical trials, such as AN1792, 
were halted after 6% of participants developed 
meningoencephalitis.
Several active immunotherapy candidates show 
improved safety but mixed efficacy in slowing 
cognitive decline and reducing Aβ plaque burden 
in phase 1/2 trials.75 The ACI-24 vaccine showed 
beneficial effects on Aβ plaque load and microglial 
phenotypes in preclinical studies and was well 
tolerated in a randomized trial of young adults 
with Down’s syndrome.74  76 Additionally, the first 
human phase 1/2a study of autologous Aβ mutant 
peptide-pulsed dendritic cells (ALZN002) is ongoing 
in participants with mild to moderate Alzheimer’s 
disease (NCT05834296).
BACE1 inhibition
BACE1 inhibitor drugs block β-secretase cleavage 
of APP, reducing Aβ40 and Aβ42 production by 
binding to BACE1’s active site.77  78 However, 
randomized clinical trials of BACE1 inhibitors 
(including verubecestat, atabecestat, lanabecestat, 
and umibecestat) have shown worsening cognitive 
performance compared with placebo. Some studies 
have shown significant decline (eg, verubecestat 
trials: ADAS-Cog worsening v placebo; P<0.05), along 
with reductions in brain volume on neuroimaging. 
These findings suggest disruption of normal synaptic 
function, and consequently, several late stage

lack of efficacy and safety concerns. Key challenges

Recent findings have renewed interest in γ-secretase 
modulator drugs as safer alternatives to inhibitor 
drugs. These agents selectively lower toxic Aβ42 while 
increasing shorter, less amyloidogenic peptides

signaling.82 In rodent models, oral modulators

no γ-secretase modulator drugs have advanced to 
late stage trials, they remain promising for early 
intervention of Alzheimer’s disease.
Targeting Aβ oligomers
Several agents are in development to inhibit the 
formation of Aβ oligomers by stabilizing soluble 
Aβ40/42 monomers,86 dismantling toxic oligomers,

placebo, while heterozygotes and non-carriers 
showed no benefit.

Sodium oligomannate is an oral therapy that binds

Pyroglutamate Aβ42, a toxic derivative of Aβ formed

Tau-targeting therapy
Neurofibrillary 
tangles, 
composed 
of 
hyperphosphorylated tau, are a central hallmark 
of Alzheimer’s disease and correlate closely with 
cognitive decline and neuronal loss.102  103 Their 
spread follows a predictable pattern described by 
Braak staging—beginning in the medial temporal 
lobe (stage I/II), extending through limbic regions 
(stage III/IV), and eventually reaching the neocortex 
(stage V/VI).104 This progression aligns more strongly 
with dementia severity than amyloid burden. Beyond 
Alzheimer’s disease, abnormal tau aggregation 
defines several tauopathies, including frontotemporal 
dementia variants, Pick’s disease, progressive 
supranuclear 
palsy, 
corticobasal 
degeneration, 
and chronic traumatic encephalopathy, each with 
distinct tau isoform profiles.105 106
The mature human central nervous system 
expresses six tau isoforms, which arise from the 
alternative splicing of pre-mRNA transcribed from 
the microtubule-associated protein tau gene.107 
Cryogenic electron microscopy studies have unveiled 
near atomic structures of tau filaments, showing 
disease specific folds that explain the diversity among 
tauopathies. In Alzheimer’s disease, tau containing 
both 3R and 4R isoforms aggregates into paired 
helical and straight filaments with a characteristic 
C shaped protofilament structure. In contrast, Pick’s 
disease, which is composed predominantly of 3R tau, 
shows a distinct J shaped configuration. Tauopathies 
involving 4R tau, such as progressive supranuclear 
palsy 
and 
corticobasal 
degeneration, 
exhibit 
alternative filament conformations with different 
β sheet arrangements. These structural differences 
likely reflect disease specific molecular contexts, 
although the exact roles of post-translational 
modifications and associated cofactors are not 
yet fully established.108-110 These conformational 
fingerprints reveal that tau misfolding follows 
strain-like, templated pathways underlying clinical 
and pathological heterogeneity.111 112 Such insights 
support the development of isoform selective 
therapies—such as 4R targeted antibodies or 
antisense oligonucleotides—and combined 3R/4R 
strategies for Alzheimer’s disease, which advance 
precision medicine across the tauopathy spectrum.
Under physiological conditions, microtubule-
associated 
protein 
tau 
regulates 
microtubule 
dynamics and facilitates intracellular transport in 
neurons.113 The balance between 3R and 4R tau 
isoforms is vital for neuronal stability, and disruptions 
in this ratio are linked to neurodegenerative 
disorders. Alternative splicing of tau varies across 
species, with six isoforms expressed in the adult 
human brain and up to four in rodents.114-117 In vitro 
studies show that 4R tau isoforms aggregate more 
readily,118 forming oligomers and undergoing post-
translational modifications such as phosphorylation, 
particularly at KxGS motifs, which destabilizes 
microtubules and damages synapses.119 Studies on 
astrocytes derived from human induced pluripotent 
stem cells revealed that both 3R and 4R tau are 
internalized, though vesicles that contain 3R exhibit 
greater toxicity.118 In Alzheimer’s disease, both 
isoforms become hyperphosphorylated, forming 
neurofibrillary tangles that disrupt cytoskeletal and 
nuclear function.

isoform-specific tau-targeting strategies that address 
aggregation, clearance, and microtubule dysfunction

Tau

multiple

that 
influence

Suppressing proinflammatory properties of 
microglia
Reducing neuroinflammation in Alzheimer’s disease 
might involve anti-inflammatory compounds or 
cytokine inhibition. Canakinumab, an antibody that 
neutralizes interleukin 1β, is under a 20 week phase 
2 trial in participants with mild cognitive impairment 
or mild Alzheimer’s disease with peripheral 
inflammation (NCT04795466). Low dose interleukin 
2 (1.5 million IU administered subcutaneously, three 
times daily for five consecutive days every four weeks) 
showed 
anti-inflammatory 
and 
neuroprotective 
effects in rodents and was well tolerated in a phase 
2a trial, expanding regulatory T cells, lowering 
proinflammatory mediators, and improving CSF 
Aβ42, with cognitive trends favoring slower decline 
though not statistically significant (P=0.061).130 131
NE3107 is an orally active small molecule 
that modulates insulin signaling and suppresses 
neuroinflammation 
by 
inhibiting 
extracellular 
signal-regulated kinase and nuclear factor kappa-
light-chain-enhancer of activated B cell mediated 
pathways. It aims to reduce inflammatory insulin 
resistance and improve neuronal metabolism. 
Phase 3 trials are ongoing to evaluate its efficacy in 
slowing cognitive decline and disease progression in 
Alzheimer’s disease.132
Masitinib, a selective tyrosine kinase inhibitor drug 
that reduces mast cell mediated cytokine release, 
improved cognition in Alzheimer’s disease.133 In a 
phase 2 trial (34 participants), oral masitinib given 
twice daily for 24 weeks improved ADAS-cog scores 
versus placebo. In a phase 3 study (358 participants), 
masitinib 4.5 mg/kg/day again showed benefit over 
placebo.134
Earlier trials with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory 
drugs were ineffective, likely owing to their broad, 
non-specific suppression of both proinflammatory 
and anti-inflammatory responses.135 These results 
underscore the need for targeted therapies that 
selectively 
modulate 
detrimental 
inflammatory 
pathways in Alzheimer’s disease.
Modulating microglial phenotypic change in favor of 
the anti-inflammatory properties
In early Alzheimer’s disease, microglia help maintain 
neuronal health by clearing toxic material, but 
sustained activation drives inflammation. Modulating 
microglial function to promote anti-inflammatory, 
phagocytic activity offers therapeutic promise. 
The KCa3.1 channel inhibitor senicapoc mitigates 
Alzheimer’s disease-like deficits in the five mutations 
associated with familial Alzheimer’s disease mouse 
model,136 while AL002, a TREM2 monoclonal 
antibody, enhances microglial proliferation and has 
shown good tolerability in humans.137 Targeting glial 
responses also includes pepinemab, a monoclonal 
antibody against semaphorin-4D that limits reactive 
astrogliosis and showed a favorable safety profile in 
a phase 2 trial in Huntington’s disease,138 139 which 
supports its potential relevance to Alzheimer’s 
disease.
Influencing microglial priming early in the disease 
trajectory
Microglial priming, associated with aging and 
systemic inflammation, leads to exaggerated immune 
responses in the brain.140 Preventing this priming 
during the preclinical phase of Alzheimer’s disease 
could mitigate neuroinflammation. Raised levels of 
low density lipoprotein in Alzheimer’s disease might 
also promote vascular inflammation by endothelial 
activation.141 MIB-626, an oral β-nicotinamide 
mononucleotide 
polymorph, 
improves 
glucose 
and lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function, 
showing good tolerability in a placebo controlled trial 
of 32 participants who were overweight.142 Dietary 
interventions, including folic acid and omega 3 fatty 
acid supplementation, have further shown potential 
to reduce inflammatory biomarkers in Alzheimer’s 
disease.143 144
Neuromodulation
Synaptic and neuronal dysfunction are central to 
Alzheimer’s disease, with accelerated neuronal loss 
beginning even in preclinical stages.145 Multiple 
therapeutic strategies are being developed to 
ameliorate synaptic and neuronal function because 
synaptic loss strongly correlates with cognitive 
decline (fig 5).146
Blarcamesine is an orally available sigma-1 
receptor agonist that aims to restore cellular 
homeostasis by using pathways including autophagy 
enhancement and mitochondrial stabilisation. In a 
recent 48 week phase 2b/3 trial in early Alzheimer’s 
disease, blarcamesine substantially slowed cognitive 
decline and brain atrophy relative to placebo, with a 
favorable safety profile and no adverse effects related 
to imaging.147
Bryostatin is a natural product that activates 
protein kinase C to enhance synaptic numbers. 
Results of bryostatin efficacy have received mixed 
results in two phase 2 trials.148 149 A pooled analysis 
of 94 participants with moderate to advanced 
Alzheimer’s disease showed that bryostatin, in 
the absence of memantine, improved the severe 
impairment battery score by week 13.150 A phase 2 
trial showed benefit of bryostatin versus placebo in 
individuals with a moderate-severe mini mental state 
examination score (10-14) on the severe impairment 
battery score at week 28.151

Non-invasive brain stimulation
Non-invasive brain stimulation is a rapidly emer­
ging technique that inhibits or excites neuronal 
activity to exert a therapeutic benefit.153 Transcranial 
electromagnetic stimulation generates an electroma­
gnetic field that excites neuronal cells affected by the 
magnetic field. A short lasting electrical current is 
passed through the loops of conducting wire, which 
is delivered to the brain to depolarize neurons.154
A total of eight participants with mild to moderate 
Alzheimer’s disease completed a clinical trial of 
two months of daily transcranial electromagnetic 
treatment.155 Transcranial electromagnetic treat­
ment revealed a trend toward improved ADAS-cog 
13 performance compared with baseline at two 
months. After the removal of one non-responder, the 
treatment noticeably improved ADAS-cog 13 at two 
months and 14 days post-treatment in the remaining 
seven participants.
Another approach involves transcranial magnetic 
stimulation that uses magnetic fields to induce 
electrical currents in targeted brain regions, thereby 
modulating neuronal activity. A recent phase 2, 
double blind, sham controlled trial showed that 24 
weeks of precuneus repetitive transcranial magnetic 
stimulation substantially slowed cognitive decline in 
patients with mild to moderate Alzheimer’s disease.156
Transcranial direct current stimulation provides a 
cheap, portable alternative that delivers low intensity 
current.157 Meta-analysis of 14 case-control/clinical 
studies evaluated the influence of transcranial direct 
current stimulation in participants with Alzheimer’s 
disease. Positive effects were shown on general 
cognition and memory, although the influence on 
cognitive subdomains showed high heterogeneity 
between studies.153

Neuromodulation therapy for Alzheimer’s disease 
currently 
faces 
several 
challenges, 
including 
limited long term efficacy data, variable patient 
responses, and uncertainty about optimal stimula­
tion parameters and targets. Future research 
should focus on refining patient selection by using 
neurophysiological and biomarker-based profiling, 
integrating neuromodulation with pharmacological or 
cognitive interventions, and establishing standardized 
protocols to enhance reproducibility and clinical 
benefit. Advancing these areas will be essential 
to move neuromodulation from an experimental 
application toward reliable therapeutic practice.
Metabolic therapies
Deficient 
cerebral 
glucose 
metabolism 
is 
an 
established hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease, with 
patients also showing deficiencies in peripheral and 
central insulin signaling.158 The brain utilizes about 
20% of the body’s total glucose supply, however, 
during the early stages of Alzheimer’s disease glucose 
utilization can be reduced by 45%.159 Deficits in 
hippocampal glucose metabolism are apparent 
before the appearance of clinical symptoms.15 
Metabolic decline within the entorhinal cortex can 
predict conversion from cognitively normal to mild 
cognitive impairment.160 At a cellular level, glucose 
transporter deficiencies that occur in the brain of 
patients with Alzheimer’s disease accentuate disease 
pathology.15 Alzheimer’s disease has been suggested 
by some as a metabolic disease related to age and 
coined as type 3 diabetes.161 Strategies based on 
targeting insulin signaling dysfunction, use of 
diabetes drugs and dietary interventions demonstrate 
promise in the search for a novel therapeutic strategy 
for Alzheimer’s disease (fig 5).162
Insulin receptors are widespread in the brain 
and activate key signaling cascades including 
phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase, AKT, and mechanistic 
target of rapamycin pathways with crucial roles 
in memory processing, cell metabolism, neuronal 
survival, synaptic plasticity, and neuroprotection.163 
Postmortem analysis of Alzheimer’s disease brain 
tissue has revealed impaired insulin signaling, 
marked by raised serine phosphorylation of insulin 
receptor substrate 1 in the hippocampus and 
cortex, even in the absence of peripheral insulin 
resistance.164 
This 
abnormal 
insulin 
receptor 
substrate 1 phosphorylation is linked to tau 
hyperphosphorylation and Aβ plaque formation.165 
In patients with Alzheimer’s disease, neuronal 
derived exosomes reveal abnormal insulin signaling, 
including increased serine and reduced tyrosine 
phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate 1, 
detectable up to 10 years before clinical onset.166
Insulin
In a pilot trial enrolling 104 participants with 
amnestic mild cognitive impairment or mild to 
moderate Alzheimer’s disease, 20 IU intranasal 
insulin improved delayed memory compared with 
placebo.167 The co-primary analysis showed that 20 
IU and 40 IU insulin improved dementia severity 
rating scores. A larger trial enrolling 260 participants 
with amnestic mild cognitive impairment or 
Alzheimer’s disease evaluated the influence of 40 IU 
intranasal insulin for a period of 12 months.168 No 
substantial differences were observed in the primary 
outcome of the ADAS-cog 12 score between the 
insulin and placebo treatment groups.

Triglycerides
Ketone bodies can provide an alternate source 
of energy for the brain in glucose deficiency.178 
Tricaprilin is the oral formulation of caprylic 
triglyceride, a source of ketone bodies to improve 
mitochondrial metabolism. Meta-analysis revealed 
a benefit of medium chain triglycerides compared 
with placebo in participants with mild cognitive 
impairment or Alzheimer’s disease, with a greater 
effect in APOE ε4 non-carriers.178 However, a clinical 
trial of tricaprilin showed no benefit on ADAS-cog 11 
scores relative to placebo.179

Viral infections
Emerging evidence suggests that viral infections 
might contribute to the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s 
disease. Herpesviruses found in the brains of patients 
with Alzheimer’s disease can induce inflammation, 
oxidative stress, and Aβ accumulation, supporting the 
“antimicrobial protection hypothesis” that Aβ traps 
pathogens but promotes plaque formation.182  183 
Other viruses, including hepatitis viruses and 
severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2, 
might also influence Alzheimer’s disease processes, 
with coinfections worsening neurodegeneration. 
Although causality remains unproven, these links 
highlight potential for antiviral or vaccine based 
strategies. Supporting this, a large Welsh study 
found herpes zoster vaccination reduced dementia 
risk by 20% over seven years (P=0.019), suggesting a 
feasible preventive approach.184

Proposed patient treatment pathway
Clinical expertise remains central to the diagnosis 
of Alzheimer’s disease, with biomarker testing 
conducted under clinician supervision to support 
evaluation of cognitive impairment. Rapid progress 
in fluid based biomarkers enhances diagnostic 
potential, but clinical judgment is critical when 
results conflict with presentation or are influenced 
by confounders such as trauma, medications, kidney 
dysfunction, or CSF disorders.17  187-194 Biomarkers 
mainly aid trial participant selection and are not 
recommended for cognitively normal individuals 
outside research settings. Standardization of biofluid 
assays, tau PET methods, and biomarker cut-offs 
is needed, alongside real world studies to validate 
findings. As biologically based diagnosis transitions 
into clinical use, accessible blood based biomarkers 
will be vital, particularly in low and middle income 
countries, to enable equitable early detection and 
guide emerging non-Aβ targeted therapies.

Decisions on continuation or discontinuation should

or evolving comorbidities. Scalable delivery also 
depends on investment in infusion services, imaging 
capacity, skilled personnel, and integrated referral

Aβ therapy in routine practice.
From a public health perspective, anti-Aβ 
therapies produce modest short term slowing of 
cognitive decline but are associated with frequent 
adverse events, uncertain long term benefits, and 
substantial treatment burden for patients and 
caregivers. Shared decision making is crucial, 
with clear discussion of potential risks, treatment 
logistics, and the need to modify existing drugs—
such as discontinuing anticoagulant drugs to 
reduce bleeding risk. Participants enrolled in 
clinical trials were relatively young and had early 
stage Alzheimer’s disease with few comorbidities, 
contrasting sharply with the complex, multimorbid 
populations seen in practice. Real world data show 
high rates of mixed dementia, depression, and 
vascular disease, which could diminish treatment 
efficacy and increase the risk of adverse events such 
as ARIA.195 Consequently, eligibility for current 
anti-Aβ therapies remains limited—only about 8% 
of patients with mild cognitive impairment or mild 
dementia meet trial criteria—and implementation 
will demand substantial healthcare resources and 
costs for a small subset of individuals.51

Earlier 
guidance, including that from the American 
Academy of Neurology and the National Institute 
for Health and Care Excellence, primarily follows 
a clinical model, where diagnosis is based on 
symptoms, and biomarkers are reserved for selected 
cases with diagnostic uncertainty. In contrast, more 
recent recommendations from the Alzheimer’s 
Association reflect a shift toward a biologically 
defined framework, in which Alzheimer’s disease 
can be identified by using abnormal biomarker 
findings even at earlier stages.196-199
Differences are particularly evident in the use of 
biomarkers, especially blood based tests. Traditional 
guidelines either limit or do not include these tools, 
whereas newer recommendations support their use in 
specialist settings as part of the diagnostic pathway, 
provided they meet defined performance standards. 
Guidance also varies regarding genetic testing and 
screening strategies, with some recommending 
selective use in higher risk individuals and others 
discouraging routine application.196-199
Despite these variations, there is general agreement 
across guidelines that currently available drug 
treatments are symptomatic rather than disease-
modifying, that non-pharmacological strategies are 
essential, and that ongoing monitoring is important. 
Overall, the main distinction lies in the transition 
from a symptom based approach to one increasingly 
supported by biomarkers, with differences reflecting 
how quickly this shift is being adopted in different 
regions (table 3).196-199
Recent Alzheimer’s disease guidelines increasingly 
emphasize early and preclinical diagnosis, driven by 
advances in biological and clinical assessments.17 
This shift follows repeated failures of trials that 
enrolled patients at later stages of the disease. 
International working groups now recommend 
biomarker based diagnosis, focusing on individuals 
showing 
clear 
Alzheimer’s 
disease 
related 
pathophysiology through validated biomarkers and 
characteristic phenotypes. However, PET and CSF 
testing remain limited by high cost, invasiveness, 
and standardization challenges, restricting large 
scale use. Therefore, optimizing a global framework 
for 
biomarker 
guided, 
targeted 
interventions 
is essential. Emerging blood based biomarkers 
reflecting core Alzheimer’s disease pathology could 
serve as practical triage tools to prioritize individuals 
for confirmatory PET imaging.187
The Alzheimer’s Association Workgroup research 
framework 
describes 
a 
six 
stage 
numerical 
clinical staging scheme for individuals who are 
in the Alzheimer’s disease pathophysiological 
continuum.17 The six clinically defined stages are: 
(1) biomarker evidence of Alzheimer’s disease in 
asymptomatic individuals; (2) transitional decline, 
which denotes the earliest detectable clinical 
symptoms possibly owing to Alzheimer’s disease 
in individuals who are cognitively unimpaired; (3) 
objective cognitive impairment of insufficient severity 
resulting in noticeable functional loss (ie, inefficient 
activities of daily living but still independent); and 
(4-6) loss of independence with progressively worse 
functional loss. Alzheimer’s disease is defined as a 
biological process beginning with the appearance 
of Alzheimer’s disease neuropathological change 
despite individuals remaining asymptomatic. As the 
neuropathological burden progresses, this results in 
occurrence and worsening of clinical symptoms.17 200 
Early changing core biomarkers are Aβ-PET, approved 
CSF biomarkers, and accurate plasma biomarkers 
such as p-tau 217. Abnormalities in the early core 
biomarkers are sufficient to establish an Alzheimer’s 
disease diagnosis and inform clinical decision across 
the Alzheimer’s disease continuum. Later changing 
core biomarkers such as biofluid and tau PET are 
informative in deciphering prognosis, and if found to 
be abnormal, increase the confidence in Alzheimer’s 
disease driving the disease process and its clinical 
manifestation.
Conclusion
Emerging Alzheimer’s disease drugs aim to relieve 
symptoms and slow disease progression, with anti-
Aβ treatments offering cautious optimism. Effective 
management 
will 
likely 
require 
combination 
approaches targeting tau, inflammation, neuronal, 
synaptic, and metabolic dysfunctions, currently 
in late stage development. Future research should 
prioritize the validation of blood based biomarkers 
across heterogeneous, real world populations to 
ensure clinical reliability and equitable access. Long 
term outcome studies are needed to determine the 
durability of cognitive benefit and safety of disease 
modifying therapies, particularly regarding ARIA 
management. Parallel efforts should advance tau 
targeted and combination approaches to address non-
amyloid pathology. Research on implementation that 
should examine health system capacity, workforce 
training, and cost effectiveness will be crucial to 
translate therapeutic advances into sustainable 
clinical practice. Together, these directions will 
shape a more comprehensive, personalized, and 
scalable framework for the treatment of Alzheimer’s 
disease.

a b s t r a c t
Gold nanoclusters, consisting of a few to several hundred Au atoms with a core size below 2 nm, have
attracted worldwide attention in the biomedicine because of their supernormal physicochemical proper-
ties and excellent biocompatibility. In recent years, signiﬁcant efforts have been devoted to the develop-
ment of gold nanoclusters for diagnostic and therapeutic applications. In this review, we focus on the
research progress and new prospects in this ﬁeld, and introduce the burgeoning advances on the utiliza-
tion of gold nanoclusters for disease-related diagnostic (involving biological analysis, and biological
imaging), and therapeutic applications. Based on these efforts, we also discussed the future development
of functionalized gold nanoclusters for theranostic applications. With the deepening of research, we
expect versatile gold nanoclusters to become an essential platform for biomedical applications.

1. Introduction
Gold, as a biologically inert noble metal, has excellent bio-
compatibility and has a substantial history in biomedical appli-
cations. In traditional Chinese medicine, gold is widely used as
a mineral drug, which has a tranquilizing effect and improving
physical ﬁtness [1–3]. Gold salts are used to treat rheumatoid
arthritis by reducing inﬂammation and slowing disease progres-
sion in the 1930s [4]. In the 16th century Europe, drinking gold
was used to alleviate many mental illnesses, and its main com-
ponent was gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) [5]. Besides, AuNPs have
been used as radiotracers and contrast agents for radiology since
1950s [6]. In recent years, plentiful researches have focused on
the development of AuNPs due to their promising applications
[6,7].
With the development of nanotechnology, researchers have
found that when the size of AuNPs is further reduced to the nan-
oclusters range, they will show different characteristics from those
of larger counterparts and bulk gold [8]. Gold nanoclusters
(AuNCs), as deﬁned, are a new class of molecular-like aggregates
consist of a few to several hundred gold atoms, with their dimen-
sions (~2 nm) below a critical size for electronic energy quantiza-
tion [9]. At this scale, the strong quantum effect of electrons
causes continuous energy states to be divided into discrete elec-
tronic states. As a result, AuNCs exhibit distinctive physicochemi-
cal characteristics, such as strong photoluminescence, signiﬁcant
Stokes shift, and HOMO-LUMO transition [10–12]. Compared with
larger AuNPs, AuNCs exhibit no surface plasma resonance (SPR)
absorption in the visible region but possess apparent ﬂuorescence
emission in the near-infrared (NIR) to visible region [8]. The ﬂuo-
rescence of AuNCs is affected by many factors such as size, surface
chemistry, oxidation state and surrounding environment. Also, it
was found that the catalytic activity of AuNCs is entire different
from that of AuNPs [13,14]. A recent study has shown that biocom-
patible AuNCs possess potent antibacterial activity, while larger
AuNPs counterparts are incompetent [15]. Moreover, the superior
pharmacokinetics, renal clearance, and biodegradation characteris-
tics of AuNCs have also been widely demonstrated [16–20]. Thanks
to these unusual properties as well as inherent biocompatibility,
ultrasmall AuNCs have signiﬁcant promise in biomedical applica-
tions [8,21–23].
Although several review articles have been devoted to AuNCs,
these contributions mainly focused on the fabrication, physical
chemistry, and analytical applications of AuNCs [24–27]. As an
innovative nanomedicine, AuNCs have made tremendous progress
in the diagnosis and therapy of diseases, and there is an urgent
need for a comprehensive review of their theranostic applications.
Herein, based on a brief overview of the synthesis and properties
of AuNCs, we will focus on recently presented their diagnostic
and therapeutic applications, including disease-related biological
analysis, biological imaging, and therapy (Scheme 1). Besides, a
brief discussion on the existing problems and future development

2. Synthesis and properties of AuNCs
2.1. Synthesis
A prerequisite for the application of AuNCs in biomedicine is the
development of robust synthesis approaches to obtain high-quality
materials. The procedure needs to assist the formation of AuNCs
with excellent physicochemical characteristics, while surface func-
tionality needs to endow the cluster with bioactivity. Currently,
there are many methods for synthesizing AuNCs, including
‘‘bottom-up” chemical reduction, photoreduction, electroreduc-
tion, bioreduction, and ‘‘top-down” chemical etching. However, a
detailed discussion and comparison of synthetic strategies pro-
posed for the preparation of AuNCs is beyond the purpose of this
review, and these contents have also been conducted by several
recent review articles [8,24–26]. Therefore, we will brieﬂy discuss
this aspect in the present section.
2.1.1. Chemical reduction
Chemical reduction is the most commonly used strategy for the
synthesis of AuNCs, which is generally carried out to reduce Au
precursors to Au atoms in order to form the core of AuNCs in the
presence of capping agents and reducing agents. To prepare stable
and highly ﬂuorescent AuNCs, thiolates, dendrimers, polymers,
peptides, and DNA oligonucleotides that act as capping agents
are required [28]. As a typical paradigm of the chemical reduction
strategy, the sodium borohydride (NaBH4) reduction method has
been widely used to prepare thiolated AuNCs [29–31]. The size of
the Au core could be precisely controlled via regulating the reduc-
ing condition. However, this method involves a fast NaBH4 reduc-
tion of Au ions, which often forms AuNCs with mixed sizes in the
reaction solution. To address the issue, Xie’s group developed a
novel NaOH-mediated NaBH4 reduction method for the synthesis
of mono-, bi-, and tri-thiolate stabilized Au25NCs with precise con-
trol of both the Au core and thiolate ligand surface (Scheme 2) [32].
The introduction of NaOH in the reaction solution can concurrently
reduce the reduction ability of NaBH4 and accelerate the etching
ability of free thiolate ligands, leading to a well-balanced reversible
reaction for cluster formation, which could rapidly achieve reac-
tion equilibrium and result in the formation of thermodynamically
favorable
Au25NCs.

tathione (GSH), bovine serum albumin (BSA), and cyclodextrin,
can act as both capping agents and reducing agents, thus additional
reducing agents are not required [34–36].

2.1.2. Photoreduction
Compared with chemical reduction method, photoreduction
process is a green chemistry approach, which avoids additional
harmful reducing agents such as NaBH4. Zhang and coworkers
demonstrated that polymer-protected AuNCs synthesized by pho-
toreduction procedure [37]. The prepared water-soluble ﬂuores-
cent AuNCs exhibited excellent photostability. Similarly, using
three multidentate thioether-terminated polymers as ligands, ﬂuo-
rescent AuNCs with high quantum yield were also obtained under
UV irradiation [38]. In addition, formation of AuNCs by photore-
duction can be realized directly on the matrix surface [39].
2.1.3. Electroreduction
Electroreduction is also an alternative method for the green
synthesis of ultrasmall AuNCs. Using poly(N-vinylpyrrolidone)
(PVP) homopolymer as the surface ligand, the smallest Au clusters
(Au2NCs) with stable photoluminescence properties were synthe-
sized by electroreduction [40].
2.1.4. Bioreduction
Recent investigations have demonstrated that metal NPs can be
synthesized by plants, fungi, and bacteria [41]. In fact, the biore-
duction of metal ions into NPs acts as a survival mechanism of
organism against metal ions [42]. In recent studies, we have suc-
cessfully realized the biosynthesis of AuNCs in cancer cells for
tumor imaging and therapy [43–45]. Relying on the intracellular
reduction components, such as GSH, it can spontaneously reduce
the Au precursor to biosynthesize AuNCs, achieving diagnostic
and therapeutic applications. Through a similar strategy, Zheng’s
group successfully synthesized ﬂuorescent AuNCs in situ in biolog-
ical tissues and achieved tissue imaging [46].
2.1.5. Chemical etching
Chemical etching is a top-down approach, which mainly forms
small-sized AuNCs by etching large AuNCs in the presence of
excess etching agents. Lin and coworkers synthesized AuNCs with
precursor-induced AuNPs etching [47]. This strategy is not only
robust over a large pH range, but can also produce AuNCs with con-

2.2. Properties
Compared with bulk gold and larger AuNPs, the size of ultra-
small AuNCs commensurate to the Fermi wavelength of electrons
causes the SPR to no longer occupy the dominant position. Due
to electron transition between discrete energy levels and the
strong interaction with photons, AuNCs effectively compensate
for the energy level gap by emitting intense photoluminescence
[50,51]. The photoluminescence emission wavelength covers the
range from the NIR to visible region [52]. Due to the photolumines-
cence advantage, AuNCs can be used as excellent ﬂuorescent
probes to understand intracellular processes [24].
Except for unique size-dependent optical properties, versatile
AuNCs also possess good chemical and photostability, easy surface
modiﬁcation, unique two-photon absorption effect, excellent bio-
compatibility and superior pharmacokinetics [22,28,53]. Such
properties of AuNCs highly depend on their size, surface chemistry,
structure, oxidation state, and metal doping as well as environ-
mental factors such as solvent, temperature, and ionic strength
[24,54–59]. In particular, the size and surface chemistry pro-
foundly affect the pharmacokinetics, biodistribution and renal
clearance of AuNCs [18], which are essential for the in vivo appli-
cations of AuNCs. These excellent properties paved the way for
the wide application of AuNCs, and also provided a new perspec-
tive their clinical theranostic applications.
2.2.1. Size effect
The size is an internal factor that affects the physicochemical
properties of AuNCs. The optical properties, catalytic characteris-
tics, biocompatibility, renal clearance and pharmacokinetics of
AuNCs are all affected by the cluster size [18,60–63]. Akin to quan-
tum dots, the absorption onset of AuNCs generally shifts toward
shorter wavelengths with reducing cluster size. For example, the
absorption onsets of GSH-protected Au10-12, Au15, Au18, and Au25
are 450, 650, 700, and 900 nm, respectively, suggesting an increase
in the optical gap with decreasing cluster size (Fig. 1a) [58]. Zheng
and coworkers also demonstrated that the photoexcitation, pho-
toemission, Stokes shift, ﬂuorescence lifetime, and quantum yield
are largely determined by core atom number of dendrimer-
encapsulated luminescent AuNCs [64]. The emissions of these
AuNCs exhibited a red shift with an increase of the number of Au
atoms (Fig. 1b). In the mercaptopyrimidine-conjugated AuNC sys-
tems, the size signiﬁcantly affects their mimic enzyme activity
and antibacterial properties [65]. Jin and coworkers proposed that
the different number of core atoms in NC systems is due to the dif-
ferent stability of the polymerization intermediates during the

tive in extending the absorption range of AuNCs while maintaining
the number of Au atoms [32,68]. In addition, the density,
hydrophobicity, electrophilicity and charge characteristics of sur-
face ligands also directly affect the optical properties, cellular
interaction, and pharmacokinetics of AuNCs [55,69–71].
2.2.3. Structure, oxidation state and doping effects
In addition to size and surface ligand, the geometrical structure,
oxidation state, and metal doping also contribute to the photolu-
minescence properties of AuNCs. For example, the common geo-
metric structure of Au25NCs is a core–shell structure composed
of icosahedral Au13 core and remaining 12 Au atoms (form six S-
Au-S-Au-S motifs by bonding with the thiol ligands) [72]. However,
when their structure was transformed into a bi-icosahedron, sig-
niﬁcantly different optical properties were observed [73]. The oxi-
dation state of Au core also affects the optical properties of AuNCs.
Polyethylenimine-stabilized Au8NCs before and after reduction
with NaBH4 emit green and blue photoluminescence, respectively
[74]. The effect of oxidation state of Au core on the optical proper-
ties of thiolated AuNCs is mainly attributed to LMCT/LMMCT
mechanisms [64]. Oxidation of the Au core can promote the charge
transfer efﬁciency and thus enhances the photoluminescence.
Besides, reducing Au(I) shell to Au(0) can impede the charge trans-
fer and leads to the dramatic decrease of photoluminescence [24].
On the other hand, replacing one or more of the Au atoms in the
AuNCs with other metallic elements can have a dramatic effect
on
their
photoluminescence
properties
as
well
[51,75].
For
instance, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the
lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels of Au25(SR)18
can be shifted to more negative values when one of the Au atoms
is replaced by a Hg atom (Fig. 1c) [57]. Furthermore, rigidifying the
Au(I)-thiolate shell using bulky surfactants or spatially conﬁning
Au(I)-thiolate complexes in a polymer matrix have been demon-
strated as new strategies to enhance photoluminescence [11,76].
The common strategy of photoluminescence boost found in all
these examples is to enhance LMCT/LMMCT while suppressing
nonradiative recombination in the core.

ing the photoluminescence of AuNCs [78]. This photoluminescence
boost is driven by the aggregation of Au(I)-thiolate complexes onto
an Au core that can reinforce the Au–Au aurophilic interaction,
which leads to the intense photoluminescence from the Au(I) com-
plexes. For example, Xie’s group demonstrated that Au(I)-thiolate

3. Biological analysis
The analysis of elements, small molecules, and biological
macromolecules in living organisms is the basis of disease diagno-
sis. Fluorescent AuNCs have huge application prospects in bioanal-
ysis because of their unique optical properties and excellent
biocompatibility. In this section, we mainly summarize the appli-
cations of AuNCs in disease-related biological analysis, including
the detection of harmful ion, small biomolecule, protein biomarker,
nucleic acid, cancer cell, and pathogenic bacteria.

many diseases and poisoning, so the detection of harmful ions is
crucial in disease diagnosis, detoxiﬁcation, and forensic applica-

luminescent sensors for ion detection in the biological system
[8,24,81]. As a typical example, Hg2+ is the main cause of Minamata
disease, with highly toxic and bioaccumulative characteristics, and
the development of suitable Hg2+ detection methods is essential.

As a vital cofactor for several enzymes, copper (Cu2+) regulates
the human body’s biochemical and physiological functions and
plays a crucial role in human health [96]. However, the excessive
intake will cause Cu2+ to accumulate in the human body for a long
time, leading to various diseases, such as liver damage, cell apop-
tosis, and neurodegenerative disorders [97–99]. Therefore, the
determination of Cu2+ in biological samples or drinking water has
always been an essential issue in biological analysis and environ-
mental health management. The versatile AuNCs have been used
for the detection of Cu2+. For instance, polysaccharide-templated
AuNCs were applied to sensitive and selective sensing of Cu2+ via
d10-d10 metallophilic noncovalent bond, with a LOD of 8 nM [98].
Using methionine as a template, yellow-emitted ﬂuorescent AuNCs
were synthesized [100]. The AuNCs have high colloidal stability
and can act as a reliable nanoprobe for selective sensing of Cu2+
by optical quenching. Similarly, lysine-stabilized AuNCs and
papain-directed AuNCs with ultra-bright red-emitting were also
developed for sensitive detection of Cu2+, respectively [101,102].
In another study, a sensitive ﬂuorescent nanosensor was realized
for the selective sensing of Cu2+ in blood based on DNA-
templated AuNCs and unique Fenton-like reactions [103]. The
BSA-AuNCs can also be used to detect Cu2+ in living cells, and the
nanoprobe can be reused [104,105]. However, mercury and lead
can also induce photoluminescence quenching of the BSA-AuNCs,
and further use of masking agents such as EDTA and stannum (II)
is required to minimize interference.
Ferric ion (Fe3+) is the most abundant essential trace metal ele-
ment in the human and is an essential component of hemoglobin
groups. Iron deﬁciency and excess can both cause severe patholog-
ical processes, leading to various diseases such as anemia, low
blood pressure, and iron poisoning [106–108]. Therefore, the deter-
mination of Fe3+ in biological systems is of considerable signiﬁ-
cance. Based on the ﬂuorescence quenching caused by the
analyte, AuNCs were also widely used for the sensing of Fe3+ in bio-
logical systems. For example, Zhang and coworkers developed
multidentate polymer-stabilized AuNCs as a photoluminescenct
nanoprobe for the sensing of Fe3+ in cardiac muscle cells, with a
LOD of 3.0 lM [107]. However, this strategy will be disturbed by
Hg2+. Because the o-quinone-containing ligands can speciﬁcally
complex with Fe3+, the employ of an o-quinone unit containing
L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine stabilized AuNCs can avoid the
interference of other ions [108]. This nanosensor offers high selec-
tivity and an acceptable detection range owning a LOD of 3.5 lM
for the determination of Fe3+. Based on the aggregation-caused
quenching (ACQ) mechanism, AuNCs mediated by amino acid
and peptides were also used for the selective determination of
Fe3+ [109–111].
Contrary to the ACQ mechanism, AuNCs can also sense metal
ions based on the AIE enhancement mechanism. For example,
non-luminescent AuNCs can be ultrafast visualized ﬂuorimetric
detection of Zn2+, the second most abundant essential trace ele-
ment in human body, because Zn2+ can induce a million-fold ﬂuo-
rescence enhancement through the AIE of NCs [112]. Recently, we
have used GSH-AuNCs as a photoluminescenct nanoprobe to real-
ize the detection of aluminum ions (Al3+) in living cells based on
the AIE mechanism [113]. Also, di-cysteine protected AuNCs have
been used for the determination of hypertoxic As3+ due to AIE
enhancement, with a LOD of 53.7 nM [114]. The ﬂuorescent probe
can be reused because the simple introduction of a chelating agent
can quickly remove As3+ from the surface of AuNCs.
The versatile GSH-AuNCs have also been demonstrated for
selective detection of chromium ions in different valence states.
Under weak acid conditions (pH 3.5 ~ 6.5), Cr3+ selectively
quenches the photoluminescence of the AuNCs. On the contrary,
under more acidic conditions, a redox reaction between Cr6+ and
the AuNCs occurs, resulting in photoluminescence quenching,
while Cr3+ is incompetent. Thus, the selective differentiation of
Cr6+ and Cr3+ under different pH conditions could be realized
[115]. Encapsulating GSH-AuNCs within silica-coated microcap-
sules can further improve the detection capability of Cr6+ [116].
Apart from the detection of heavy metal ions, luminescent
AuNCs have also been demonstrated to possess determination
capabilities of toxic anion, such as CN, NO2, and S2. Cyanides
(CN) are highly toxic anion because they can halt cellular respi-

3.2. Small biomolecule detection
Small biomolecules, such as glucose, reactive oxygen species
(ROS), biological thiols, dopamine, uric acid, urea, cholesterol,
bilirubin, creatinine, and 5-hydroxytryptamine, play essential roles
in many physiological processes and have been regarded as vital
factor in diseases and their treatment. Fluorescent AuNCs can be
used for selective sensing of such small biomolecules. For example,
a simple detection platform employing glucose oxidase decorated
AuNCs has been developed for the sensing of glucose, a straightfor-
ward indicator of diabetes, hypoglycemia, or acute brain injury,
with a LOD of 0.7 lM [130]. The decorated glucose oxidase
remained the enzyme activity and could catalyze the oxidation of
glucose to generate hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which eventually
results in the photoluminescence quenching of the AuNCs. The
platform could quickly analyze the glucose content in serum and
urine samples and has huge potential for clinical applications.

As one kind of particular protein, enzymes participate in most of
the biochemical processes of organisms, and irregular enzyme
expression levels are usually closely related to many diseases
[171]. Therefore, the determination of enzyme activity is of crucial
importance for disease diagnosis. For example, proteases can
hydrolyze protein and play a crucial role in the adjustment of many
physiological processes. Aberrant expression of proteases has a
direct link to many diseases, including inﬂammation, cancer, and
thrombosis. Wang and coworkers demonstrated a ﬂuorescence

the BSA shell was hydrolyzed by protease, the O2 could rapidly dis-

ysis. Based on a similar mechanism, protein post-translational
modiﬁcation enzymes were also sensitively detected by peptide-
templated AuNCs [173]. To overcome the lack of speciﬁcity and
sensitivity of abundance-based blood biomarkers, Loynachan and

lize the catalytic AuNCs, which were further coupled to neutravidin
(NAv) (Fig. 5c). This protease-responsive nanosensor was then
intravenously administered and speciﬁcally disassembled by pro-
teases at the site of disease. Once proteolytically liberated from

3.4. Nucleic acid detection
The determination of nucleic acids is crucial in molecular diag-
nostics and treatment, pathogen identiﬁcation, and environmental
analysis [197]. Versatile AuNCs have also been developed to the
probing of DNA and RNA. For example, West and coworkers cre-
ated a time-saving DNA biosensor based on DNase-stabilized
AuNCs [198]. The prepared ﬂuorescent AuNCs retained the endo-
deoxyribonuclease activity, allowing simultaneous digestion and
detection of DNA through ﬂuorescence quenching, with a LOD of
2 lg/mL. With an exonuclease-assisted recycling ampliﬁcation
approach, Wang and coworkers developed an electrochemical sen-
sor based on graphene-stabilized AuNCs modiﬁed glassy carbon
electrode for the detection of HIV gene (Fig. 6a) [199]. This
approach has high speciﬁcity and sensitivity for the sensing of tar-
get HIV-DNA, with a LOD of 30 aM. Based on urchin-like carbon
nanotube conjugate AuNCs, a highly sensitive electrochemical
DNA sensor has been manufactured with a LOD of 5.2 fM [197].
Using streptavidin-HRP-templated AuNCs as the biosensor, Phane-
rochaete chrysosporium manganese peroxidase DNA can be ultra-
sensitively (LOD = 8.0  1018 M) sensed [200]. Moreover, the gene
expression process can also be quantitatively analyzed by DNA-
templated AuNCs [201].
Except for DNA detection, ingenious AuNCs can also be used to
detect some functional microRNA. As a type of noncoding RNA,
microRNA regulates fundamental cellular processes via the modu-
lation of gene expression [202]. It has been veriﬁed that aberrant
expression of microRNA is associated with cancers, viral infections,
and neurodegenerative diseases. Therefore, the expression of
serum microRNA has been accepted as potential diagnostic and
prognostic biomarkers. For instance, the microRNA expressing
level in peripheral blood has been well developed to be a vital indi-
cator for cancer diagnostics [203]. Based on duplex-speciﬁc nucle-
ase (DSN) auxiliary recycling ampliﬁcation, Xu’s group presented a
novel dual-wavelength ECL resonance energy transfer (ECL-RET)
ratiometric probe for the ultra-sensitively detection of microRNA,
with a LOD of 9.4 aM (Fig. 6b) [204]. Based on similar principles,
polydopamine nanosphere/AuNCs-based nanoplatform has been
developed for the sensing of cancer-related microRNA [205]. More-
over, employing lysozyme-functionalized AuNCs and carbon nan-
otubes, a sensitive FRET-biosensor has been developed for the
sensing of microRNA-21 [206]. By direct imaging of intracellular
microRNA-21, normal cells and cancer cells can be effectively
distinguished.

In general, the photoluminescent sensing applications of AuNCs

4. Biological imaging
The development of imaging technology plays a vital role in the
disease diagnosis. The superior photophysical characteristics of
AuNCs, combined with their excellent biocompatibility and phar-
macokinetics, make them ideal candidates as contrast agents for
biological imaging. In particular, the enhanced permeability and
retention (EPR) effect of tumor and NCs-based targeting strategy
allow AuNCs to target aggregation at tumor sites [22]. In addition
to ﬂuorescence imaging, multimodal imaging combined with X-
ray computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography
(PET), magnetic resonance (MR), and/or photoacoustic (PA) imag-
ing based on AuNCs have also been developed because of the high
atomic number of gold.
4.1. Fluorescence imaging
As a proven powerful tool in the diagnosis and guiding the ther-
apy of diseases, ﬂuorescence imaging was widely used in biomedi-
cine. Thanks to their excellent ﬂuorescence properties, AuNCs have
received extensive attention in ﬂuorescence bioimaging. Among a
variety of AuNCs, peptide and protein functionalized AuNCs are
most widely used in biological imaging due to their unique advan-
tages [8]. For instance, Wu and coworkers showed the tumor imag-
ing potential of AuNCs employing NIR BSA-AuNCs as imaging
agents [226]. Using HeLa and MDA-MB-45 tumor-bearing mice,
ex vivo and in vivo investigates revealed that the AuNCs were cap-
able to accumulate in the tumor sites due to the EPR effects. Wang
and coworkers used GSH-capped AgNCs as templates to prepare
strongly ﬂuorescent AuNCs by a galvanic replacement method
[227]. The synthesized GSH-AuNCs can be used for ﬂuorescence
imaging of CAL-27 cancer cells. Using purine-stabilized AuNCs as
probes, a method of targeting nuclear ﬂuorescence imaging has
been developed [228]. Besides, by in situ synthesis of ﬂuorescent
AuNCs in biological matters, their potential biological imaging
applications can be further broadened (Fig. 8a) [46,229]. By adjust-
ing the surface chemistry of AuNCs, their cellular interaction and
biological imaging categories can be signiﬁcantly changed [69,70].
In addition to AuNCs alone, AuNCs-based nanocomposites have
also been developed for biological imaging. Sun et al. designed a
novel strategy to assemble paired Au clusters within apoferritin
nanoreactor [230]. The obtained nanocomposites have better bio-
compatibility and organ-speciﬁc targeting ability and are ideal
for targeted imaging. Similarly, the self-assembly of cationic poly-
mer poly(allylamine hydrochloride) and GSH-AuNCs induced a
strong ﬂuorescence enhancement via AIE [231]. In vitro studies
indicated strongly enhanced uptake of the self-assembly nanocom-
posites compared to GSH-AuNCs alone. Our recent studies showed
that graphene or carborane assembled AuNCs could also be utilized
as contrast agents for tumor cell imaging [232,233].
To improve the targeted cell imaging, recognition motifs
such as folic acid, aptamer, targeting peptide, and antibody were
functionalized on the surface of AuNCs [234–237]. Compared with
BSA-AuNCs, folate-modiﬁed BSA-AuNCs showed better cancer cell
targeting imaging ability due to the high expression of tumor folate
receptors [238]. Similarly, folic acid-conjugated GSH-AuNCs also
showed superior targeted tumor imaging [239]. Vankayala et al.
developed a novel nucleus-targeting AuNCs (TAT peptide-AuNCs)

cancer has been established [235]. Further functionalized with
the ﬂuorescent dye, the nanoplatform showed favorable tumor-
targeting ability, indicating its clinical potential for tumor imaging.

have also been used for two-photon cell imaging [242,243].
Among all ﬂuorescence imaging technologies, ﬂuorescence life-
time imaging microscopy (FLIM) is particularly attractive because
lifetime cellular imaging can signiﬁcantly reduce the signal inter-
ferences from the autoﬂuorescence background within the cells
or tissues and power of excitation light source in the visualization
of cellular processes. Compared with organic ﬂuorescent dyes, ﬂu-

dye Alexa Fluor 488 as well as the lifetime of cellular autoﬂuores-
cence in FLIM. On a time-resolved CLSM, intensity and lifetime
images were recorded in which the emission from the AuNCs
was readily differentiated from the autoﬂuorescence due to their
more energetic emissions and longer lifetimes (Fig. 8d). Using
dihydrolipoic acid-templated AuNCs as probes, similar lifetime
imaging has also been demonstrated in HeLa cells [245]. In another
study, Shang et al. presented the use of lipoic acid capped AuNCs as
talented nanothermometry devices by utilizing the thermosensi-

with increasing temperature (Fig. 8e). Relying on FLIM, aptamer-
decorated AuNCs have also been utilized for visualizing the specif-

cells [247]. These studies have conﬁrmed that the ultrasmall ﬂuo-
rescent AuNCs hold great potential as alternative lifetime imaging
agents in biological imaging applications.

Aside from biological analysis and imaging, much other contri-

5.1. Cancer therapy
5.1.1. Chemotherapy

5.1.3. Radiotherapy
Radiotherapy remains a leading cancer treatment strategy in
clinic. The major objective of radiotherapy is to kill cancer cells
and shrink tumors by using X-ray high-energy radiation. However,
one of the critical challenges in radiotherapy is that the X-ray radi-
ation also collateral damage to neighbouring normal cells. One
path to meet the challenges in radiotherapy is to develop possible
targeted radiosensitizers, which can locally aggravate the radiation
damage to tumors and minimize the damage to proximal tissues.
Gold-based materials were considered one of the most attractive
radiosensitizers for cancer radiotherapy due to the ﬁne gold
[282]. Recently, ultrasmall AuNCs have been ﬁrst introduced into
the ﬁeld of cancer radiotherapy by Zhang and coworkers [283–
285]. A series of atomically precise GSH-AuNCs, such as Au1012,
Au25, and Au2943, have been found to accumulate at the tumor site
via the EPR effect speciﬁcally and therefore had a strong radiosen-
sitizing effect for cancer radiotherapy. These NCs also showed efﬁ-
cient renal clearance and therefore had excellent biocompatibility
in the body. Besides, through the depletion of intracellular GSH
level by histidine-templated AuNCs, an enhanced cancer radiother-
apy strategy has been developed through both external and inter-
nal regulations [286]. In vivo studies have shown that the GSH-
depleting strategy possesses good tumor radiotherapy capability.
Through careful surface chemistry investigations, Broekgaarden
and coworkers found that AuNCs functionalized with GSH are
indeed more suitable for radiotherapy enhancement [287]. In
another study, Jia et al. demonstrated the structurally deﬁned
levonorgestrel-capped AuNCs (Au8NCs) with bright ﬂuorescence
(quantum yield 58.7%) could as a nanoradiosensitizer for enhanced
cancer radiotherapy [288]. Under X-ray radiation, the Au8NCs
induced intracellular ROS production, resulting in irreversible
apoptosis (Fig. 12a). In order to improve tumor targeting, more-
over, AuNCs functionalized with various targeting molecules such
as targeting peptides, folic acid, and oligonucleotide aptamer have
also been used as nanoradiosensitizers for enhanced cancer radio-
therapy [289–293]. These results indicated that ultrasmall AuNCs
are becoming one of the most promising radiosensitizers for cancer
radiotherapy.
5.1.4. Photodynamic therapy
As a clinical approval therapeutic modality, photodynamic ther-
apy (PDT) plays the role of generating cytotoxic ROS such as 1O2,
via the oxygen consumption upon light activation of photosensitiz-
ers. By taking advantage of the ﬂuorescence characteristics and
surface chemistry, functionalized AuNCs have also been used as
useful photosensitizers for imaging-guided cancer PDT. For exam-
ple, by incorporating folic acid and protoporphyrin IX, lipoic acid-
templated Au quantum cluster could be used as an energy-transfer
type II photosensitizer in ﬂuorescence imaging-guided tumor-
targeted PDT [294]. Singlet oxygen efﬁciency of the nanoplatform
was dramatically high compared to that of the protoporphyrin IX
alone. The enhanced 1O2 production with low loading of protopor-
phyrin IX and the better localization of the AuNCs on cancer cells
facilitated targeted cell death with a sufﬁciently low laser irradia-
tion dose. Similarly, through the loading of photosensitizer chlorin
e6, decorated AuNCs were also used to promote 1O2 generation and
inhibit thioredoxin reductase, an antioxidant defense system in
cancer for enhanced ﬂuorescence imaging-guided PDT of tumors
[295,296]. To improve the light energy conversion efﬁciency of
cancer PDT, AuNC photosensitizer was further functionalized with
TiO2NPs and graphene [297]. The narrow band gap in AuNCs, and
staggered energy bands between AuNCs, TiO2NPs, and 2D gra-
phene can lead to efﬁcient use of light and separation of electron
hole pairs, promoting the generation of luxuriant ROS. In addition,
through surface manipulation, the 1O2 generation ability of the
AuNCs can also be signiﬁcantly improved [298]. To overcome
tumor hypoxia, AuNCs with catalase-like activity and catalase-
loaded AuNCs were also used for tumor PDT, including NIR-II light
activated PDT [299,300]. These AuNCs triggered decomposition of
cancer endogenous hydrogen peroxide to generate O2, thereby
enhancing PDT’s efﬁcacy by circumventing cancer hypoxia.
In another elegant study, Han and coworkers reported using of
dihydrolipoic acid templated AuNCs (DHLA-AuNCs) as electron-
transfer type I photosensitizers for efﬁcient two-photon PDT
in vivo [301]. The synthesized DHLA-AuNCs exhibited good two-
photon optical properties and superior photodynamic property.
Compared with the 1O2 mechanism (type II) of other conventional
photosensitizers, the photochemical electron-transfer mechanism
of DHLA-AuNCs involved the type I process, resulting in the gener-
ation of superoxide anion (O2
) (Fig. 12b). The principle of anti-
cancer effect triggered by DHLA-AuNCs PDT is intricate, referring
to cell necrosis, apoptosis, autophagy, and others.

5.3. Diabetes treatment
As an excellent DDS, AuNCs have also been tried for the delivery
and controlled release of the hypoglycemic insulin for blood glu-
cose regulation in diabetes. Gluconic acid-modiﬁed insulin was
grafted onto the surface of AuNCs functionalized with phenyl-
boronic acid and its derivatives to obtain glucose-responsive
insulin-release nanocomplexes [322,323]. These nanocomplexes
exhibited high sensitivity to glucose levels and could rapidly
release insulin in the hyperglycemic state. Similarly, insulin-
mediated directly AuNCs have been found to retain the bioactivity
of insulin and can be used to regulate blood glucose in vivo [241].
5.4. Cardiovascular disease treatment
Recently, Jiang’s group developed a triple-targeting approach to
produce a loss-of-function mutation in Pcsk9 gene, which is a key
regulator of plasma cholesterol levels, using an AuNC-based
carrier-delivered CRISPR/Cas9 [324]. Nuclear localization signal-
tagged Cas9 and Pcsk9-targeted sgRNA were complexed with TAT
peptide-modiﬁed
GSH-AuNCs and
further
encapsulated
in
a
galactose-decorated lipid layer to target the NCs to the liver. The
nanocomplexes had a Pcsk9-editing efﬁciency of approximately
60% and led to reduce in plasma low-density lipoprotein choles-
terol in mice of about 30%. No off-target mutagenesis was observed
at ten highly similar sites. This strategy may pave a novel avenue
for the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular diseases.
5.5. Neurodegenerative disease treatment
AD and PD are two severe neurodegenerative diseases induced
by irregularly abnormally folded and aggregated monomeric pro-
teins. Recently, functionalized AuNCs have been found to inhibit
these abnormal protein processes, thereby beneﬁting related dis-
eases. For example, in order to increase the inhibitory capability
of LVEEA, a central hydrophobic fragment of amyloid beta (Ab)
and can suppress the aggregation of the AD’s pivotal biomarker
Ab40, hexa-peptide CLVFFA were decorated at the surface of AuNCs
to develop a nanoinhibitor (CLVFFA-AuNCs) [325]. Unlike the neg-
ligible inhibitory effect of free CLVFFA, CLVFFA-AuNCs could sup-
press
Ab40
ﬁbrillogenesis,
ﬁbrils’
prolongation,
and
mature
ﬁbrils’ disaggregation. Indeed, ultrasmall AuNCs have been found
to signiﬁcantly inhibit the process of protein amyloidosis [326],
and therefore CLVFFA-AuNCs were expected to have synergetic
inhibiting effects on Ab ﬁbrillogenesis. Similarly, AuNCs can effec-
tively prevent aggregation and ﬁbrillation of a-synuclein and be a
new candidate drug for the treatment of PD [327]. Studies have
shown that functionalized AuNCs have good neuroprotective
effects and can largely improve the behavioral disorders of PD
mice. In addition, neuroinﬂammation associated with neurodegen-
erative diseases can also be improved by ultrasmall AuNCs [328].
In summary, versatile AuNCs have great prospects in the treatment
of neurodegenerative diseases.
6. Summary and outlook
As a versatile platform, AuNCs have received widespread atten-
tion in materials chemistry, analytical chemistry, energy, catalysis,
and biomedical applications. In the present review, we provided an
all-around understanding of the current situations in diagnostic
and
therapeutic
applications
of
ultrasmall
AuNCs,
including
disease-related biological analysis, biological imaging, and thera-
peutic applications. In terms of disease-related bioanalysis, ﬂuores-
cent AuNCs have been widely utilized for the selective analysis of
various harmful heavy metal ions, inorganic anions, biomolecules
biomarkers as well as cancer cells and pathogenic bacteria. In
bioimaging, AuNCs have achieved targeted NIR, two-photon, FLIM,
SWIR, NIR-II imaging as well as multimodal imaging combined
with CT, MR, PET, and PA. In terms of disease treatment, AuNCs
have been widely utilized as nanotherapeutics and targeted DDSs
for cancer, MDR bacterial infections, diabetes, cardiovascular dis-
eases, and neurodegenerative disorders such as AD and PD. The
current state of the art AuNCs exhibits great potential to diagnose
and cure various diseases including cancer in the near future.
However, systemic administration of these NCs still needs to be
solved in many ways. Au nanocluster design should be synthesized
to maintain bioactivity in complex biological media and escape
recognition by the cellular immune system. Investigations of
structure–activity
relationship
based
on
atomically
precise
water-soluble AuNCs will help in generating strategies to develop
effective diagnostic and therapeutic agents [6,17]. The current
research on cellular uptake is mainly focused on Au nanoparticles,
while the understanding of the uptake of ultrasmall AuNCs by cells
is lacking. The detailed investigations on the cellular uptake of
AuNCs and nano-bio action mechanism should be performed. In
addition, due to the nanomaterial-induced endothelial leakiness
(NanoEL) effect, inorganic NPs including AuNPs can destroy
endothelial cell junctions and promote cancer metastasis [329–
332]. Therefore, the potential for NanoEL effect needs to be taken
into consideration when designing future AuNCs, especially nan-
otherapeutics to treat cancer. On the other hand, there is a rise in
utilizing the NanoEL effect of inorganic NPs to deliberately induce
endothelial leakiness, creating a window of opportunity to control
drug delivery across the endothelium [333,334]. Therefore, in-
depth investigation of the potential applicability of the NanoEL
effect in vascular-related diseases, especially cancer, will provide
a new paradigm for the theranostic applications of AuNCs. To ame-
liorate the imaging and therapeutic effect of cancer, AuNCs-based

NIR-II multimodal imaging agents and nuclear-targeted therapeu-

coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) in the context of the current
global epidemic? Nanotechnology has shown great potential in
detection of COVID-19 and vaccine research [335–337]. As excel-
lent ﬂuorescent probes and superior nanocarriers, AuNCs have
played an important role in virus identiﬁcation and vaccine devel-
opment [199,338,339]. Therefore, biofunctionalized AuNCs should
be used in the rapid diagnosis of COVID-19 and vaccine develop-
ment in future research. Overall, interdisciplinary research evalu-
ating distinct aspects of nanoclusters will establish AuNCs as
effective next-generation theranostics. The ‘‘Golden Era” of biome-
dicine is emerging.
Toward the end of the present review, it is worthy of a brief dis-
cussion on silver NCs. Similar to AuNCs, silver NCs also possess
sizes comparable to the Fermi wavelength of electrons, and exhibit
molecule-like properties, including discrete electronic transitions
and strong photoluminescence [340]. Compared with AuNCs, how-
ever, the reports on silver NCs are surprisingly scarce although
both belong to the superfamily of NCs. This is due mainly to the
oxidation and cytotoxicity of AgNCs. Nevertheless, substantial
effort has been made to synthesize AgNCs and explore their poten-
tial applications. First of all, progress has been made in the synthe-
sis of well-deﬁned AgNCs. Various atomically precise AgNCs have
been synthesized, and their structure and properties have also
been extensively investigated [75,341]. In addition, thanks to the
strong photoluminescence, AgNCs are also widely used in chemical
sensing, single molecule microscopy, and biological imaging [342–
345]. Moreover, AgNCs were used as a new generation of metal-
based antibacterial agents to treat MDR bacterial infections due
to the inherent cytotoxicity of silver [346–348]. The antibacterial
mechanism and inﬂuencing factors have also been investigated
in detail. Overall, AgNCs also show great promise in the diagnosis
and treatment of diseases, especially in vitro analytical and
antibacterial applications, and research on AgNCs should be further
strengthened.

ABSTRACT 
Nanozymes, a promising class of enzyme mimics based on nanostructures, have attracted considerable research interest. However, 
in sharp contrast to the structural precision of natural enzymes, most nanozymes are poorly defined structurally. The absence of 
nanozyme systems that mimic natural isoenzymes—which catalyze similar reactions despite slight differences in their chemical 
structures—has particularly hindered the understanding of their structure–performance relationships. Such nanozyme analogues, 
termed iso-nanozymes, remain largely unexplored. Here, we report the first pair of iso-nanozymes. Two analogous copper 
nanoclusters—[Cu32 (SC2 H5 )16 (PPh3 )8 Cl9 ]+ (Cu32 ) and [Cu30 (SC2 H5 )16 (PPh3 )6 Cl9 ]+ (Cu30 )—were synthesized and structurally 
characterized. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis reveals that Cu30 possesses an identical metal framework and ligand types 
as Cu32 , with a comparable ligand distribution. The only structural difference is the absence of two PPh3 Cu+ units in Cu30 , 
which results in a substantial enhancement of its catalytic performance in the horseradish peroxidase-mimicking reaction. Under 
identical conditions, the specific activity (SA) of the Cu30 nanozyme is approximately 6.5 times higher than that of Cu32 . Density 
functional theory calculations indicate that the notable difference in the SA between the two cluster nanozymes is attributed 
to variations in adsorption energies, which stem from their different geometric and electronic structures. This study not only 
introduces the novel concept of iso-nanozymes using atomically precise metal nanoclusters, but also establishes a model system 
for investigating the critical influence of nanozyme structure, down to the atomic level, on catalytic efficiency. These findings are 
anticipated to inspire further research interest in atomically precise metal nanoclusters within the nanozyme community.

Introduction 
Enzymes, which often contain metal sites within protein cavities, 
play crucial roles in both biological systems and industrial 
processes [ 1–3 ]. However, their practical utility is limited by high 
costs, sensitivity to environmental conditions, and challenges in 
recovery and reuse [ 4, 5 ], driving the exploration of alternatives. 
In this context, nanozymes—nanomaterials that exhibit enzyme- 
like activities—have attracted significant attention [ 6, 7 ]. These 
materials offer high stability, robust catalytic performance, low 
cost, ease of synthesis, and recyclability, making them promis- 
ing for applications such as immunoassays, biosensing, tissue 
engineering, and environmental remediation [ 8–10 ]. Over the 
past decade, a wide variety of nanozymes have been devel- 
oped, including metal nanoparticles, metal oxides, metal–organic 
frameworks, carbon-based materials, and single-atom catalysts 
[ 11–14 ], capable of mimicking the functions of peroxidases 
(PODs), oxidases (OXD), catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase 
(SOD), and hydrolases [ 15–19 ]. Despite these advancements, the 
structural heterogeneity of most nanozymes impedes precise 
characterization and manipulation of their catalytic sites at 
the atomic level [ 20, 21 ]. In particular, the lack of nanozyme 
systems that accurately mimic natural isoenzymes has limited 
the understanding of structure–performance relationships [ 22 ]. 
Isoenzymes are groups of enzymes that catalyze the same reaction 
yet differ in structure and catalytic efficiency [ 23 ]. A well- 
studied example is lactate dehydrogenase, which exists in five 
distinct forms separable by electrophoresis [ 24 ]. Isoenzymes often 
exhibit tissue or organ specificity, making them valuable tools in 
diagnostic medicine [ 25 ]. 
Ligand-stabilized, atomically precise metal nanoclusters have 
emerged as a promising class of nanozymes [ 26–28 ]. These ultra- 
small nanoparticles ( < 3 nm in diameter) consist of a metal core 
with a defined number of atoms and a functionalizable ligand 
shell, endowing them with well-defined chemical compositions 
and geometric structures [ 29–33 ]. Their molecular architectures 
can be precisely tailored, and their catalytic properties are highly 
sensitive to structural details—down to the single-atom level—
enabling atomic-scale investigation of structure–performance 
relationships [ 34–39 ]. Several metal nanoclusters have recently 
demonstrated potential as nanozymes, primarily focusing on 
employing distinct structures for different types of enzyme-like 
catalysis. Despite these advances, a significant gap remains in 
the development of atomically precise metal nanoclusters as 
nanozymes. For instance, systematic studies on the structure–
performance relationships of cluster-based nanozymes are still 
lacking [ 40–43 ]. In particular, there is a notable absence of 
nanozyme systems that mimic natural isoenzymes, even though 
isostructural nanoclusters have been extensively documented 
[ 44–47 ]. It is envisioned that exploring such nanozyme ana- 
logues, which we term “iso-nanozymes,” will greatly advance 
the understanding of structure–performance correlations in 
nanozymes. 
Herein, we report the first pair of iso-nanozymes constructed 
from atomically precise metal nanoclusters. These isostructural 
copper nanoclusters possess analogous architectures yet exhibit 
markedly distinct enzymatic activities, offering a model sys- 
tem to elucidate the structure–performance relationships of 
nanozymes at the atomic level. The core structures investigated 
are [Cu32 (SC2 H5 )16 (PPh3 )8 Cl9 ]+ and [Cu30 (SC2 H5 )16 (PPh3 )6 Cl9 ]+ 
(denoted as Cu32 and Cu30 , respectively, where SC2 H5 
− is 
ethanethiolate and PPh3 is triphenylphosphine). Structural anal- 
yses reveal that Cu32 adopts a half-cubic configuration, while Cu30 
can be described as deriving from Cu32 via the removal of two 
PPh3 Cu+ units. Both clusters display POD-like activity; however, 
their catalytic performances differ significantly. Under identical 
conditions, the specific activity (SA) of the Cu30 nanozyme is 
approximately 6.5-fold higher than that of Cu32 . The following 
studies demonstrate the significant dependence of nanocluster 
electronic structures on their molecular configurations, which 
in turn explains the observed discrepancies in their nanozyme 
properties.

Results and Discussion 
The two copper nanocluster nanozymes in this study were 
synthesized using different protocols. The Cu30 cluster was 
synthesized using the synthetic procedure developed by Bakr 
et al., while the Cu32 cluster was prepared based on our recently 
reported synthetic strategy (See Figure S1 ) [ 48, 49 ]. The synthesis 
of Cu30 was performed following the same procedure as that 
reported recently for the Cu66 cluster (details see Supporting 
Information) [ 49 ]. Briefly, a mixture containing (CF3 CO2 )2 Cu, 
PPh3 , NaSbF6 , and (C2 H5 S)2 was reduced with NaBH4 in a 
mixture of acetonitrile, methanol, and chloroform, yielding 
an orange-yellow solution. Photographs of the crude product 
solutions of Cu30 and Cu32 , along with their corresponding 
ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectra, are presented in Figure S2 . 
Due to the distinct synthesis methods employed for Cu32 and 
Cu30 clusters, their crude product UV spectra exhibit significant 
differences. After a crystallization period of approximately 7 days, 
yellow flaky crystals were obtained, with a yield of ∼ 6% (based 
on Cu, Figure S3 ). The Cu32 cluster was synthesized via a copper 
borohydride-mediated reduction strategy. Typically, the reduction 
of a mixture containing [Cu(CH3 CN)4 ]BF4 , PPh4 Cl, and (C2 H5 S)2 
in a mixed solvent of methanol and dichloromethane using 
(PPh3 )2 CuBH4 yields an orange-colored solution. Subsequently, 
after approximately 7 days of crystallization, orange needle-like 
crystals were obtained, with a yield of ∼ 56% (based on Cu, 
Figure S4 ). 
The molecular structure of both clusters was successfully 
determined by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis 
(Figures S5 and S6 ) prior to enzyme-mimicking catalysis, which 
facilitates the study of structure-performance relationships (vide 
infra). As detailed in Tables S1 and S2 , the two clusters crystallize 
in I 2/ m (Cu32 ) and 𝑃̄1 (Cu30 ) space groups, demonstrating 
their structural difference and distinct packing arrangements 
(Figures S7 and S8 ). Careful analysis reveals that the Cu32 
cluster consists of 32 Cu atoms, 9 Cl atoms, 16 SC2 H5 
− ligands, 
and 8 PPh3 ligands, resulting in the overall structural formula 
[Cu32 (SC2 H5 )16 (PPh3 )8 Cl9 ]+ (Figure 1 , left). The overall architec- 
ture of Cu32 can be described as a half-cube with dimensions of 
19.594 Å in height and 20.391 Å in width (Figures S9 and S10 ). In 
contrast, the structure of the Cu30 cluster represents the removal 
of two PPh3 Cu+ units from the surface of Cu32 , specifically from 
two corner sites of the half-cube (Figure 1 , right). Thus, a pair 
of model nanoclusters with similar geometric structures were 
obtained.

Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) is a signif- 
icant analytical technique employed to determine the precise 
composition and charge of cluster compounds [ 55 ], which is 
not achievable with other common nanozyme systems. We 
subsequently performed ESI-MS in the positive mode to confirm 
the molecular composition of the two clusters. Two main peaks 
at m / z = 2493.15 (A) and 2714.72 (B) are observed in the ESI-MS 
spectrum of Cu32 (Figure 3a ). The peak labeled B in Figure 3a cor- 
responds to the molecular formula [Cu32 (SC2 H5 )16 (PPh3 )8 Cl9 H]2 + 
(B), whereas peak A corresponds to half the size of the molec- 
ular formula [Cu18 Cl5 (PPh3 )3 (SC2 H5 )6 (H2 O)]+ (Figure S15 ). The 
simulated isotopic patterns of the two peaks match well with 
the experimental results, validating this formula assignment. We 
note that no hydride is present in the structure of Cu32 . As 
shown in Figure S16 , the ESI-MS of the Cu32 cluster synthe- 
sized from CuBD4 (PPh3 )2 is identical to that synthesized from 
CuBH4 (PPh3 )2 . The hydrogen in the above mass assignment 
corresponds to a proton introduced during the measurement. 
In this regard, six free valence electrons are present in Cu32 
structure. To further determine the valence states of the Cu atoms, 
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was conducted 
(Figure S17 ). XPS studies of the Cu32 cluster revealed binding ener- 
gies of 952 eV for Cu2p 1/2 and 932 eV for Cu2p 3/2 (Figure 3b ), 
indicating the absence of Cu2 + in the cluster. As shown in 
Figure 3c , the copper LMM spectra (918.9 eV and 916.2 eV) 
indicate that the copper atoms in the cluster possess both zero 
and + 1 valence states, with 912 eV representing the other orbital 
electron peak. The energy-dispersive spectrogram of Cu32 reveals 
that Cu, S, P, and Cl are uniformly distributed on the crystal 
surface (Figure S18 ). The Cu32 cluster in N,N-dimethylformamide 
(DMF) exhibits a pale orange color (Figure S19 ) and displays 
characteristic peaks in its ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) spectrum 
at wavelengths of 268 and 383 nm (Figure 3d ). The optical 
band gap of the Cu32 cluster, calculated from the Tauc plot, 
is 1.89 eV (Figure 3e ). The results indicate the high electronic 
stability of the cluster. Moreover, the time-dependent UV–Vis 
spectra confirmed the stability of the Cu32 nanocluster over a 
period of 3 h (Figure 3f ). Similarly, a series of characterization 
analyses were performed on the Cu30 cluster (Figures S20–S24 ), 
and the molecular formula of the Cu30 cluster was determined 
to be [Cu30 (SC2 H5 )16 (PPh3 )6 Cl9 ]+ , indicating it is a four-electron 
system. We observe that the UV–Vis diffuse reflectance spectrum 
of Cu30 differs significantly from that of Cu32 , which aligns 
with their distinct electronic structures (Figure S25 , as discussed 
below). In addition, as shown in Figure S26 , we obtained the 
PXRD spectra of Cu30 and Cu32 . Their XRD patterns exhibit 
significant differences in the range of 10◦–20◦in the bulk phase. 
A number of studies have demonstrated that the structural char- 
acteristics of nanozymes—such as shape, composition, charge, 
and size—signif icantly influence their catalytic activity [ 56–58 ]. 
In this context, we identified a pair of copper nanoclusters 
with atomically precise and geometrically similar structures, 
which serve as an ideal iso-nanozyme system for elucidat- 
ing the structure–activity relationships of nanozymes. Notably, 
although several recent studies have demonstrated the potential 
of atomically precise metal nanoclusters as nanozymes, most 
previous studies have focused on gold and silver [ 59, 60 ]. Few 
studies have reported the utilization of copper nanoclusters in 
enzyme-mimicking catalysis. 
As a proof of concept, we evaluated the POD catalytic activity 
of Cu32 and Cu30 in the typical horseradish peroxidase (HRP)- 
mimicry reaction. Using 3,3 ′ ,5,5 ′ -tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) as 
a common chromogenic substrate, the catalytic properties of Cu30 
and Cu32 clusters were systematically explored and compared. 
As depicted in Figure 4a , in the presence of the H2 O2 , colorless 
TMB was converted to blue oxTMB by the catalytic oxidation 
of clusters with a peak at 652 nm. In contrast, the control 
group without H2 O2 or clusters displayed weak chromogenic 
change. The results illustrated that the Cu30 and Cu32 clusters 
both displayed typical POD-mimicking activity. Besides, from 
Figure 4a,b , it can be observed that Cu30 clusters exhibit a 
higher catalytic ability for TMB oxidation than Cu32 clusters. 
In addition to POD-like activity, we systematically evaluated 
whether the Cu30 and Cu32 clusters possess other enzyme- 
mimicking activities, including OXD, SOD, and CAT activities. 
The experimental results in Figure S27 indicate that neither Cu30 
nor Cu32 can catalyze the conversion of the colorless substrate 
TMB into its characteristic absorption product, implying the 
absence of OXD-like activity in both clusters. As for SOD-like 
activity, a nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT) assay was used

To further assess the catalytic efficiency of Cu30 and Cu32 
clusters, typical enzyme kinetic parameters were determined. 
The Michaelis–Menten constant ( Km ) was used as a measure of 
substrate affinity, where a lower Km value indicates a stronger 
binding interaction between the nanozyme and the substrate. 
Consequently, a lower Km value correlates with higher catalytic 
efficiency. As shown in Figure S32 , the Km values of Cu30 with 
respect to H2 O2 and TMB are 220.7 and 3.1 mM, respectively, 
whereas those for Cu32 are 250.3 and 6.7 mM. Notably, the signif- 
icantly lower Km value for Cu30 toward TMB indicates a stronger 
substrate affinity, which likely accounts for its enhanced POD- 
like activity compared to Cu32 . Besides, the catalytic efficiency 
( kcat / Km ) of Cu30 and Cu32 toward TMB was calculated (Table S5 ). 
The results show that the kcat / Km value of Cu30 toward TMB (33.5 
M− 1 s− 1 ) is significantly higher than that of Cu32 (18.1 M− 1 s− 1 ), 
clearly indicating that Cu30 possesses superior overall catalytic 
efficiency. 
The two nanoclusters exhibit significant differences in catalytic 
activity during the HRP-mimicking reaction, which can be 
primarily attributed to their distinct geometric and electronic 
structures. First, from a geometric perspective, Cu30 lacks two 
PPh3 Cu+ units compared to Cu32 , resulting in a more open surface 
architecture that provides greater accessible space for substrate 
adsorption. This structural feature reduces steric hindrance and 
facilitates stronger interactions between the cluster surface and 
the substrate TMB, as illustrated in Figure S33 . The increased 
exposure of copper active sites in Cu30 enhances its ability to 
bind and activate reactant molecules, thereby contributing to 
its superior catalytic performance. Second, the removal of the 
two PPh3 Cu+ units not only alters the overall symmetry of the 
cluster but also perturbs its electronic environment. The change 
in symmetry affects the electronic density distribution and the 
energy levels of the molecular orbitals, which in turn influences 
the redox properties and substrate affinity of the cluster. Such 
electronic modulation is critical in POD-like catalysis, where the 
electron transfer process plays a key role [ 20, 63 ]. 
To gain deeper insight into these effects, we carried out density 
functional theory (DFT) calculations to compare the electronic 
structures of the two nanoclusters and their adsorption behaviors 
with TMB. Based on their crystallographically determined struc- 
tures, both clusters were relaxed to their local energy minima. 
The optimized geometries show key interatomic distances that 
are consistent with experimental values, validating the compu- 
tational models. Notably, to reduce computational cost without 
significantly compromising accuracy, the phenyl groups on the 
Ph3 P ligands are replaced by hydrogen atoms. This approach is 
widely adopted in DFT studies of ligand-protected metal clusters 
and is expected to retain essential electronic and steric features 
relevant to substrate interaction [ 64, 65 ]. 
The analysis of the frontier molecular orbitals provides crucial 
insight into the divergent electronic structures of the Cu32 and 
Cu30 nanoclusters, which underpin their distinct chemical 
behaviors. For the parent Cu32 cluster, the character of its 
highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) is distinctly P- 
like (dumbbell-shaped), while the lower lying HOMO − 1 and 
HOMO − 2 orbitals exhibit P and S-like (spherical) characteristics, 
respectively (Figure 5a ). This indicates that the six free electrons 
in Cu32 adopt a 1S2 1P4 electronic configuration. In contrast, 
the Cu30 cluster, generated by the removal of a PPh3 Cu+ units 
from Cu32 , possesses only four free electrons. Its nominal 
electronic configuration is 1S2 1P2 . The loss of two PPh3 Cu+ units 
induces a pronounced symmetry breaking within the cluster’s 
core, leading to a significant distortion of the 1P orbital. In 
addition, the change in symmetry leads to a redistribution of 
electron density. As illustrated by charge analysis (Figure 5b ), this 
redistribution results in the surface copper atoms of Cu30 carrying 
a lower positive charge compared to those in Cu32 . This effectively 
creates a more electron-rich, nucleophilic surface on Cu30 . This 
electronic environment is highly favorable for interacting with 
and adsorbing neutral organic molecules like TMB. Conversely, 
the copper atoms in Cu32 , which carry a higher positive charge, 
are less effective in these interactions (Figure 5b ). The adsorption 
energy calculations provide definitive, quantitative support for 
this proposal. Results obtained from three different density 
functionals are consistent and conclusive: Cu30 exhibits a 
significantly stronger adsorption affinity for TMB (Table S6 ). 
At the B3LYP-D3/def2-TZVP level of theory, the adsorption 
energy for Cu30 is − 43.62 kJ/mol, which is substantially more 
negative than the − 27.41 kJ/mol calculated for Cu32 . This greater 
exothermicity confirms a stronger and more stable adsorbate–
adsorbent complex. To further validate the electronic structure 
differences revealed by DFT calculations, we performed cyclic 
voltammetry measurements on both clusters (Figure S34 ). 
The Cu30 cluster exhibited a narrower electrochemical gap 
between its primary redox peaks compared to Cu32 . These 
observations provide experimental evidence for its reduced 
electron transfer barrier and superior charge transfer capability, 
correlating well with the calculated more electron-rich surface

3 
Conclusion 
In summary, our investigation of two geometrically analogous 
copper nanoclusters as nanozymes provides the first atomic- 
level insight into the structure–performance relationships of 
iso-nanozymes. The well-defined molecular structures of these 
atomically precise metal nanoclusters allow precise identification 
of reactive sites and their surrounding chemical environments—
a key advantage over conventional nanozyme systems. The 
focal materials of this study, [Cu32 (SC2 H5 )16 (PPh3 )8 Cl9 ]+ and 
[Cu30 (SC2 H5 )16 (PPh3 )6 Cl9 ]+ , are both novel compounds that have 
been comprehensively characterized using a suite of advanced 
techniques. Structurally, both clusters adopt a half-cube con- 
figuration, with the main difference being the absence of two 
PPh3 Cu+ units in Cu30 . Remarkably, this subtle structural mod- 
ification leads to an approximately 6.5-fold enhancement in 
catalytic activity for HRP mimicry. Both geometric and electronic 
factors underlie the divergent catalytic performance. This work 
holds significance in several aspects: (1) It introduces the novel 
concept of “iso-nanozymes” by analogy to natural isoenzymes, 
which is expected to inspire further discoveries in nanozyme 
science. (2) It offers fundamental insights into how nanozyme 
structure—even at the single-molecule level—governs catalytic 
activity, thereby addressing a critical knowledge gap and facili- 
tating the rational design of high-performance nanozymes. (3) It 
demonstrates the promise of atomically precise copper nanoclus- 
ters in nanozyme chemistry. Building on these findings, we antic- 
ipate exploring additional atomically precise nanozymes with 
superior activities and elucidating further structure-performance 
relationships, which remain largely unexplored in the field.

Experimental Procedure 
4.1 
Materials 
Sodium borohydride (NaBH4 , 98%), diethyl disulfide (S2 C4 H10 , 
99%), copper trifluoroacetate (Cu(CF3 CO2 )2 , 99%), triphenylphos- 
phine (PPh3 , 99%), sodium hexafluoroantimonate (NaSbF6 , 
99%), tetraphenylphosphonium chloride (PPh4 Cl, 99%), copper 
borohydride (CuBH4 (PPh3 )2 , 99%), acetonitrile (CH3 CN, A.R.), 
trichloromethane (CHCl3 , A.R.), dichloromethane (CH2 Cl2 , 
A.R.), methanol (CH3 OH, A.R.), H2 O2 (30 wt%), NaAc (A.R.), 
HAc (G.R.), and ether (C4 H10 O, A.R.) were purchased from 
Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). DMF 
(99.8%) and TMB (A.R.) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 
Cuprous oxide (Cu2 O, 97.0%), tetrafluoroboric acid (HBF4 ), 
nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT, 98%), and xanthine (99%) 
were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology 
Co., Ltd. Xanthine oxidase (9.7 U/mg) was purchased from 
Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. All other reagents do 
not require further purification before use. 
4.2 
General synthetic process of 
[Cu30 (SC2 H5 )16 (PPh3 )6 Cl9 ]+

Cu(CF3 CO2 )2 , was dissolved in a mixed solvent consisting of 1 mL 
methanol and 1 mL acetonitrile under gentle stirring to form 
a homogeneous solution. To this solution, a solution of 50 mg 
(0.19 mmol) triphenylphosphine (PPh3 ) in 0.5 mL chloroform

reaction mixture. Subsequently, 25 mg (0.09 mmol) of NaSbF6 , 
dissolved in 0.5 mL methanol, was introduced. Following this, 13.5 
µL (0.1 mmol) of diethyl disulfide S2 C4 H10 in 1 mL acetonitrile 
was added slowly via syringe. The reaction was initiated by 
the rapid addition of 50 mg (1.35 mmol) NaBH4 dissolved in 
0.5 mL methanol in a single portion. Upon this final addition, 
the mixture was aged at room temperature for 3 h, during which 
an orange precipitate gradually formed. After the aging period, 
the solid product was collected and thoroughly dissolved in an 
excess of trichloromethane (chloroform). The resulting solution 
was filtered to remove any insoluble particulates. For the final 
purification, the filtered chloroform solution was subjected to 
diethyl ether vapor diffusion in a sealed vessel. Slow diffusion 
over 7 days yielded yellow block-shaped crystals suitable for XRD 
analysis. The crystalline samples of the Cu30 cluster were stored 
in a refrigerator at 4◦C–8◦C. The final product was obtained 
with a yield of 6% based on the initial amount of copper. All 
chemicals and solvents used in the synthesis were obtained from 
commercial suppliers and used without further purification. 
4.3 
General synthetic process of 
[Cu32 (SC2 H5 )16 (PPh3 )8 Cl9 ]+ 
A total of 32 mg (0.1 mmol) of Cu(MeCN)4 BF4 was dissolved 
in 0.5 mL of methanol under gentle stirring to form a clear 
solution. To this solution, a separately prepared solution of 10 mg 
(0.03 mmol) PPh4 Cl in 0.5 mL methanol was added, resulting 
in a homogeneous mixture. Subsequently, 3.1 µL (0.02 mmol) of 
S2 C4 H10 dissolved in 0.5 mL dichloromethane was introduced 
dropwise. After stirring for 10 min to ensure thorough mixing, 
the reduction was initiated by adding 40 mg (0.07 mmol) of the 
reducing agent CuBH4 (PPh3 )2 in 1 mL dichloromethane. The 
reaction mixture was then stirred continuously at room temper- 
ature for 12 h, during which a color change was observed. After 
the reaction, the mixture was allowed to settle, and the orange- 
colored supernatant was carefully separated and collected. This 
solution was then transferred to a small vial for crystallization 
via diethyl ether vapor diffusion. Over a period of 7 days, well- 
formed orange needle-like crystals grew from the solution. The 
crystalline samples of the Cu32 cluster were stored in a refrigerator 
at 4◦C–8◦C. The final yield was calculated to be 56% based on 
the initial quantity of copper. All chemicals and solvents used 
in the synthesis were obtained from commercial suppliers and 
used without further purification. CCDC 2419661 and CCDC 
2419662 contains the supplementary crystallographic data for 
this paper. These data can be obtained free of charge from The 
Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre via www.ccdc.cam.ac. 
uk/data_request/cif.

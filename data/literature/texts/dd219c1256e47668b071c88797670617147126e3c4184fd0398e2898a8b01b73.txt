A B S T R A C T   
Silver nanomaterials represent one of the most efficient and promising strategies for bacterial disinfection 
considering the growing resistance threats of conventional antibiotics. Ultrasmall silver nanocluster (Ag NC) is 
the most exciting next generation silver based nanomaterial with intriguing physiochemical properties of sub-2 
nm core size and abundant active Ag+ ions, while the study of Ag NC based antibiotics is still in its infancy. The 
current applications of Ag NCs under physiological condition are susceptibly hindered by spontaneous aggre­
gation and silver oxidation, resulting in the loss of antimicrobial efficiency. In this work, a core-shell structured 
Ag NC based nanocomposite was synthesized through the encapsulation of Ag NCs in the phosphatidylcholine 
liposomes (Ag NC@liposome). This liposomal Ag NC was demonstrated as a broad-spectrum antimicrobial agent 
with killing efficiency >99.99 % and wide pH range tolerance of pH = 5‒9. An in-depth investigation shows that 
the core-shell construct of this nanocomposite is essential for controlled release of Ag+ ions and sustainable 
reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. More importantly, this nanocomposite possesses minimum cytotox­
icity on mammalian cells and can be successfully applied for wound disinfection in vivo. To our knowledge, this is 
the first example of Ag NC based liposomal formulation. This sophisticated design would be indicative to the 
research and development of Ag NCs in portable bandages and implantable devices for topical antimicrobial 
delivery.   
1. Introduction 
Metallic silver (Ag) has been utilized as wound dressing since ancient 
times owing to its profound antiseptic effect [1,2]. Nevertheless, the 
direct antimicrobial applications of Ag+ ions and the associated com­
plexes are adverse, which cause considerable cytotoxicity and induce 
tremendous silver-mediated oxidative stress on mammalian cells [3,4]. 
Fortunately, decades of progress in nanoscience offers opportunities in 
the synthesis of nanoscale silver nanomaterials in high precision. The 
resultant silver nanomaterials not only reserve sufficient Ag+ ions for 
bactericidal effect but also reduce cytotoxicity via controlled release of 
Ag+ ions [5,6]. 
Recently, the ultrasmall silver nanoclusters (Ag NCs) with the core 
size less than 2 nm have attracted significant attention as a promising 
next generation silver based antimicrobial agent [7]. Ag NCs are con­
sisted of less than a hundred silver atoms, whose size is comparable to 
the Fermi wavelength of electrons, presenting distinctive physico­
chemical characteristics [8–10], including high local concentration of 
Ag+ ions, appropriately timed silver release kinetics, and tunable 
cellular uptake behaviors [11–14]. All these convincing features endow 
them with exceptional bactericidal performance. However, Ag NCs are 
reactive and unstable under physiological condition. The application of 
Ag NCs is susceptibly hindered by their poor stability due to spontaneous 
aggregation and rapid silver oxidation [15]. To address these problems, 
an emerging strategy to stabilize Ag NCs for effective antimicrobial 
delivery, especially for topical administrations, is to encapsulate Ag NCs 
into a biocompatible carrier to achieve long-term efficiency. Previously, 
we reported a mesoporous silica nanoparticle supported Ag NC as an 
effective antimicrobial agent for long-term and efficient release of Ag+
ions [16], while loading efficiency and biodegradability of the silica 
supported Ag NC was unsatisfied. Hence, the development of alternative 
biodegradable carriers is imperative. 
Liposomes are a spherical vesicle consisted with at least one bilayer 
structure of natural or synthetic amphiphilic lipid molecules. They have 
been extensively exploited as a vehicle for administration of pharma­
ceutical drugs because of desired safety profile, high drug loading ca­
pacity, and readily modifiable formulation properties [17–19].

More importantly, the liposomal coating can prevent Ag NCs from ag­

the enhanced stability of Ag NCs during delivery and treatment. To this 
end, it is hypothesized that liposome is a robust platform to stabilize Ag 
NCs for topical antimicrobial delivery. 
In this work, Ag NCs impregnated in the phosphatidylcholine lipo­
some (referred to as Ag NC@liposome) were prepared by a facile self- 
assembly method, which exhibit improved stability and antibacterial 
activity compared to the counterpart Ag NCs. Furthermore, it was 
demonstrated that this liposomal Ag NC can be easily applied to the 
topical antimicrobial delivery for wound healing. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Preparation of silver nanoclusters (Ag NCs) 
The synthesis of Ag NCs was previously reported with modification 
[16]. Briefly, the solutions of AgNO3 (125 μL, 20 mM) and L-glutathione 
reduced (GSH, 125 μL, 20 mM) were added into ultrapure water (4.75 
mL) at 25 ◦C and reacted under magnetic stirring of 500 rpm to form 
GSH-Ag(I) complex as a white precipitate. Subsequently, NaBH4 solu­
tion (50 μL, 112 mM, in 1 M NaOH) was added dropwise to the solution 
until the dissolving of white precipitates. After vigorous stirring at 25 ◦C 
for 30 min, the reaction mixture was placed in a refrigerator at 4 ◦C for 
12 h to allow slow etching. Consequently, the raw product was purified 
by PD-10 desalting columns (Sephadex® G-25 Medium, GE Healthcare, 
USA). The resultant Ag NC solution was stored in a refrigerator at 4 ◦C 
for further use. 
2.2. Preparation of Ag NCs encapsulated liposome (Ag NC@liposome) 
Phosphatidylcholine (PC) powders (10 mg) and the aqueous solution 
(1 mL) of NaH2PO4 (138 mg) and Na2HPO4 (142 mg) were mixed with 
the as-synthesized Ag NCs (500 μM, 1 mL, on basis of Ag atoms) under 
gentle stirring of 500 rpm at 25 ◦C (Note: the pH of solution was adjusted 
to 6.0), and the reaction was run for 12 h. After completion, the reaction 
mixture was subjected to sonication under 30 W (Vibra-Cell™ 505, 
Sonics & Materials, Inc., USA) for 1 h in an ice bath, and the color of the 
solution changed from cloudy light brown to transparent light brown. To 
form narrowly distributed small unilamellar vesicles, the solution was 
further extruded through a 0.2 μm pore-sized polycarbonate membrane 
(Avanti Polar Lipids, Inc., USA) 10 times. The final product was Ag 
NC@liposome with the yield of 49 % and was stored in a refrigerator at 4 
◦C (Note: the product was stable at 4 ◦C in dark for up to three months). 
The impact of 1 -h sonication in an ice bath on the stability of Ag NCs 
without the liposomes were shown in Fig. S1. 
2.3. Antibacterial activity test 
2.3.1. Bacterial inhibitory growth curve 
Staphylococcus aureus strain ATCC 6538 (S. aureus), Bacillus subtilis 
strain ATCC 6633 (B. subtilis), Escherichia coli strain ATCC 25922 
(E. coli), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa strain ATCC 27853 (P. aeruginosa) 
were used in this test. The fresh bacterial culture (1.8 mL, 1 × 106 CFU 
mL−1, see supplementary information) and Ag NC@liposome solution in 
serial concentrations (0.2 mL, 0.2 mM, 0.5 mM, 1 mM, or 2.5 mM, on 
basis of Ag atoms), or the control groups, Ag NC solution (0.2 mL, 2.5 
mM, on basis of Ag atoms) or ultrapure water (0.2 mL), were added in a 
sterile culture tube. The mixture was incubated at 37 ◦C with constant 
agitation at 200 rpm. Bacterial growth in the culture media was moni­
tored every 2 h by means of a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV- 
1800, Japan) to record the optical density at 600 nm (OD600). 
2.3.2. Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plate counting 
Ag NC@liposome solution (0.2 mL, 2.5 mM, on basis of Ag atoms) or 
the control groups, Ag NC solution (0.2 mL, 10 mM, on basis of Ag 
atoms) or ultrapure water (0.2 mL), was added to the fresh bacterial 
suspension (1.8 mL, 1 × 107 CFU mL−1) in phosphate buffered saline 
(PBS, 0.01 M, pH = 7.4) and was incubated in a sterile reaction tube at 
37 ◦C with constant agitation at 200 rpm for 12 h. After that, serial di­
lutions from the mixture solution (1, 10−2, 10−4, 10−6) were prepared 
and 100 μL of aliquot was plated on LB agar. The bacterial colonies were 
formed and counted after 24 -h static incubation at 37 ◦C. 
2.3.3. Fluorescent live/dead staining of the bacteria 
Ag NC@liposome solution (0.2 mL, 2.5 mM, on basis of Ag atoms) 
was added to the fresh bacterial suspension (1.8 mL, 1 × 107 CFU mL−1) 
in PBS (0.01 M, pH = 7.4) and was incubated in a sterile reaction tube at 
37 ◦C with constant agitation at 200 rpm for 12 h. Thereafter, the su­
pernatant liquid was extracted, and the remainder was fixed with 
glutaraldehyde aqueous solution (2.5 wt%). SYTO® 9 (10 μL, 100 μM) 
and propidium iodide (PI, 10 μL, 100 μM) were added into the mixture, 
and incubated in a shaker at 200 rpm, 37 ◦C for 15 min, followed by 
washing with PBS (0.01 M). Bacteria were imaged by a fluorescence 
microscope (Olympus IX73, Japan). 
2.4. Ag NC@liposome stability test 
To study the long-term stability of Ag NCs in the liposomes, the as- 
prepared Ag NC@liposome solution was placed at 4 ◦C in dark for 15 
days. On day 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 15, the Ag NC@liposome solution (250 
μM) was measured for absorption by using a spectrophotometer (Shi­
madzu UV-1800, Japan). 
To study the pH-dependent stability of Ag NCs in the liposomes, a 
series of buffer solutions (pH = 4, 5, 7, 9, and 10) were prepared by 
adding appropriate NaOH solution (0.1 M) or HCl solution (0.1 M). After 
adding the Ag NC@liposome solution (1 mL, 250 μM) into these buffer 
solutions, the absorption was measured immediately. The time- 
dependent change of Ag NC@liposome UV–vis absorbance at 480 nm 
was plotted. 
Notably, to exclude possible UV–vis absorption from background and 
the cationic liposomes, free liposomes at the same pH value and con­
centration as Ag NC@liposome were measured, whose signal was sub­
tracted from the measured Ag NC@liposome UV–vis absorption spectra. 
2.5. Degradation and silver release of Ag NC@liposome 
Ag NC@liposome (10 mL, 5 mM, on basis of Ag atoms) was injected 
to a dialysis tube (MWCO = 7000, Viskase, USA). The dialysis tubes 
were immersed in PBS (200 mL, 0.01 M, pH = 7.4). The dialysis process 
was conducted under gentle stirring at 37 ◦C mimicking the physiolog­
ical condition. At the selected time point, 10 mL of dialysis solution was 
collected. Then, the dialysis tube was transferred to newly prepared PBS 
(200 mL, 0.01 M) for continuous incubation. The silver content in the 
dialysis solution was analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass 
spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7700 ICP-MS, USA). This dialysis process 
was run for up to 7 days and repeated in triplicate.

2.7. Analysis of intracellular ROS 
Solutions of 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, 10 μL, 10 
μM) and bisBenzimide H 33342 trihydrochloride (Hoechst 33342, 10 μL, 
1 μg mL−1) were added to the Ag NC@liposome treated bacterial sus­

with constant agitation at 200 rpm. Successively, the bacterial cells were 
isolated in a centrifuge at 10,000 rpm and 4 ◦C for 2 min and rinsed with 
PBS (0.01 M) several times. The cell pellet was resuspended in an equal 
volume of PBS (0.01 M). The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of DCF 
at excitation/emission wavelength of 488/525 nm, as a reflection of the 
cellular ROS level, and the mean fluorescence intensity of Hoechst 
33342 at excitation/emission wavelength of 350/461 nm, as a reflection 
of the total bacterial cell number, were measured by a microplate reader 
(Tecan Spark®, Austria), respectively. The relative ROS level was 
calculated by normalizing the ratio of MFI525nm/MFI461nm of the treated 
samples with those of the untreated samples. 
2.8. Evaluation of silver internalization by Bacteria 
Ag NC@liposome solution (10 mL, 250 μM, on basis of Ag atoms) 
was mixed with the fresh E. coli suspension (10 mL, 1 × 108 CFU mL−1) 
in PBS (0.01 M) and was incubated in a sterile reaction tube at 37 ◦C 
with constant agitation at 200 rpm for up to 12 h. At the selected time 
point, the bacterial cells were isolated in a centrifuge at 10,000 rpm and 
4 ◦C for 2 min, incubated with 0.5 wt% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid 
(EDTA) in PBS (0.01 M) at 4 ◦C for 10 min, and rinsed with PBS (0.01 M) 
for five times. The cell pellets were resuspended in sterile ultrapure 
water (9 mL) containing lysozyme (10 mg mL−1) and ascorbic acid (5 mg 
mL−1), and were incubated at 25 ◦C and 200 rpm for 2 h. The mixture 
solution was subjected to ultrasonic treatment for 1 h at 25 ◦C. Then, 
aqua regia (1 mL) was added into the resultant solution and was incu­
bated for 2 h. Consequently, the internalized silver content in E. coli was 
analyzed by ICP-MS (Agilent 7700 ICP-MS, USA). E. coli without Ag 
NC@liposome treatment was used as the control group.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Synthesis and characterization of Ag NCs encapsulated liposome 
The ultrasmall GSH-protected Ag+ rich nanoclusters (Ag NCs) were 
synthesized through a cyclic NaBH4 reduction-decomposition and size- 
focusing 
process 
[16]. 
The 
tripeptide 
GSH 
is 
a 
reducing-cum-protecting ligand. Ag NCs were formed by self-assembly 
of GSH monolayer on the Ag core, due to the high affinity between Ag 
species and the thiol groups of GSH. To embed Ag NCs in the liposomes, 
the pH of the reaction mixture was fine-tuned to 6.0 to stabilize the 
cationic PC (pKa = 0.8) as well as the carboxylates of GSH layer (pKa1 =
2.12 and pKa2 = 3.53) [28,29]. The electrostatic interaction between 
positively charged ammonium groups of PC and negatively charged 
carboxyl groups of Ag NCs promotes the formation of Ag NC-PC com­
plexes. As a consequence of sonication and extrusion [30], Ag NCs were 
ubiquitously encapsulated in the nanosized liposomes (Fig. 1a). 
The electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) manifests 
that the as-prepared Ag NCs have two distinctive peaks at m/z 1538 and 
1650 (Fig. 1b), assigned to [Ag16(GSH)9]3– and [Ag16(GSH)5]2–. The 
morphology of Ag NCs and liposomal Ag NCs were characterized by 
transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Fig. 1c depicts the formation 
of ultrasmall nanoclusters with the particle size 1.66 ± 0.83 nm (Fig. 1c, 
inset). Fig. 1d presents an omelet shape of Ag NC@liposome, whose 
diameter is 141 ± 53 nm (Fig. 1d, inset). The dynamic light scattering 
(DLS) determines that the hydrodynamic diameter of Ag NC@liposome 
is 155 ± 52 nm (Fig. 1e), which is about 1.1-fold larger than the TEM 
measurement, probably due to the dehydration during TEM sample 
preparation. The high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) and energy- 
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) in scanning transmission electron 
microscopy (STEM) mapping images of Ag NC@liposome reveals that 
the multiple Ag NCs were encapsulated within the individual liposome 
as a core-shell structure (Fig. 1f‒h). Both Ag NCs and Ag NC@lipsome 
appear a light brown color (Fig. 1i, inset, Photo 1 and 2), where the 
maximum absorption peaks are at 480 nm in the UV–vis spectra (Fig. 1i). 
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was exploited to identify sur­
face elemental chemical states of Ag NC@liposome. The full-scale XPS 
spectrum (Fig. S2) indicates the presence of O, N, Ag, and C elements on 
the surface of Ag NC@liposome. The Ag 3d spectrum (Fig. 1j) was 
deconvoluted to Ag(I) (367.7 eV and 373.7 eV, pink curves) and Ag(0) 
(367.2 eV and 373.3 eV, blue curves), and the Ag(I) : Ag(0) atomic ratio 
is 43 : 57. The C 1s spectrum (Fig. 1h) was deconvoluted into three sub-

3.2. Release kinetics and pH-dependent stability 
The release kinetics of Ag NC@liposome was investigated. The 
accumulative released Ag+ ions were quantified by ICP-MS for up to 7 
days in PBS at 37 ◦C mimicking the physiological condition. It was found 
that less than 80 % Ag+ ions in Ag NC@liposome were released after 7 
days (Fig. 2a, red curve). In comparison, over 90 % Ag+ ions in Ag NC 
were released after 3 days (Fig. 2a, black curve). To gain a quantitative 
understanding of how this architecture affected the Ag+ ion release, the 
Ag NC@liposome and Ag NC release profiles were analyzed by the 
mathematical models established in previous liposome-mediated drug 
release studies [31]. A diffusion-dominant Higuchi model was employed 
to analyze the release profiles: Mt = Kt1/2, where Mt is drug release at 
time t and K is the Higuchi constant [32,33]. The Ag+ ion release per­
centage was plotted against the square root of time, which yielded linear 
fittings with R2 = 0.98 and 0.95 for Ag NC@liposome and Ag NC, 
respectively (Fig. 2b), implying a diffusion-controlled Ag+ ion release 
mechanism. On this basis, the Higuchi constants of Ag NC@liposome 
and Ag NC were determined to be 33.5 ± 1.8 and 42.6 ± 3.6 day−1/2, 
respectively. Moreover, the long-term stability of Ag NC@liposome was 
explored. Fig. S3 confirms that Ag NC@liposome was stable for at least 
15 days. 
Next, the pH-dependent stability of Ag NC@liposome was examined. 
In Fig. 2c and S4, no apparent decrease in absorbance at 480 nm was 
detected, suggesting that Ag NC@liposome was stable at pH = 5‒9. 
Otherwise, a distinguishable decline in absorbance at 480 nm occurred 
at pH = 4 and 10, on account of the rapid decomposition of Ag NCs in 
liposomes. Concomitantly, it was observed that Ag NC@liposome

3.4. Antibacterial mechanisms 
Oxidative stress is an essential antibacterial factor of many silver 
based materials [35]. Particularly, oxidative stress originated from the 
reactive oxygen species (ROS) production can destroy the cellular 
structures or components and cause the disruption of microbial 
biochemical processes [36,37]. To elucidate the exact mechanism of 
ROS production induced by Ag NC@liposome, the electron spin reso­
nance (ESR) spectroscopy was employed. Fig. 5a shows that hydroxyl 
radical (
•OH), superoxide anion radical (

−), and singlet oxygen (1O2) 
were generated by Ag NC@liposome. Furthermore, to clarify which 
species are predominant in the antibacterial process, a ROS scavenger 
quenching assay was carried out that isopropanol (IPA), 2,2,6,6-tetra­

quench 
•OH, 
•O2

−is the primary anti­

•OH and 1O2. 
More importantly, the time-dependent antibacterial activity and ROS 
production were scrutinized in order to unveil the underlying relation­
ship between ROS and antibacterial activity of Ag NC@liposome. It was 
recognized that the viable bacterial counts declined with the increasing 
incubation period (Fig. 5c). Meanwhile, the intracellular ROS level of Ag 
NC@liposome raised gradually with the increasing incubation period 
(Fig. 5d). In contrast, the intracellular ROS level of Ag NC grew quickly 
and reached the apex at 4 h, but diminished immediately thereafter. 
Remarkably, the unique liposome nanostructure is beneficial for the 
controlled release of Ag+ ions and sustainable ROS production. There­
fore, the elevated level of ROS production is closely associated with 
enhanced antibacterial activity of Ag NC@liposome and the correlation 
coefficient (r) is -0.98. 
To inspect the possible interaction between Ag NC@liposome and 
bacteria, the morphological changes of bacteria before and after treat­
ment with Ag NC@liposome were compared. Apart from the intact 
membrane shape of these fresh cultured bacteria (Fig. 6a), it was 
discovered that a large portion of bacteria became deformity after 
treatment with Ag NC@liposome (Fig. 6b). Moreover, the percentage of 
silver internalization in bacteria during the incubation was analyzed by 
ICP-MS. Fig. 6c displays that the silver internalization rised with the 
increasing incubation period. Up to 3% of Ag species from Ag NC@li­
posome were internalized by bacteria, which might disrupt miscellenous 
biochemical process inside the bacterial cells [39]. 
Altogether, the enhanced antibacterial activity is principally attrib­
uted to the protection of ultrasmall Ag NCs in liposomes, which avoids 
the rapid decomposition of Ag NCs during delivery and treatment, and 
concurrently the core-shell nanoconstruct allows a controlled release of 
Ag+ ions from liposomes for a sustainable efficiency.

3.6. Biosafety evaluation 
The biocompatibility of Ag NC@liposome was tested using human 
umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). The cell viability was 
determined using a CCK-8 in vitro. As shown in Fig. 7d, no evident in­
fluence on cell viability was discerned with 250 μM Ag NC@liposome 
within 48 -h incubation, comparable to that of the control group. In 
contrast, Ag NC and AgNO3 containing the equivalent silver concen­
tration presented extraordinarily higher cytotoxicity, where the viability 
of cells after 48 -h treatment with Ag NC and AgNO3 was reduced to 26 
% and 3%. All these data illustrate that the biocompatible liposome 
protection strategy could effectively reduce the cytotoxicity of Ag NC.

Abstract
Neurodegenerative diseases associated with gut dysbiosis are an important health concern, particularly reactive oxygen 
species (ROS) generation playing a critical role. However, therapeutic methods are hindered by the inadequate absorption 
of plant secondary metabolites into the brain. In our study, rutin trihydrate (RTH), a flavonoid, was used to synthesise 
rutin trihydrate-conjugated zinc oxide nanoparticles (RTH-ZnO) for addressing gut dysbiosis-generated ROS and its 
implications on neurodegeneration. We tested its antimicrobial properties against gut dysbiosis-associated pathogens 
and antioxidant activity against oxidative stress-induced zebrafish larvae. Our in vitro results indicated that the synthe-
sised RTH-ZnO can efficiently scavenge free radicals and maintain redox homeostasis. The zebrafish developmental 
toxicity analysis does not reveal any lethal malformation in the RTH-ZnO-treated group with an increased survival rate. 
RTH-ZnO potentially regulated the intercellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and stress-induced apoptosis in the 
larvae exposed to ­H2O2. Notably, cognitive functions were also improved with increased acetylcholinesterase (AChE) 
and antioxidant gene expression. In conclusion, our study supports the potential of RTH-ZnO nanoparticles to treat gut 
dysbiosis-generated ROS-induced neurodegeneration.

1  Introduction
Neurodegenerative disorders are a major global health 
concern. It is characterised by progressive neurodegenera-
tion, which causes a variety of cognitive and motor deficits. 
Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and amyotrophic 
lateral sclerosis (ALS) are three of the common neurode-
generative diseases. Despite several research, many of these 
diseases remain poorly understood, and the treatment strate-
gies for reversing their course are difficult to discover [1]. 
The upregulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the 
brain is one of the markers for neurodegenerative diseases 
[2]. ROS are natural by-products of cellular metabolism, 
which include superoxide and hydroxyl radicals. When they 
are upregulated, they become highly reactive and damage 
lipids, proteins, and DNA [3]. This oxidative stress plays 
an important part in the progression of neurodegenerative 
disorders, also contributing to neuronal death and neuronal 
dysfunction. Notably, a link has been identified between the 
gut microbiome and the upregulation of oxidative stress, 
which is suspected to play a role in the development of neu-
rodegenerative disorders [4]. Gut dysbiosis is an abnormal 
change of microbial communities present in the gastroin-
testinal tract, which in turn disrupts the balance of redox 
homeostasis. This disturbance causes an increase in ROS 
production, escalating oxidative stress levels throughout the 
system [5]. The consequences of this dysregulated redox 
environment stretch far beyond the gut by affecting neuro-
physiology. Antioxidant-based treatments targeted at lower-
ing ROS levels have been investigated in the fight against 
neurodegenerative disorders. Endogenous (e.g. superoxide 
dismutase, catalase) and exogenous (e.g. vitamins C and

E) antioxidants are important defence mechanisms against 
oxidative stress [6]. However, their therapeutic applica-
tion has been fraught with difficulties, including restricted 
blood-brain barrier permeability and inadequate targeting 
of afflicted brain areas.
Recent breakthroughs in nanotechnology offer promis-
ing solutions as they have unique physicochemical features 
and customisable surface changes. Nanoparticles provide the 
potential to improve medicinal component delivery, stabil-
ity, and bioavailability [7]. Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO 
NPs) have gotten a lot of interest because of their multi-
farious features, such as high biocompatibility, low toxicity, 
and potent antioxidant activity [8]. Even though nano has 
high potential, when coupled with natural compounds, its 
therapeutic efficiency can be increased. Rutin trihydrate, a 
naturally occurring flavonoid found in fruits and vegetables, 
has strong antioxidant characteristics and is reported to have 
neuroprotective benefits in various studies [9]. However, 
insufficient absorption and inefficient transport to the brain 
was a serious limitation. Conjugation of rutin trihydrate 
with ZnO NPs has enormous potential for overcoming such 
obstacles. In addition, in the zebrafish (Danio rerio) model, 
it will be easy to analyse the efficacy and safety of this novel 
neuroprotective drug. Its large neural system makes them 
as an ideal model for investigating human neurodegenera-
tive diseases. During the early stages of development, their 
optical transparency helps to perform real-time imaging 
and assessment of therapeutic actions [10]. Our goal is to 
evaluate the potential of rutin trihydrate-conjugated ZnO 
nanoparticles as an effective drug for the treatment of gut 
microbiome dysfunction and neurodegenerative diseases.
2  Materials and Methods
2.1  Reagents
Rutin trihydrate (RTH) was purchased from SRL, RTH 
stock solution (1 mM) was prepared in 1×PBS (pH 7.4), 
hydrogen peroxide ­(H2O2) was purchased from Thermo 
Fisher Scientific, and zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2 
was purchased from SRL. 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl 
(DPPH), potassium persulfate, Acridine orange (AO, purity 
99%), dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFD), and 2,2′-azino-
bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS salt) were 
purchased from Sisco Research Laboratories (SRL).
2.2  Fabrication of RTH‑ZnO Nanoparticle
Rutin trihydrate-conjugated zinc oxide (RTH-ZnO) nano-
particles were fabricated with minor modifications in the 
previously reported method [11]. Briefly, 200 mM zinc 
nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O) was dissolved with 
0.6 mM of RTH under constant stirring for 60 min using 
a magnetic stirrer. After the zinc nitrate hexahydrate and 
RTH were completely dissolved, a 10% sodium hydroxide 
(NaOH) was prepared and slowly added dropwise without 
disturbing the container. The process was permitted to con-
tinue for another 60 min after the addition of aqueous NaOH 
solution, and the container was sealed. Then, the precipitate 
was removed by centrifuging at 6500 rpm for 10 min with 
a temperature of 5 ℃. This process will be repeated until 
no precipitate is formed. The precipitates were transferred 
to a sterile Petri plate and placed in a hot air oven at 60 ℃ 
for drying. The dried samples were stored at 4 ℃. These 
conjugated RTH-ZnO nanoparticles were redispersed for 
characterisation studies.
2.3  Characterisation of RTH‑ZnO Nanoparticles
The synthesised RTH-ZnO nanoparticles were analysed 
using a D8-advance X-ray diffractometer (BRUKER, USA) 
to analyse its X-ray diffraction (XRD) and crystallographic 
data. The presence of functional groups in the RTH-ZnO 
nanoparticles was analysed using ALPHA II Fourier trans-
form infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (BRUKER, USA) from 
400 to 4000 ­cm−1. The high-resolution images of the surface 
morphology and the structure and texture of the RTH-ZnO 
were analysed using the Apreo-S SEM (Thermo Fisher Sci-
entific). The ImageJ software was employed to analyse the 
individual size distribution of the nanoparticles [12].
2.4  In Vitro Antioxidant Analysis of RTH‑ZnO 
Nanoparticles
To assess the in vitro antioxidant properties of RTH-ZnO 
nanoparticles, the stock solutions of 1 mM Trolox and 1 mg/
ml of RTH-ZnO nanoparticles were prepared. From the 
stock solution, the working solutions of RTH-ZnO nano-
particles were prepared as 10, 20, 40, 80, and 160 µg/ml.
2.4.1  DPPH Assay
The DPPH assay was performed as previously described 
with a few modifications. A 300 µM DPPH solution was 
mixed with Trolox and RTH-ZnO nanoparticles (10 to 
160 µg/ml) in an ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorb-
ent assay) plate. The plates were incubated in the dark for 
30 min. Then, the samples were quantified at 517 nm using 
a microplate (96-well plate) reader (Thermo Fisher Scien-
tific) [13].
2.4.2  ABTS Assay
The ABTS assay was performed as previously described 
with a few modifications. The ABTS solution was made by

dissolving the 7 mM ABTS salt in 2.45 mM potassium per-
sulfate. Then, it was diluted with 0.2 M phosphate buffer 
saline (PBS) until it achieved 0.7 absorbance at 734 nm. 
Then, it was mixed with Trolox and RTH-ZnO nanopar-

for 60 min in the dark at room temperature. After 60 min, 
the samples were quantified at 734 nm using a microplate 
(96-well plate) reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the 
percentage of inhibition was calculated [14].
2.4.3  H2O2 Scavenging Assay
The hydrogen peroxide scavenging assay was performed 
as previously described with a few modifications [15]. The 
reaction mixture was prepared by adding 40 mM hydrogen 
peroxide in 50 mM phosphate buffer. Then, the reaction mix-
ture was mixed with Trolox and RTH-ZnO nanoparticles 
(10 to 160 µg/ml) and incubated in the dark for 10 min. The 
percentage of inhibition was calculated after calculating the 
absorbance at 230 nm.
2.4.4  Superoxide (O2•–) Radical Scavenging Assay
The O2•– radical scavenging assay was performed as previ-
ously described with a few modifications [16]. The reaction 
mixture was prepared by adding 50 µM nitroblue tetrazolium 
(NBT), 15 µM phenazine methosulfate, 73 µM nicotinamide 
adenine dinucleotide, and 20 mM phosphate buffer. Then, 
the reaction mixture was mixed with Trolox and RTH-ZnO 
nanoparticles (10 to 160 µg/ml) and incubated in the dark for 
5 min. After the end of the incubation process, the absorb-
ance was measured at 562 nm and the percentage of inhibi-
tion was calculated.
2.5  Nitric Oxide (NO) Scavenging Assay
The NO scavenging potential of the RTH-ZnO nanoparti-
cles was estimated by using the Griess method with a few 
modifications [17]. The reaction mixture was prepared by 
making 10 mM sodium nitroprusside using 1X phosphate 
buffer saline. Then, the Griess reagent was added followed 
by Trolox and RTH-ZnO nanoparticles (10 to 160 µg/ml) 
and incubated for 25 min. The inhibitory percentage of nitric 
oxide was measured at 546 nm.
2.6  In Vitro RTH‑ZnO Release
The RTH-ZnO nanoparticles concentration of 80 µg/ml was 
taken in a dialysis membrane and suspended in Hydrochlo-
ric acid (pH-1.2) and Phosphate buffered saline (pH-6.8) 
to analyse RTH-ZnO’s association with oral intake. The 
membrane was sunk in a suspension medium and main-
tained at 100 rpm. The solution was collected every 24 h 
and measured using a UV-visible spectrometer at 360 nm 
after being filtered using a 0.2 μm membrane syringe [18].
2.7  Anti‑bacterial Activity of RTH‑ZnO 
Nanoparticles
The anti-bacterial activity of RTH (rutin trihydrate), ZnO 
(zinc oxide) nanoparticles, and RTH-ZnO nanoparticles was 
determined by using the agar well diffusion method [19]. 
The gram-negative (E. coli, Citrobacter spp.) and gram-
positive (Streptococcus spp., Staphylococcus spp.) bacte-
ria were obtained from the Department of Microbiology, 
Saveetha Medical College, Saveetha Institute of Medical and 
Technical Sciences, Thandalam, Chennai. The bacteria were 
inoculated in the nutrient broth and incubated at 37 ℃ for 
24 h. After incubation, 50 µl of bacteria were added to the 
sterile Petri plates followed by molten nutrient agar media. 
Then, the wells were made with cork borer and 80 µg/ml of 
RTH, ZnO, and RTH-ZnO was added. The zone of inhibition 
was measured after 24 h of incubation.
2.8  Zebrafish Maintenance and Treatment
Both male and female adult zebrafish were acquired from 
the Tarun Fish Farm, Manimangalam, Chennai. The fish are 
maintained by following the rules and regulations suggested 
by the ethical committee of the institution (SU/CLAR/
RD/001/2023). They are kept in a 3-l tank at a tempera-
ture of around 28 ℃ and maintained in a light/dark cycle of 
14/10 h. They were fed with live brine shrimps and allowed 
to acclimatise for 2 weeks. The breeding groups were kept 
in a 2:1 ratio (male to female) and the eggs were collected 
after spawning. The embryos were segregated by micro-
scopic observation and the fertilised embryos were kept in 
an embryo medium for further studies.
2.9  Developmental Toxicity Assessment
To determine the developmental toxicity induced by oxida-
tive stress and the protective effect of RTH-ZnO nanopar-
ticles, 3 h post-fertilisation (hpf) embryos were used. The 
groups (n = 30/group) were divided into RTH (rutin trihy-
drate), ZnO (zinc oxide) nanoparticles, and RTH-ZnO nano-
particles. The control groups were maintained in embryo 
media and 1 mM hydrogen peroxide ­(H2O2) was used as a 
stress-inducing agent [20].
2.10  Evaluation of In Vivo ROS‑Reducing Capacity 
of RTH‑ZnO
The ROS-reducing capacity of RTH, ZnO nanoparticles, and 
RTH-ZnO nanoparticles was analysed in the zebrafish larvae 
(96 hpf) by using 2′-7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate

(DCFDA) staining. The stress-induced zebrafish larvae were 
treated with RTH, ZnO nanoparticles, and RTH-ZnO nano-
particles and anaesthetised using tricaine. Then, they were 
stained with the fluorescent dye and incubated for 60 min. 
The pictographs were recorded with the help of a fluorescent

2.11  Evaluation of Oxidative Stress‑Induced 
Apoptosis
The oxidative stress-induced apoptosis was analysed in 
the zebrafish larvae (96 hpf) by using acridine orange 
(AO) staining. The ­H2O2 stress-induced zebrafish larvae 
were treated with RTH, ZnO nanoparticles, and RTH-ZnO 
nanoparticles. After the treatment, they were anaesthetised 
using tricaine and stained with the fluorescent dye AO. 
After 30 min of incubation in the dark, the pictographs 
were recorded with the help of a fluorescent microscope 
(CKX53 Microscope, Japan) and analysed with ImageJ 
software [22].
2.12  Zebrafish Locomotory Analysis
The alterations in the locomotory behaviour of the lar-
vae were checked using a method suggested by Krishnan 
et al. (2019) with a few changes [23]. Briefly, the larvae 
(n = 3/well) for each group were placed in a white ice cube 
tray with dimensions 2.5 × 3.5 cm. After acclimatisation 
(10 min), the larvae movement was captured in a noise-free 
environment and the video was analysed by using the UMA-
Tracker software.
2.13  Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) Level Estimation
The zebrafish larvae were exposed to ­H2O2 to induce neu-
rotoxicity and they were treated with RTH, ZnO nanoparti-
cles, and RTH-ZnO nanoparticles. The larvae were anaes-
thetised with tricaine and homogenised using phosphate 
buffer (0.1 M). Then, it was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 
15 min. The supernatant was collected and mixed with the 
reaction mixture containing 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic 
acid) (DTNB) (3.3 mM). After 20 min of incubation, the 
absorbance shift was measured following the addition of 
acetylcholine iodide in a 1-min interval at 412 nm [24].
2.14  Real‑Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‑PCR) 
Analysis
The zebrafish larvae were homogenised and the RNA was 
extracted by using the RDP Trio™ Reagent (HiMedia). 
The primers for zebrafish superoxide dismutase (SOD), 
catalase (CAT), glutathione reductase (GSR), glutathione 
reductase (GPx), and beta-actin were designed using the 
NCBI’s Primer-BLAST. The nucleic acid sequence of 
the primers is mentioned in Table 1. AURA 2x One-Step 
RT-PCR Master Mix (AURA Biotechnologies Pvt Ltd., 
Ayanambakkam, Chennai) was used to analyse the expres-
sion of the genes. The one-step RT-PCR programming 
involves a series of steps to amplify the RNA sequences. 
The reverse transcription process begins with a single 
cycle lasting 15 min at temperatures ranging from 44 to 
50 ℃, followed by enzyme activation, which is performed 
once at a temperature of 95 ℃ for 3 min. Then, 40 cycles 
of denaturation for 10 s at 95 ℃, annealing for 45 s at 60 
℃, and finally extension happening at 72 ℃ for 15 s were 
performed [25].
2.15  Statistics
All the data represented in this investigation were provided 
as mean ± standard deviation. The data were analysed in 
GraphPad Prism 5.0 using the one-way analysis of vari-
ance (ANOVA) and Tukey multiple range test. The data 
were considered significant and marked by the symbol “*” 
when p < 0.05.

3  Results
3.1   Characterisation of RTH‑ZnO Nanoparticles
Characterisation techniques play a crucial role in nano-
technology, which provides insights into the structural, 
chemical, and morphological attributes of the synthe-
sised nanoparticles. In this study, we employed fun-
damental techniques like X-ray diffraction (XRD), 
Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to investigate 
the characteristics of the RTH-ZnO nanoparticles. 
The results from the XRD analysis showed peaks at 
angles 29.470°, 31.975°, 34.594°, 36.359°, 42.393°, 
43.475°, 47.403°, 56.626°, 62.888°, 67.955°, and 
69.207° which correspond to the planes (101), (110), 
(200), (111), (210), (211), (220), (311), (222), (321), 
and (400), respectively (Fig. 1). The FTIR analysis 
revealed absorption peaks at 575.9 ­cm−1 (M-O stretch), 
863.3 ­cm−1 (C-H bending), 1383.1 ­cm−1 (C-H bending), 
1727.7 ­cm−1 (C-O stretch), 2713.9 ­cm−1 (C-H stretch), 
2952.8  ­cm−1 (C-H stretch), and 3437.8  ­cm−1 (O-H 
stretch) (Fig. 2). Further, the morphology of the RTH-
ZnO nanoparticles observed under the SEM at 1.5 × ­105 
× magnification exhibits a near-spherical shape with 
a tendency for agglomeration (Fig. 3). Also, by using 
the ImageJ software, the average area and length of the 
nanoparticles were observed as 72.656 and 38.313 nm. 
Some particles exhibited this range. This may be due to 
the agglomeration of RTH-ZnO nanoparticles.
3.2  In Vitro Antioxidant Analysis of RTH‑ZnO 
Nanoparticles
3.2.1  DPPH Scavenging Assay
The DPPH scavenging assay was employed to evaluate the 
antioxidant potential of the RTH-ZnO nanoparticles. The 
violet-coloured DPPH solution becomes colourless when 
reduced by an antioxidant. Trolox was used as the positive 
control, which exhibited 85% of inhibition. Notably, RTH-
ZnO nanoparticles demonstrated a concentration-dependent 
antioxidant activity. Specifically, at a concentration of 80 µg/
ml, RTH-ZnO nanoparticles significantly reduced the DPPH 
radical by 69% (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4a), exhibiting substantial 
antioxidant efficacy.

3.2.2  ABTS + Cation Scavenging Assay
The ABTS assay involves measuring the reduction of the 
blue-green chromophore ABTS + by an antioxidant. Here, 
RTH-ZnO nanoparticles demonstrated concentration-
dependent ABTS radical scavenging capability, where the 
inhibition was observed as 22% at 10 µg/ml, 33% at 20 µg/
ml, 43% at 40 µg/ml, 56% at 80 µg/ml, and 44% at 160 µg/
ml when compared to the positive control Trolox with 76% 
inhibition (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4b).
3.2.3  Hydrogen Peroxide Scavenging Assay
Hydrogen peroxide can give rise to highly reactive hydroxyl 
radicals in a process known as the Fenton reaction, which 
can cause oxidative damage to various cellular components. 
The results indicate that RTH-ZnO nanoparticles have a 
strong hydrogen peroxide scavenging property at all con-
centrations. The highest percentage of inhibition (63%) was 
observed at the 80 µg/ml concentration, which was signifi-
cant when compared with Trolox (82%) (Fig. 4c).
3.2.4  Superoxide Scavenging Assay
Despite being a weak oxidant, the superoxide anion can cre-
ate very strong hydroxyl radicals and singlet oxygen, causing 
oxidative stress. The superoxide scavenging assay was per-
formed to evaluate the antioxidant potential of the RTH-ZnO 
nanoparticles, with Trolox serving as the positive control. 
The superoxide radical scavenging activity of RTH-ZnO 
nanoparticles was observed to be concentration depend-
ent, where 80 µg/ml of RTH-ZnO nanoparticles showed a 
substantial (p < 0.05) percentage of inhibition (52%) when 
compared with positive control (Fig. 4d).
3.3  NO Scavenging Assay
Nitric oxide can react with various molecules in the body 
and give rise to reactive nitrogen species. It also causes neu-
rotoxicity and is involved in various neurodegenerative dis-
eases. The NO scavenging assay was performed to evaluate 
the antioxidant properties of the RTH-ZnO nanoparticles 
at various concentrations (Fig. 5). Among them, 80 µg/ml 
showed a notable percentage of inhibition of about (68%) 
with a statistical significance of p < 0.05 when compared 
with the Trolox (88%).

3.5  Anti‑bacterial Activity of RTH‑ZnO 
Nanoparticles
The anti-bacterial properties of RTH, ZnO nanoparticles, 
and RTH-ZnO nanoparticles were analysed against both 
gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria to evaluate their 
ability to treat gut dysbiosis. The zone of inhibition was 
measured in centimetres and the results are compiled in 
Table 2. For Staphylococcus spp., RTH-ZnO nanoparticles 
exhibited a zone of inhibition of 3.2 cm, surpassing the 
inhibitory effect of both RTH and ZnO nanoparticles, with 
values of 2.8 cm and 3 cm, respectively. Similarly, against 
Streptococcus spp., RTH-ZnO nanoparticles produced a 
zone of 3.5 cm, surpassing RTH (3 cm) and closely match-
ing antibiotics (3.8 cm). For E. coli, RTH-ZnO nanoparticles 
displayed a zone of inhibition of 2 cm, surpassing ZnO nan-
oparticles (1.5 cm) and outperforming RTH, which shows no 
zone of inhibition. In the case of Citrobacter spp., RTH-ZnO 
nanoparticles displayed a zone of 2.9 cm, closely matching 
the antibiotic’s effect at 3.2 cm (Fig. 7).
3.6  Developmental Toxicity Assessment
To attenuate oxidative stress-induced neurotoxicity, the 
antioxidant ability of RTH-ZnO nanoparticles was exam-
ined using zebrafish embryos. The developmental toxicity 
assay results display normal morphological architecture in 
the RTH, ZnO nanoparticles, and RTH-ZnO nanoparticle-
treated groups (0–72 hpf). However, the larvae in the hydro-
gen peroxide group were observed with malformations like 
bend spine and yolk sac edema (Fig. 8a, b, c). The mortal-
ity rates, observed at 96 h post-fertilisation (hpf) revealed 
that the number of surviving larvae in the RTH, ZnO nano-
particles, and RTH-ZnO nanoparticles groups were 18 ± 1, 
19 ± 2, and 25 ± 1, respectively. In contrast, the hydrogen 
peroxide-treated group recorded a lower survival rate at 96 
hpf (6 ± 1) (Fig. 8d).
3.7  Evaluation of ROS‑Mitigating Capacity 
of RTH‑ZnO
To determine the in vivo ROS-reducing capacity of RTH-
ZnO, the DCFDA staining was performed in the stress-
induced zebrafish larvae (96 hpf) (Fig. 9a). The larvae 
treated with hydrogen peroxide showed increased fluorescent 
intensity of about 89%. Notably, the intensity was reduced 
to 23.7%, 21.3%, and 9.6% in the RTH, ZnO nanoparticles, 
and RTH-ZnO nanoparticles treatment groups, respectively 
(Fig. 9b). This highlights that RTH-ZnO nanoparticle treat-
ment can effectively reduce the ROS in the oxidative stress-
induced zebrafish larvae.

3.8   Evaluation of Oxidative Stress‑Induced 
Apoptosis
To assess the neuroprotective property of RTH-ZnO nano-
particles, the stress-induced zebrafish larvae (96 hpf) were 
subjected to AO staining (Fig. 9c). The fluorescent intensity 
of AO serves as the measure of stress-induced cell death in 
the zebrafish larvae. The results highlight that the control 
group has less intensity with more intact cells, while the 
hydrogen peroxide-treated group displayed a significantly 
(p < 0.05) higher intensity of about 92%. Notably, the inten-
sity was reduced to 17.6%, 10.6%, and 4.2% in the RTH, 
ZnO nanoparticles, and RTH-ZnO nanoparticle treatment 
groups, respectively (Fig. 9d). These findings reflect RTH-
ZnO’s neuroprotective efficacy in preventing stress-induced 
cell death in this in vivo experimental model.
3.9   Zebrafish Larvae Locomotory Analysis
The zebrafish larvae locomotory analysis is a sensi-
tive method for measuring the consequences of oxidative 
stress on locomotory activity. The oxidative stress-induced 
zebrafish larvae were treated with RTH, ZnO nanoparticles, 
and RTH-ZnO nanoparticles and their movements were 
analysed. The cognitive function was measured by using 
the distance travelled by the larvae (meters). The hydro-
gen peroxide group showed reduced movement (4.5 ± 1.5) 
with an abnormal pattern (Fig. 10a). Among the treatment 
groups, the distance travelled by the larvae was recorded 
to be 25.2 ± 6.5 for RTH, 29.9 ± 2.5 for ZnO nanoparticles, 
and 47.9 ± 4.7 for RTH-ZnO nanoparticles (Fig. 10b). These 
results suggest that RTH-ZnO has the potential to alleviate 
stress-induced cognitive impairment and restore locomotory 
patterns to a more normal state.

The expression of antioxidant genes in the oxidative stress-

is a large class of bioactive compounds reported with anti-
oxidant properties. Rutin trihydrate, a flavonoid known for 
its antioxidant properties, faces a significant limitation due 
to its inadequate absorption and inefficient transport to the 
brain. Nanotechnology involves the use of materials at the 
nanoscale, typically ranging from 1 to 100 nanometres. 
Drugs can be encapsulated in nanoparticles, improving 
their solubility, stability, and targeted delivery to specific 
cells or tissues. In our study, the rutin trihydrate-conjugated 
zinc oxide nanoparticles were synthesised to overcome the 
limitations of the drug in its native form. The RTH-ZnO 
nanoparticles were subjected to the basic characterisation 
techniques to obtain comprehensive information regarding 
the structural, chemical, and morphological aspects of nano-
particles. The XRD analysis revealed that the nanoparticle 
is mostly crystalline in nature with an average crystal size 
of 4.63 nm, calculated by using the Scherrer formula. The 
peak of 29.470°, corresponding to the plane (101), indicates 
the presence of RTH. A similar plain was observed by Ivan 
M. Savic et al. (2016), in their study to enhance the solubil-
ity of rutin trihydrate by complexation with β-cyclodextrin, 
which indeed confirms the presence of RTH [26]. The 
peaks such as 31.975°, 34.594°, 36.359°, 47.403°, 56.626°, 
62.888°, 67.955°, and 69.207° were aligned consistent with 
the wurtzite structure of ZnO nanoparticles. Based on the 
sharp diffraction patterns, it was observed that synthesised 
nanoparticles exhibited robust crystallinity with elevated 
intensity levels, and well-defined peaks as shown in Fig. 1. 
Similar peaks were reported by Mansab Ali Saleemi et al. 
(2022), confirming the presence of zinc oxide nanoparticles 
[27]. The FTIR analysis was employed to identify and char-
acterise the distinct chemical bonds that contribute to the 
unique properties of the nanoparticles. The absorption peak 
at 1383.1 ­cm−1 (C-H bending), 1727.7 ­cm−1 (C-O stretch), 
and 2713.9 ­cm−1 (C-H stretch) was observed to correspond 
to RTH in a study involving its isolation from the aquatic 
fern Azolla and investigation of its antioxidant properties 
[28]. These detected peaks collectively confirm the pres-
ence of RTH-associated functional groups in the nanoparti-
cles. Specifically, the C-H bending vibrations represent the 
bending motion of carbon-hydrogen bonds, highlighting 
RTH’s organic character. The C-O stretching vibrations at 
1727.7 ­cm−1 indicate the carbonyl groups integral to the fla-
vonoid core of RTH. Furthermore, the C-H stretching vibra-
tions at 2713.9 ­cm−1 support the presence of organic com-
ponents in nanoparticles. Similarly, the peaks 575.9 ­cm−1 
(M-O stretch), 863.3 ­cm−1 (C-H bending), 2952.8 ­cm−1 
(C-H stretch), and 3437.8 ­cm−1 (O-H stretch) were observed 
in a study investigating the germination and growth prop-
erties of mung bean seeds influenced by synthesised zinc 
oxide nanoparticles [29]. The presence of C-H bending and 
C-H stretching vibrations indicates the presence of organic 
materials. The absorption peak at 575.9 ­cm−1 corresponds 
to the metal-oxygen stretching confirming the presence of 
zinc oxide in the RTH-ZnO complex. Furthermore, the O-H 
stretch found at 3437.8 ­cm−1 could be caused by surface 
hydroxyl groups in ZnO or by water molecules adsorbed 
onto the nanoparticles. The morphological characteristics 
of RTH-ZnO nanoparticles were investigated through SEM. 
The SEM images revealed a near-spherical shape with a pro-
pensity for agglomeration. This finding aligns with previous 
studies indicating that agglomeration is a common occur-
rence in zinc oxide nanoparticles [30].
The in vitro antioxidant assays were used to assess the 
nanoparticle’s antioxidant capacity. In the DPPH assay, 
antioxidants diminish the stable DPPH radical, resulting 
in a colour shift that can be measured spectrophotometri-
cally, whereas the ABTS assay assesses the antioxidant’s 
ability to neutralise the ABTS radical cation. Furthermore, 
the hydrogen peroxide scavenging assay analyses the anti-
oxidant’s ability to break down hydrogen peroxide, whereas 
the superoxide scavenging assay measures its ability to 
neutralise superoxide radicals. These assays provide insight 
into various aspects of antioxidant function, providing an 
extensive overview of the nanoparticle’s potential to combat 
oxidative stress by scavenging distinct forms of free radi-
cals. A dose-dependent upregulation in the inhibition per-
centage from 10 to 160 µg/ml concentration of RTH-ZnO 
nanoparticles was noted in all the in vitro antioxidant assays. 
The 80 µg/ml showed a higher percentage of inhibition with 
significant p < 0.05 when compared with Trolox. A similar 
dose-dependent percentage of inhibition was reported by 
Guru et al. (2020), where a peptide isolated from cysteine 
and glycine-rich protein 2 of Channa striatus showed a con-
centration-dependent increase in free radicals scavenging 
activity [31].
The NO scavenging assay is essential to determine the 
nanoparticle’s ability to neutralise the highly reactive nitric 
oxide radical. The NO scavenging ability of RTH-ZnO 
nanoparticles was examined in this study to analyse its 
therapeutic applications. In biological systems, nitric oxide 
functions as both a signalling molecule and an oxidative 
stress mediator. The NO scavenging assay revealed a con-
centration-dependent response for RTH-ZnO nanoparticles 
in the scavenging of NO. The percentage of NO inhibition 
at different concentrations of RTH-ZnO’s demonstrates 
its capacity to regulate excess nitric oxide. The concentra-
tion of 80 µg/ml showed increased NO scavenging activity 
(p < 0.05). So, it was chosen as an optimal concentration for 
further analysis to evaluate its possible therapeutic proper-
ties. The potential of RTH-ZnO nanoparticles to scavenge 
nitric oxide radicals suggests that they will be used to treat 
oxidative stress-induced diseases [32].
The in vitro drug release assay is crucial for evaluat-
ing the release of the nanoparticles inside the body. The 
in  vitro drug release of RTH-ZnO nanoparticles was

analysed in this work to study its release kinetics. The 
released drug concentration was observed at several time 
periods at two different pH levels (1.2 and 6.8) until 
120 h. The results demonstrated a time-dependent pattern 
of drug release and highlighted the regulated nature of 
RTH-ZnO. The overall drug release percentage at pH 6.8 
was greater when compared to pH 1.2, indicating a higher 
rate of release in the intestine over the stomach. This 
pH-dependent release pattern is similar to the previously 
reported oral drug delivery systems, which involve regu-
lated release in the stomach followed by an accelerated 
release in the intestine for maximum absorption [33]. The 
time-dependent drug release characteristics observed at 
both pH levels highlight RTH-ZnO nanoparticle’s poten-
tial for regulated and targeted drug delivery applications.
Gut dysbiosis is the imbalance in the microbial commu-
nity of the gut, which leads to neurological diseases, obe-
sity, diabetes, allergies, inflammation, stress, autoimmune 
disorders, and infectious diseases [34]. Recent investiga-
tions have identified a potential connection between gut 
dysbiosis and the generation of ROS [35], which in turn 
may contribute to neurological diseases. Gut dysbiosis is 
usually dealt with the changes in dietary supplementation, 
intake of prebiotics and probiotics, and therapeutic inter-
ventions [36]. Understanding the relationship between the 
gut and metabolic pathways is crucial to treat neurological 
diseases. The results of the antimicrobial assay against 
the gut dysbiosis-associated microorganisms revealed a 
notable inhibitory activity of RTH, ZnO nanoparticles, and 
RTH-ZnO nanoparticles. Against both gram-positive and 
gram-negative bacteria, RTH-ZnO nanoparticles showed a 
better zone of inhibition when compared to RTH and ZnO 
nanoparticles, highlighting its potential to regulate these 
bacterial populations in the gut.
The zebrafish embryo and larvae model were used for 
the in vivo experimental studies. The concentration of the 
treatment groups (RTH, ZnO nanoparticles, and RTH-ZnO 
nanoparticles) was chosen based on an initial investigation 
conducted between concentrations 10 to 200 µg/ml. The 
concentrations above 160 µg/ml were noted to be lethal for 
the zebrafish larvae. Therefore, the optimal concentration 
of 80 µg/ml was selected for the in vivo investigations. The 
mortality and developmental deformation of the treatment 
groups under oxidative stress have been studied. Our study 
noted that the mortality rate was reduced in all the treatment 
groups (RTH, ZnO nanoparticles, and RTH-ZnO nanopar-
ticles) when compared with the stress group. The survival 
rate of the RT-ZnO nanoparticle-treated group was signifi-
cantly (p < 0.05) higher than both RTH and ZnO nanopar-
ticle-treated groups. Also, lethal morphological malforma-
tions like the yolk sac edema and bent spine were observed 
in the larvae which belong to the stress group after 3 dpf; 
such parameters were already reported in the literature for 
hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative stress in vivo models 
[37]. Notably, the larvae exposed to RTH-ZnO did not reveal 
any lethal malformations indicating its non-toxic effect, 
which can be potentially used as therapeutics.
The cell-permeable DCFDA fluorogenic dye was uti-
lised to quantify the intracellular ROS amount in the oxi-
dative stress-induced larvae. The fluorogenic dye produces 
green signals upon oxidation by ROS, which was initiated 
after being cleaved by the cellular esterases. The intensity 
of the green signals is equivalent to the amount of ROS in 
the larvae. In the fluorescent images, we clearly noted that 
the ROS level was substantially (p < 0.05) upregulated in 
the hydrogen peroxide-treated larvae. Similar upregulation 
in the level of ROS has been reported in an investigation 
involving zebrafish larvae under exposure to hydrogen per-
oxide [38]. On the other hand, the reduced mean fluorescent 
intensity of the RTH-ZnO-treated zebrafish larvae highlights 
its potential to scavenge intracellular ROS.
Excess of intracellular ROS can activate the caspase-
dependent pathway and regulate stress-induced apoptosis. 
The metachromatic AO dye has the ability to bind to both 
dsDNA and ssDNA or RNA and emit green and red signals, 
respectively. Studies reported that the larvae treated with 
hydrogen peroxide will emit a bright red signal while viewed 
under a fluorescent microscope [39]. Similar upregulation 
has been observed in the larvae belonging to the stress group 
in this study. On the contrary, the mean fluorescent intensity 
was drastically reduced in the groups treated with RTH, ZnO 
nanoparticles, and RTH-ZnO nanoparticles. Notably, the 
intensity of the RTH-ZnO group was recorded to be 4.2%, 
more resembling the control group.
Behavioural responses have been validated as a marker 
of an organism’s motor neuron functioning [40]. The 
zebrafish larvae will exhibit a variety of simple and com-
plicated behavioural patterns, and cognitive actions when 
exposed to various stimuli [41]. The behavioural analy-
sis on the zebrafish larvae revealed that hydrogen perox-
ide exposure caused confused abnormal behaviour with 
reduced movements. The stress-induced larvae failed to 
travel all the regions of the ice tray when compared to lar-
vae without stress. Similar patterns were observed in the 
zebrafish larvae subjected to acrylamide, where the neuro-
toxin exposure leads to alterations in neural communica-
tion and neural plasticity abnormalities [23]. On the other 
hand, the larvae treated with RTH, ZnO nanoparticles, and 
RTH-ZnO nanoparticles explored the entire ice tray, much 
like the control group larvae. Notably, the RTH-ZnO larvae 
travelled 47.9 ± 4.7 m, which indicates that RTH-ZnO treat-
ment has prevented the larvae from stress-induced cognitive 
impairment.
Acetylcholine is a neurotransmitter which will be 
released at the nerve synapsis in the central nervous 
system, peripheral nervous system, and enteric nervous

system. After the signal transmission, the enzyme acetyl-
cholinesterase will break down acetylcholine into acetate 
and choline. It is a crucial step to terminate signal trans-
mission and ensure postsynaptic membrane stability [42]. 
The vagus nerve carries signals between the gut and brain 
and connects the enteric nervous system with the cen-
tral nervous system. Studies reported that acetylcholine 
released in the gut via the vagus nerve can alter mood 
and various aspects of cognitive function [43]. When the 
zebrafish larvae were treated with the RTH-ZnO nano-
particles, the AChE levels were upregulated to a similar 
amount to that of the control larvae.
In the zebrafish larvae, several antioxidant enzymes 
have a key role in maintaining the redox balance. This 
mechanism was mostly overlooked by SOD, CAT, and 
GSH-related enzymes like GSR and GPx [44]. Studies on 
oxidative stress-induced in vivo zebrafish embryos and 
larvae revealed that the antioxidant genes will be signifi-
cantly downregulated under hydrogen peroxide treatment 
at 96 hpf [45]. Similar downregulation was observed in 
the zebrafish larvae exposed to hydrogen peroxide in our 
study. Interestingly, among the treatment groups with 
higher significance (p < 0.05), the RTH-ZnO nanoparti-
cles upregulated the expression of the key antioxidant 
genes in the stress-induced zebrafish larvae to scavenge 
the free radicals and maintain redox balance. Since the 
brain is more sensitive to oxidative damage, it requires a 
supplementation of exogenous antioxidants. An investiga-
tion by Murugan et al. (2023) recorded the anticonvulsant 
and antioxidant properties of naringenin-conjugated gra-
phene oxide nanoparticles [33]. In our study, we recorded 
a similar outcome which backs the hypothesis that RTH-
ZnO nanoparticles can prevent cognitive impairment by 
reducing oxidative stress induced by gut dysbiosis.
Briefly, we have fabricated the RTH-ZnO nanopar-
ticles and characterised their structural, chemical, and 
morphological attributes. We have identified that the 
RTH-ZnO nanoparticles showed significant antioxi-
dant effects in the in vitro analysis. The results of the 
antimicrobial study showed that RTH-ZnO exhibited 
a substantial zone of inhibition against gut pathogens. 
The zebrafish embryo and larvae developmental toxicity 
analysis highlights its non-toxic effects in the in vivo 
model. The fluorescent staining revealed that RTH-
ZnO has the potential to regulate ROS levels and sub-
sequently reduce stress-induced apoptosis in the larvae. 
The behavioural analysis showed that RTH-ZnO has the 
potential to regulate oxidative stress-induced cognitive 
impairment. Similarly, the AChE levels were also sig-
nificantly upregulated in the RTH-ZnO nanoparticle-
treated group. Notably, the RTH-ZnO nanoparticles 
indeed upregulated the expression of crucial antioxidant 
genes in the in vivo model.
5  Conclusion
Our study is a potential start towards a multimodal approach 
to address the complex connection between gut health and 
brain well-being. The results of our zebrafish larvae model 
showed that RTH-ZnO has the potential to significantly 
reduce the ROS in the biological systems without causing 
lethal adverse effects. RTH-ZnO’s efficacy in scavenging 
ROS, inhibiting gut dysbiosis-associated pathogens, and 
reducing oxidative stress-induced neurodegeneration high-
lights its therapeutic potential for the treatment of gut dys-
biosis-generated ROS-induced neurodegeneration. Hence, 
we conclude that RTH-ZnO nanoparticles can be taken for 
further studies in higher model organisms to develop it as an 
efficient dual intervention for gut dysbiosis and neurological 
diseases.

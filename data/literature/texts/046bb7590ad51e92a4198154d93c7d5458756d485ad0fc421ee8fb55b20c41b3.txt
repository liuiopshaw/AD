ABSTRACT: Immunotherapy is an advanced therapeutic strategy
of cancer treatment but suffers from the issues of off-target adverse
effects, lack of real-time monitoring techniques, and unsustainable
response. Herein, an ultrasmall Au nanocluster (NC)-based
theranostic probe is designed for second near-infrared window
(NIR-II) photoluminescence (PL) imaging-guided phototherapies
and photoactivatable cancer immunotherapy. The probe
(Au44MBA26-NLG for short) is composed of atomically precise
and NIR-II emitting Au44MBA26 NCs (here MBA denotes water-
soluble 4-mercaptobenzoic acid) conjugated with immune
checkpoint inhibitor 1-cyclohexyl-2-(5H-imidazo[5,1-a]isoindol-5-
yl)ethanol (NLG919) via a singlet oxygen (1O2)-cleavable linker.
Upon NIR photoirradiation, the Au44MBA26-NLG not only enables
NIR-II PL imaging of tumors in deep tissues for guiding tumor therapy but also allows the leverage of photothermal property
for cancer photothermal therapy (PTT) and the photogenerated 1O2 for photodynamic therapy (PDT) and releasing NLG919
for cancer immunotherapy. Such a multiple effect modulated by Au44MBA26-NLG prompts the proliferation and activation of
effector T cells, upshifts systemic antitumor T-lymphocyte (T cell) immunity, and finally suppresses the growth of both
primary and distant tumors in living mice. Overall, this study may provide a promising theranostic nanoplatform toward NIR-
II PL imaging-guided phototherapies and photoactivatable cancer immunotherapy.
KEYWORDS: Au nanocluster, Ligand engineering, NIR-II luminescence imaging, Photothermal therapy, Immunotherapy

mmunotherapy that leverages the body’s natural immune
system to fight cancer has been demonstrated to be an
advanced therapeutic strategy to solve cancer troubles
because it eliminates many concerns raised from traditional
cancer treatments.1−6 In spite of the soaring recruitment of
such immunotherapeutic strategies in oncology treatment,
there are several challenging issues unsolved: 1) Direct
administration of immunotherapeutic agents (e.g., immune
checkpoint inhibitors and cytokines) fates their nonspecific
biodistribution in normal tissues, and thus impairs their
therapeutic efficacy with potential immune-related adverse
effects;3,7 2) Treating those tumors in invisible or inaccessible

immunotherapy is rarely developed,4 which may place the
immunotherapy in a black box with the lack of real-time
monitoring; 4) Activatable yet atomically precise nano-
medicines are barely designed,9 which not only hinders
selective and controllable immunotherapy at designated sites
but also curtails atomic/molecular level understanding on the
correlations between the therapeutic performance and the
size/structure of immunotherapeutic nanomedicines.10,11 In
addition, effective cancer therapy usually requires the
combinational action of immunotherapy and other therapeutic
modalities (e.g., photothermal and photodynamic therapies),
which is also hardly realized due to the lack of such
multifunctional theranostic probes. Taken all together,
developing activatable yet atomic-precision nanomedicine
with high spatiotemporal imaging ability toward activatable
cancer immunotherapy that synergistically acts with other
therapeutic modalities is highly desired.

Recently, atomically precise coinage metal nanoclusters
(NCs, < 3 nm in kernel size) have been demonstrated to be
promising functional nanomaterials,12−14 and are widely
applied in fields including catalysis,15−19 energy conversion,20
environmental protection,21 sensing,22 and biomedicine.23−27
With the benefits of molecular size,28 engineerable surface
chemistry,29 engineerable structure/composition,30−32 chiral-
ity,33,34 good renal clearance (i.e., hydrodynamic diameter or
HD of ≤5.5 nm),35 neglectable biotoxicity, tailorable photo-
luminescence (PL) from visible to the first near-infrared range
(NIR-I: 650−900 nm) and even to second near-infrared
window (NIR-II) range (1000−1700 nm) with higher
spatiotemporal resolution (e.g., approximately 1 ms and 10
μm) and deeper tissue penetration (e.g., 3−5 cm depth),36−41
and enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect,42 metal
NCs are particularly attractive in biomedicine.10,43−48 For
instance, luminescent metal NCs have been developed as
molecular probes for bioimaging,49,50 real-time monitoring of
in vivo metabolism,44 and disease diagnosis.10,11 Moreover,
metal NCs are also successfully deployed in disease therapy,
nanomedicine, and drug delivery.10,11 Considering the out-
standing merits and powerful engineerable functionalities, we
hypothesized that if we can design a water-soluble and atomic-
precision Au NC with strong NIR-II PL and good photo-
thermal/photodynamic properties, and delicately engineer the
surface of the Au NC with immunotherapeutic drug molecules
via an activatable linker, we may acquire a high-performance
NC-based theranostic probe for NIR-II PL imaging-guided
cancer immunotherapy that synergistically acts with other
phototherapies and is activatable by certain stimuli for
controllable yet enhanced therapeutic efficacy. Unfortunately,
such an NC-based theranostic probe is not yet developed,
which is the driving force of this study.
Herein we report the design of a Au NC-based theranostic
probe for NIR-II PL imaging-guided phototherapies and
photoactivatable tumor immunotherapy. The key design of
the probe is the conjugation of atomically precise and NIR-II
emitting Au44MBA26 NCs with small-molecule immune
checkpoint inhibitor 1-cyclohexyl-2-(5H-imidazo[5,1-a]-
isoindol-5-yl)ethanol (NLG919) via a singlet oxygen (1O2)-
cleavable linker (here MBA denotes water-soluble 4-
mercaptobenzoic acid; Figure 1a). In this study, NLG919 is
selected to reverse kynurenine (Kyn)-induced immune
inhibition because it is able to demote immunosuppressive
Kyn via the indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO)-mediated
metabolization of tryptophan (Trp) into immunologically inert
compounds (i.e., alanine and anthranilic acid).51 After systemic
administration, the as-developed theranostic probe (i.e.,
Au44MBA26-NLG for short) could accumulate at tumor sites
due to the EPR effect. Upon NIR photoirradiation, the
Au44MBA26-NLG not only makes NIR-II PL imaging of
tumors possible in deep tissues for the guidance of tumor
therapy but also enables cancer photothermal therapy (PTT),
photodynamic therapy (PDT) and the photogenerated 1O2-
induced release of NLG919 for cancer immunotherapy. Such a
combinational action of the Au44MBA26-NLG boosts the
proliferation and infiltration of effector T cells and upshifts
systemic antitumor T-lymphocyte (T cell) immunity, which
ultimately inhibits the growth of both primary and distant
tumors in living mice.

With ultrasmall NIR-II emitting Au44MBA26 NCs in hand,
we engineered the surface of Au44MBA26 NCs for constructing
the immunotheranostic probe by grafting immune checkpoint
inhibitor NLG919 molecules via 2,2′-(Propane-2,2-diylbis-
(sulfanediyl))diacetic acid (PSDA) as a 1O2-cleavable linker
(Figure 1a and Figure S3). Briefly, the hydroxyl group (−OH)
of NLG919 first reacted with the carboxyl group (−COOH) of
PSDA to form a PSDA-caged prostimulant (PPS, Figure S4),
and the as-obtained PPS was then modified via an amination
process to form EDA-PPS (Figure S5−S7). Afterward,
Au44MBA26 NCs with carboxyl groups were conjugated with
EDA-PPS via a simple EDC/NHS cross-linking strategy,
resulting in the formation of Au44MBA26-NLG probe.
An interesting issue may arise regarding whether those
salient attributes of Au44MBA26 NCs such as NIR-II PL for
theranostic application can be maintained or not after surface
engineering, and thus we performed relevant characterizations
to clarify this issue. As shown in Figure 1b, the as-obtained
Au44MBA26-NLG probe shows two obvious peaks at 274 and
398 nm in the UV−vis absorption spectrum, and the former
peak should be contributed by the conjugated NLG919
(Figure S8a), indicating the successful conjugation of
Au44MBA26 NCs with NLG919. In addition, we further
identified the number of conjugated NLG919 in each Au
NC to be nine (Figure S8−S9 and Supplementary Note I),
which means only around 1/3 MBA ligands on the Au NCs
surface were conjugated with NLG919 due to the bulky steric
hindrance of NLG919. The TEM image and DLS measure-
ment manifest that the surface engineering of Au44MBA26 NCs
with NLG919 did not change their metallic core size but
slightly increased their HD from ∼2.7 to ∼3.1 nm (Figure S10
and Figure 1d). Such an ultrasmall HD of Au44MBA26-NLG
NCs is still lower than the kidney filtration threshold (∼5.5
nm), which may allow them to have good renal clearance
toward in vivo applications. Furthermore, the NIR-II PL
emission peaks at ∼1080 and ∼1280 nm of Au44MBA26 NCs

Besides, both the photothermal property and the photo-
dynamic producibility of 1O2 of the Au44MBA26-NLG probe
were also examined. As shown in Figure 1f, the Au44MBA26-
NLG solution gradually increases its temperature with 808 nm
laser irradiation and reaches the highest temperature value
(∼56 °C) at the time point of 10 min, showing almost the
same photothermal behavior as that of pristine Au44MBA26
NCs. Moreover, both Au44MBA26-NLG and Au44MBA26 NCs
perform a good temperature response with their concen-
trations under NIR light irradiation (Figure S11a and S11b),
showing promise in those PTT applications where the
concentration-dependent photothermal activities of probes
are required. To further assess the photothermal stability of the
probe that is also critically important for cancer PTT,52 we
conducted repeated radiation-cooling experiment, and the
results show that both Au44MBA26-NLG and Au44MBA26 NCs
have decent photothermal stability with consistent photo-
thermal behavior after five cycles of radiation-cooling test
(Figure S11c). The photothermal conversion efficiencies of
Au44MBA26 NCs and Au44MBA26-NLG NCs were calculated
to be 35.6% and 30.8%, respectively (Figure S11d). In
addition, these two probes are also capable of photodynami-
cally producing abundant 1O2 under NIR light irradiation,
which can be supported by the decreased PL intensity at 520
nm of the probe solutions with prolonging the irradiation time
of 808 nm laser by using 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) as
a PL indicator of
1O2 (Figure 1g). On this basis, we
demonstrated that the surface engineering of Au44MBA26
NCs with NLG919 not only did not significantly influence
their photothermal and photodynamic properties but also
maintained their ultrasmall HD and strong NIR-II PL, making
NIR-II PL imaging-guided photoactivatable cancer immuno-
therapy possible by using the designed Au44MBA26-NLG
probe.
To confirm the photoactivatable release of NLG919 from
Au44MBA26-NLG, an ultrafiltration process was applied to treat
the Au44MBA26-NLG solution with different NIR-irradiation
time, and the optical absorption of the redissolved Au44MBA26-
NLG solution was monitored by an UV−vis absorption
spectrometer to check the photoactivatable release of NLG919
from the Au44MBA26-NLG. As shown, upon 808 nm laser
irradiation, the optical peak at 274 nm corresponding to the
NLG919 of Au44MBA26-NLG decreases with prolonging the
irradiation time (Figure S12), which indicates the photo-
activatable release of NLG919 from Au44MBA26-NLG because
of the photodynamic production of 1O2 that specifically cleaves
the 1O2-cleavable PSDA linker to release NLG919 (Figure 1a).
Besides, the release percentage of conjugated NLG919 is
45.8%, which was calculated based on the decreased
absorbance at 274 nm.
Subsequently, using 4T1 cancer cells as a cell model, the in
vitro PTT/PDT performance and cytotoxicity of the
Au44MBA26-NLG probe were analyzed via 3-[4,5-dimethylth-
iazole-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay.
Specifically, as the live/dead staining result revealed (Figure
2a), the 4T1 cells solely treated with Au44MBA26-NLG NCs
are almost all alive (green color), while those combinatorially
treated with the NCs and laser irradiation for 6 min are nearly
all dead (red color). This result suggests that the Au44MBA26-
NLG probe has both a low cytotoxicity and excellent in vitro
PTT/PDT efficacy. With the quantitative statistics of the MTT
assay (Figure 2b), it is found that there is severe cell necrosis
with 42.8% viability in the sample of 4T1 cells treated with
12.5 μM Au44MBA26-NLG probe under 808 nm laser
irradiation, which affirms the effective PTT/PDT activity of
the probe for cancer cell killing. By contrast, a cell viability of
as high as 83.5% is retained for the sample even treated with 4-
fold Au44MBA26-NLG probe (50 μM) but without laser
irradiation, further validating the low cytotoxicity.
In addition, the in vitro mechanistic studies of the
Au44MBA26-NLG probe were then conducted to investigate
the probe-mediated immunometabolic intervention. In princi-
ple, immunotherapy relies on the regulation of immune system
to realize the tumor suppression. For example, upon certain
stimulation, the immune system could generate a number of
tumor-specific T cells such as CD8+ that can get into tumor
tissues to kill tumor cells via releasing interferon-γ (IFN-γ).
However, tumor tissues could upregulate IDO expression to
intervene the immunotherapeutic process by depriving the Trp
supply (note: sufficient Trp content is essential for these T
cells) because abundant IDO would elevate Kyn production
through Trp catabolism. When the Trp supply is lacking, these
T cells would lose their ability to kill tumor cells. Therefore,
the high IDO expression is the main cause for tumor
immunosuppression, including inhibitory effector T cells,
natural killer cells and activated regulatory T cells (TREGs).
In this study, 1O2 was used to activate NLG919 immunother-
apeutic molecules for IDO suppression, and thus, we applied 2,
7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetic acid (H2DCFDA) as a
1O2 PL turn-on indicator for detecting photogenerated 1O2. As
shown in Figure 2c and Figure S13, upon NIR light irradiation,
obvious PL signals could be detected from 4T1 cell samples
treated with Au44MBA26-NLG or Au44MBA26 NCs, which
validates the photodynamic production of
1O2. To further
verify the IDO suppression by Au44MBA26-NLG, IFN-γ was
utilized to induce 4T1 cancer cells to upregulate IDO
expression and enhance Kyn production via Trp catabolism,
followed by incubating with irradiated Au44MBA26-NLG or
Au44MBA26 NCs for 24 h, which were then colorimetrically
analyzed for determining Kyn and Trp contents (Figure 2d).
Comparing to the blank control group and the irradiated
Au44MBA26-treated group, the irradiated Au44MBA26-NLG-
treated group shows a 2.4-fold reduction in Kyn content (from
79.4% to 32.6%), which further confirms the photoactivated
immunotherapeutic activity of Au44MBA26-NLG through
suppressing the IDO expression and Kyn production (Figure
2d).
The excellent NIR-II PL property of the Au44MBA26-NLG
probe allows its in vivo PL imaging for guiding tumor therapies.
In this study, a tumor-bearing mouse model was established by
injecting 4T1 cells into the right flank of the mouse for an in
vivo tumor imaging experiment. The Au44MBA26-NLG NCs
perform excellently in in vivo NIR-II PL imaging, as
demonstrated by Figure S14. Particularly, under 808 nm
laser irradiation, the hindlimb vessels could be extremely clear
in the NIR-II PL window at 2 min after intravenous injection
of Au44MBA26-NLG NCs in mice, and still visible at 15 min.52

Au44MBA26-NLG probe: its in vivo PL imaging function can be
used for monitoring lymphatic metastasis. It is documented
that intraoperative lymph node dissection has been proven to
be a primary strategy to reduce the risk of tumor recurrence
and metastasis; however, its implementation is largely curtailed
by the limited availability of techniques in monitoring small

The in vivo PTT/PDT and synergistic immunotherapy of
the Au44MBA26-NLG probe was also studied in 4T1 tumor-
bearing BALB/c mice. To establish a tumor-bearing mouse
model, we implanted 4T1 cells under the skin on the right
posterior side of each mouse as the primary tumor, and after 6
d, 4T1 cells were implanted under the skin on the left posterior
side of each mouse to simulate the distant tumor. After that,
the 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were injected intravenously with
Au44MBA26-NLG or Au44MBA26, and upon NIR laser
irradiation, the tumor evolution and lung metastasis were
then monitored to evaluate the therapeutic efficacies of the
probe for mice (Figure 4a). The primary tumor was irradiated
with 808 nm laser 24 h after injection, and the IR thermal
mapping images of both Au44MBA26-NLG-treated and
Au44MBA26-treated 4T1 tumor-bearing mice groups display
that the temperature of the tumor elevates quickly and climbs
to the highest value of ∼54 °C with the 808 nm laser
irradiation of 10 min (Figure 4b and 4c). The NLG-treated
and (Au44MBA26 + NLG)-treated groups increased to ∼42 and
∼55 °C under the same irradiation conditions, respectively
(Figure S15). The generation of 1O2 was detected by recording
the PL intensity of intratumorally injected singlet oxygen
sensor green (SOSG). As shown in Figure 4d, about 6-fold
green PL signals could be detected from both the Au44MBA26-
NLG-treated and Au44MBA26-treated tumors with laser
irradiation in comparison with the nonlaser irradiation-treated
groups.
The in vivo tumor therapeutic efficacy was assessed by
continuously tracking the growth of both primary and distant
tumors with a time interval of 2 days after photodynamic
immunometabolic therapy (Figure 5a). Upon 808 nm laser
illumination, it is found that the primary tumors of
(Au44MBA26 + NLG)- (Figure S16a), Au44MBA26-NLG- and
Au44MBA26 NCs-treated mice were entirely eradicated, while
the distant tumor of Au44MBA26-NLG-treated mice was
significantly inhibited (Figure 5b, 5c and Figure S16b).
Moreover, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining results
show that almost no metastatic nodules could be found from
the lungs of the Au44MBA26-NLG-treated mice group upon 14
d treatment, while significant tumor metastasis was detected in
the lungs of other treated mice groups (Figure 5d). The
numbers of lung metastatic nodules in the lungs of mice after
saline or Au44MBA26 treatment with or without laser
irradiation and Au44MBA26-NLG treatment without laser
irradiation are all above 10, while no detectable lung metastasis
could be observed in the Au44MBA26-NLG treated group with
laser irradiation (Figure S17). These results manifest that the
Au44MBA26-NLG probe has excellent therapeutic efficacy on
primary tumor while efficiently suppressing the growth of
distant tumor and fully inhibiting lung metastasis. To assess the
systemic immune response activation, we quantitatively
analyzed the T cell population in tumor tissues following
photodynamic immunometabolic therapy. As shown in Figure
5e−h, cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CD3+ and CD8+ T cells) and
the T cells producing IFN-γ are significantly increased in
distant tumors of mice treated with Au44MBA26-NLG injection
and laser irradiation, accompanying the decrease in the
population of Treg cells. However, there was no significant

Why could the Au44MBA26-NLG probe possess such a good
immunotherapeutic efficacy for distant tumors? It should be
ascribed to the conjugation of water-soluble Au44MBA26 with
NLG919 via a photoactivatable linker. Although the small-
molecule inhibitor NLG919 could effectively interfere with the
metabolism of Trp/Kyn through suppressing the activity of
IDO in tumor cells, the poor water solubility, short plasma
half-life, and poor tumor accumulation severely limit its direct
administration for tumor immunotherapy. Such drawbacks of
NLG919 could be fully remedied by conjugating them on the
surface of water-soluble Au44MBA26 NCs. In addition to
improving the solubility and prolonging the plasma half-life of
NLG919, the photothermal and photodynamic properties of
Au44MBA26 NCs also make the conjugated NLG919 more
efficient in enhancing antitumor immune response. It is known
that converting immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones
is a feasible way to modulate the response of tumor
immunotherapy. Currently, very few solid tumors can be
cured by immuno-oncology therapy due to the relatively sparse
infiltration of T cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME)
of “cold” tumors. Owing to their higher infiltration of T cells,
“hot” tumors are characterized by stronger immunogenicity
and are more sensitive to tumor immunotherapy. As reported,
the PTT of in situ tumors can transform them from “cold”
tumors into “hot” ones, thus promoting various immune cells
to infiltrate the tumor site and trigger tumor-specific immune
response.54,55 Moreover, the PDT effect of the probe also
synergistically contributes to tumor therapy. Besides, the
photoactivatable release of NLG919 from Au44MBA26-NLG
could reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment,
enhance tumor suppression, and also generate systemic
immune memory to prevent tumor recurrence.

CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we have designed a Au NC-based diagnostic
probe for highly spatiotemporal NIR-II PL imaging-guided
tumor phototherapies (i.e., PTT and PDT) and photo-
activatable immunotherapy. Experimental results verified that
the generated heat and 1O2 from the Au44MBA26-NLG probe
upon NIR photoirradiation could trigger PTT/PDT for tumor
ablation and initiate the release of immunosuppressive
NLG919 from Au44MBA26-NLG for reversing Kyn-induced
immune inhibition in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice. Consequently,
the proliferation and activation of effector T cells in
Au44MBA26-NLG-treated mice were greatly upshifted, in
comparison with those in other groups. Such a combinational
therapeutic effect of Au44MBA26-NLG could eliminate primary
tumors, effectively suppress the growth of distant tumors, and
completely inhibit lung metastasis, which can hardly be
realized by laser irradiation treatment and PTT/PDT alone.
Furthermore, the Au44MBA26-NLG probe displayed low
cytotoxicity and excellent biosafety, as evidenced by its renal
clearance, absence of damage to the main organs, and
unaffected blood biochemistry and liver functions. These
findings underscore its potential for clinical translation in
tumor theranostics.

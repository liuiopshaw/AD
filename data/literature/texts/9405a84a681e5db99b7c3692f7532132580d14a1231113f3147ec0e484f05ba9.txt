REVIEW
Dysbiosis and Alzheimer’s disease: role of probiotics, prebiotics 
and synbiotics
Ami Thakkar1 · Amisha Vora1 · Ginpreet Kaur1 · Jamal Akhtar2
Received: 11 February 2023 / Accepted: 26 May 2023 / Published online: 7 June 2023 
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2023
Abstract
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disease characterized by dementia and the accumulation of amyloid beta 
in the brain. Recently, microbial dysbiosis has been identified as one of the major factors involved in the onset and progres-
sion of AD. Imbalance in gut microbiota is known to affect central nervous system (CNS) functions through the gut-brain 
axis and involves inflammatory, immune, neuroendocrine and metabolic pathways. An altered gut microbiome is known to 
affect the gut and BBB permeability, resulting in imbalance in levels of neurotransmitters and neuroactive peptides/factors. 
Restoration of levels of beneficial microorganisms in the gut has demonstrated promising effects in AD in pre-clinical and 
clinical studies. The current review enlists the important beneficial microbial species present in the gut, the effect of their 
metabolites on CNS, mechanisms involved in dysbiosis related to AD and the beneficial effects of probiotics on AD. It also 
highlights challenges involved in large-scale manufacturing and quality control of probiotic formulations.
Keywords  Alzheimer’s disease · Dysbiosis · Probiotics · Gut microbiota
Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disease 
characterized by gradual cognitive decline. It is associated 
with the deposition of fibrillar β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques in the 
brain parenchyma. It also includes the presence of neurofi-
brillary tangles, senile plaques and synaptic loss (Rodríguez 
et al., 2009; Sultana & Butterfield, 2010). The deposition of 
fibrillar β-amyloid plaques can lead to oxidative stress, and 
the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and may 
progress to AD (Ahmad et al., 2017; Umeno et al., 2017). 
Also, AD progresses with the downregulation of cholin-
ergic basal forebrain neurons leading to reduced choline 
acetyltransferase activity (Mufson et al., 2014). Chronic 
inflammation damages the brain leading to the release of 
cytokines, further progressing to dementia and AD (McGeer 
& McGeer, 2002). Neurological diseases were the top cause 
of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) (276 million) and 
the second greatest cause of deaths worldwide in 2016. AD 
and other dementia accounted for 10.4% of total DALYs and 
was third largest contributor of neurological DALYs (Fei-
gin et al., 2019). According to AD International as of 2020, 
over 55 million people worldwide are living with dementia. 
It is predicted that these numbers would double every 20 
years reaching 78 million in 2030 and 139 million in 2050. 
The number of cases has been found to increase with age. 
India, China and their West Pacific and South Asian neigh-
bours have the fastest-growing elderly population (Dementia 
statistics | Alzheimer’s Disease International (ADI), n.d.; 
2021 Alzheimer’s disease facts and figures, 2021). The 
major pathological markers of AD-like amyloid plaques, 
hippocampal atrophy, neurofibrillary tangles and neuronal 
death can be identified years before cognitive decline and 
neuropsychiatric disturbances (Blennow et al., 2006). There 
is presently no permanent therapy option for AD. Fewer 
drugs exist for symptomatic relief of AD symptoms like 
cholinesterase inhibitors and N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor 
(NMDAR) antagonists. Donepizil, rivastigmine and galan-
tamine are the medications together classified as acetylcho-
linesterase inhibitors and memantine classified as NMDA 
antagonist. They have been used currently for treatment of

Singh et al., 2021a; Singh et al., 2021b). Age, genetics and 
oxidative stress play an established role in the causes of AD. 
Furthermore, traumatic brain injury, infections and several 
environmental risk factors are also found to be associated 
with AD (Armstrong, 2019). Among them, alteration in the 
gut microbiota is found to be largely associated with AD 
(Zhao et al., 2015).
In the human digestive system, the gut microbiota is 
extremely varied, including trillions of microorganisms. 
The formation and growth of the gut microbiome begin at 
birth, whereas the composition of the microbiome is mostly 
determined by genetic, dietary and environmental fac-
tors. Dysbiosis occurs when the normal gut microbiota is 
replaced with dysfunctional array of microorganisms that 
promote disease conditions (Tyszkowski & Mehrzad, 2023). 
Intestinal permeability, digestion, metabolism and immu-
nological responses can all be impacted by variations in the 
gut microbiota’s composition and functioning leading to 
dysbiosis (Gomaa, 2020). Gut microbiota plays an impor-
tant role in human health and disease. Resident microbiota 
influence physiology, host metabolism and immune system 
development (Guinane & Cotter, 2013). Dysbiosis plays an 
important role in gastrointestinal inflammation, and inflam-
mation is known to be a primary cause of various degen-
erative diseases and metabolic disorders (Thursby & Juge, 
2017; Hakansson & Molin, 2011). Host genetics, age, diet 
and antibiotics are several factors affecting dysbiosis (Hasan 
& Yang, 2019). External factors like the type of food eaten, 
feeding habits and microbial load of the immediate envi-
ronment affect the composition of the gut microbial flora. 
Internal factors like microbial interactions, environmental 
temperature, intestinal pH; physiological factors like bile 
acids, peristalsis, host secretions and immune responses also 
affect the gut flora (Bull & Plummer, 2014). In certain situ-
ations, dysbiosis appears years before the onset of illness, 
providing indicators for early diagnosis of disease risk and 
potential preventive therapies.
Microbial causality is used for the identification and 
development of a specific mechanism of pathogenesis for a 
disease, thus correcting this microbiota imbalance could be 
a promising strategy for therapeutic development (Durack & 
Lynch, 2019). Probiotics are beneficial active microorgan-
isms that colonize the human intestines. They can inhibit the 
colonization of pathogenic bacteria and improve host immu-
nity (Wang et al., 2021). Probiotics-induced balanced micro-
biome can improve AD symptoms through various mecha-
nisms (Arora et al., 2020). Using the Medical Expenditure 
Panel Survey, a major longitudinal epidemiological research 
investigated the connection between intestinal illness and the 
eventual emergence of anxiety disorder suggesting a strong 
correlation that gut dysbiosis could lead to neurological dis-
orders (Morais et al., 2020).
Growing trends in the use of probiotics in AD have 
prompted research in the field of neurodegeneration caused 
by the imbalance of normal microbial flora present in the 
gut. This in turn would reduce associated healthcare costs 
and improve the quality of life of affected individuals. The 
key aim of this review paper is to highlight the role of gut 
microbiota in normal brain functioning and their association 
with the phenomenon of neurodegeneration and the poten-
tial use of probiotics in the prevention of AD. The current 
review focuses on the varied microbiome present in the gut 
and the effect of dysbiosis leading to AD. It also illustrates 
the various mechanisms involved in dysbiosis-associated 
memory impairment and beneficial effects of restoring gut 
microbiome by administration of probiotics in patients suf-
fering from AD.
Microbiota present in the gut
The human gastrointestinal tract (GIT) represents the largest 
interface between the environmental factors, host and anti-
gens in the human body. The number of organisms residing 
in the GIT exceeds ­1014 and they benefit the host body. The 
gut microbiota strengthens gut integrity, protects against 
pathogens and regulates host immunity (Thursby & Juge, 
2017). Human gut microbiota varies in each part of the GIT 
and undergoes variation in the same individual due to age 
and environmental factors (Kho & Lal, 2018). Microorgan-
isms present in the gut consist of various species of bacteria, 
bacteriophages, yeast, eukaryotic virus and fungi. A total 
of 90% of the gut microbiota consists of bacteria belonging 
to the Phyla Bacteriodetes, Actinobacteria and Firmicutes. 
The Firmicutes phyla are composed of more than 200 differ-
ent genera of which Clostridium represent 95% (Arumugam 
et al., 2011; Rinninella et al., 2019). The Actinobacteria 
phyla are less abundant and are mainly represented by the 
Bifidobacterium genus (Ruiz et al., 2017). Table 1 comprises 
the various species of gut microbiome, the metabolites 
released by them and the effect of the metabolites on the 
central nervous system (CNS).

and metabolic pathways. Alteration in the composition of

Prebiotics, probiotics and synbiotics
The term “probiotics” is derived from the Latin word which 
means “for life”. Probiotics are live non-pathogenic bacte-
ria and yeast that are beneficial for maintaining a healthy 
gut ecosystem (Islam, 2016). Probiotics ingested in suffi-
cient amount provide not only nutritional benefit but also 
a positive effect on health. Probiotics impact human health 
by interacting with resident microbiota, intestinal epithelial 
cells and the immune system (Wieërs et al., 2019). Probi-
otics modulate the inflammatory process and counteract 
the oxidative stress precursor in AD (Naomi et al., 2021). 
Inflammation and oxidative stress are the known precursors 
of AD. Strong evidence suggests that probiotics offer pre-
vention against cognitive decline in AD. A probiotic for-
mulation consisting of bacterial strains of Streptococcus 
thermophilus, Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis, Lac-
tobacillus acidophilus, Lactobacillus helveticus, Lactobacil-
lus paracasei, Lactobacillus plantarum and Lactobacillus 
brevis showed that its oral administration improved glucose 
transportation in mice by restoring brain levels of glucose 
transporters like GLUT3, GLUT4 and insulin-like growth 
receptor β (Bonfili et al., 2020). A meta-analysis of rand-
omized control trials conducted for checking the efficacy 
of probiotics concluded that probiotics improved cognitive 
performance in AD patients possibly through decreasing lev-
els of inflammatory and oxidative biomarkers (Deng et al., 
2020). Similarly, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 
randomized control trials conducted shows that probiotics 
improved levels of triglycerides, very-low-density lipopro-
tein cholesterol, insulin resistance and plasma malondialde-
hyde (Krüger et al., 2021).
Prebiotics are undigestable dietary fibres like fructoo-
ligosaccharides and xylooligosaccharides on which gut 
microbiota feed (Davani-Davari et al., 2019). These dietary 
fibres act as substrates for fermentation by microbiota result-
ing in the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). The 
SCFAs activate the host’s immune system through T-cell 
differentiation (Quigley, 2019). In addition, they promote 
the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-10 
and inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines 
that may benefit patients suffering from AD. Moreover, they 
promote the proliferation of probiotic bacteria Lactobacillus 
and Bifidobacterium (Bedu-Ferrari et al., 2022). Synbiotics 
are a combination of probiotics and prebiotics that improve 
the survival and activity of beneficial microorganisms in the 
gut. Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Escherichia and Bacil-
lus are some of the most often employed bacterial genera 
in probiotic formulations (Gupta & Garg, 2009). Table 2 
illustrates different species of bacteria present in probi-
otic formulation. It is proved that symbiotic supplementa-
tion improves cognitive deficiencies, which appears to be 
linked to lowering systemic inflammation, oxidative stress 
and blood cell damage and could be a promising adjuvant 
therapy to slow the progression of the disease (Ton et al., 
2020). Probiotics are not regulated by the Food and Drug 
Administration (FDA) as they are considered healthy food 
(Islam, 2016).

synaptic transmission. Alterations in the levels of specific 
neurotransmitters are observed in neurodegenerative dis-
orders like AD (Sheffler et al., 2022). Dysbiosis is shown 
to affect the levels of neurotransmitters and neurotrophic

Neurotransmitters are shown to regulate synaptic plasticity, 
neuronal communication and signalling pathways. GABA is 
an inhibitory neurotransmitter of the CNS and GABAergic 
neurotransmission in the hippocampus region is related to 
the modulation of spatial memory in the CNS. Many species 
of Actinobacteria and Firmicutes convert the monosodium 
glutamate to GABA, thereby increasing its availability in 
CNS. Dysbiosis leads to an imbalance in the levels of these 
species of microbiota leading to downregulation of signal-
ling pathway through GABAergic neurons and an imbalance 
of the GABA/glutamic activity (Park et al., 2021). Lower 
levels of ­GABAA receptors in the prefrontal cortex and hip-
pocampus are seen in the case of patients suffering from AD. 
Evidence suggests that the inactivation of ­GABAB receptors 
in the hippocampal region improves memory. A study con-
ducted on mice treated with species of Lactobacillus rham-
nosus caused reduced levels of ­GABAB in the hippocampus 
leading to enhanced memory (Bravo et al., 2011; Limon 
et al., 2012). Gut microbiota is shown to regulate the levels 
of glutamate in the brain. Glutamate is an excitatory neu-
rotransmitter that acts on the NMDAR and is required for 
synaptic signalling, neuronal differentiation, neuronal sur-
vival and transmission (Liu et al., 2019). Survival of neurons 
requires synaptic NMDAR signalling. Astrocytes releases 
glutamate leading to activation of extrasynaptic NMDAR 
causing excitotoxicity, synaptic damage and neurodegenera-
tion in AD (Wang & Reddy, 2017). Probiotic bacteria resid-
ing in the human GI tract are capable of producing gluta-
mate. Although studies show that glutamate cannot cross the 
BBB, the altered BBB permeability as a result of dysbiosis 
could possibly assist in transport of glutamate to the brain 
and thereby regulate neuronal development and synaptic 
plasticity (Mazzoli & Pessione, 2016).
Gut microbiota is shown to metabolize tryptophan which 
acts as a precursor for the synthesis of serotonin. Altera-
tion in gut microbiota affects serotonin levels in the gut as 
well as the brain, affecting the functioning of the central 
and enteric nervous systems. Low levels of serotonin have 
been reported to have a link to impaired cognitive functions 
(Kaur et al., 2019). In patients suffering from AD, multispe-
cies probiotic therapies regulate tryptophan metabolism and 
help restore the normal composition of the gut microbiota. 
Studies conducted on mice suffering from AD and treated 
with probiotics consisting of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacte-
rium strains showed improved learning capacity and behav-
ioural response, and partially alleviated cognitive decline by 
increasing the release of neurotransmitters and the bacterial 
metabolite acetate (Zhu et al., 2021; Kobayashi et al., 2017). 
Acetylcholine is an excitatory neurotransmitter present at 
the neuromuscular junction and in the autonomic ganglia. It 
alters the presynaptic release of neurotransmitters and coor-
dinates neuronal signalling (Picciotto et al., 2012). Inhibition 
of acetylcholinesterase is a proven approach for symptomatic 
improvement of AD (Saify & Sultana, 2014). It is also seen 
that the combination of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium 
strains along with exercising leads to increased acetylcho-
linesterase and antioxidant capacity by reducing acetylcho-
linesterase activity in the brain (Shamsipour et al., 2021).
Neurotropic factors are small proteins that maintain cell 
morphology, and neuronal survival and play a key role in 
cognition and memory (Schindowski et al., 2008). The intes-
tinal microbiota is shown to affect central levels of brain-
derived neurotropic factor (BDNF) in the hippocampus, 
cortex and other brain regions. BDNF is considered to be an 
important neurotrophin involved in CNS growth, enhancing 
cell growth, differentiation, neurogenesis and neurotrans-
mission (Bercik et al., 2011). When compared to healthy 
individuals, BDNF levels were shown to be significantly 
lower in patients suffering from AD (Ng et al., 2019). The 
study conducted on Sprague Dawley rats treated with probi-
otics containing Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium showed 
enhanced brain BDNF expression due to the involvement of 
gut hormones showing its advantage in increasing BDNF 
levels in individuals suffering from AD (Savignac et al., 
2013).
Gut microbiota and neural system
The gut has a neural connection with the brain through the 
vagus nerve and the vagus nerve carries a wide range of 
signals from the gut to the brain. Gut microbiota affects 
brain function through the vagus nerve (Zhu et al., 2017). 
Intestinal microbiota plays a critical role in the homeostasis 
of the host immune system and any alteration in gut micro-
biota activates the immune system possibly via an impact on 
intestinal permeability (Guerreiro et al., 2018; Kelly et al., 
2015). Probiotic supplementation reduced intestinal inflam-
mation and permeability with having minimal effect on brain 
amyloid, cytokine or gliosis levels (Kaur et al., 2020). Dis-
turbance in the intestinal permeability leads to mucosal bar-
rier dysfunction and systemic inflammation which can lead 
to neuronal inflammation by affecting blood-brain barrier 
(BBB) permeability (Houser & Tansey, 2017). Microglia, 
the cytokine expression of the CNS, play a vital role in the 
development and function of the CNS and regulate BBB 
permeability and synaptic connectivity. Some disruptive 
BBB alterations are mediated directly by cytokines lead-
ing to neuroinflammation (Varatharaj & Galea, 2017). It is 
also shown that neural activity coordinates microglial and 
cytokine function in the brain (Ferro et al., 2021). Under 
normal physiological conditions, microglia maintain neu-
roprotection and neuronal repair by the release of BDNF 
and transforming growth factor beta (TGF β). Compromised

Trehalose, a natural disaccharide, modulates inflammatory

AD (Khalifeh et al., 2020). The combination of lactulose and 
trehalose has a neuroprotective effect by acting on inflamma-

were obtained with probiotic lactulose when used suggesting 
that lactulose acts on macroautophagy and chaperone-medi-
ated autophagy pathways and could be used as a preventive 
and therapeutic disaccharide for AD (Khalifeh et al., 2020). 
A study conducted on Clostridium butyricum showed that 
microglia-mediated neuroinflammation can be reduced by 
modulating the GM-gut-brain axis (Sun et al., 2020).
Gut microbiota and neuroactive metabolites: SCFA
Fermentation of dietary fibres and resistant starch by bacte-
ria in the colon leads to the production of acetate, propion-
ate and butyrate SCFAs as metabolites in the colon. SCFAs 
possess neuroactive properties and SCFA administration has 
proved to be an important treatment alternative in AD (Silva 
et al., 2020). Probiotic bacteria have been found to influence 
the equilibrium of the intestinal microbiome and produce 
metabolites, such as SCFAs by gut microbes. Gut barrier 
dysfunction, low-grade inflammation and altered glucose, 
lipid and energy homeostasis are a result of an imbalance 
in the intestinal microbiota and a decrease in the number of 
bacteria generating metabolites such as SCFAs like acetic, 
propionic and butyric acid (Markowiak-Kopeć & Śliżewska, 
2020). A clinical trial conducted on 89 cognitive impaired 
patients showed that lipopolysaccharides and SCFAs are 
potential pathophysiologic linkages between the gut micro-
biota and AD (Zhou et al., 2021; Marizzoni et al., 2020). 
Intestinal microbiota may help in the protection of AD by 
the generation of selective SCFAs that prevent the formation 
of harmful β-amyloid aggregates (Ho et al., 2018). A pro-
biotic Clostridium butyricum produces SCFA butyrate by 
fermenting fibre in the gut that prevents the formation of 
β-amyloid aggregates in the brain (Stoeva et al., 2021).
Gut microbiota and neuroendocrine system: 
hypothalamic‑pituitary‑adrenal axis (HPA) 
axis
HPA axis is a system comprising numerous neuroendo-
crine pathways that function to maintain physiological 
homeostasis. Abnormal development of the HPA axis can 
lead to disruptive production of neuropeptides and neu-
rotransmitters in the CNS resulting in negative effects on 
autonomic, neuroendocrine, metabolic and behavioural 
functioning (Sheng et al., 2021). Cortisol and corticos-
terone are the end products of the HPA axis and regulate 
the body and brain’s response to stress (Ronald de Kloet 
et al., 2005). The alteration in the gut microbiota can 
lead to dysbiosis that affects stress response, behaviour 
and cognitive functions. Chronic stress-modulated altera-
tions are a significant pathological marker for AD and 
affect the gut-brain axis through downstream signalling 
mechanisms (Rutsch et al., 2020). Chronic stress leads 
to the production of cortisol which triggers the activa-
tion of the HPA axis. Activation of the HPA axis leads 
to an imbalance in the levels of neurotransmitters in the 
brain, hippocampal injury and cognitive injury (Stephens 
& Wand, 2012). The effect of stress on the brain depends 
on the duration of stress exposure and its interaction with 
effector genes (Lupien et al., 2009). Acute and chronic 
stress are found to affect the endocrine system via acti-
vation of the HPA axis leading to the release of inflam-
matory markers. It is also reported that stress activates 
the sympathetic nervous system and causes an imbalance 
in the release of neurotransmitters in the brain leading 
to neuronal apoptosis and a series of neurodegenerative 
changes (Won & Kim, 2016). Elevated stress can lead to 
AD by causing rapid development in pathology and loss 
of cognitive function (Saeedi & Rashidy-Pour, 2021). The 
gut microbiota plays a role in responding to stress through 
communication via the release of neurotransmitters and 
the vagus nerve (Madison & Kiecolt-Glaser, 2019). Find-
ings suggest that psychological stress induces changes 
in gut microbiota, stimulates inflammatory responses to 
release cytokines and disrupts intestinal membrane integ-
rity and BBB (Geng et al., 2020). A randomized control 
trial carried out on healthy individuals showed that pro-
biotics were effective in reducing stress without a signifi-
cant effect on cortisol levels (Zhang et al., 2020). A ran-
domized placebo-controlled study conducted on athletes 
consuming probiotics showed that probiotics improved

aerobic capacity and relieve stress by increasing intestinal 
permeability and reducing oxidative stress and inflamma-
tion (Salleh et al., 2021). Gut microbiota also activates 
the HPA axis through microbial antigens and cytokines 
that cross the BBB. Also, gut microbiota shows its action 
on certain neurotransmitters and BDNF leading to HPA 
axis activation (Misiak et al., 2020). Studies performed 
on germ-free mice and rats showed that stress modifies 
gut microbiota. Germ-free mice exposed to acute stress 
lead to activation of the HPA axis with elevated levels of 
corticosterone (Vagnerová et al., 2019). A similar study 
conducted on female rats treated with probiotics contain-
ing Lactobacilli showed that probiotics suppressed stress 
response and neuroinflammation. It also prevented HPA 
axis-induced stress response and reduced the levels of 
pro-inflammatory cytokines (Ait-Belgnaoui et al., 2012).
Gut microbiota and age
Age is one of the major risk factors leading to AD. Deposi-
tion of Aβ is an important in vivo biomarker in AD and it 
progresses with the increase in age. Aβ triggers a series of 
biochemical processes that determine tau deposition, neu-
ronal disruption and neuronal death that manifest in AD 
(Zecca et al., 2021). Recent research suggests that ageing has 
a negative impact on the composition, function and diversity 
of microbiota and ageing-related gut dysbiosis leads to the 
initiation and progression of many metabolic diseases. Diet 
supplementation with probiotics counteracts ageing-related 
negative consequences and would improve health in older 
individuals (Coman & Vodnar, 2020). Probiotics have been 
studied in an increasing number of clinical studies for their 
potential health benefits. Table 3 summarizes the clinical 
trials conducted on use of probiotics in AD and the probable

Challenges
As probiotics are considered a healthy food and are not regu-
lated by the FDA, there is a need for a regulatory body for 
monitoring the manufacturing, and sale of probiotics to the 
community. The manufacturing process is highly sensitive 
and requires the selection of the correct strain and concen-
tration of microbial species (in regards to CFU) and the use 
of the right media for growth. The process of maintaining 
the quality of the product until its shelf life and the sterility 
checking during the process of cultivation of bacterial spe-
cies for 6 weeks and further for other downstream processes 
is a major challenge. It is important to determine the antibi-
otic resistance profile of these probiotics as this resistance 
could be transmitted to pathogens and could pose a challenge 
for the host in a scenario where the host has to consume 
antibiotics. The co-administration of probiotics with other 
drugs has to be studied to determine the safety and efficacy 
profile of both drugs and probiotics. There is a need for the 
development of the newer type of formulations that would 
help in achieving targeted activity of the probiotic bacteria. 
The development of enteric-coated probiotics would help 
in achieving targeted delivery of probiotics, and promote 
enhanced efficiency of bacteria in the targeted tissue.
Future directions
Numerous studies carried out in recent years suggest that 
gut dysbiosis is a major contributing factor to the initiation 
and progression of AD. Alterations in the gut, inflammation, 
oxidative stress, alteration in BBB permeability, imbalance 
in the levels of neurotransmitters and stress response have 
been proposed for gut dysbiosis-related AD. Studies show 
that imbalance in the gut microbiota is seen years before 
the onset of AD, suggesting that the use of probiotics and 
prebiotics would help in restoring the gut microbiome and 
it could be a promising strategy for delaying the onset and 
progression of AD. Currently, several probiotic formula-
tions are available in the market as dietary supplements 
and functional foods. Future work may be directed towards 
understanding which strain of microbes could be beneficial 
in pathology related to AD. Controlled human trials can 
be conducted for understanding the effects of probiotics in 
patients suffering from AD.

In this research, a novel enzyme mimetics based on the photochemical property of gold nanoclusters was
demonstrated. It was found that the bovine serum albumin (BSA) stabilized red or blue emitting gold
nanoclusters (Au NCs) exhibited enzyme-like activity under visible light irradiation. The BSA–Au NCs had
better stability against stringent conditions compared to natural enzyme. In addition, the photostimu-
lated enzyme mimetics of BSA–Au NCs showed several unprecedented advantages over natural
peroxidase or other existing alternatives based on nanomaterials, such as the independence of hydrogen
peroxide on activity and the easily regulated activity by light irradiation. The mechanism of the
photoresponsive enzyme-like activity of BSA–Au NCs was investigated. The photoactivated BSA–Au
NCs was designed to develop a facile, cheap, and rapid colorimetric assay to detect trypsin through
trypsin digestion of the protein template of BSA-stabilized Au NCs. The limit of detection for trypsin was
0.6 μg/mL, which was much lower than the average level of trypsin in patient′s urine or serum.

1. Introduction
Natural enzymes are proteins (except for a few RNA molecules
called ribozymes) that catalyse chemical reactions. Compared to
chemical catalysts, enzymes possess remarkable merits (e.g., high
substrate speciﬁcity and high catalytic activity). So, natural en-
zymes have found extensive applications in various ﬁelds includ-
ing biosensing, agrochemical production, pharmaceutical pro-
cesses, and food industry (James, 2009). Unfortunately, natural
enzymes are easily denatured by environmental change and can
be digested by protease. In addition, high cost and rigorous storage
conditions also limit their applications. Thus, developing enzyme
mimetics is highly desirable (Wei and Wang, 2013). The rapidly
growing ﬁeld of nanotechnology provides excitingly new possibi-
lities for the development of enzyme mimics. Up to now, different
nanostructures including metallic or bimetallic nanostructures (Jv
et al., 2010; He et al., 2010), metal oxides (Gao et al., 2007; André
et al., 2011; Mu et al., 2012), carbon nanomaterials (Song et al.,
2010; Cui et al., 2011) were found to possess peroxidase-like
activity, which could catalyze the oxidation of the typical chromo-
genic substrates using H2O2 as an oxidant. Compared to natural
enzymes, nanomaterials may serve as promising candidates for
artiﬁcial enzymes, due to several advantages, including low cost,
tunability in catalytic activities, improved stability, and ease of
storage and treating. However, problems in applications of the
natural peroxidases/peroxidase mimetics also arise from the harsh
reaction conditions due to the presence of a large amount of H2O2,
which makes the in vivo applications of peroxidases/peroxidase
mimetics difﬁcult (Cook, 1997; Tao et al., 2013). To develop
enzyme mimetics that have high catalytic activity under mild
reaction conditions is appealing (Wang et al., 2014).
The development of new types of nanomaterials has led to the
discovery of metal nanoclusters (NCs) which have elucidated new
frontiers in science and gathered tremendous attention. Due to
enhanced quantum conﬁnement, these ultrasmall metal NCs
exhibit interesting and unusual optical, electronic and charging
properties (Ingram et al., 1997; Wang et al., 2005; Ramakrishna
et al., 2008; Zhu et al., 2008; Devadas et al., 2011). For example,
experiment work found that the NCs possessed discrete energy
levels and molecule-like properties in the absorption and ﬂuores-
cence features (Zhu et al., 2008; Wu and Jin, 2010; Aikens, 2008;
Wen et al., 2012). The mechanism and the origin of the lumines-
cence of metal NCs are still a mystery due to their complex
structure (Wen et al., 2012). Moreover, the NCs are too small to
support surface plasmon resonance and thus they are signiﬁcantly
differentiated from their counterpart plasmonic metal nanoparti-
cles (Wu and Jin, 2010).

It seems that metal NCs are novel nanostructures with intri-
guing properties that need to be explored. Herein, it was interest-
ing to ﬁnd that Au NCs exhibited high enzyme-like activity upon
visible light (λZ400 nm) triggering without using powerful oxi-
dants. Au NCs themselves demonstrated almost no (for red-
emitting Au NCs) or weak enzyme-like activity (for blue-emitting
Au NCs). Under visible light (λZ400 nm) stimulation, Au NCs
catalyzed the oxidation of the typical substrate (3.3′,5.5′-tetra-
methylbenzydine (TMB)), whose function was identical to that of
the peroxidase. Unlike natural peroxidase or peroxidase mimetics
based on nanomaterials (Jv et al., 2010; He et al., 2010; Gao et al.,
2007; André et al., 2011; Mu et al., 2012; Song et al., 2010; Cui
et al., 2011), the photostimulated Au NCs demonstrated enzyme-
like activity without using destructive hydrogen peroxide as an
oxidant, indicating they were better biocompatible enzyme mi-
metics for promising applications in biocatalysis, biosensing and
bioimaging systems, etc. Considering that proteases are involved
in the control of a variety of essential physiological and patholo-
gical processes and the activity of proteases are associated with
diseases, the photoactivated Au NCs possessing enzyme-like activ-
ity was applied as a robust nanoprobe for facile, selective, and fast
colorimetric detection of one kind of proteases―trypsin. Not only
this study provides a novel enzyme mimetics with good and
biocompatible catalytic activity, but also the photochemical prop-
erty of Au NCs demonstrated in this research may provide
reference for the further investigation and application of metal
NCs in biocatalysis, optical biosensors, phototherapy, etc.
2. Experimental
2.1. Chemicals and materials
Bovine serum albumin (BSA), HAuCl4  3H2O, ascorbic acid (AA),
t-butanol, ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA), KI, 3.3′, 5.5′-
tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), and trypsin were all obtained from
Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Horse-
radish peroxidase (HRP) was purchased from Aladdin Industrial
Corporation. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) from bovine liver was
purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). All other chemicals used

A 300 W Xe lamp (NBeT, China) equipped with an ultraviolet

2.3. Synthesis of Au nanocluster (Au NCs)
All glasswares were washed with Aqua Regia (HCl:HNO3 of
volume ratio 3:1), and rinsed with ultrapure water. The red or blue
emitting Au NCs were prepared according to previous methods
(Wang et al., 2011; Das et al., 2012) with modiﬁcations. For the
synthesis of red emitting Au NCs, 5 mL of aqueous HAuCl4 solution
(10 mM, 37 °C) was added to BSA solution (5 mL, 50 mg/mL, 37 °C)
under vigorous stirring. Then, 0.5 mL of 1.0 mol/L NaOH solution
was introduced 5 min later, and the mixture was allowed to
incubate at 37 °C under vigorous stirring for 24 h. The color of
the solution changed from light yellow to deep brown. The
solution was then dialyzed in ultrapure water for 48 h to remove
unreacted impurities.
The blue emitting Au NCs were prepared by adding 5 mL
aqueous solution of HAuCl4 (10 mM, 37 °C) to BSA solution
(5 mL, 20 mg/mL, 37 °C) under vigorous stirring. Then, 50 μL of
ascorbic acid (0.35 mg/mL) was added dropwise to induce the
formation of Au NCs. The reaction proceeded for 24 h at 37 °C
while the pH was maintained at 8.0. The solution was then
dialyzed
in
ultrapure
water
for
48 h
to
remove
unreacted
impurities.
2.4. The process of the oxidation of the substrate using Au NCs under
visible light irradiation (λZ400 nm)
To examine the capability of red emitting BSA–Au NCs (or blue
emitting BSA–Au NCs) as a catalyst for the oxidation of 3, 3′, 5, 5′-
tetramethylbenzidine
(TMB)
under
visible
light
irradiation
(λZ400 nm), experiments were carried out using 50 μL red
emitting BSA–Au NCs or 500 μL blue emitting BSA–Au NCs in a
reaction volume of 5 ml acetate buffer solution (20 mM, pH 3.0)
with 300 μM TMB as substrate. The above solution was irradiated
with a 300 W Xe lamp equipped with an ultraviolet cutoff ﬁlter
(λZ400 nm) to provide visible light.
2.5. The colorimetric detection of trypsin
The stock solution of trypsin was freshly prepared before use.
For trypsin detection, 50 μL of the as-prepared red BSA–Au NCs
were mixed with 500 μL of different concentrations of trypsin
(from 0.9 μg/mL to 1.0 mg/mL) in 50 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 8.0)
containing 5.0 mM CaCl2. After incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, 500 μL
of 5 mM TMB, and 500 μL of acetate buffer (200 mM, pH 3.0) were
added into the solution. Subsequently, the mixed solution was
illuminated under visible light irradiation (λZ400 nm) for 10 min
to allow development of the blue color, and the absorbance of the
oxidized TMB (oxTMB) at 652 nm was measured.

3.1. Characterization of BSA–Au NCs
BSA directed synthesis of Au NCs is popular because of the
stability it imposes on the sculptured materials. The BSA–Au NCs
of two different sizes (with average diameter of about 2.5 nm

reported previously (Wang et al., 2011; Das et al., 2012). The
larger Au NCs emitted at 425 and 620 nm (λex¼365 nm), and
the smaller ones emitted at 425 nm (λex¼365 nm) (Fig. 1C). We
referred to them as red and blue BSA–Au NCs, respectively. As
the size of gold nanoclusters decreases to the Fermi wavelength
of an electron, discrete energy levels begin to form, allowing the
interaction with light through electronic transitions between

et al., 2013; Guével et al., 2011). The as-prepared red BSA-Au NCs
(λem¼620 nm) consisted about 25 gold atoms (Xie et al., 2009).
The Jellium EFermi/N1/3 energy scaling law was used as a model
to describe the size-dependent electronic structure and relative
electronic transitions of the smaller clusters (Guével et al.,
2011). Application of the spherical jellium model to the blue
BSA–Au NCs (λem¼425 nm) indicated that it contained 8 gold
atoms (see Supporting Information for details). The ﬂuorescence
of the as-synthesized Au NCs could be easily seen by the naked
eye under UV lamp and recorded by digital camera (Fig. 1C,
inset). The ﬂuorescence of Au NCs demonstrated that they had
distinctive property from that of the bulk material and Au
nanoparticles.
3.2. Visible light triggered enzyme-like activity of BSA–Au clusters
To investigate the enzyme-like catalytic activity of Au NCs, we
chose the commonly used 3.3′, 5.5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB)
as a typical substrate (Jv et al., 2010; Jang and Roper, 2011; Su
et al., 2012). The red BSA–Au NCs alone showed almost no
catalytic activity for TMB oxidation (Fig. 2A) in the absence of
light under aerobic condition. The blue BSA–Au NCs alone
showed a slight catalytic activity for TMB oxidation (Fig. 2B)
under aerobic condition in the absence of light. The slight
catalytic activity for TMB oxidation under aerobic condition in
the absence of light might be due to the fact that NCs with small
size could activate oxygen to oxidize TMB (Gao and Zeng, 2012;
Pal et al., 2012). However, the catalytic activity for TMB oxidation
by blue Au NCs and oxygen was relatively weak. The BSA alone
under aerobic condition in the present of light barely catalyzed
the chromogenic reaction of TMB (Fig. S1). It was found that the
enzyme-like catalytic activity of Au NCs could be obviously
stimulated by light irradiation. In order to avoid the direct
photo-oxidation of TMB by ultraviolet light, visible light was
adopted to investigate the catalytic activity of BSA–Au NCs. The
visible light (λZ400 nm) irradiation alone could not induce TMB
oxidation and the enzyme-like catalytic activity for TMB oxida-
tion enhanced greatly in the presence of Au NCs (Fig. 2A and B).
A typical blue color and characteristic absorption peaks at 370
and 652 nm originating from the oxidized TMB (oxTMB) (Jang
and Roper, 2011) were observed after the Au NCs were added
into the TMB solution under visible light irradiation for 10 min.
This catalytic activity of Au NCs for the oxidation of TMB was
similar to that of the natural enzyme of horseradish peroxidase
(HRP) (He et al., 2011), demonstrating its enzyme-like activity.
The HRP can catalyse the oxidation of TMB to produce the oxTMB
(with an absorption coefﬁcient of 3.9  104 M1 cm1 ) using
H2O2 as an oxidant (Marquez and Dunford, 1997), whose reaction
has been widely adopted in traditional enzyme linked immuno-
sorbent assays (ELISA) (Gao et al., 2007). The oxTMB has
characteristic absorbance peaks at 370 and 652 nm originating
from its distinctive blue charge-transfer complex of diamine and

The catalytic property of red/blue BSA–Au NCs was further

and 232.2 nM S1. Compared with that of HRP (Km¼0.434 mM)

NCs with TMB as substrate was much lower, indicating that BSA–
Au NCs had a higher afﬁnity than HRP considering TMB as a
substrate. In addition, the BSA–Au NCs with TMB as the substrate

3.3. Catalytic reaction mechanism
After bubbling the reaction solution with high pure nitrogen to
remove dissolved oxygen, the catalytic activity of the photo-
activated red/blue BSA–Au NCs for TMB oxidation decreased (Fig.
S7), conﬁrming that dissolved O2 involved in the photocatalytic
reaction. To illustrate the catalytic mechanism of BSA–Au NCs
under illumination, a series of quenchers were employed to
scavenge the relevant reactive species including hydroxyl radicals
(OH), superoxide anion (O2) and photo-generated holes (hþ) in
the catalytic system (Fig. S7). t-Butanol as an effective OH
scavenger (An et al., 2012; Cao et al., 2011) exhibited a slight
depression on the oxidation of TMB under photoactivated red/blue
BSA–Au NCs. After the addition of the O2 scavenger of super-
oxide dismutase (SOD) (Cao et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2012) into the
reaction solution, the absorbance of the oxTMB exhibited a slight
decrease. The above results demonstrated that OH and O2 were
the reactive species responsible for TMB oxidation. Furthermore,
the catalytic activity of the red/blue BSA–Au NCs exhibited an
obvious inhibition in the presence of EDTA, indicating that the
photo-generated holes (hþ) were reactive species responsible for
TMB oxidation (Feng et al., 2012). Promptly generated stable
photocurrents of red/blue BSA–Au NCs were observed with a
reproducible response to on/off visible light cycles (Fig. S8),
demonstrating the effective charge generation and transfer of
the BSA–Au NCs under visible light irradiation. Red BSA–Au NCs
demonstrated higher photocurrent than that of blue BSA–Au NCs,
indicating the more efﬁcient charge generation and transfer of red
BSA–Au NCs than that of blue ones. From the absorption spectra,
we can see that the BSA–Au NCs especially the red BSA–Au NCs
had a wide absorption range between 300-500 nm (Fig. S9). The
BSA–Au NCs could absorb visible light to generate electron (e)–
hole (hþ) pairs. The generated e or hþ reacted with oxygen or
water molecules to produce reactive species such as OH and
O2, which were responsible for TMB oxidation.
NCs contain only a few atoms and the enhanced quantum
conﬁnement effects result in discrete energy levels and molecule-
like properties in the electronic transitions features (Wu and Jin,
2010; Zheng et al., 2004). Upon visible light excitation, electrons
are excited from the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) to
the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), generating
excited electrons in LUMO level and holes in the HOMO level
(Kogo et al., 2013). We performed cyclic voltammetry (CV) mea-
surements to probe the HOMO–LUMO level and the energy gap
(Das et al., 2012; Liang et al., 2006) (Fig. S10). The electrochemical
gap between the oxidation onset (þ0.80 V vs Ag/Agþ) and
reduction onset (0.31 V vs Ag/Agþ) indicated an energy gap of
Eg∼1.11 eV for red BSA–Au NCs. While the oxidation onset of

3.4. Trypsin detection using red BSA–Au NCs
Natural enzymes or enzyme mimetics have wide applications
in biocatalysis, biosensing, bioimaging (Xie et al., 2012). On the
basis of the intrinsic enzymatic property of BSA–Au NCs, we
designed a novel, simple colorimetric method for the detection
of one kind of proteases–trypsin. As known, proteases are involved
in the control of a variety of essential physiological and patholo-
gical processes and play their roles by catalyzing the hydrolytic
cleavage of speciﬁc peptide bonds in target proteins and breaking
up proteins into smaller fragments (Alfonta et al., 2004). It is
highly desirable to probe the activities of proteases because
altered protease activities are associated with many diseases such
as inﬂammation, cancer, cardiovascular and neurodegenerative
diseases (Grifﬁths and Cooney, 2002). Trypsin, known as the most
important digestive enzyme, is employed for the degradation of
proteins into peptides in proteomics, in particular in mass spectro-
scopy based proteomics (Olsen et al., 2004; Havliš et al., 2003;
Strader et al., 2006). In addition, trypsin plays a key role in
controlling the pancreatic exocrine function and is a biomarker
for pancreatitis (Byrne et al., 2002). Therefore, new convenient
assays for trypsin screening are highly desired for the develop-
ment of efﬁcient diagnostic and therapeutic methods toward these
pancreatic diseases and application in the proteomics area. Several
methods for trypsin assay were reported based on solid-phase
cleavage, turbidity, radiometry, and electrochemistry (Koritsas and
Atkinson, 1995; Walker et al., 2006; Artigas et al., 1981; Ionescu
et al., 2006). However, they either were time-consuming or
required specialized instruments. Colorimetric biosensing has
attracted much attention because of its low cost, simplicity, and
practicality (Liang et al., 2013). Based on the fact that trypsin
digested the protein template of BSA–Au NCs and decreased the
catalytic activity of BSA–Au NCs, a facile and novel colorimetric
sensing platform for trypsin was developed. The Au NCs formed in
the BSA solution were stabilized by a combination of Au–S bond
with the protein (via the 35 cysteine residues in BSA) and the
steric protection due to the bulkiness of the protein (Xie et al.,
2009). BSA molecule has only one peptide chain containing 607
amino acids in length, among which there are 59 lysines and 24
arginines that can be cleavaged by trypsin (Xie et al., 2009). When
BSA was degraded, the catalytic activity of the BSA–Au NCs was
inhibited (Scheme 2). The catalytic activity decrease of BSA–Au
NCs in the presence of trypsin may be due to that the surface state
of Au NCs changed and they aggregated (Fig. S11) after the
cleavage of BSA. The absorbance intensity of oxTMB at 652 nm
decreased linearly with the logarithmic concentrations of trypsin
over the range from 0.9 μg/mL to 1.0 mg/mL (Fig. 3), with a
detection limit of 0.6 μg/mL. As can be seen from Table S1, the
detection limit of the present method for trypsin detection was
comparable or even lower than other methods (radioimmunassay,
ﬂuorescence, electrochemical method) reported, and a wider
linear range was obtained by this method compared to other
methods. This colorimetric method for trypsin detection is con-
venient and low-cost compared to other methods which are time-
consuming or require specialized instruments. Although there is
no trypsin in healthy person′s urine, the average level of trypsin in
patient′s urine is 84.4 μg/mL in pancreas transplant patients (Hu
et al., 2012). Healthy individuals have a mean serum trypsin
concentration of 0.2570.1 μg/mL, whereas acute pancreatitis

The selectivity of red BSA–Au NCs toward trypsin was also
studied. To explore the selectivity of detection of trypsin, control
experiments showed that other enzymes and potential interfer-
ents, such as lysozyme, thrombin, GOx, glucose, urea, Kþ, Ca2þ,
Naþ had no obvious effects on trypsin detection (Fig. 4), demon-
strating that the enzymolysis of BSA by trypsin speciﬁcally led to
selectivity of the method. Trypsin is with substrate speciﬁcity
based on positively charged lysine and arginine side chains (Hu
et al., 2012). The selectivity of the proposed method for trypsin
detection was further evaluated with various coexistence sub-
stances added. Table S2 showed the effect of interferents on the
determination of trypsin, a relative error of 75.0% was considered
to be tolerable. The tolerable concentration ratios of coexisting
substances to 5.0 μg mL1 trypsin was hundred-fold for proteins
or thousand-fold for metal ions, showing that there was little
interference from commonly existing substances. In addition, the
absorption response of the photo-stimulated catalytic activity of
BSA–Au NCs towards 100 μg/mL trypsin was recorded after the
BSA–Au NCs were stored at 4 °C for half a month, which showed
no obvious change after storage, indicating the trypsin biosensor
based on BSA–Au NCs had acceptable stability.
In order to demonstrate the potential application of the
proposed method for trypsin assays in clinical diagnosis, biological
samples were prepared by adding ﬁxed amount of trypsin into
human urine taken from four healthy volunteers. For real sample
determination, the human urine was diluted 10-fold with Tris–HCl
buffer before the detection. Certain amount of trypsin was added
in the samples and the ﬁnal concentration of trypsin was calcu-
lated according to the obtained spectra information and the
regression equation of the standard curve. The accuracy of the
method was investigated by recovery studies. The recoveries of
spiked trypsin in the diluted human urine ranged from 96.1 to
98.4% (Table S3), thus demonstrating the potential applicability of
the BSA–Au NCs as colorimetric probe for the quantitative detec-
tion of trypsin in biological samples. The proposed method has
potential in facile and rapid clinical diagnosis.
4. Conclusions
This study demonstrated a novel photochemical property of Au
NCs. The oxidation of the typical substrate TMB with visible light
stimulated Au NCs was realized, which provided a colorimetric
platform for trypsin. The enzyme-like activity of the photostimu-
lated Au NCs showed typical Michaelis–Menten kinetics exhibiting
higher efﬁciency with TMB as a substrate compared to natural
HRP. The milder catalytic conditions (needless of destructive H2O2)
of Au NCs than that of natural HRP or peroxidase mimetics based
on nanomaterials may represent a promising biocompatible can-
didate as mimetic enzymes, and may ﬁnd a wide range of
applications, such as biocatalysis, biosensing, and bioimaging. In
addition, this study may serve as a foundation for the photoche-
mical property of metal NCs, which may lead to more theoretical
and experimental studies.

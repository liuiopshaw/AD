Photothermal-Propelled Au-CeO2 Micromotors for
Synergistic Photocatalytic-Photothermal Antibacterial
Systems
Fengyan Shi, Mengna Ding, Qin Zhang, Ya Zhao, Liping Zhou, Liulin Luo,*
Yingchun Miao,* and Yuning Huo*
Facing the great challenge for eﬃcient utilization of solar light, the
design of photothermal-propelled micromotors is signiﬁcant for converting
optical energy into thermal energy to achieve the in situ manipulated motion.
Assisted by the photothermal-propelled function, a synergistic photocatalytic-
photothermal antibacterial system is successfully constructed in this work,
based on the Au-CeO2 micromotor. The selective growth of CeO2 nanoparticles
on the surface of Au nanorods (NRs) is achieved with the adjustable Au expo-
sure ratio. The strong interaction of CeO2 with Au NRs realizes the enhanced
visible light harvesting and the promoted photo-induced charge separation.
Especially, the self-induced thermophoretic force on asymmetric lollipop-like L-
Au-CeO2 with higher Au exposure ratio is more powerful than that on symmet-
ric core-shelled CS-Au-CeO2 and dumbbell-like D-Au-CeO2. As a result, its local
temperature gradient is greater and thus realizes the in situ manipulated mo-
tion with higher velocity and stronger directionality. It further facilitates the con-
tact with bacteria and promotes the synergistic photocatalytic-photothermal
antibacterial performance for the probe bacteria of Escherichia coli. This pow-
erful photothermal-propelled Au-CeO2 micromotor shows signiﬁcant potential
for the microorganism control in biomedical and environmental applications.

1. Introduction
Pathogenic bacteria and viruses pose a
serious threat to public health due to their
rapid
multiplication
to
induce
various
diseases.[1,2] For example, the COVID-19
pandemic has further emphasized the
seriousness of microbial infections, show-
ing the necessity to address the issue of
microbial contamination.[3] In recent years,
optical technologies with rapid develop-
ment has introduced into the biomedical
ﬁeld.[4] Especially, photocatalytic and pho-
tothermal technologies have emerged as
promising approaches to combat bacte-
rial
infections.[5]
Photocatalytic
process
generates reactive oxygen species by light
excitation, disrupting the cell membrane
structure of bacteria.[6,7]
On the other
hand, photothermal conversion realizes
the elimination eﬀect to bacteria by the
elevated temperature.[8] However, the in-
dividual optical technique presents the
obvious limitations for the antimicrobial
activity,
including
the
restricted
light
utilization and low quantum eﬃciency.[9] In recent years, it
has been believed that combining photocatalysis with photother-
mal contribution to create a synergistic eﬀect is a promising
route.[10–12] The synergistic photocatalytic-photothermal system
can eﬀectively improve the utilization of solar energy, promote
the electron transfer, and beneﬁt the generation of ROS for
achieving more reactive sites.
At the same time, the recently emerged technology of micro-
motors has presented the broad applicability in biological sci-
ences, including targeted drug delivery,[13] disease diagnosis,[14]
and disinfection.[15] Micromotors (2 to 20 μm) with the feature of
inherent motion, are capable of autonomously moving in the so-
lution, which is diﬀerent from the general Brownian motion and
passive diﬀusion.[16] Therefore, it will promote the contact with
bacteria by the driving force converted from external energy into
mechanical tasks, including light irradiation[17–19] magnetism,[20]
sound,[21] and chemical energy.[22] Optical energy is particularly
attractive due to its biocompatibility, easy control, high propul-
sion facilitation, and no requirement of additional fuel.[23,24]
Especially, photothermal-propelled micromotors with the struc-
tural asymmetry can self-induce the temperature gradient by
thermophoretic force, and thus alter the surface tension of the

surrounding liquid region without chemical fuel.[25] Conse-
quently, the local liquid ﬂows from low to high area of surface
tension to propel the micromotor with rapid speed, overcom-
ing the inherent potential of the diﬀusion limit.[25] For exam-
ple, Au nanorods (NRs) with tunable aspect ratio and curvature
are suitable for serving as micromotor due to its longitudinal
surface plasmon meta-wavelength tunability, strong scattering
absorption, and eﬀective photothermal eﬀect.[26–28] Additionally,
the linear structures are more sensitive for achieving the high
velocity.[29]
Therefore, it can be predicted that the introduction of micro-
motors into the synergistic photocatalytic-photothermal antibac-
terial system is beneﬁcial for improving the dynamic movement
to overcome the diﬀusion limit. As a result, it can eﬀectively pro-
mote disinfection eﬃciency by eﬃcient contact with bacteria and
the generation of oxidation species and thermal energy. Further-
more, the adjusted motion speed and direction of micromotors
are desired for enhancing antibacterial eﬃciency. Therefore, the
construction of photothermal-driven micromotors is expected to
achieve enhanced synergistic photocatalytic-photothermal disin-
fection performance.
Meanwhile, CeO2 acted as a typical n-type semiconductor and
has been studied for the applications in electrochemical cells, en-
ergy storage, photocatalysis, and biotechnology.[30–32] Due to the
reversible conversion between Ce (III) and Ce (IV), CeO2 can
eﬃciently generate oxygen vacancies and produce reactive oxy-
gen species.[33] However, the utilization of carriers is greatly hin-
dered by the rapid recombination of electron-hole pairs during
the photocatalytic process. Fortunately, the formation of Schot-
tky barriers between semiconductors and metals (for example,
Au and etc.) can facilitate the electron transfer and prolong their
lifetime.[34,35]
Herein,
we
rationally
designed
photothermal-propelled
Au-CeO2
micromotors
for
the
synergistic
photocatalytic-
photothermal antibacterial process. The obtained Au-CeO2
micromotors were constructed by seed-mediated method and
CeO2 selectively grew on the surface of Au NRs. Compared
to pristine CeO2, the introduction of Au NRs could eﬀectively
promote visible light harvesting and charge separation. More
importantly, Au NRs in Au-CeO2 composites played a key role in
photothermal conversion for generating the local temperature
gradient to self-induce the thermophoretic motion. Especially,
the high velocity and directional movement of asymmetric
lollipop-like Au-CeO2 micromotor were signiﬁcant for achieving
eﬀective contact with bacteria, in comparison to core-shell and
dumbbell-like Au-CeO2 composites. As a result, the synergis-
tic photocatalytic-photothermal antibacterial contribution was
greatly promoted, oﬀering a prospect for practical bacterial
elimination.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Characterization of Au-CeO2 Micromotors
The morphologies of various samples were observed in TEM im-
ages in Figure 1. It could be found that Au NRs presented the
smooth surface with the uniform diameter and length of about
12 and 52 nm, respectively. As we know, the density of CTAB
molecules was lower on the end than that on the side wall of
Au NRs due to its curvature diﬀerence, resulting in the non-
uniform distribution of CTAB molecules. Thus, the steric hin-
drance for the growth of CeO2 onto the end of Au NRs was
weaker,[28,29] and the growth of CeO2 on the Au NRs could be
controlled by adjusting the CTAB concentration. It could be ob-
served in Figure 1A(a), the Au presented a smooth nanorod mor-
phology. The low CTAB concentration of 0.010 mmol L−1 ben-
eﬁted the uniform adsorption on the whole surface of Au NRs,
and thus achieved the Au-CeO2 composite with core-shell struc-
ture (CS-Au-CeO2) (Figure 1A(b)). The increased CTAB concen-
tration of 0.10 mmol L−1 led to the growth of CeO2 on both
ends of Au NRs to dominantly form the dumbbell-like Au-
CeO2 composite (D-Au-CeO2) (Figure 1A(c)). While further in-
creasing CTAB concentration to 0.30 mmol L−1, the asymmet-
ric Au-CeO2 with lollipop shape (L-Au-CeO2) could be achieved
with the selective growth of CeO2 on one end of Au NRs
(Figure 1A(d)). In comparison, it was diﬃcult for the CeO2 with
high CTAB concentration of 0.80 mmol L−1 to grow on the
surface of Au NRs, resulting in the mixture of Au NRs and
CeO2 nanoparticles (Au/CeO2) (Figure 1A(e)). From Figure 1A(f),
pristine CeO2 nanoparticles showed the mesoporous structure
and the partial agglomeration, when Au nanoparticles were
not used as carriers. This could restrict further research into
micromotors.
Furthermore, HRTEM images of L-Au-CeO2 sample in
Figure 1B clearly revealed the strong combination of CeO2 with
one end of Au nanorod. The lattice fringes of CeO2 in re-
gion 1 and Au nanorod in region 2 corresponded to the (200),
(111), and (222) crystal planes of CeO2 and (200) of metal-
lic Au, respectively. At the same time, EDS mapping image
in Figure 1C provided the distribution of Au, Ce, and O ele-
ments in L-Au-CeO2, D-Au-CeO2, and CS-Au-CeO2, respectively.
It further demonstrated the successful preparation of diﬀer-
ent Au-CeO2 composites, which showed the uniform distribu-
tion with clear boundaries of corresponding elements to their
morphologies.
Figure 2A show XRD patterns of Au NRs, Au-CeO2 compos-
ites, and CeO2 samples. In comparison to typical (111), (200),
(220), and (311) diﬀraction peaks in Au nanorods[36,37] and (200),
(220), (311) peaks in CeO2,[38] new peaks and obvious peak shift
could not be found in all of Au-CeO2 composites. It indicated the
individual crystal growth of Au NRs and CeO2 nanoparticles. The
peak intensity of Au NRs in Au-CeO2 composites gradually de-
creased with the wrapping eﬀect of CeO2, further demonstrat-
ing the deposition of CeO2 on Au NRs. The chemical valences in
CeO2, Au NRs, and L-Au-CeO2 samples were measured by XPS
spectra, as shown in Figure 2B–D. Ce 3d spectra in CeO2 sample
displayed Ce4+ with Ce 3d5/2 (916.5, 907.1, 900.8, and 898.2 eV)
and Ce 3d3/2 peaks (888.4 and 882.2 eV), respectively.[39] The other
two ﬁtting peaks at 903.2 and 884.7 eV could be attributed to
Ce3+.[39] In contrast, Ce 3d spectra in L-Au-CeO2 sample showed
the obviously positive peak shift with a new Ce3+ peak (880.6 eV).
Moreover, the peak area ratio of Ce3+ to Ce4+ was 44% to 56%
in L-Au-CeO2, which was much higher than that of 20% to 80%
in CeO2. Obviously, it could be attributed to the reduction role of
Au NRs in the stable interaction with CeO2. From Figure 2C, O
1s spectra of CeO2 sample presented the peaks of surface oxygen
vacancies at 531.2 eV and the lattice oxygen (Ce-O) at 529.2 eV,
respectively.[40] After binding with Au NRs, the peak intensity of

2.2. Photothermal Property
In Figure 3, the photothermal conversion property of diﬀerent
samples was recorded by IR thermal images under the irradia-
tion of 808 nm laser NIR light (2.0 W cm−2), which was gener-
ally utilized.[43] The temperature evolution proﬁles in Figure 3A,
depending on the wavelength of irradiation light, presented the
temperature change corresponding to the optical energy when
the light was turned on or oﬀ. As a result, the temperature in-
creased promptly with the generation of optical energy after the
light was turned on and decreased gradually with the loss of opti-
cal energy when the light was turned oﬀ. Pristine CeO2 presented
little photothermal eﬀect with a similar temperature evolution as
deionized water. However, the temperature increment was about
27.6 °C on Au NRs, which could signiﬁcantly contribute to the
enhanced photothermal conversion eﬀect of Au-CeO2 compos-
ites. As a result, more exposure of Au NRs in Au-CeO2 compos-
ites induced a higher temperature, corresponding to their light-
harvesting at 808 nm (Figure 2F). It proved that Au NRs played
a key role in the photothermal conversion, while CeO2 coating
inhibited the increase of temperature. The more exposure of Au
NRs induced the stronger photothermal conversion eﬀect in Au-
CeO2 composites. Thus, the local thermal gradient induced by
light irradiation was greater in asymmetric lollipop-like L-Au-
CeO2 than that in D-Au-CeO2 and CS-Au-CeO2 samples. It bene-
ﬁted to in situ manipulate the motion of L-Au-CeO2 by powerful
driving force. Furthermore, photothermal conversion eﬃciency
(𝜂) was calculated in Figure S1 (Supporting Information) accord-
ing to the temperature drop evolutions, which was summarized
in Figure 3B. Au NRs presented the highest 𝜂value of 46.6%,

2.3. Motion of Au-CeO2 Micromotors
Based on the study of photothermal conversion property of vari-
ous samples, the feature of photothermal-propelled motion was
further investigated on diﬀerent samples, as shown in Figure 5A
(two motion tracks in each sample). The blue and red trajec-
tory lines in one ﬁgure were denoted as the movement traces of
the same catalysts observed in the microscope lens. The circles
of each track represented the moving position of the micromo-
tor. It could be found that short displacement of pristine CeO2
could be observed, obviously due to its poor photothermal con-
version capability. In contrast, other Au-involved samples showed
much longer movement distance with high velocity, since the
photothermal eﬀect of Au NRs provided the suﬃcient mechani-
cal energy for motion. Additionally, the linear-like motion could
be found on L-Au-CeO2, in comparison to other samples. The av-
erage velocity of various samples was further quantiﬁcationally
calculated by using image J tracking, as shown in Figure 5B. It
could be found that the average velocity of CeO2 (1.50 μm s−1) was
lower than other samples. L-Au-CeO2 (1.90 μm s−1) presented a
higher average velocity than D-Au-CeO2 (1.75 μm s−1) and CS-Au-
CeO2 (1.74 μm s−1), but lower value than Au NRs (2.20 μm s−1). It
consisted well with the aspect ratio of Au exposure and the pho-
tothermal conversion properties (Figure 3). It could be ascribed
that the eﬃcient photothermal conversion induced the increased
surface temperature of the micromotors and thus generated the
local thermal gradient to the surrounding aqueous environment
(Figure 4). As a result, the eﬀective thermophoretic force was
generated to drive micromotors. Furthermore, the mean square
displacement (MSD) deviation was shown in Figure 5C, express-
ing the square of displacement distance of micromotors from the
initial point. As we know, both velocity and directionality of the
movement greatly inﬂuence the feature of MSD.[16] The conver-
sion of micromotor movement could be determined from Brow-
nian motion to directional motion, according to the parabolic
curve.[16] Thus, no signiﬁcant MSD deviation could be found on
CeO2 sample, due to its poor photothermal conversion and ex-
tremely short displacement. In comparison, the asymmetry of
Au-CeO2 micromotors induced the greatly enhanced MSD inten-
sity, reﬂecting the self-propulsion of the nanomotors under light
irradiation. Especially, MSD deviation of L-Au-CeO2 sample in-
creased rapidly and reached a higher value than other samples af-
ter the light irradiation for 30 s. It indicated the high velocity and
directionality of the movement were achieved on L-Au-CeO2 mi-
cromotor. Its average velocity was lower than Au NRs (Figure 5B),
which was mainly related to the Au exposure area for light irradi-
ation. However, the stronger MSD value on L-Au-CeO2 than that
on Au NRs implied the in situ manipulated motion with more di-
rectional feature, obviously due to its asymmetric thermophoretic
force (Figure 4). Thus, the rapid and directional movement for
reaching distant spots could be realized on L-Au-CeO2. Accord-
ingly, MSD intensity of D-Au-CeO2 and CS-Au-CeO2 were much
lower than that of L-Au-CeO2, corresponding to their weaker Au
exposure and lower photothermal conversion eﬃciency. Figure
S3 (Supporting Information) analyzed the linear ﬁtting of move-
ment trajectory by using two tracks of each sample to further il-
lustrate their directionality features, and their linear correlation
coeﬃcients ([R2]) were summarized in Figure 5D. It could be
found that [R2] value of pure CeO2 with the irregular vibration
was extremely low (<0.2), obviously due to its poor photother-
mal conversion ability. Owing to the uniformly distributed ther-
mal ﬁelds on both CS-Au-CeO2 and Au NRs (Figure 4), their
random motion direction could be conﬁrmed, since the [R2] val-
ues presented the great diﬀerences for two tracks. In compari-
son, D-Au-CeO2 and L-Au-CeO2 presented the high [R2] values
for both two tracks. Notably, [R2] values for both tracks of L-Au-
CeO2 were higher than 0.9, in addition to its higher MDS devi-
ation than D-Au-CeO2. It indicated that the motion trajectory of
L-Au-CeO2 well ﬁtted the linear trend due to the signiﬁcant direc-
tionality of motion besides the high velocity. As a result, it could
be found that the motion direction and velocity greatly depended
on the combination of Au and CeO2. The asymmetric structure of
Au-CeO2 micromotors facilitated the directional thermophoresis
force and photothermal driving for movement. It was well con-
sisted with the theoretical simulation in Figure 4.

2.4. Antibacterial Activity
Furthermore, antibacterial activity for E. coli under visible light
irradiation was investigated to conﬁrm the photothermal-driven
eﬀect on Au-CeO2 micromotors, as shown in Figure 6. Due
to the eﬀective photothermal-driving force (Figure 6A), all CS-
Au-CeO2, D-Au-CeO2, and L-Au-CeO2 samples presented sig-
niﬁcant antibacterial properties, compared to that without cat-
alyst (Figure 6B). It was obviously ascribed to the synergis-
tic photocatalytic-photothermal antibacterial eﬀect under the
condition without cooling water. More importantly, the eﬀec-
tive photothermal-propelling force on L-Au-CeO2 facilitated the
achievement of the highest antibacterial activity with killing al-
most all the bacteria after 30 min. It was obviously owing to its
in situ manipulated motion with high velocity and strong direc-
tionality, and thus eﬀectively contacted bacteria, facilitating the
synergistic photocatalytic-photothermal antibacterial process. In
comparison, Both D-Au-CeO2 and CS-Au-CeO2 presented lower
activity than L-Au-CeO2. Although the proportion of contained
CeO2 was higher, their symmetrical structures led to the weaker
photothermal driving and directional thermophoresis force for
movement. Additionally, the temperature evolutions of the sus-
pension (Figure 6C) exhibited a similar trend of temperature
increase during the antibacterial process on L-Au-CeO2, D-Au-
CeO2, and CS-Au-CeO2 micromotors. The temperature increase
of about 5 °C was higher than that of blank bacteria suspen-
sion after 30 min, since the amount of micromotor compos-
ites was very low. Thus, the temperature diﬀerence of micromo-
tors from the aqueous environment could be achieved, beneﬁ-
cial for enhancing the local thermal gradient and in situ manip-
ulated motion. Meanwhile, the positive zeta potentials of diﬀer-
ent samples further facilitated the contact with negative E. coli
(Figure S5, Supporting Information) and promoted the synergis-
tic photocatalytic-photothermal process.

the synergistic photocatalytic-photothermal antibacterial process.

velocity and strong directionality, further beneﬁcial for the syn-

know, the performance of photothermal driving greatly depends
on the velocity and directionality, both of which are crucial for
achieving the long movement distance.[45] The thermophoretic
eﬀect mainly inﬂuences the speed of motion, which is governed
by the intrinsic photothermal conversion capability and the tem-
perature gradient to the surrounding. At the same time, the di-
rectionality of motion is determined by the thermophoretic direc-
tion and can overcome the resistance of ﬂuid. Furthermore, the
thermophoretic direction is mainly controlled by the asymme-
try of local temperature gradient and can be adjusted by the mi-
cromotor morphology.[46,47] Obviously, asymmetric lollipop-like
L-Au-CeO2 micromotor with strong photothermal conversion ef-
fect showed more powerful photothermal driving capability than
other Au-CeO2 micromotors. For example, both symmetric sam-
ples of Au NRs and dumbbell-like D-Au-CeO2 were lack of suf-
ﬁcient temperature gradient and exhibited weaker photothermal
driving performance, although their photothermal conversion ef-
ﬁciency was similar to that of L-Au-CeO2. Therefore, the antibac-
terial property could be promoted by the multi-functioned optical
energy. Firstly, the photocatalytic property of CeO2 was greatly
enhanced by its strong interaction with Au NRs to extend the
region of light absorption and accelerate the electron transfer.
The strong oxidization ability of photo-generated holes acted as
the main active species and was beneﬁcial for killing the bacte-
ria. Furthermore, the unique photothermal conversion feature of
Au NRs resulted in the high temperature at the surface of Au-
CeO2 composite to kill the bacteria and generated the local ther-
mal gradient. More importantly, photothermal conversion eﬀect
of asymmetric Au-CeO2 induced the strong thermophoretic force
to achieve the in situ manipulated motion with high velocity and
strong directionality. Therefore, the eﬃcient attack on bacteria
could be realized by microscopic movement with long distance,
facilitating the synergistic photocatalytic-photothermal antibacte-
rial process. As a result, the bacteria was destroyed with the shriv-
eled and broken morphology by the powerful eﬀect of Au-CeO2
composites.
3. Conclusion
Photothermal-propelling eﬀect was successfully achieved on
asymmetric Au-CeO2 micromotors, facilitating the synergistic
photothermal-photocatalytic antibacterial activity. Asymmetric
lollipop-like L-Au-CeO2 micromotor with high Au exposure ra-
tio was obtained by constructing the strong interaction of CeO2
nanoparticles with Au NRs. As a result, the enhanced visible light
harvesting and the promoted photo-induced charge separation
were achieved. Especially, L-Au-CeO2 exhibited the powerful pho-
tothermal conversion eﬀect, and obtained the great local thermal
gradient. Then, the powerful photothermal-propelling force well
realized the in situ manipulated motion with high velocity and
strong directionality. The resulting microscopic long-distance
movement was beneﬁcial for the contact with bacteria. Conse-
quently, the synergistic photocatalytic-photothermal antibacterial
process was further promoted by the photothermal-driven ef-
fect, in addition to the individual eﬀect of thermal energy and
photocatalytic process. The signiﬁcance of this work highlighted
the great potential for practical sterilization applications in the
future.

Preparation of Materials:
Au NRs: Au NRs were prepared by a seed-
mediated growth method.[27] First, the gold seed solution was prepared by
mixing 0.25 mL HAuCl4 (0.01 mol L−1) and 9.75 mL CTAB (0.10 mol L−1)
and stirred at 300 rpm for 2 min in agitation device (MS-H340-S4, NAN-
BEI INSTRUMENT, Shanghai, China). Then, 0.60 mL ice-cold NaBH4
(0.01 mol L−1) was injected. The solution was then stirred for 2 min and
maintained at 25 °C for 2 h. The solution for the growth of gold seed
was obtained by mixing 40 mL CTAB (0.01 mol L−1) with 2.0 mL HAuCl4
(0.01 mol L−1), 0.40 mL AgNO3 (0.01 mol L−1), 0.80 mL HCl (1.0 mol L−1)
and 0.32 mL ascorbic acid (0.10 mol L−1), and then stirred until achieving
the transparent solution. Then, 0.10 mL gold seed solution was immedi-
ately introduced into the growth solution and kept at 37 °C for 12 h. The
products of Au NRs were washed by deionized water three times and ﬁnally
dispersed into 40 mL deionized water.
Au-CeO2 Composites: Au-CeO2 composites were prepared by guiding
the selective growth of CeO2 on Au NRs.[28] In the typical preparation pro-
cess, 40 mL Au NRs suspension was initially centrifuged on a centrifugal
machine (TGL-22M, Centrifugation device, Shanghai, China) at 8000 rpm
for 2 min, followed by being dispersed into 20 mL CTAB (0.30 mmol L−1)
and mixed with 0.80 mL K2PtCl4 (0.10 mmol L−1), 2.0 mL Ce(AC)3 (10
mmol L−1) and 17.2 mL deionized water. Then, the mixture was heated at
110 °C for 1.5 h, followed by the centrifugation at 8000 r/min and being
washed by deionized water at least twice. The obtained Au-CeO2 compos-
ite presented the asymmetric lollipop-like morphology and was denoted as
L-Au-CeO2. For comparison, core-shell Au-CeO2 (CS-Au-CeO2), dumbbell-
like Au-CeO2 (D-Au-CeO2), and the separated Au-CeO2 (Au/CeO2) was
achieved at the CTAB concentration of 0.010, 0.10, and 0.80 mmol L−1, re-
spectively. Pristine CeO2 was synthesized as the above route of Au/CeO2
without adding Au NRs.

Theoretical Simulation:
Calculation of Thermal Conductivity: The
phonon thermal conductivity of CeO2 in nanoscale was calculated by
Phonopy, and the phonon properties were obtained by ﬁrst-principles cal-
culations, since the thermal conductivity of CeO2 is dominated by quasi-
particles called phonons.[56] The thermal conductivity of metallic Au was
dominated by electrons and the electron thermal conductivity was calcu-
lated by the Electron-Phonon Physics from ﬁrst-principles (EPW) packet.
Its phonon thermal conductivity could be neglected due to the low ratio
to electron thermal conductivity (<2.0%). The calculation process and re-
lated parameter were in accordance with the previous report,[57] except
that a constant electron mean free path approximation was used. Bound-
ary scattering of electrons was included by Matthiessen’s rule.
Simulation of Photothermal Temperature Distribution: The temperature
distribution of micromotors was calculated by thermal diﬀusive equation
of COMSOL (4.x version, Svante Littmarck, Cambridge, Massachusetts,
America). In COMSOL, the water was set in a 200 × 200 × 200 nm cube and
considered as the surrounding environment, which was much larger than
the size of diﬀerent Au-CeO2 composite samples. The external light source
introduced into the system was an 808 nm NIR laser of 2.0 W cm−2 and
sustained for 0.5 s. Based on the particle size statistics in TEM images, the
average sizes of diﬀerent samples were simpliﬁed for simulation (Scheme
S1, Supporting Information), not aﬀecting the calculation accuracy.

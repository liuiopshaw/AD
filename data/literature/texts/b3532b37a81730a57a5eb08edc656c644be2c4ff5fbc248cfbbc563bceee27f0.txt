ABSTRACT
The rapid emergence of drug-resistant bacterial infections poses a significant global challenge to wound management, necessi-
tating the development of alternative antibacterial strategies beyond conventional antibiotics. Herein, a plant-mediated green
synthesis approach is employed for fabricating visible-light-responsive copper oxide/attapulgite (CuO/APT) nanocomposites
using Olea europaea leaf extract. The one-dimensional APT serves as an effective biological template to promote heterogeneous
nucleation and stabilization of ultrafine CuO nanoparticles (5–20 nm) with enhanced visible-light catalytic activity. The
nanocomposites are subsequently incorporated intoκ-carrageenan/carboxymethyl chitosan (KC/CC) matrices to develop advanced
photocatalytic wound dressings. The strong interfacial interactions between CuO/APT nanocomposites and biopolymer chains
ensure homogeneous dispersion and improved mechanical performance. The optimal composite films exhibit good hydrophilicity
with a water contact angle of 56.7◦, superior moisture retention capacity of 64.9 ± 13.4% after 1 h drying, well-balanced mechanical
properties, and enhanced antibacterial effect with 99.8% inhibition rate against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus. In
a rat model of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus -infected full-thickness wounds, the composite films significantly
accelerated healing through effective bacterial clearance, inflammation modulation, enhanced collagen deposition, and tissue
regeneration. This study provides a sustainable and scalable strategy for designing light-activated wound dressings with synergistic
antibacterial/pro-healing functions, offering a promising solution for combating drug-resistant wound infections.

Introduction
The escalating prevalence of drug-resistant bacterial infections
represents a critical challenge in modern wound care manage-
ment, leading to prolonged healing times, increased healthcare
costs, and elevated mortality rates. [1] Traditional antibiotic
therapies have become increasingly ineffective due to the rapid
evolution of microbial resistance mechanisms, urging the devel-
opment of alternative antibacterial strategies [2]. Among vari-
ous wound dressing formats, hydrogels and films have gained
considerable attention since the 1970s due to their favorable
wound microenvironment maintenance capabilities. Recently,
natural carbohydrate polymers, particularly polysaccharides,
have emerged as promising base materials for wound dressings
based on their inherent biocompatibility, biodegradability, low
cost, and minimal environmental impact [3, 4].

Effective wound management requires addressing multiple
simultaneous challenges, including excessive exudate control,
microbial invasion prevention, and promotion of tissue regen-
eration [5]. Particularly, the management of wound exudates
plays a crucial role in healing progression, as excessive mois-
ture can macerate periwound tissue and delay recovery. In
this context, the wettability and fluid handling properties of
dressing materials significantly influence cell adhesion, dis-
tribution, and proliferation processes. Our previous research
demonstrated that incorporating attapulgite (APT), a natural
one-dimensional clay mineral with a diameter of 20-70 nm and
a length of 0.5–5 µm, could enhance the hydrophilicity and
mechanical strength of polysaccharide-based hydrogels through
synergistic effects between APT nanorods and ionic crosslink-
ing [6]. Furthermore, ZnO-anchored APT nanocomposites were
developed to simultaneously improve mechanical strength and
antibacterial properties of chitosan-based films [7], which pro-
vided an inspiration for designing multifunctional wound dress-
ings through integration of active components into biopolymer
matrices.
Metal and metal oxide nanoparticles (NPs) have shown great
promise as antibacterial agents. Among various metal oxides,
copper oxide (CuO) NPs have attracted considerable interest due
to their excellent physicochemical properties, cost-effectiveness,
and broad-spectrum antibacterial activity [8]. The narrow band
gap (1.2 eV) and chemical stability of CuO NPs confer superior
photocatalytic performance under visible-light irradiation, which
presents a much greater ability for visible-light absorption and
utilization than ZnO NPs (∼3.37 eV), making them particularly
suitable for photocatalytic antibacterial applications in wound
management [9, 10]. However, rapid charge carrier recombina-
tion and limited specific surface area remain significant chal-
lenges that hinder photocatalytic efficiency. Besides, the tendency
of NPs to aggregate substantially compromises their antibacterial
efficacy and compatibility with polymer matrices [11, 12]. Recent
studies indicate that structural engineering of NPs, including size,
morphology, and defect manipulation, represents an effective
approach to suppress the recombination of photogenerated e−/h+
pair, thereby enhancing catalytic activity for reactive radical
generation [13–15]. Meanwhile, the immobilization of NPs onto
suitable supports has been proven effective in addressing these
limitations [16, 17]. APT has also been demonstrated to be an
excellent platform for immobilizing NPs, effectively regulating
their properties [18–20].
Conventional chemical synthesis of metallic nanomaterials often
involves toxic reagents, leaving harmful residues that com-
promise biocompatibility and environmental sustainability. In
contrast, plant-mediated green synthesis offers a promising
alternative due to its environmental friendliness and inherent
biocompatibility. However, a major challenge remains that the
complex and variable composition of plant extracts often leads to
insufficient control over the morphology, size, and dispersion of
NPs, limiting property optimization. Our previous success with
APT-supported NPs revealed that a biological template could
guide nucleation and improve structural outcomes [18, 21] This
study advances the concept by applying this templating strategy to
CuO NPs to achive a more controllable heterogeneous nucleation
process, allowing for precise regulation of the size, dispersion,
and defect density of CuO NPs for enhanced photocatalytic
activity [22–25] This approach not only retains the safety and
sustainability advantages of green synthesis but also aims to
endow CuO/APT nanocomposites with enhanced photocatalytic
and antibacterial properties. This facilitates the development of
light-activated antibacterial wound dressings, which are difficult
to achieve by simply using plant extracts or traditional chemical
methods.
Herein, we report an O. europaea leaf extract (OLE)-mediated
green synthesis strategy for preparing CuO/APT nanocomposites
with ultrasmall CuO NPs and enhanced visible-light-driven
antibacterial activity, and then the obtained nanocomposites
were incorporated into κ-carrageenan/carboxymethyl chitosan
(KC/CC) matrices to fabricate CuO/APT-KC/CC composite
antibacterial films (Scheme 1). O. europaea leaves are rich
in phenolic compounds, in which water-soluble oleuropein is
particularly effective in promoting metal oxide NPs formation
[26]. The structure, morphology, surface activity, photocat-
alytic performance, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation,
and antibacterial efficacy of CuO/APT nanocomposites were
systematically investigated. Furthermore, the physical prop-
erties, in vitro antibacterial activity, wound healing promo-
tion, and biocompatibility of the resulting composite films
were also comprehensively evaluated. The as-developed mate-
rials demonstrate significant potential as antibacterial wound
dressings for promoting healing of drug-resistant bacterial
infections.

Results and Discussion
2.1
Fabrication and Characterizations of
CuO/APT Nanocomposites
CuO/APT nanocomposites were successfully synthesized via an
OLE-mediated green approach (Figure 1a). Based on previous
studies, oleuropein in OLE plays a crucial role in metal oxide
formation [26], which can promote CuO formation through
chelation with copper ions [27], and subsequent annealing at
400◦C promotes the crystallization of CuO NPs. The structure
and morphology were characterized by transmission electron
microscopy (TEM). As shown in Figure S1, pristine attapulgite
(APT) displays a typical nanorod morphology. In contrast, the
images of CuO/APT nanocomposites clearly reveal that well-
dispersed CuO NPs (5–20 nm in size) are anchored on the
surfaces of APT nanorods (Figure 1b and Figure S2). The small
particle size distribution reflects effective capping by bioactive
phytochemicals and stabilization by APT nanorods. Most CuO
NPs exhibited near-spherical morphology, with occasional larger
particles showing multidimensional shapes. Selected-area elec-
tron diffraction (SAED) confirmed the polycrystalline nature and
phase purity of the synthesized product. X-ray diffraction (XRD)
patterns of CuO/APT nanocomposites (Figure 1c) displayed
characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to the monoclinic
tenorite phase of CuO (JCPDS card No. 21-1272).

insights into the chemical states and surface composition of
CuO/APT nanocomposites. As can be seen from the survey

2.2
Light-Triggered Responsive Performance and
Antibacterial Activities of CuO/APT
Nanocomposites
Most metal oxide semiconductor materials possess large band
gaps, such as TiO2 and ZnO with band gaps of 3.2 eV [31] and
3.37 eV [32], respectively, limiting their visible-light absorption
primarily to UV regions. However, UV irradiation poses potential
mutagenicity and biohazards that restrict biological applications
[33]. In this regard, CuO with a narrow band gap could produce
ROS triggered by visible-light, offering significant promise for
biomedical applications. The visible-light catalytic activity of
CuO/APT nanocomposites was evaluated through photocatalytic
degradation of methyl orange (MO) dye. After establishing
adsorption/desorption equilibrium in darkness, H2O2 was added
to initiate the photocatalytic reaction. Figure 2a shows time-
dependent decreases in absorption intensity at 462 nm, with a
complete MO degradation after 60 min of irradiation.
For the quantitative analysis of dye degradation, the decrease in
dye content (Ct/C0) as a function of exposure time was plotted
for APT and CuO/APT nanocomposites (Figure 2b), where Ct
and Co refer to the final (at time t) and initial dye concentration,
respectively. It revealed that the degradation rate of CuO/APT
nanocomposites to MO was about 98% after 60 min, which
was significantly higher than that of APT, and the apparent
reaction rate constant (kapp) was 6.3 × 10−2 (Figure 2c), which is
prominently bigger than that of APT (9.3 × 10−4). These results
suggest that the CuO/APT nanocomposites exhibited excellent
visible-light catalytic activity in the presence of H2O2. The gradual
discoloration of MO as a result of the catalysis degradation can
also be directly visualized from the digital image of the dye
solutions at different exposure times, where the initial deep
orange color of MO gradually faded as the light exposure time
increased (Figure 2d).
H2O2 is a kind of better electron trapping agent, and is more likely
to react with photogenerated electrons to generate ∙OH, which
could subsequently inhibit the recombination of electron and
hole pair, thereby improving the photocatalytic efficiency greatly
[34, 35]. In order to elucidate the underlying photocatalytic
mechanism, ∙OH radical formation during photocatalytic degra-
dation was detected using a terephthalic acid photoluminescence
probing (TA-PL) technique, which has been previously used as
a probe for in situ quantification of ∙OH radicals that form
in bulk solutions [35]. This method is based on the reaction
between terephthalic acid (TA) and ∙OH radicals to produce a
fluorescent product of 2-hydroxyterephthalic acid (TAOH). The
resulting fluorescence (FL) peak intensity is directly propor-
tional to the amount of ∙OH generated, thereby providing a
measure of ∙OH production during the photocatalytic process.
As shown in Figure 2e, the CuO/APT + H2O2 + light system
exhibited strong photoluminescence signals (λ > 420 nm), indi-
cating substantial ∙OH generation. Minimal ∙OH production was
detected for CuO/APT nanocomposites with H2O2 in darkness or
with light alone, suggesting that ∙OH generation predominantly
occurs through a CuO/APT-mediated Fenton-like reaction that
facilitates H2O2 decomposition [36].
ROS-induced oxidative stress represents a primary antibacterial
mechanism of metal oxide NPs [37]. Herein, the antibacterial
properties of CuO/APT nanocomposites against two resistant
bacteria were evaluated by a simple colony counting method,
and the results are shown in Figures 2f, g. All sample-containing
groups showed reduced bacterial colonies and significantly
increased inhibition ratios compared with the controls. For
MRSA, the groups with H2O2 and light treatment exhibited
superior antibacterial effects compared with the group of
CuO/APT alone. The increase in the antibacterial activity in the
presence of H2O2 primarily resulted from ROS generation, while
light irradiation promoted Cu2+ release. The combination of
CuO/APT nanocomposites, H2O2, and visible-light demonstrated
the strongest antibacterial activities against extended-spectrum
β-lactamase-producing
Escherichia coli
(ESBL-E. coli)
and
methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). The synergy
indices of between CuO/APT+H2O2 and CuO/APT+light groups
against ESBL-E. coli and MRSA were 0.9 and 0.92, respectively,
with both values below 1, indicating synergistic antibacterial
effects between Cu2+ release and photocatalytic ROS production.
2.3
Characterizations and Properties of
CuO/APT-KC/CC Composite Films
Given their excellent visible-light-driven antibacterial activity,
CuO/APT nanocomposites were incorporated into KC/CC matri-
ces to fabricate CuO/APT-KC/CC composite films. Scanning elec-
tron microscopy (SEM) images of film cross-sections (Figure 3a
i-vi) showed increasing roughness with higher CuO/APT con-
tents in the composite films. Films containing 2.5% (v) and 5%
(vi) of CuO/APT nanocomposites exhibited noticeable granular
structures, indicating significant morphological changes induced
by the incorporation of CuO/APT nanocomposites. The energy
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mappings of 5%CuO/APT-
KC/CC films surface are shown in Figure 3b. The signals of
C, O, Cu, and Si were uniformly co-localized, corresponding to
the polymer matrix (C, O), CuO NPs (Cu), and APT nanorods
(Si, along with characteristic elements such as Mg, Al, and
Fe). This homogeneous elemental dispersion directly confirms
the uniform distribution of the CuO/APT nano-fillers within
the polymer matrix, which was facilitated by the interactions
of hydrogen bonding between hydrophilic groups on APT and
hydroxyl groups of the polymer chains [38]. The Fourier trans-
form infrared (FTIR) spectra are presented in Figure 3c. A broad
peak at 3302 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibrations of
O-H and N-H bonds. The peaks at 2927 and 2880 cm−1 can be
assigned to C-H stretching vibrations. Furthermore, the band at
1590 cm−1 is attributed to the bending vibration of -NH2, while
the peak at 1035 cm−1 arises from the C-O-C stretching vibration
of the glycosidic structure [39]. Notably, the shifts in these
characteristic bands with increasing CuO/APT nanocomposites
content indicate a molecular interaction between the CuO/APT
nanocomposites and the polymer matrix.

Mechanical properties of CuO/APT-KC/CC films depend sig-
nificantly on interfacial interactions between the CuO/APT
nanocomposites and the polymer matrix. The uniform dispersion
of CuO/APT nanocomposites and consequent physical crosslink-
ing enhanced stress transfer under tensile loading [6]. Tensile
strength (Figure 4a) showed significant improvements (p ≤
0.05) for composite films with 0.25% and 0.5% of CuO/APT
nanocomposites compared with pure KC/CC films. At higher
loadings, tensile strength maintained comparable to that of pure
KC/CC films. The enhanced stiffness at lower concentrations
of CuO/APT nanocomposites resulted from good interfacial
compatibility and uniform filler dispersion, whereas at higher
concentrations, aggregation of CuO/APT nanocomposites cre-
ated structural defects under tensile stress [40, 41]. Interest-
ingly, the elongation at break decreased at lower CuO/APT
concentrations (0.25%–0.5%) but increased gradually at higher
concentrations (1%-5%). By contrast, composite films containing
2.5% and 5% of CuO/APT nanocomposites showing significant
improvements (p ≤0.01). FTIR results indicated pronounced
peak shifts at higher CuO/APT loadings, suggesting strong
interactions that might restrict polymer-polymer interactions
while enhancing macromolecular mobility and film flexibility
[42]. Overall, the incorporation of CuO/APT nanocomposites
improved the mechanical properties of KC/CC films, and the
composite films with 2.5% and 5% of CuO/APT nanocomposites
exhibited an optimal balance between toughness and stiffness,
which was crucial for practical wound dressing applications.
It is well-known that KC and CC are inherently hydrophilic due
to abundant -OH groups in KC and -NH2/-OH groups in CC,
providing strong water affinity and moisture retention capacity.
Water contact angle measurements (Figure 4b) showed that pure
KC/CC films had an initial contact angle of 68.1◦, and then
decreased to 44.6◦after 30 s, indicating good wettability and rapid
water absorption. CuO/APT-KC/CC composite films exhibited
reduced contact angles with increasing CuO/APT nanocompos-
ites content, and films containing 1%, 2.5%, and 5% of CuO/APT
nanocomposites had similar values. The 5%CuO/APT-KC/CC
films showed an initial contact angle of 56.7◦, which decreased to
35.3◦within 30 s, demonstrating improved wettability alongside
rapid water absorption.
Swelling and water retention properties were further investi-
gated. As presented in Figure 4c, all films reached swelling
rates of 240%-330% after 24 h immersion, with pure KC/CC
films exhibiting higher swelling degrees than composite films.
The observed decrease for composite films could be attributed
to the enhanced structural integrity and restricted polymer
chain mobility induced by the nano-fillers. The incorporation
of CuO/APT nanocomposites acted as multifunctional physical
cross-linking points within the KC/CC matrix via hydrogen
bonding interactions [38]. This increased cross-linking density
reinforces the hydrogel network, thereby limiting the expansion
space available for water absorption [40]. In addition, it’s worth
noting that the swelling rates of the composite films presented a
non-monotonic trend with increasing CuO/APT nanocomposites
content. The films with 1% loading displayed the lowest swelling
rate, which is attributed to the optimal formation of a cross-linked
network that effectively restricts polymer chain mobility. In
contrast, the higher swelling of films with 0.5% loading indicates

2.4
In Vitro Antibacterial Activity and
Mechanism of CuO/APT-KC/CC Composite Films
Although KC and CC possess inherent antibacterial properties,
their efficacy against drug-resistant bacteria is limited. Incor-
porating visible-light-active CuO/APT nanocomposites could
enhance the antibacterial performance of KC/CC films against
drug-resistant pathogens. As shown in Figures 5a–d, the antibac-
terial results indicate that KC/CC films had moderate activity
against MRSA but limited efficacy against ESBL-E. coli, which
might be attributed to a basal bactericidal effect from polysac-
charide molecules released from the dissolved films (which could
be found only for the pure KC/CC films in solution). Statis-
tical analysis of the data from three independent experiments
confirmed that antibacterial activity was significantly enhanced
when the CuO/APT nanocomposites content in the composite
films reached 0.5% compared with pure KC/CC films. However,
the composite films with 0.25% loading showed only a marginal
improvement and displayed no statistically significant difference
from the pure films, which is likely due to the insufficient
density of active sites and incomplete formation of an effective
photocatalytic network at this very low filler concentration.
Furthermore, for both ESBL-E. coli and MRSA, the results
showed that there was no statistically significant difference in
antibacterial activities of the composite films when the filler
content was increased from 2.5% to 7.5%. SEM images revealed
the facts of aggregation of CuO/APT nanocomposites within
the polymer matrix at higher loading levels (e.g., 7.5%). This
aggregation likely reduced the effective surface area available for
photocatalytic reactions and ion release, hindered light penetra-
tion, and increased the recombination rate of photogenerated
charge carriers. Consequently, the generation and diffusion of
ROS might be compromised, leading to the observed stagnation
in antibacterial performance. This phenomenon of diminishing
returns beyond an optimal filler concentration is commonly
observed in photocatalytic nanocomposites.
Antibacterial activity improved significantly with increasing
CuO/APT nanocomposites content under visible-light irradiation
with H2O2. Films exposed to light without H2O2 also showed bac-
terial inhibition (Figure S4), consistent with the results observed
for CuO/APT nanocomposites, which is potentially due to the
bacterial adhesion and enhanced Cu2+ release under light irradi-
ation. The enhanced bactericidal efficacy of 5%CuO/APT-KC/CC
films stemed from combined ∙OH generation and Cu2+ release.
Additionally, the adhesive properties of KC/CC films promote
bacteria-material interactions, shortening ROS diffusion path-
ways and enhancing antibacterial activity. While antibacterial
efficacy improved significantly at higher CuO/APT nanocompos-
ites concentrations (2.5%-7.5%), the 5% formulation was selected
as optimal for wound dressing applications based on the consid-
erations of that, the increased cytotoxicity of the composite films
with 7.5% of CuO/APT nanocomposites (Figures 7a–f) and the
superior water absorption/retention of 5%CuO/APT-KC/CC com-
pared with 2.5%CuO/APT-KC/CC. Notably, CuO/APT-KC/CC
composite films showed superior efficacy against MRSA (99.8%
inhibition) than ESBL-E. coli, indicating higher Gram-negative
bacterial tolerance due to their unique cell wall structure (con-
taining phospholipids and lipopolysaccharides) and antioxidant
defense mechanisms that hinder Cu2+ and ROS penetration [26,
43].
FL images of live/dead staining (Figures 5e,f) revealed a greater
number of dead (red) bacteria in the 5%CuO/APT-KC/CC + H2O2
+ Light and 5%CuO/APT-KC/CC + Light groups. Quantitative
analysis of dead cell ratios for MRSA (Figure 5g) showed that
the 5%CuO/APT-KC/CC + H2O2 + Light group was significantly
more effective than the groups of 5%CuO/APT-KC/CC + H2O2,
5%CuO/APT-KC/CC + Light, and KC/CC + H2O2 + Light,
while the latter two groups showed no significant difference
from each other. For ESBL-E. coli (Figure 5h), the dead cell
ratio in the 5%CuO/APT-KC/CC + H2O2 + Light group was
not significantly different from that in the 5%CuO/APT-KC/CC
+ Light group, but both of them were significantly higher

2.5
In Vitro Biocompatibility and Healing
Performance of CuO/APT-KC/CC Composite Films
Biocompatibility remains a critical consideration for metal-
based medical materials. Copper is an essential trace element
maintained at low cellular concentrations through homeostatic
mechanisms. Once it exceeds the threshold, copper will become
toxic and lead to cell death [49]. CuO NPs can enter cells
via endocytosis and release Cu2+ in acidic lysosomal environ-
ments, causing cellular toxicity [50]. CuO NPs are immobi-
lized through APT-polymer interactions, restricting release and
reducing endocytosis-mediated toxicity. Thus, the cytotoxicity of
CuO/APT-KC/CC composite films primarily depends on ROS
generation and extracellular Cu2+ levels. Cell Counting Kit-8
(CCK-8) assays (Figure 7a) showed no significant cytotoxic-
ity for KC/CC films and CuO/APT-KC/CC composite films
with 0.25%-1% of CuO/APT nanocomposites, as the cells grew
and proliferated well over 24 h. Films with 2.5% and 5% of
CuO/APT nanocomposites showed some inhibition of cell prolif-
eration but performed better than 7.5%CuO/APT-KC/CC, which
significantly suppressed proliferation. Live/dead cell images
and cell visibility rate (Figures 7b,c) for KC/CC, 5%CuO/APT-
KC/CC, and 7.5%CuO/APT-KC/CC further demonstrated that
the composite films exhibited no significant cytotoxicity, con-
firming good biocompatibility of the composite films at optimal
concentrations.
Wound healing potential was evaluated through in vitro scratch
wound healing assays using NIH3T3 cells. This assay simulated
the healing process by creating a scratch in a confluent mono-
layer, and the rate of gap closure, driven by cell migration and
proliferation, was quantified to assess the pro-healing effect of
the test materials. The migration images and scratch closure
area are shown in Figures 7d and 7e, respectively. The migration
images showed that 5%CuO/APT-KC/CC films showed signif-
icantly enhanced cell migration compared with KC/CC films
and controls after 96 h incubation, indicating that 5%CuO/APT-
KC/CC films are more capable of promoting cell growth and
wound healing. Interestingly, KC/CC films showed poorer
migration than the controls, which was likely due to medium
viscosity increases from polymer dissolution hindering cell
movement.

2.6
In Vivo Antibacterial and Wound Repair
Performance
Based on promising in vitro results, the effect of CuO/APT-
KC/CC composite films on a MRSA-infected skin wound was
evaluated. Different groups, including control groups, KC/CC
films, and CuO/APT-KC/CC composite films, were used to treat
the infected wounds. As shown in Figures 8a–c, wounds in
different groups showed distinct healing patterns. By day 7, an
evident inflammatory response could be observed in the con-
trol groups, while inflammatory symptoms gradually subsided
and the wound defects were noticeably reduced in the groups
of KC/CC films and CuO/APT-KC/CC composite films. The
CuO/APT-KC/CC composite films groups achieved 78% wound
closure (22% remaining area), significantly outperforming both
the controls (p < 0.01) and KC/CC groups (p < 0.05). The
enhanced healing in CuO/APT-KC/CC composite films groups
correlated with strong antibacterial activity, as measured by
colony-forming unit (CFU) assays (Figure 8d and S5).
Furthermore, the CuO/APT-KC/CC composite films groups
demonstrated an obvious reduction in bacterial load compared
with the control groups at day 7. This bactericidal effect was
attributed to the combined actions of CuO/APT nanocom-
posites and the KC/CC matrix, which effectively controlled
MRSA proliferation in the wound bed. However, no statisti-
cally significant difference was found for wound closure rates
among the three groups by day 14, which could primarily be
attributed to the robust innate healing capacity of rat models,
whereby even untreated wounds could achieve substantial clo-
sure over time through natural repair processes. Notably, even the
closure rates were similar by day 14, wounds treated with the
composite films exhibited a visually superior appearance with
better flatness compared with the control groups. Accordingly,
the observed therapeutic advantages of the composite films,
including accelerated healing, infection inhibition, and improved
healing quality, were predominantly manifested during this
critical early period, as clearly evidenced by the effect within the
first week.
Histopathological evaluation provided mechanistic insights into
the healing process (Figures 8e,f). H&E staining revealed numer-
ous inflammatory cells in the control and KC/CC groups,
while CuO/APT-KC/CC groups showed reduced inflammatory
cell infiltration, indicating the resolution of pro-inflammatory
phases (green arrows). Masson staining revealed that little
collagen deposition was detected in the control groups, while
increased collagen deposition with the formation of collagen
fibril bundles could be observed in KC/CC and CuO/APT-
KC/CC groups. By contrast, the increased degree of collagen
deposition was highest in the CuO/APT-KC/CC group, highlight-
ing enhanced tissue repair. Additionally, the CuO/APT-KC/CC
group exhibited better-organized collagen fibril architecture
and early reappearance of dermal appendages in the wound
region (yellow arrows), which was indicative of the recon-
stitution of adnexal architecture, indicating favorable healing
outcomes [55]. The improved healing in CuO/APT-KC/CC groups
demonstrates that enhanced antibacterial activity effectively
eliminated bacteria, improved inflammatory microenvironments,
and created conditions conducive to infected wound repair [56],
which is consistent with the results of in vitro wound healing
assays.
Immunohistochemical analysis elucidated the molecular mecha-
nisms underlying these observations. As shown in Figure 8g, the
results observed at day 7 indicated that the expression level of
IL-10 was significantly higher in the CuO/APT-KC/CC treatment
groups than in the other groups. In contrast, the expression
level of the pro-inflammatory genes TNF-α was significantly
lower in the CuO/APT-KC/CC groups compared with the control
and KC/CC groups (Figure 8h). These findings indicate that
CuO/APT-KC/CC treatment effectively promoted the expres-
sion of anti-inflammatory genes while concurrently suppressing
inflammatory responses, thereby contributing to reduced tissue
damage [57].
These findings demonstrate that CuO/APT-KC/CC composite
films promoted wound healing through a multimodal mecha-
nism involving direct bactericidal activity against MRSA, mod-
ulation of the inflammatory microenvironment, stimulation
of extracellular matrix regeneration, and acceleration of tis-
sue remodeling. The therapeutic superiority over pure KC/CC
films highlights the critical role of antibacterial functionality
in achieving these synergistic effects, establishing CuO/APT-
KC/CC composite films as a promising bioactive dressing for
infected wound management.

Experimental Section
5.1
Preparation of CuO/APT Nanocomposites
OLE was prepared following the procedure reported in our
previous study [11], which was then used to synthesize CuO/APT
nanocomposites. In brief, 2 g of APT were dispersed into 30 mL
of ultrapure water, and ultrasonically treated for 30 min, and then
1.5 g of copper acetate was added into the suspension, which was
stirred for 1 h and transferred into a three-neck flask. 100 mL of
OLE was added gradually into the three-neck flask and stirred for
30 min, and the pH was maintained to 9-10. Then the mixture
was heated to 60 ◦C and reacted for 7 h, and the precipitation was
collected by centrifugation after cooling, washed for three times,
dried at 80◦C for 15 h, and the solid product after grinding and
sifting was annealed at 400◦C for 2 h to get the final CuO/APT
nanocomposites.
5.2
Preparation of CuO/APT-KC/CC Composite
Films
KC solution was prepared by dissolving 0.6 g of KC into 20 mL
of distilled water with vigorous stirring at 70-90◦C for 30 min.
While 0.9 g of CC was completely dissolved in 30 mL water at
40◦C to obtained CC solution. A certain amount of samples were
ultrasonically dispersed in 10 mL of water, then poured into CC
solution and stirred for 30 min. After that, the KC solution and
1% glycerol were added into the mixture with vigorous stirring.
The final film solution was sonicated for 30 min, and 20 mL of
the solution was poured onto a glass dish with a diameter of
8.5 cm and dried at room temperature (20 ± 2◦C) for 48 h to
form the film. Finally, the dried films were conditioned at 25◦C
and 50% RH for 3 days before further testing. The prepared film
samples were labeled as KC/CC, 0.25% CuO/APT-KC/CC, 0.5%
CuO/APT-KC/CC, 1% CuO/APT-KC/CC, 2.5% CuO/APT-KC/CC,
5% CuO/APT-KC/CC, 7.5% CuO/APT-KC/CC according to the
added amounts of CuO/APT nanocomposites.
5.3

irradiated with visible-light, where the distance between lamps
to suspension surface was kept fixed at 15 cm. Small volumes of
dye solution were withdrawn at regular intervals (0, 10, 20, 30,
40, 50, 60 min) from the reactor and centrifuged to separate the

5.4
Detection of ∙OH by TA probe and EPR
spectra
The generated ∙OH was detected using the TA-PL probing
technique. TA solution (5.0 mm) was added into the CuO/APT
solution (3 mg/mL) in sodium citrate buffer with and without
H2O2 and irradiated by light or in darkness. After incubation for
30 min at room temperature, the solution was centrifuged for
fluorescence measurement with 315 nm as excitation wavelength
and 435 nm as emission wavelength. Electron paramagnetic
resonance (EPR) spectroscopy with 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-
oxide (DMPO) as a spin trap was employed to detect hydroxyl
radical (∙OH) generation. Briefly, a mixture was prepared by
adding 1 mL of 0.5 m DMPO to 8 mL of 0.1 m HAc-NaAc buffer
containing 20 mg of 5%CuO/APT-KC/CC and 1 mL of 0.5 m H2O2.
The resulting mixtures were then incubated for 10 min under light
irradiation and in the dark, respectively. Finally, the resulting
solution was extracted by a quartz capillary tube and analyzed
using the EPR spectrometer.
5.5
Cu2+ Release Measurement
The release profiles of Cu2+ ions from samples were evaluated
using graphite furnace atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS,
AA-3800). Specifically, samples of CuO/APT nanocomposites
and the nanocomposite film were immersed in sodium citrate
buffer and then subjected to either simulated visible-light irra-
diation or maintained in complete darkness. At predetermined
time points, 1 mL of each dispersion was withdrawn and filtered
through a 0.22 µm syringe filter to collect the supernatant for
subsequent analysis. After appropriate dilution, the released Cu2+
was quantified using an AAS system. A calibration curve was
established using a series of standard copper solutions. The
cumulative mass of released Cu2+ was determined at each time
point from the measured concentration, and the release rate (%)
was calculated based on the total copper content in the initial
sample.
5.6
Antibacterial Activity Assay
Bacteria were cultured in nutrient agar media overnight at 37
◦C, and then a single colony on agar media was used to prepare
a bacterial suspension of 1 × 106 CFU/mL in MH medium. For
CuO/APT nanocomposites, the 10 µL of CuO/APT (0.5 mg/mL for
ESBL-E. coli and MRSA), 10 µL of H2O2 (1 mm), 20 µL of bacterial
suspension and 60 µL of sodium citrate buffer were added to 48-
well plate, while for films, the 10 µL of CuO/APT nanocomposites
was replaced by 10 µL of sodium citrate buffer and a piece of film
with diameter of 1 cm. After visible-light irradiation (40 W) for
1 h, 20 µL of the bacterial suspension was spread on a standard
agar medium plate. Meanwhile, the groups without visible-light
irradiation, the groups without H2O2 and visible-light irradiation,
and the groups without H2O2 were tested for the control. Next,
the bacteria were incubated at 37◦C for 18–20 h, and the bacterial
colonies were recorded.
5.7
Morphology Observation of Bacteria
The morphology of bacteria after treatment with the films under
visible-light irradiation for 60 min was observed by SEM. The
bacterial suspensions after concentrated by centrifugation were
dropped on the surface of a microscope slide. After incubated at
37◦C for 4 h, the bacteria were fixed with 2.5% paraformaldehyde
for 4 h, and dehydrated in a gradient by 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%,
90%, and 100% alcohol for 15 min, respectively. Following that,
the samples were dried for the morphology observation.
5.8
In Vitro Healing Performance Evaluation
NIH-3T3 was incubated in a cell incubator (5% CO2) at 37◦C. The
complete culture medium containing dulbecco’s modified eagle
medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS,
Cytiva, USA), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (10,000 U/mL,
GIBCO, USA) was refreshed every 2–3 days. When the fusion rate
of adherent cells reached more than 90%, subculture was carried
out.
5.9
Scratch Assays
1 × 105 CFU/mL NIH/3T3 was seeded into a 6-well plate and
incubated for 24 h until the fusion degree reaches over 90%.
At 0 h, a 10 µL pipette tip was used to create a scratch zone
in the cell monolayer. The old DMEM WAS refreshed with a
complete DMEM containing samples with the concentration of
0.06 mg/mL. The scratched wound was recorded by an inverted
fluorescence microscope every 24 h. An Image J software is used
to quantify the scratch area and compare it with the initial scratch
area.
5.10
Transwell Migration Assays
The migration of NIH/3T3 fibroblasts was evaluated by Transwell
chamber. Complete DMEM containing different materials with a
concentration of 0.06 mg/mL was added to a 24-well plate, and
200 µL of NIH/3T3 cell suspension (DMEM medium containing
1% FBS) with a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU/mL was added
to a Transwell chamber. After 24 h of culture, the cells on
the Transwell membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde,
stained with 1% crystal violet for 45 min, and observed and
recorded under optical microscope.

5.12
In Vivo Wound Healing Evaluation with
MRSA-Infected Full-thickness Skin Defect Model
The wound repair ability of the composite films was assessed
by constructing a model of MRSA-infected full-thickness skin
defect. 3 m Tegaderm dressings were used as a control group.
Infected wounds of 10 mm in diameter were constructed on
the back of rats, and the composite film was directly applied to
cover the wound area, secured with 3 m Tegaderm dressings,
and actively irradiated at the wound site using a xenon lamp
simulating visible-light for approximately 30 min. The wounds
were sampled at different times (0, 3, 7, and 14 days) to assess
the wound repair status. The details are shown in Supporting
Information.
5.13
Statistical Analysis and Ethics
Each experiment was performed with at least three replicate
measurements. Data are presented as the means ± standard
deviations (means ± SD). Statistical analysis was performed using
Microsoft Excel and IBM SPSS Statistics 22. One-way analyses
of variance (ANOVA) test and Student’s t-test were used to
determine the statistical differences among groups. The values
of *p < 0.05, and **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001 were considered
as significant. All experiments were approved by the medical
Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital & Clinical Medical
School, Lanzhou University (Approval Number: 2023A-053), and
performed in compliance with the guidelines of the Animal
Committee of the second hospital & clinical medical school,
Lanzhou University.

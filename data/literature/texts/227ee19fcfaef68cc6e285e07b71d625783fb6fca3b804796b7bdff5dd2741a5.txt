ABSTRACT
Graphene oxide (GO) has attracted research attention as a promising biomedical material principally owing to its biocompatibility
as well as excellent antibacterial properties, although the exact mechanism for the apparently conflicting both activities remains
controversial yet. We present controlled physicochemical and biomimetic features of GO that exert antibacterial effects via selective
destabilization of the bacterial membrane. Our model cell study, exploiting artificial vesicular phospholipid assembly along
with spectroscopic analyses, finds that surface oxygen functionalities of GO determine antibacterial activity by highly specific
interaction with POPG, a phospholipid selectively present in membranes of various bacterial species, including drug-resistant
bacteria. Furthermore, GO-incorporated nanofibers were evaluated in infected wound models in mice and pigs, where they
effectively suppressed bacterial growth and accelerated wound healing with minimal inflammation. These findings highlight the
potential use of GO as a safe and sustainable antibacterial to avoid repeated overuse of conventional antibiotics.
1
Introduction
Widespread overuse of antibiotics and the consequent emergence
of antibiotic resistance have prompted the need for newer and
safer alternatives. There is a need for better treating antibiotic-
resistant bacterial variants, minimizing nosocomial infections
in implanted biomaterials, treating chronic infections and sur-
face ulcers, among many others. Biomimetic engineering of
nanoparticles provides an avenue to potent antibacterials by
offering strong antibiotic-like activity on their often intricately
shaped surfaces [1–4]. Adoption of nanomaterials for clinical
use, nonetheless, necessitates thorough assessment of efficacy,
toxicity, and feasibility of production [5–8]. For instance, metal
nanoparticles, such as silver or copper, act via ion release and are
simple to make, but their in vivo use induces oxidative stress and
cellular damage [9–11].
Low-dimensional carbons, such as carbon quantum dots, carbon
nanotubes (CNTs), graphene, and their derivatives, have attracted
interest as antibacterials [12–14]. CNTs are well-known for the

strong antibacterial effect due to its needle-like morphology,
however, it can lead to damage of cell membranes [15–19]. On
the other hand, graphene has a sheet-like morphology and thus
offers a large surface area and mechanical flexibility, enabling
intimate interactions with bacterial cell surfaces [20–23]. Unlike
chemically inert pristine graphene, graphene oxide (GO), an
oxidized form of graphene [24–26], possesses abundant oxygen
functional groups that enable processability in aqueous disper-
sion and chemical functionalization [27–37]. These distinctive
features promote more effective adhesion to and intimate contact
with biological cell membranes, contributing to antibacterial
activity [38, 39]. The antibacterial action of GO has been suggested
by physicochemical mechanisms primarily based on simulations
[40, 41]. Unfortunately, the exact antibacterial mechanism of
GO has rarely been elucidated under experimentally relevant
conditions, particularly along with the consideration of its
biocompatibility.
To date, the biocompatibility of GO has prompted its exploitation
for biomedical applications, such as drug delivery and tissue
engineering [42–45]. Noticeably, an intriguing recent study pre-
sented that straightforward respiratory inhalation of small-sized
GO sheets by human subjects was well tolerated without causing
any negative effects in the pulmonary or cardiovascular systems,
nor any inflammatory responses [46, 47]. Such a biocompatibility
has been suggested to be driven by the rapid excretion of GO after
administration, which may depend on the degree of oxidation
or purity of GO [48–51]. Other proposed explanations have
primarily been based on the phenomenological observations [52].
While considering the commonly conflicting feature between
biocompatibility and antibacterial activity in the absence of a
carefully designed antibiotic functionality, the molecular-level
interaction between GO and the biological system needs to be
clarified.
In this study, we investigate the sustainable antibacterial activity
of GO at the molecular level, focusing on its selective interaction
with palmitoyloleoylphosphatidylglycerol (POPG), a phospho-
lipid representative of bacteria. By investigating chemically
modified GOs with various physical and chemical features, we
clarified that the oxygen functional groups on the GO surface
offer highly selective interaction with the bacterial membrane
phospholipids (Figure 1A). The antibacterial activity of GO in
various forms (e.g., films, nanofibers, and powders) was assessed
in vivo using infected wound models in mice and, notably, in a
porcine model that is histologically analogous to humans. Apply-
ing GO on the wounds resulted in potent antibacterial effects
that led to accelerated wound closure rates, while exhibiting
minimal blood clotting and inflammation reactions. This study
not only uncovers the origin of the biocompatible antibacterial
effect by GO, but also prospects the potential use of GO for generic
antibacterial therapy that can be safely applied in the long term.
2
Results and Discussions
2.1
Antibacterial Activity of Chemically
Functionalized GO
A series of chemically modified graphene nanosheets was pre-
pared by physical and chemical modification of GO, and their
antibacterial effect was examined [53–57]. GO nanosheets with
various lateral dimensions were generated (Figure 1B; Figure
S1), and either modified by thermal reduction to control the
oxygen content (Figure 1C; Figures S2 and S3, and Table S1),
or by nitrogen-doping (Figure 1D; Figure S4). All GO sheets
displayed strong antibacterial efficacy, indicating the dominant
surface-to-surface interactions with the membranes, with the
growth suppression of 96–99% (Figure 1E). Notably, the oxygen
content significantly influenced the antibacterial efficacy, with
the higher oxygen level leading to the stronger antibacterial effect
(Figure 1F). While moderately reduced GO (MrGO) and rGO
displayed reduced efficacies to 68% and 57% suppression, respec-
tively, the unmodified GO exhibited the strongest antibacterial
activity up to > 99% suppression of bacterial growth. Nitrogen-
doped GO (NGO) resulted in the lowest suppression (60%),
indicating that chemical N-doping weakens the antibacterial
effects (Figure 1G). rGO and reduced nitrogen-doped GO (rNGO)
also showed inferior antibacterial performances with 65% and
79% suppression, respectively, compared to unmodified GO, con-
firming that these chemical modifications reduced the efficacy.
Increasing the amount of GO resulted in a higher antibacterial
efficacy, which remained sufficiently strong even at high bacte-
rial densities (Figure S5). Notably, this antibacterial effect was
consistently observed against E. coli and K. pneumoniae, as well
as strains harboring antibiotic resistance genes (NDM-1) (Figure
S6). Moreover, GO significantly suppressed the growth of rep-
resentative Gram-positive bacteria, reducing bacterial growth to
approximately 14–16% in methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA)
and ∼30% in methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) relative to
untreated controls, although the underlying antibacterial mecha-
nism may differ from that of gram-negative ones in the presence
of thick peptidoglycan cell wall (Figure S7). Taken together, the
GO sheets with abundant oxygen groups were found to exhibit
potent antibacterial effects, even in drug-resistant bacteria.
2.2
Selective Interaction of GO With Bacterial
Phospholipids
As a simplified model for GO-bacterial membrane interaction,
we prepared artificial membrane models mimicking E. coli,
purely composed of the two typical phospholipids, including
palmitoyloleoylphosphatidylethanolamine (POPE) and POPG.
While POPE is commonly found in the membranes of both
bacteria and mammalian cells, POPG is selectively present only
in the membranes of various bacterial species, including those
of drug-resistant bacteria [58–61], constituting over 15% of the
bacterial membrane phospholipids. POPG stabilizes bacterial
membranes under the environmental challenges in diverse and
often hostile conditions, such as aqueous conditions or varying
ionic strengths. Its anionic nature facilitates interactions with
divalent cations like Mg2+ and Ca2+, and thereby protects the
membrane against osmotic pressure, antimicrobial peptides, and
other external threats [62]. Cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-
EM) shows that the GO film, prepared by typical vacuum
filtration, induced selective disruption of the artificial mem-
brane, including POPG with punctured, collapsed structures,
whereas no particular modification of pure POPE-based mem-
branes (Figure 2A, Figures S8 and S9) was observed. Further
investigation using GO powder revealed that nanoscale GO
flakes selectively adhered onto bacterial-mimicking membranes,

We also performed zeta potential measurements for the surface
charge states of model cell membranes. Anionic POPG liposomes
are more negatively charged than POPE counterparts (Figure
S12). Our NMR results suggest that parallel adsorption of GO on
the POPG-rich surface could maximize the hydrogen bonding
opportunity, while involving abundant basal oxygen functional
groups, and overcome the electrostatic repulsion from carboxylic
acid-rich edges. Indeed, the zeta potential value significantly
increased (less negative) for POPG and POPE/POPG-mixed lipo-
somes after GO film treatment, illustrating the effective shielding
of negative charges with GO adsorption. By contrast, the POPE
membrane remained nearly unchanged without specific inter-
action with GO. Noteworthy, while bacterial membrane lipids
can be chemically modified to exhibit a weaker negative change,
such as through MprF-mediated POPG lysinylation. This may
attenuate initial membrane association, but the inherent POPG-
dependent interaction is still active for eventual antibacterial
activity. Together, this multifaceted mode of action suggests that
GO-based nanomaterials may retain antibacterial efficacy even in
the presence of emerging resistance mechanisms.
The morphological changes of bacteria with GO treatment
were characterized by real-time 3D holotomography, a unique
technique for the high-resolution and rapid time-lapse anal-
ysis of live cells without sample processing or labeling, thus
minimizing the potential artifacts. Gradual distortion in the
spherical morphology of bacterial cells was monitored over time
upon exposure to GO (Figure 2E; Figure S13). The significant
disruption of the plasma membrane was accompanied with the
leakage of intracellular substances (Figure 2E). Scanning electron
microscope (SEM) analysis also revealed that bacterial cells
treated with the GO film exhibited membrane instability, leading
to surface wrinkling, followed by structural disruption of the cells
(Figure 2F).
2.3
GO Nanofibers as an Antibacterial Material
As a conventional material form for biomaterial application,
nylon nanofiber, including GO were prepared to examine the
antibacterial activity. ∼200 nm diameter fibers with the GO
compositions of 1 wt.% (GO-1%) and 2 wt.% (GO-2%) were
produced by electrospinning process. SEM observation confirmed
that GO sheets were evenly distributed at the cores as well as the
surfaces of the nanofiber matrix (Figure 3A; Figure S14). It is note-
worthy that, for GO contents exceeding 2 wt.%, nozzle clogging
was observed during electrospinning, leading to bead formation
and nonuniform fiber geometry; therefore, the GO content was
limited to 2 wt.% (Figure S15). Thermogravimetric (TGA) analysis
detected an increase in the thermal decomposition temperature
with GO (Figure S16). The composite fibers, as well as their
randomly accumulated textile, demonstrated strong antibacterial
effects for E. coli, which increases with the GO content and
total volume of spinning dope (Figure 3B, Figures S17 and S18).
The antibacterial activity was well-maintained even after the
thorough washing in water, confirming the stable incorporation
of GO within the fiber matrix (Figure S19). Visual observation
of the live and dead bacteria confirmed that the GO nanofiber
induced the substantial deaths of bacterial cells (Figure 3C),
compared to the GO-free counterparts without noticeable effect.
It is noteworthy that several different matrix polymers were tested
for the antibacterial effect. Interestingly, nanofibers composed of
polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and GO exhibited negligible antibacterial
efficacy, presumably due to the strong multivalent hydrogen
bonding between GO and the hydroxyl-rich PVA chains [68],
which may restrict the interaction among GO surfaces and
bacterial membranes (Figure S20). By contrast, polyamide groups
in nylon are predominantly engaged in intrinsic interchain
hydrogen bonding [69], thereby preserving the accessibility to
GO surface functional groups. Despite their strong antibacterial
effect, the cytotoxicity evaluation of GO fibers by treating NIH-
3T3 cells presents minimal toxicity toward mammalian cells even
after a prolonged treatment over 72 h (Figure 3D; Figures S21 and
S22). This secures the potential use of GO-based materials for
biomedical applications.
2.4
In Vivo Antibacterial Efficacy of GO
We examined the in vivo antibacterial efficacy as well as the
therapeutic potential of GO by employing wound infection
models in mice and pigs. Various GO-based forms, including
pure GO film, nylon composite fiber, and pure GO powder, were
applied to the mice with full-thickness wounds infected with
E. coli (Figure 3E). All GO forms resulted in the substantial
reduction of bacterial growth within 7 days (Figure 3F; Figures
S23 and S24). Notably, GO powder abrogated the bacterial growth
within 3 days (no colony detected) (Figure 3G). By contrast, the
pure nylon control suffered from substantial bacterial growth
throughout the observed period. Moreover, all GO forms led to
noticeably accelerated wound closure rates (Figure 3H), well-
correlated to the antibacterial efficacy. Histological analysis of
the skin tissues confirmed that GO forms induced minimal
inflammation, reduced bleeding, and enhanced wound healing
effects (Figure 3I; Figure S25). Since mice are known to exhibit
rapid turnover rates of body fluids that can lead to high tolerance
to foreign body reactions, validation in larger animal models that
are physiologically similar to humans is highly desired.
We employed a porcine model to validate the effect of GO in
skin tissue, which is known to be highly similar to human
skin. Full-thickness wound infections of E. coli were generated
on the skin tissues of pigs, followed by the application of GO
forms (Figure 4A; Figure S26). All GO forms (powder, film, and
nanofiber) could successfully eradicate the infection, validating a
clear contrast to the controls without GO (Figure 4B; Figures S27
and S28). Quantification of bacterial colonies revealed the major
reductions in bacterial growth, with the GO nanofiber resulting
in > 99.9% reduction within 9 days (Figure 4C). The enhanced

Conclusion
Understanding the molecular-level antibacterial mechanism of
GO alongside the in-depth characterization in animal models
proposes a sustainable and reliable design principle of GO-
based materials for antibacterial therapy, which has been a
longstanding controversy due to the inconsistency of the previous

surface-to-surface interaction with the bacteria-specific phos-

across various bacterial species, while preserving the integrity of
mammalian cells. Moreover, owing to the generic presence of
bacteria-specific phospholipids, this intriguing activity also holds

The unique antibacterial activity of GO provides a sustainable
and efficacious approach for infection control. Various material
forms, including GO (film, nanofiber, and powder), generally
demonstrated excellent antibacterial efficacy even with a small
dose of GO. No significant inflammation was observed in the in
vivo wound model tests, even with the intended bacterial infec-
tions, together with the noticeably accelerated wound healing
rate. Noticeably, GO-integrated nanofibers retained antimicrobial
efficacy after repeated textile washing, prospecting their potential
applicability as a reusable medical and protective equipment.
This high stability, combined with its unique mode of antibacte-
rial action, clearly distinguishable from other pharmaceuticals,
and the general applicability to a broad spectrum of bacteria,
including drug-resistant bacteria, highlights the potential of GO
to move beyond passive infection prevention and contribute to
reducing the repeated overuse of antimicrobial drugs. Overall, GO
offers a versatile and valuable platform for the next generation
of wound dressings, surgical instruments, and wearable textiles
designed to address both clinical and functional demands in
dynamic healthcare environments.

Experimental Section
4.1
Materials
Large-sized graphene oxide, synthesized via the modified
Hummers’ method and dispersed at a concentration of 10 mg/mL,
was supplied by Grapheneall (Republic of Korea). Ammonia
solution and nylon 6, and polyvinyl alcohol (Mw 89,000-98,000)
were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 16:0-18:0 1-Palmitoyl-2-
oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (POPE) and 16:0-18:0
1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycro-3-phospho-(1’-rac-glycerol)
(sodium salt) (POPG) were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids.
2-Cyclohexen-1-ol, 3,4-epoxy-1-cyclohexene, benzoic acid, and
chloroform-d (CDCl3, 99.8 at%) for NMR analysis was purchased
from Sigma-Aldrich.
4.2
Preparation and Characterization of
Chemically Modified GO
To obtain highly purified GO, pre-washing of large GO was
performed three times by centrifugation at 17,000 rpm. The
GO dispersion (3.5 mg/mL) was tip-sonicated under ambient
temperature for 20 min and 1 h to produce medium- and small-
sized GO, respectively. To prepare the NGO, the obtained GO
powder (method explained in the next section) was placed in
ammonia solution and bath-sonicated for 3 h at 10◦C, followed
by centrifugation at 17,000 rpm to achieve neutral pH. MrGO
and rGO were prepared by thermal reduction of GO at 200◦C
and 800◦C, respectively, under Ar atmosphere (Ar flow rate:
100 sccm). NGO film was thermally reduced at 800◦C under
Ar and ammonia atmosphere, to form rNGO (flow rates: Ar at
100 sccm and ammonia at 50 sccm). For SEM, GO dispersed at
5 µg/mL was applied and spin-coated on a silicon oxide wafer
by treating with UV ozone. The GO layers and lateral sizes were
observed using SEM (Hitachi S-4800) and analyzed using Image
J. XPS spectroscopy was performed with GO films produced by
a vacuum filtration method by a survey scan and narrow scans
of the C1s and N1s regions using Nexsa G2. Chemical compo-
sitions were obtained from the XPS spectra using the Avantage
software.
4.3
Fabrication of Various GO Forms
To prepare GO film, GO dispersions of 8.6 mL in deionized
(DI) water were vacuum-filtrated through a PVDF membrane
(pore size 0.22 µm) and precipitated on the membrane by drying
in a vacuum at room temperature for 24 h. GO powder was
obtained by freezing GO dispersion in liquid nitrogen, followed
by freeze-drying for 2 days. To fabricate nylon/GO and PVA/GO
composite nanofibers, nylon chips were dissolved in formic
acid (Samchun Chemicals) to a concentration of 20 wt.%, while
PVA was dissolved in DI water to 20 wt.% at 90◦C in an oil
bath for 2 h, followed by stabilization at room temperature for
24 h. Subsequently, GO powder was added to the nylon/GO or
PVA/GO dispersion at a concentration of 0–2 wt.% and bath-
sonicated at below 10◦C, then stabilized by magnetic stirring for
24 h at room temperature. A syringe containing the nylon/GO
or PVA/GO dope was connected to an electrospinning system
(MTDI), then the electrospun nanofiber nonwovens were col-
lected on a conductive carbon-coated teflon film under a potential
of 15 kV between the spinneret (25 gauge needle). SEM (Hitachi
S-4800) analysis was conducted for observation. TGA (TG209 F1
Libra) analysis was conducted under a nitrogen atmosphere with
10◦C/min heating rate.
4.4
Bacteria-Mimicking Artificial Membrane
Model
An E. coli-mimicking bacterial membrane model including POPG
and POPE was produced according to a previous report [65,

(Avanti Polar Lipids), creating 1 µm-size POPE/POPG liposomes.

and incubated at room temperature for 24 h under gentle shaking.

4.5
Bacterial Culture
Bacterial strains of E. coli (KCTC 22366) and K. pneumoniae
(KCTC 12385) were obtained from the Korean Collection for Type
Cultures. NDM-1-positive E. coli (AMC 22365) and K. pneumoniae
(AMC 22503) were obtained from Asan Medical Center. For
culture, bacteria were inoculated into Luria-Bertani broth (LB,
DifcoTM, BD) or tryptic soy broth (TSB, DifcoTM, BD) and
incubated in suspension at 37◦C with gentle agitation (160 rpm) in
a shaking incubator (Jeiotech) overnight. Bacterial suspensions
were transferred to fresh media and incubated for another 2 h.
Bacterial growth was examined by measuring optical densi-
ties at 600 nm (OD600) using NanoPhotometer (P330, Implen
GmbH).
4.6
In Vitro Antibacterial Efficacy
Microbial Viability Assay Kit-WST (Dojindo Molecular Technolo-
gies, Inc.) was used to examine antimicrobial activity according to
the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, cultured bacteria were
repeatedly washed with PBS (pH 7.4), and resuspended in fresh
PBS to 2 × 105∼9 CFU/mL. Various GO forms or controls were
treated to bacteria in a microwell plate, incubated for 4 h at
37◦C, and bacterial cells were collected using a 0.1 µm centrifugal
filter. Bacteria were resuspended in Mueller Hinton Broth (MHB)
media (DifcoTM, BD) containing the WST reagent, followed
by incubating at 37◦C for 1 ∼2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm
was measured using Infinite 200 PRO (Tecan Group, Ltd.), and
bacterial growth was determined based on normalization with
values for the untreated control.
Live/Dead BacLight Bacterial Viability kit (Thermo Fisher Scien-
tific) was used to visualize live and dead bacteria, according to the
manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, bacteria were suspended in
fresh LB medium to reach an OD600 of 0.6, followed by incubation
for 2.5 h. Bacteria were washed repeatedly with PBS (pH 7.4)
and resuspended in fresh PBS to 106 CFU/well in microscopic
chamber slides (SPL Life Science) and treated with GO film
(1 × 1 cm2) for 2 h at 37◦C. Then SYTO9 and propidium iodide
were added and incubated for 15 min in the dark at room
temperature. Slides were covered with a coverslip and observed
using a confocal microscope (LSM 980). Images were analyzed
using Zeiss Zen 3.2 (blue edition) software.
4.7
Morphological Analyses
A thin film was formed by applying a 3.5 mg/ml GO dispersion
onto a glass-bottom confocal plate for over fifteen coating cycles.
Then, 106 cells of E. coli were placed on the GO thin film
and observed for 3 h using a holotomography instrument (HT-
X1). Sphericity of the bacterial cells was quantified using the
TomoAnalysis 2.0 software.
For examining the morphology of bacterial cells by SEM, E. coli
cells (107∼8 cells) treated with GO for 1 h 30 min were fixed in
2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS (pH 7.0) at RT for 30 min, and then
incubated for overnight at 4◦C, followed by washing twice with
PBS. Samples were then treated with 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4)
for 30 min, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and dried
overnight in a desiccator. Samples were then analyzed using a
scanning electron microscope (Hitachi S-4800).
4.8
In Vitro Cytotoxicity
NIH-3T3 mouse embryonic fibroblast cells were obtained from
the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were
cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), includ-
ing 25 mm 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid
(HEPES) and L-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific), supple-
mented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone) and 1% peni-
cillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a CO2 incubator
at 37◦C (BB15, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cytotoxicity was deter-
mined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo Molecular
Technologies, Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
In brief, NIH-3T3 cells were inoculated in a 96-well culture plate
at 2 × 104 cells/well. After 24 h incubation for attachment, cells
were washed with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS)
and treated with GO for 6 ∼72 h at 37◦C. Then, the CCK-8 reagent
in DMEM was added, incubated at 37◦C for 1 ∼2 h, and the
absorbance at 450 nm was measured using Infinite 200 PRO. Cell
viability was determined based on normalization with values for
the untreated control.

4.10
Mouse Wound Infection Model
Mice were randomly divided into groups of n = 3 and anesthetized
with avertin. The dorsal region of each mouse was shaved,
sterilized with alcohol, and a full-thickness circular wound (8 mm
in diameter) was created using a biopsy punch. E. coli (5 × 104
CFU/ml) suspended in 0.05 mL PBS was immediately applied
to the wound, and various forms of GO or control (1 × 1 cm2)
were placed to cover the wound area, followed by applying
Tegaderm (3 m) to firmly secure the dressing to the wound site.
Wound closure was monitored by taking gross images during
the observed time period and analyzed by measuring wound
area using ImageJ. Bacteria were collected from the wound
sites at different time intervals using sterilized cotton swabs and
suspended in LB containing 20% glycerol. Bacteria were then
diluted in PBS solution, inoculated on agar plates for incubation
overnight at 37◦C, and colonies were counted.
4.11
Pig Wound Infection Model
All procedures were conducted using strict aseptic techniques
and performed by qualified veterinary surgeons (HLB biostep).
Pigs’ dorsal hair was carefully shaved with clippers and sterilized
with 70% ethanol. All subsequent procedures were performed
under general anesthesia with isoflurane. Full-thickness wounds
measuring 2 × 2 cm2 were created with a blade, spaced 6 cm
apart. Hemostasis was achieved using sterile gauze, and E. coli
(106 CFU/mL) diluted in 0.1 mL PBS was immediately applied
to the wounds. Various GO forms were then applied to each
wound, followed by coverage with Tegaderm (3 m) film. Wound
closure was monitored by taking photographs of the wound areas
at various time points for 13 days, and wound area was measured
using Image J software. Bacterial samples were collected at the
time intervals and analyzed as described for the mouse model.
4.12
Histological Analysis
After sacrifice of the animals, skin tissues, including the wounds,
were excised, fixed in 4% PFA solution at room temperature for
24 h, and embedded in paraffin for histological sectioning. Tissue
sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and
Masson’s trichrome (MT), and bright-field microscopic images
were acquired using Pannoramic SCAN II (Hungary).
4.13
Statistical Analysis
For all in vitro experiments, at least three independent sets of
experiments were performed in triplicate for each group. All
statistical data were expressed as mean ± standard error of mean
(SEM). Statistical significance was determined using One-way
ANOVA or Two-way ANOVA to obtain the p-value. A value
of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

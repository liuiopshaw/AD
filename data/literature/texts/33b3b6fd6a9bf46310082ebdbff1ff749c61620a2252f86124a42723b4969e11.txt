From understanding the roles of
tetraoctylammonium bromide in the two-phase
Brust–Schiﬀrin method to tuning the size of gold
nanoclusters†
Xinyue Dou,‡a Xiangyu Wang,‡a Shuyu Qian,a Naiwei Liua and Xun Yuan
*a,b
The two-phase Brust–Schiﬀrin (B–S) method has been widely used for synthesizing small-sized Au nano-
particles (NPs) of size 2–6 nm, as well as Au nanoclusters (NCs) of size <2 nm. However, size tuning of Au
NCs at the atomic level by this method is challenging probably due to a lack of in-depth understanding of
its mechanism. Herein, we report the identiﬁcation of two roles of tetraoctylammonium bromide (TOAB)
in the two-phase B–S method: TOAB not only transfers Au(III) precursors but also transfers the reducing
agent NaBH4 from the aqueous to the organic phase. On this basis, we developed a novel two-phase syn-
thetic strategy by decoupling the roles of the TOAB: (1) using the hydrophobic selenolate ligand to trans-
fer Au(III) precursors from the aqueous to the organic phase via the formation of selenolate–Au(I) com-
plexes and (2) deploying a small amount of TOAB as “shuttles” to transfer NaBH4 into the organic phase
for controlled reduction of selenolate–Au(I) complexes in organic phase. Using this strategy, size tuning of
Au NCs at the atomic level could be achieved by simply varying the amount of TOAB. The high yields of
Au NCs (≥76%) together with the short synthetic time (≤3 h) and size-tuning capability further illustrate
the attractiveness of this synthetic strategy. These advantages also present the classical B–S method with
greater strength and ﬂexibility towards NC synthesis.
Introduction
Since the initial synthesis of thiolated Au NPs reported in
1994,1 the two-phase B–S method has been the most com-
monly used method for the synthesis of small Au NPs
(2–6 nm) protected by thiolate, selenolate, and tellurate
ligands based on the usage of TOAB as the phase-transfer
agent.2–7 In the past decades, this method has been success-
fully extended to the synthesis of ultrasmall Au NCs (<2 nm)
with atomic precision.8–17 Extensive eﬀorts have been devoted
to
the
stepwise
investigation
of
the
two-phase
B–S
method,18–20 which enabled the synthesis of uniform Au NPs
with size control.21–23 However, tuning the size of ultra-small
Au NCs with the two-phase B–S system remains a major chal-
lenge probably due to a lack of deep understanding of the
underlying mechanism.12–14,24 Of particular note, as an emer-
ging functional material, the application performance of metal
NCs is highly sensitive to their size, and the change of even
one metal atom in the NCs may cause dramatic diﬀerences in
their
physicochemical
properties
as
well
as
application
performance.25,26 Therefore, pursuing the exquisite synthetic
strategies for tuning the size of metal NCs is an everlasting
goal in the field, which could enable us to explore the corre-
lations between NC size and their application performance.
Considering the powerfulness of the two-phase B–S system in
NC synthesis, we hypothesized that if the roles of TOAB in the
B–S method could be clearly identified, a highly eﬀective strat-
egy might be developed for tuning the size of metal NCs.
Herein, we identified two roles of TOAB in the two-phase B–
S method: (1) phase transfer of Au(III) precursors and (2) phase
transfer of the reducing agent NaBH4 from the aqueous to the
organic phase via the combination of electrostatic interactions
(e.g., TOA+-Au(III)Cl4
−and TOA+-BH4
−pairs) and TOAB micelle
encapsulation. On this basis, a novel two-phase synthesis by
decoupling the roles of TOAB in the B–S method was devel-
oped: (1) using the hydrophobic selenolate ligand to deprive
the role of TOAB in transferring Au(III) precursors from the
aqueous to the organic phase via the formation of selenolate–
Au(I) complexes and (2) deploying a small amount of TOAB as

Results and discussion
The ability of TOAB in transferring Au(III) precursors from the
aqueous to the organic phase is the key feature of the two-
phase B–S method.27 As in a typical two-phase B–S method, we
mixed TOAB (3 equiv.) in toluene with aqueous HAuCl4 solu-
tion (1 equiv.) and stirred for a particular period of time (e.g.,
1 h) to ensure the complete transfer of AuCl4
−species into
toluene.18,19
Ultraviolet–visible
(UV–vis)
absorption
spec-
troscopy,28
electrospray
ionization
mass
spectrometry
(ESI-MS), and 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)29,30 were
used to examine the phase transfer of AuCl4
−species by TOAB.
As shown in Fig. 1a, after mixing hydrophilic HAuCl4 with
TOAB in the two-phase system, the color of the toluene layer
changed from colorless (item 1) to red (item 2, inset), and a
dominant absorption peak at ∼440 nm appeared in the UV–vis
spectrum of the toluene phase (black curve), indicating the
successful phase transfer of AuCl4
−species via the formation
of TOA-Au(III)X4 complexes based on the equation: TOAB +
HAu(III)Cl4 →TOA+-Au(III)X4
−+ H+ + X−(X denotes Cl−or
Br−).18 The slight shift of TOAB after mixing with HAuCl4 in
the
1H NMR spectrum also supported the above finding
(Fig. S1†).6,18,19 In addition, compared with TOAB (blue curve,
Fig. 1b), three additional peaks were observed in the m/z range
of 1200–1400 in the ESI mass spectrum of the toluene phase
after mixing TOAB with HAuCl4 (black curve, inset of Fig. 1b),
which
could
be
assigned
to
[(TOA)2(AuCl4)]+,
[(TOA)2(AuCl3Br)]+, and [(TOA)2(AuCl2Br2)]+, respectively (see
Fig. S2 and S3 for detailed ESI† analysis), further confirming
the phase transfer of Au(III)Cl4
−into toluene by TOAB.
Besides transferring Au(III) precursors, TOAB could transfer
NaBH4 from the aqueous to the organic phase. 1H NMR and
ESI-MS were applied to demonstrate this role of TOAB.
Compared to TOAB (blue curve), three additional intense
peaks between 0.3 and 0.8 ppm were detected in the 1H NMR
spectrum of the organic layer (black curve) after mixing TOAB
(0.06 mmol) in deuterated toluene with NaBH4 solution
(0.2 mmol) in D2O and stirring for 10 min (Fig. 1c). These
peaks corresponded to NaBH4, suggesting the presence of
NaBH4 in the organic phase. Moreover, there were three peaks
at ∼466, ∼948, and ∼1430 m/z in the ESI mass spectrum of the
toluene layer after mixing TOAB with NaBH4 in the two-phase
system (Fig. 1d), which are assigned to [TOA]+, [(TOA)2BH4]+,
and [(TOA)3(BH4)2]+, respectively (see Fig. S4 for ESI† analysis),
thus corroborating the phase transfer of NaBH4 by TOAB
(TOAB + NaBH4 →TOA+-BH4
−+ Br−+ Na+). In fact, TOAB
could also be used as a protecting ligand in the formation of
TOAB-stabilized metal NPs, if no thiol/selenol ligands are
introduced into the two-phase B–S system (Fig. S5†), which is
however out of the focus of this study.
In addition to the electrostatic interaction-induced phase-
transfer, both AuCl4
−and BH4
−might be transferred to the
organic phase by TOAB micelle encapsulation,20 which could
be indicated by the ultra-low peak intensity of TOA-Au(III)X4 in
the ESI mass spectrum (Fig. 1b, black curve). To further
demonstrate this, 1H NMR analysis was used to investigate the
organic layer of TOAB, TOA-Au(III)X4 complexes, TOA-Au(I)X2
complexes (derived from reducing TOA-Au(III)X4 complexes
with hydrophobic selenolate ligands), and TOA-BH4 com-
plexes. The 1H NMR results revealed the presence of encapsu-
lated water (∼5.3 ppm) in the organic layer (Fig. S6 and S7†),
which implied the formation of TOAB micelles in the two-
phase B–S system.7 The dynamic light scattering (DLS) results
also confirmed the formation of TOAB micelles in the B–S
system (Fig. S8†). On this basis, we concluded that the for-
mation of TOAB micelles in the two-phase B–S method was the
leading cause for the unsuccessful tuning of Au NC size at the
atomic level. It is well documented that a controllable
reduction kinetics for ligand–metal(I) precursors would be
extremely important for the size tuning of high-quality metal
NCs.31–38 However, abundant TOAB formed numerous micelles
in the two-phase B–S method, and each micelle encapsulating
water with Au(I) precursors and NaBH4 inside could act as a
micro-reactor
for
reducing
Au(I)
precursors with NaBH4.

However, the concentrations of both Au(I) precursors and
NaBH4 in each micro-reactor might be diﬀerent due to the
competitive electrostatic interactions between Au(III)X4
−or
Au(I)X2
−
and
BH4

with
TOA+,
which
rendered
the
reduction
microenvironments
complicated
and
inhomo-
geneous. Moreover, TOAB micelles could encapsulate excess
amount of NaBH4 (usually the Au : NaBH4 ratio in the B–S
method = 1 : 10), as compared to the encapsulated Au(I)
precursors,13 which made the reduction kinetics of Au(I)
precursors too fast to control. Therefore, eliminating the nega-
tive influence of TOAB micelles in the B–S method could
present a feasible approach to achieve size tuning of metal
NCs.
To eliminate the negative influence of TOAB micelles, a
straightforward method would involve replacing TOAB with
other reagents and taking over TOAB’s roles in the two-phase
B–S method. We hypothesized that hydrophobic selenolate or
thiolate ligands could replace TOAB in transferring Au(III)Cl4
−
precursors from the aqueous phase to the organic phase con-
sidering that (1) the selenol or thiol group of the ligand has
strong binding aﬃnities with noble metals, which would
ensure the anchoring of hydrophilic Au ions,6,39–41 and (2) the
hydrophobic entity of the ligand guarantees its transfer ability
from the aqueous phase to the organic phase.27 As expected,
without adding TOAB, Au ions could be successfully trans-
ferred from water to toluene with the selenolate ligand (here
benzeneselenol (PhSeH) was used as the ligand model) via the
formation of PhSe–Au(I) complexes, i.e., HAu(III)Cl4 + 3PhSeH
→PhSe–Au(I) complex + PhSe-SePh + 4H+ + 4Cl−. As shown in
Fig. 2a, the solution color of PhSeH changed from colorless
(left item, toluene layer) to light yellow (right item) and an
intense absorption peak at ∼400 nm (black curve) appeared in
the UV–vis absorption spectrum after introducing aqueous
HAuCl4 solution and suﬃcient stirring (e.g., 5 min), suggesting
the successful binding between PhSeH and Au(I) as well as the
transfer of Au(I) species from the aqueous phase to the organic
phase. Moreover, by comparing the ESI mass spectra of the
toluene solution of PhSeH (Fig. 2b) before (blue curve) and
after (black curve) introducing hydrophilic HAuCl4 solution,
three additional peaks at ∼354, ∼509 and ∼862 m/z were
observed after the introduction that could be assigned to [Au
(PhSe)]−, [Au(PhSe)2]−and [Au2(PhSe)3]−, respectively (see
Fig. S9 for detailed ESI† analysis), confirming the successful
phase transfer of Au(I) precursors with PhSeH via the for-
mation of PhSe–Au(I) complexes.
Although PhSeH could fully take over the first role of TOAB
(i.e., transfer Au ions from aqueous to the organic phase), it is
incapable of transferring NaBH4 from aqueous to the organic
phase. Without TOAB, there are no observable changes in both
optical absorptions and sizes for PhSe–Au(I) complexes before
and after adding NaBH4 (Fig. S10†). Therefore, we considered
using a small amount of TOAB to transfer reductive BH4
−
anions solely via electrostatic interactions (to form TOA+-BH4
−
pairs) for a controllable phase transfer of BH4
−anions and
subsequent reduction process, which would diﬀer from the B–
S method whereby BH4
−anions would be transferred via both
electrostatic interactions (to form TOA+-BH4
−pairs) and TOAB
micelle encapsulation. To validate this concept, we mixed a
small amount of TOAB (0.5 mM, <1% than that used in a
typical B–S method) with NaBH4 in a water–toluene system
and analyzed the resulting system using 1H NMR, ESI-MS and
DLS techniques. As shown in Fig. 2c, the peaks corresponding
to NaBH4 (0.3–0.8 ppm) were displayed in the 1H NMR spec-
trum of the organic layer, which demonstrated the successful
phase transfer of NaBH4 via electrostatic interaction with a
small addition of TOAB. The ESI-MS result also confirmed the
formation of TOA+-BH4
−pairs (Fig. 2d and Fig. S11†). Another
evidence for the successful transfer of NaBH4 by a small
amount of TOAB was provided by the toluene solution pre-
pared by mixing TOAB and NaBH4 in a water–toluene system
having the ability to reduce the PhSe–Au(I) complexes to Au
NCs (Fig. S12†). The DLS analysis showed that the dynamic
diameter
of
TOA+-BH4
−
complexes
was
below
20
nm
(Fig. S13†), excluding the formation of large TOAB micelles at
such a low concentration.
By decoupling the roles of TOAB (i.e., using hydrophobic
ligands to transfer Au(I) precursors into the organic phase,
while using a small amount of TOAB as “shuttles” to controlla-
bly transfer NaBH4 into the organic phase), the two-phase B–S
system was largely simplified due to the absence of TOAB
micelles. More importantly, the reduction kinetics of Au(I) pre-
cursors were delicately adjusted with the amount of TOAB in
the system. On this basis, we developed a novel two-phase syn-
thetic method for tuning the size of Au NCs. As shown in

It should be noted that the feeding molar ratio of NaBH4 to
Au is 2.24 : 1 for such four Au NC samples, which implies that
the reduction kinetics of the Au NCs would be uncontrollable
due to the excessive NaBH4 in the system, if without the two-
phase barrier. Fortunately, the reaction between the PhSe–Au(I)
complexes and NaBH4 in the two-phase system is controlled by
the amount of TOAB. Taking Au18(PhSe)14 NCs as an example,
while the molar ratio of TOAB to NaBH4 is 1 : 224, the molar
ratio of TOAB to Au is 1 : 100, which means that the transfer-
ring capacity of TOAB for NaBH4 is extremely insuﬃcient for
the instant reduction of PhSe–Au(I) complexes. Therefore, the
phase transfer of NaBH4 by TOAB is the rate-determining step
for the synthesis of Au NCs, and the adjustment of the amount
of TOAB made the control of the reduction kinetics of Au NCs
possible in the system.

dominant absorption peaks at 635 nm for sample #1 (black

Conclusions
In summary, two roles of TOAB in the two-phase B–S method
have been identified. TOAB not only transfers Au(III) precursors
but also transfers the reducing agent NaBH4 from the aqueous
phase to the organic phase. On the basis of this finding, a
simple two-phase synthetic method has been developed to
tune the size of Au NCs based on the modified B–S method
(i.e., using the protecting ligand to replace TOAB in transfer-
ring Au precursors and using diﬀerent amounts of TOAB to
regulate the reduction kinetics of ligand–Au(I) precursors by
controllable transfer of NaBH4 from the aqueous phase to the
organic phase). This approach is found to be versatile and
applicable for the synthesis of monodisperse thiolated Au
NCs. This study provides insights into the mechanism of the
B–S method and enables us to design new synthetic strategies
for size-tunable metal NCs.

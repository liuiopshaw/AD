CONSPECTUS: Near-infrared II (NIR-II, 900−1700 nm) fluorescence imaging is
transforming biological visualization, offering deeper, sharper, and more reliable
detection than visible or NIR-I probes. Reduced scattering and autofluorescence in
this window enable real-time imaging of tissues and organs. Gold nanoclusters
(AuNCs) are promising NIR-II agents due to their atomically precise structures,
biocompatibility, and versatile surface chemistry. However, their modest
photoluminescence (PL) in aqueous environments, which is crucial for biomedical
applications, remains a key limitation, making brightness enhancement a central
challenge.
The Au−ligand interface is critical: small changes in ligand structure or binding can
strongly affect electronic relaxation. Smart ligand design, including bidentate thiols,
electron-rich groups, or N-heterocyclic carbenes, stabilizes excited states and
suppresses nonradiative losses. Beyond ligand optimization, strategies such as
protein or polymer encapsulation, controlled self-assembly, and layer-by-layer coatings have increased quantum yields to nearly 10%
in the 900−1300 nm range, underscoring the role of the metal−ligand environment.
The nano(bio)interface also dictates practical performance. In complex milieus, proteins, redox agents, and pH fluctuations can
stabilize or quench emission. Antifouling coatings (zwitterionic ligands, PEGylation, or rigid carbene shells) help preserve brightness,
while kernel locking, heteroatom doping, and hybrid constructs with dyes or biomolecules extend emission beyond 1200 nm and
enable red-shifting via Förster energy transfer (FRET) or bioluminescence energy transfer (BRET).
Bright, stable AuNCs thus serve as both imaging agents and theranostic platforms, combining fluorescence with drug delivery,
phototherapy, or radioenhancement. Their deep-tissue sensitivity makes them powerful tools for monitoring cancer, cardiovascular
disease, and neuroinflammation. Yet environmental sensitivity also raises challenges: stability, biotransformation, and immune
activation highlight the need for standardized evaluation of colloidal stability, photostability, and biological interactions.
In this Account, we summarize strategies to boost AuNC brightness in water, including ligand design, molecular assembly, protein/
polymer encapsulation, and controlled self-assembly, achieving PL quantum yields up to 10%. We also discuss how pH, redox
conditions, protein binding, and intracellular aggregation shape NIR-II emission, highlighting key principles for advancing their
biomedical use.

1. INTRODUCTION
In optical imaging, the near-infrared (NIR) window offers
significant advantages for in vivo detection, owing to the low
water absorption and autofluorescence in the first NIR window
(NIR-I, 700−900 nm).5 These properties markedly enhance
detection sensitivity, providing a high signal-to-noise ratio in
small animal models. Building on the seminal contributions of
H. Dai6,7 in the early 2010s, the exploration of the second
biological window (NIR-II, 900−1700 nm), also known as the
shortwave infrared (SWIR) region, has rapidly expanded. Their
pioneering studies highlighted how reduced light scattering at
these wavelengths, together with low tissue autofluorescence,
enables fluorescence imaging at depths of up to 10 mm in mice,
while maintaining both high spatial (∼10 μm) and temporal
(>20 frames per second) resolution.8,9 Since then, substantial
efforts have been directed toward elucidating light diffusion and
detection in organs and tissues across the sub-NIR-II
ranges10−12 and even extending into the NIR-III window
(2100−2400 nm).11 These advances have been supported by
the development of two- and three-dimensional tissue
phantoms10,12,13 as well as Monte Carlo simulation.14 Such
investigations are essential, as absorption is higher in the NIR-II
region compared with NIR-I, making the achievable signal-to-
noise ratio strongly wavelength dependent.7,12 In parallel, the
field has witnessed remarkable progress in NIR-II imaging
technologies, including more sensitive and faster detectors,
miniaturized devices, and real-time image processing tools,
which collectively strengthen the prospects for clinical trans-
lation, particularly in image-guided surgery.15
Over the past 15 years, a wide variety of NIR-II probes has
emerged, from the first reports on single-walled carbon
nanotubes8,16 to inorganic nanomaterials such as quantum
dots,17,18 lanthanide-based-complexes,19 and rare-earth nano-
particles.20−22 Alongside these, diverse organic fluorophores
including BODIPY, polymethine, squaraine, and AIEgens6,23 as
well as semiconducting polymers24 have been developed. More
recently, luminescent proteins have been engineered for
emission in the NIR-II window, though their stability and
brightness remain limited.25
Gold nanoclusters (AuNCs), whether atomically precise or
less well-defined ultrasmall gold nanoparticles, represent
another class of luminescent probes with promising features
for near-infrared bioimaging.26−28 These ‘nanomolecules,’
previously described as monolayer-protected clusters, have
emerged over the past two decades as versatile agents for
biomedical applications, including biosensing, bioimaging,
cancer and neurodegenerative therapies, and antibacterial
treatments.29 Their unique properties stem from their ultrasmall
size (<3 nm), which favors renal clearance and reduces toxicity,
as well as their highly tunable surfaces that allow targeted
functionalization and drug delivery. In addition, their distinct
optoelectronic properties and high atomic number (Z = 79)
enable them to participate in enzymatic or redox reactions and
to induce photo- or radiotoxic effects, particularly valuable in
cancer therapy.30−32
Among these features, the luminescence of AuNCs is
especially attractive for optical detection at both the cellular
level and in living tissues. Considerable efforts have focused on
developing biocompatible, functionalized AuNCs with strong
photoluminescence (PL) and long fluorescence lifetimes (from
tens of nanoseconds to the microsecond range), enabling the
sensitive and selective detection of metal ions and biomolecules
in cells, as well as the tracking and quantification of their
accumulation in specific organs of small animals.33 Although the
exact origin of the PL of AuNCs remains under debate,
substantial evidence indicates that multiple interdependent
energy-transfer processes occur within both the metal core and
the staple motifs (AunSRm) under light excitation.26,34 These
processes are strongly influenced by the structure of the gold
core as well as the nature and organization of the protecting
ligands. For instance, ligand engineering is a key factor, as it
protects AuNCs from the surrounding environment and
modulates their PL properties by modulating electronic
relaxation pathways.35 Comprehensive reviews have discussed
these structure−property relationships in detail, integrating
experimental and theoretical perspectives.26 Building on this
foundation, numerous strategies have been explored to enhance
the molar absorptivity, photoluminescence quantum yield
(PLQY), and fluorescence lifetime of AuNCs, with the goal of
producing photostable and bright emitters suitable for cellular
and in vivo imaging in the NIR-I and NIR-II regions. These
approaches include tailoring the core by controlling its size (i.e.,
number of atoms),36 shape (rods vs spheres),37,38 chirality,39 as
well as introducing heteroatom doping with metals such as
cadmium, silver, copper, platinum, or zinc.40−43 Collectively,
these strategies have led to remarkable improvements in
brightness, as demonstrated in both in vitro and in vivo
applications.28
While NIR-II emitting AuNCs still exhibit lower photo-
luminescence quantum yields (PLQY) and broader emission
spectra compared to quantum dots17 and rare-earth NPs,21 they
offer several practical advantages. AuNCs can be synthesized in
gram-scale quantities using nontoxic precursors, typically in
aqueous media, or can be readily functionalized for dispersion in
physiological environments. Also, in contrast to NIR-II organic
fluorophores, AuNCs demonstrate superior photostability,
larger Stokes shifts44 and require fewer synthetic steps for
preparation.45

2. LIGAND ENGINEERING
The molecular structure of the ligand is a key determinant in
shaping the geometry, size, and functionality of AuNCs.45,46
Over the past two decades, numerous studies have sought to
understand how AuNCs grow in the presence of both synthetic
and biological ligands. Various strategies, including ligand
exchange and size-focusing approaches, have been employed not
only to control the formation of the clusters but also to unravel
the relationship between their structure and their physicochem-
ical and optical properties.34,45,48
The first observations of weak PL above 900 nm in water-
soluble AuNCs were reported in the early 2000s by Whetten for
glutathione-stabilized Au28 NCs49 and shortly thereafter for a
AuNC stabilized by modified poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG)
molecules (Au38PhC2PEG13).50 In both cases, the emission was
attributed to phosphorescence involving a triplet state and
ligand-to-metal energy transfer. Following these reports, studies
on NIR-II-emitting water-soluble AuNCs remained scarce,
likely due to the limited availability of suitable instrumentation
at the time. Seminal work by J.E. Millstone’s group systemati-
cally investigated the influence of water-soluble short thiol
ligands, as well as copper and gold−silver alloy NCs, on their
spectroscopic properties.51 They found that the PL emission
profile and intensity strongly depend on the nature of the
thiolated ligand (Figure 2A(a,b)): longer ligands shifted the
emission to longer wavelengths but exhibited lower brightness,
with PL quantum yields ranging from 0.13% to 3.65% (Figure
2A(c)). Their studies highlighted the critical roles of Au−S
bonding, ligand geometry, and the nucleophilic character of the
ligand in tuning NIR-I/NIR-II PL emission, as assessed through
steady-state and time-resolved measurements. Importantly, they
demonstrated that the observed PL was largely independent of
core size, underscoring the potential of ligand engineering as a
strategy to enhance NIR-II emission.
Building on these insights, several groups have reported NIR-
II PL from water-soluble, atomically precise gold nano-
clusters.43,52 Notably, Au25SG18 (SG (or GSH) = Glutathione)
and Au25Cyst18 (Cyst = cysteine) display broad NIR-II emission
with three main bands centered at 930, 1050, and 1200 nm,
albeit with relatively modest PL quantum yields below 1%.43
These AuNCs are stable in physiological media, exhibit efficient
renal clearance (up to 86% within 48 h postinjection), and
enable high-performance in vivo imaging, including (i) non-

In another study, Baghdasarayan et al. functionalized AuSG
with 4-aminophenylphosphorylcholine ligands (Figure 2B(a)),
achieving strong NIR-II PL while conferring stealth properties
(Figure 2B(b−d)), which enabled their use as lymph node
markers in preclinical cancer models54 and as photothermal
agent.55 In another example, the incorporation of the
biocompatible macrocycle β-cyclodextrin as a ligand yielded
AuNCs with broad NIR-II emission centered at 1050 nm
(PLQY = 0.11%), predominantly originating from Au(I)
complexes stabilizing the cluster core.56 Remarkably, con-
jugation of these Au−β-cyclodextrin NCs to an antibody
(CD326, epithelial cell adhesion molecule) created a host−
guest nanosystem capable of selective tumor targeting and
sensitive detection using NIR-II imaging.56
One effective strategy to enhance NIR-II PL in AuNCs is to
promote intramolecular charge transfer (ICT), which in these
systems corresponds to charge transfer between the ligand and
the metal core. A recent study demonstrated that deprotonation
of copper-doped AuNCs stabilized by para-mercaptobenzoic
acid (pMBA) can lead to a 6-fold increase in PLQY (5.59% at
pH 13, at 1000 nm). This enhancement was attributed to copper
stabilizing the Au(I) oxidation state and to strong π−π
interactions between pMBA ligands at high pH.42
Optimizing brightnessdefined as the product of molar
extinction coefficient and PLQYrequires both strong
absorption in the NIR-I window, to maximize tissue penetration
of the excitation source, and efficient emission in the NIR-II
range. A particularly elegant approach to achieve this involves
the introduction of co-ligands on the AuNC surface.1,57,58 For
example, short dithiol PEG derivatives, such as hexa(ethylene
glycol) (HDT) and tetra(ethylene glycol) (TDT), were used as
co-ligands during the synthesis of Au25NCs stabilized by
mercaptohexanoic acid (MHA), forming loops on individual
clusters (Figure 2C(a)).1 This modification induced two NIR
absorbance bands at 780 and 980 nm, which intensified with
increasing HDT content, reflecting structural microdistortions
from surface charge anisotropy (Figure 2C(b)).58 HDT
incorporation also markedly influenced PL properties, enhanc-
ing multiple energy-transfer pathways involving Au−ligand
electronic states. Consequently, PL bands at 1100 and 1200 nm
were strengthened, lifetimes were prolonged, and PLQY reached
6.1% in aqueous solution (Figure 2C(c,d)). In vivo experiments
in mice showed prolonged circulation (t1/2 = 35 min) and
efficient renal clearance, enabling pseudo-3D imaging of blood
vessels and real-time, noninvasive monitoring of vascular
disorders at 4 mm depth above 1319 nm with high spatial
resolution.59
Similarly, studies on Au25MHA18 with alternative co-
ligandscysteamine (Cystm), 2-mercaptoethanol (ME), and
DL-dithiothreitol (DTT)also demonstrated PLQY enhance-
ments up to 2%. By selecting an excitation/emission pair of 1064
nm/1200−1700 nm, capillaries containing these AuNCs could
be detected at depths of up to 7 mm in intralipid solutions,
highlighting the advantage of red-shifting both excitation and
emission to maximize signal-to-background ratio while preserv-
ing high spatial resolution. Furthermore, AuMHA/Cystm NCs
enabled sensitive monitoring of kidney dysfunction, illustrating
the potential of these engineered probes for functional in vivo
imaging.60
3. NANO(BIO)INTERFACE
A widely used strategy to enhance the PL of AuNCs involves
generating molecular interactions within the first ligand layer
stabilizing the gold core. This approach has been successfully
demonstrated for green- and red-emitting AuNCs through
covalent or electrostatic interactions with small organic
molecules, which rigidify the ligand shell.63−66 Such rigid-
ification leads to marked increases in PLQY and prolonged
fluorescence lifetimes by reducing nonradiative energy transfer
at the AuNC surface66 and limiting water accessibility to the
gold core, which otherwise acts as a quencher.67 Xie and
colleagues further exploited this strategy by creating molecular
assemblies on AuNCs, achieving PLQY enhancements of nearly
60% in aqueous solution.64,68 These effects are primarily driven
by noncovalent forcesincluding electrostatic interactions,
hydrogen bonding, dipolar, van der Waals, and hydrophobic
interactionsthat collectively reduce the nonradiative decay
rate.26 Interestingly, ligand rigidification has also been shown to
influence vibrations of the metal core, suggesting a subtle
synergy between surface chemistry and core-state emission.65
While this strategy has been demonstrated for green emission,
notably via triple-layer assemblies on Au10NCs,65 and for red-
emitting clusters,68 its applicability to NIR-II emission remains
to be validated.
In this context, in 2017, Chen et al. applied this strategy by
introducing an additional layer of the zwitterionic thioctic
sulfobetaine ligand onto the AuNC surface (Figure 2D(a)),
resulting in a 6.3-fold enhancement of PL, increasing the PLQY
from 0.6% to 3.8%, accompanied by a blue shift of the emission
maximum from 1000 to 900 nm (Figure 2D(b)). This study
provided the first demonstration of AuNCs exhibiting both
efficient renal clearance in mice after intravenous injection and
sufficient PL signal for blood vessel imaging with high signal-to-
background ratio above 1300 nm.9 Rigidifying the ligand shell
on co-ligand-stabilized AuNCs also has a profound effect on
NIR absorption bands, which can be exploited to further
enhance AuNC brightness.69 For example, by increasing the
concentration of reducing agent during the synthesis of NIR-II-
emitting AuMHA/HDT, mass spectrometry and electron
microscopy revealed a progressive thickening of the organic
ligand layer around the gold core, while the core itself remained
unaffected. This modification led to a dramatic increase in the
absorption band at 990 nm, by more than 60-fold, achieving a
molar extinction coefficient of 1.69 × 104 M−1·cm−1
comparable to that of organic dyes70 and nanocrystals71 at this

NIR-II-emitting AuNCs can also be entrapped within
polymeric matrices or proteins to enhance their PL and overall
brightness. For example, AuNCs stabilized with long aliphatic
thiols (DDT: dodecanethiol) were incorporated into biocom-
patible, water-soluble poly(ethyl)methacrylate nanoparticles,
achieving loadings of up to 10,000 AuNCs per 60 nanoparticles
with high monodispersity and colloidal stability (Figure
3A(a)).72 While the PLQY reached only a maximum of 1.3%,
the strong absorption of the AuDDT emitters allowed overall
brightness values up to 3.8 × 106 M−1·cm−1 at short wavelength
in the second NIR window (900−1300 nm) (Figure 3A(b−d)),
making them comparable to the brightest emitters such as
quantum dots73 or rare earth NPs.22 Single-particle tracking
studies further demonstrated the ability to monitor these zero-
dimensional nano-objects in 3% agarose gels, a simple model for
biological environments.74 Steady-state and time-resolved
fluorescence measurements in water and deuterated water
revealed a broad PL spectrum with three main bands at 950,
1050, and 1200 nm (Figure 3A(c)). Notably, the PL profile
differed between water and deuterated water, highlighting the
influence of water absorption in the second NIR window. The
relative intensity of these bands was also dependent on AuNC
loading, indicating strong interactions between AuNCs within
the polymeric matrix, particularly affecting the 1050 and 1200
nm bands via ligand−metal−surface energy transfer.72 Fur-
thermore, AuDDT could be encapsulated up to 1 mol % in the
lipid bilayer of liposomes while retaining PL properties, enabling
cellular and in vivo imaging.32
To achieve more biologically relevant templates, efforts have
focused on biomineralized AuNCs within proteins or on labeling
specific amino-acid residues. Xie’s group demonstrated, through
combined experimental and theoretical studies, a complex
interplay between stoichiometric interactions and synergism for
Au25(p-MBS)18 (p-MBS = p-mercaptobenzenesulfonic acid)
and bovine serum albumin (BSA). Microscopy, spectroscopy,
and molecular dynamics simulations showed that electrostatic
and hydrogen-bond interactions between the p-MBS ligands
and positively charged BSA residues could lead to a 4.5-fold
enhancement of NIR-II PL.75,76 Similarly, Bertorelle et al.
employed long incubations of Au25(p-MBA)18 (p-MBA = p-
mercaptobenzoic acid) with BSA to achieve selective equimolar
binding to the Cys34 residue, confirmed by mass spectrometry,
small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS), and molecular modeling
(Figure 3B(a,b)).2 This ligand exchange resulted in a 4.7-fold
increase in PLQY, a red-shifted emission maximum at 1050 nm
(Figure 3B(c)), and an increase in average lifetime from 72 to
396.3 ns (Figure 3B(d)), consistent with increased rigidity of
the AuNC environment within the protein. Additional
spectroscopic and TD-DFT studies on Au25pMBA18−xCysx (x
= 0, 2, 5) and Au25Cys18 indicated that cysteine, being a stronger
electron donor than pMBA, had a significant effect on the NIR
band at 830 nm. Nonetheless, data suggest that the predominant
factor enhancing PL was the rigidification of the surface within
the BSA scaffold, which reinforces intersystem crossing
pathways involved in photoluminescence.2,77
Another promising avenue involves controlled aggregation of
AuNCs, which can dramatically alter their optical properties and
enable the design of 0D, 1D, 2D, and 3D assemblies with
enhanced emission.78 While precise 1D assemblies such as
dimers and trimers of atomically defined AuNCs (Au25pMBA18,
Au18SG14) have been constructed using DNA guidance, their
NIR PL remains relatively weak due to nonradiative energy
losses.79 In our group, we developed ∼100 nm supraclusters
through the spontaneous self-assembly of positively charged
AuNCs (stabilized by arginine-containing peptides) and siRNA,
achieving a 17.6-fold increase in PL at 680 nm alongside
enhanced radiotherapeutic effects.30 Extending this approach,
bright NIR-II-emitting AuNCs stabilized by cysteamine (PLQY
= 7.1%) formed supraclusters with siRNA against heat shock
factor protein (siHSF1) by controlling stoichiometry (Figure
3C(a)).4 These supraclusters, ∼100 nm in size with interparticle
distances below 3 nm (Figure 3C(b)), exhibited compaction
over time as revealed by SAXS, suggesting dynamic reorganiza-
tion or intercluster exchange to minimize energy. Following
assembly, PL spectra showed decreased emission at shorter
wavelengths (900−1000 nm) and a bathochromic shift to 1400
nm (Figure 3C(c)), consistent with prior observations in solid
matrices (PDMS) or other solid environments80 highlighting
the influence of the local environment on AuNC surface energy
transfer. These supraclusters could be detected by single particle
tracking in the NIR-II window offering multifunctional potential
for imaging and gene-silencing therapies (Figure 3C(c,d)).4
Finally, Sun and colleagues demonstrated another approach
using a reverse-emulsion method, confining AuNCs in droplets
to finely tune assembly size (20−80 nm) and structure.81
Assemblies of AuNCs stabilized by lipoic acid (LA) exhibited
size- and cross-linker-dependent organization, influenced by the
molecular weight (1.8−25 kDa) and architecture (linear vs
branched) of polyelectrolyte cross-linkers. Under optimized
conditions, these assemblies achieved a 4-fold PL enhancement
at 1100 nm compared to free Au−LA NCs, with PLQY reaching
1.18% across 900−1300 nm and lifetimes increasing from 206.2
to 284.4 ns, reflecting restricted AuNC vibrations. Further
physicochemical and microscopic analyses (XPS, TEM, FTIR)
suggested polyelectrolyte-mediated etching and increased Au(I)
content on the AuNC surface, which likely contributed to the
observed PL enhancement via ligand−metal charge transfer.
4. BIOLOGICAL ENVIRONMENT
When optical probes such as AuNCs enter the body, they
encounter numerous biological componentsincluding pro-
teins, lipids, complement factors, and cellsunder varying salt
concentrations. These probes interact dynamically with bio-
logical fluids, which can directly influence their colloidal stability
and optical properties.82 They are also subject to immune
recognition, accumulate progressively in tissues and organs, and
can undergo biotransformation, potentially causing inflamma-
tion or acute toxicity.83 Importantly, these interactions can
induce drastic changes in PL emission profiles, intensity, and
lifetimes, potentially generating artifacts during detection and
leading to misinterpretation of results. For example, significant
optical changes have been reported for NIR-I AuNCs stabilized
with dihydrolipoic acid in cellular environments84 and for NIR-
II AuNCs stabilized with pMBA when interacting with abundant
blood proteins such as albumin2 underscoring the need to
understand these phenomena.

environment to identify key parameterssuch as pH, temper-
ature, viscosity, ionic strength, oxidation, protein complexation,
and aggregationthat affect the spectroscopic properties of
NIR-II-emitting AuMHA/HDT clusters.3 Using multiple cell
types (cancerous and noncancerous) alongside solution
controls, they analyzed PL spectra and lifetimes. Their data
highlighted the significant role of redox processes in the

5. CONCLUSION AND PERSPECTIVE
Among the different families of NIR-II emitters, gold nano-
clusters (AuNCs) stand out as promising candidates thanks to
recent progress in their synthesis and ligand engineering.
Tailoring the surface chemistry of these nanomolecular species
has enabled a significant enhancement of their brightness,
particularly in the first part of the NIR-II window between 900
and 1300 nm, with PLQY reaching up to 10% in wateran
essential feature for high-resolution deep-tissue imaging.
Strategies such as controlling the nano(bio)interface through
molecular assembly (e.g., layer-by-layer approaches), embed-
ding AuNCs in protein or polymer matrices, or inducing self-
assembly provide versatile routes to generate bright, water-
soluble AuNC systems with PL emission extending up to 1700
nm. Combining these optical properties with therapeutic
functions, such as drug delivery and photo/radio activation,
positions AuNCs as powerful theranostic agents for applications
in oncology, cardiology, neuroinflammation, and other
pathologies.
To further improve brightness (PLQY > 10%) and shift
maximum emission beyond 1200 nm, strategies developed for
hydrophobic AuNCs could be translated to water-soluble
systems. For instance, locking the kernel core with novel
hydrosoluble thiol ligands may reduce nonradiative decay,
prolong PL lifetimes, and boost PLQY.85 Recent developments
by R. Jin’s group on hydrophobic rod-shaped AuNCs (Au42,
Au60, Au78, Au96, Au114) with tunable absorption spanning
800−1800 nm are particularly encouraging.38,86 Their strong
absorption coefficients (105−106 M−1cm−1) suggest opportu-
nities to increase brightness and enable excitation with longer

Despite these advances in optical performance, the environ-
mental sensitivity of metal nanoclusters (Au, Ag, Cu, Pt, etc.)
remains a critical challenge. Their optical properties can be
significantly altered in cells and in tissues by factors such as pH,
redox conditions, or ligand disruption, as well as by protein
complexation and intracellular aggregation.3 These issues
highlight the importance of designing AuNCs with robust
surface ligand binding and stealth characteristics. Strategies such
as using bidentate thiols, NHCs, antifouling zwitterionic ligands,
or PEGylation may lead to more stable and biocompatible NIR-
II AuNCs.
Equally important for clinical translation is the long-term
safety of AuNCs. Biopersistence and possible biotransformation
by cellular metalloproteins could result in oxidative stress,
organelle dysfunction, inflammation, and immune activation,
ultimately impairing organ function.83,89 This underscores the
urgent need to standardize protocols for evaluating colloidal and
photostability, as well as oxidative/reductive behavior, in
biologically relevant environments and in artificial models that
mimic cellular and organ-level complexity.

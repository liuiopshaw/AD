Pimavanserin was the ﬁrst US Food and Drug Administration approved antipsychotic drug that does not 
block D2 dopamine receptors at therapeutic doses. Pimavanserin is a selective 5-HT2A receptor inverse 
agonist that reduces the frequency of hallucinations and delusions associated with Parkinson disease. 
Despite these attributes, pimavanserin prolongs the QT interval at therapeutic doses, limiting the dose to 
34 mg. In addition, pimavanserin has a 57-hour half-life, and reaches steady state in approximately 12 days. 
ACP-204 was identiﬁed ed as a compound with subnanomolar afﬁni nity in radioligand binding assays, and 
subnanomolar inverse agonist and antagonist potency at 5-HT2A receptors in cell-based functional assays. 
ACP-204 had 13-fold less afﬁni nity for 5-HT2C receptors in radioligand binding, and 30- to 100-fold less 
potency at 5-HT2C in the functional assays, and was over 1000-fold selective against over 70 other targets. 
ACP-204 had 9-fold lower potency inhibiting human ether-a-go-go-related gene than pimavanserin, and 
lower potency at Cav1.2 L-type calcium and Nav1.5 sodium channels, all channels associated with car­
diovascular function. ACP-204 was active in 3 rodent models of schizophrenia, was orally active, and 
potently engaged with central 5-HT2A receptors in nonhuman primates. Long-term toxicity studies 
demonstrated wide safety margins, and pharmacokinetic modeling predicted a 14.7 to 21.7 hours half-life 
in humans. It is anticipated that reducing the potential for QT prolongation will allow greater dosing 
ﬂexibility with ACP-204 compared with pimavanserin; additionally, a shorter half-life may provide faster 
onset of action because of plasma concentrations reaching steady state sooner. ACP-204 is currently being 
evaluated for efﬁca cacy and safety in Alzheimer disease psychosis and Lewy body dementia psychosis.
Significance Statement: Psychotic disorders remain a major source of burden and disability worldwide, 
with current treatments limited by inadequate efﬁca cacy or signiﬁca cant side effects. ACP-204 is a novel 5- 
HT2A receptor inverse agonist/antagonist being evaluated clinically for its potential to treat Alzheimer 
disease psychosis and Lewy body dementia psychosis. Comprehensive nonclinical evaluations shown 
herein demonstrate that ACP-204 has optimized pharmacodynamic properties resulting in a favorable 
safety/tolerability proﬁle, a broad therapeutic index, and promising effectiveness.

1. Introduction
Selective 5-HT2A receptor inverse agonists have demonstrated 
efﬁca cacy for a wide variety of neuropsychiatric conditions including 
schizophrenia,1 sleep,2 and anxiety.3,4 Subsequently, pimavanserin 
has demonstrated efﬁca cacy in dementia-related psychosis, schizo­
phrenia, and major depressive disorder, indicating the therapeutic 
potential for this mechanism across many neuropsychiatric dis­
orders.5—7 In addition, a number of retrospective analyses have 
indicated that pimavanserin poses signiﬁca cantly less mortality risk 
in patients with Parkinson disease psychosis (PDP) than treatment 
with atypical antipsychotic drugs,8,9 and lower mortality risk than 
untreated patients with PDP.10
The potential risk of QT prolongation was identiﬁed ed during the 
development of pimavanserin, with clinically relevant prolonga­
tion observed at pimavanserin doses twice that recommended for 
the treatment of PDP. Although this risk has been effectively 
managed with the US Food and Drug Administration approved 
pimavanserin (NUPLAZID) labeling,11 structurally and pharmaco­
logically related compounds with reduced risk of QT prolongation 
offer the potential to be better drugs.
ACP-204 was identiﬁed ed as a compound that may reduce the 
risk of QT prolongation. Here, we describe the nonclinical attri­
butes of ACP-204, including its pharmacological proﬁle le, and 
safety characteristics. Speciﬁca cally, we evaluated the in vitro 
proﬁle le of ACP-204 in a series of radioligand binding and cell- 
based functional assays to assess afﬁni nity, potency, and selec­
tivity. We also tested ACP-204 in 3 different rodent models of 
schizophrenia to evaluate its potential as an antipsychotic. We 
evaluated the pharmacokinetics of ACP-204 in rats and 
nonhuman primates (NHPs) to assess its potential as an oral drug, 
and we assessed the engagement of central 5-HT2A receptors in 
NHPs to assess its potential as a drug targeting the central 
nervous system. Finally, we report on long-term toxicity studies 
to establish its safety.

2.6. Cardiomyocyte assays
The effects of ACP-204 on adult human primary cardiomyocytes 
were assessed by Anabios Corporation. Adult human primary ven­

MyoPacer EP stimulator (IonOptix). Starting at 1 V, the amplitude of 
the stimulating pulse was increased until the cardiomyocytes started 
generating contraction-relaxation cycles, and a value 1.5× threshold 
was used throughout the experiment. Cardiomyocytes were then 
imaged at 240 Hz using an IonOptix MyoCam-S CCD camera and

2.7. Animals for pharmacology experiments
Male C57BL/6 mice (20—25 g) and BALB/c mice (20—25 g) were 
purchased from The Jackson Laboratory, and male Sprague—Dawley 
rats (200—225 g) were purchased from Envigo. Mice (4—8 per cage) 
and rats (2 per cage) were housed on a 12 hours light/dark cycle 
(lights on 7:00 AM, lights off 7:00 PM), with free access to food and 
water and were acclimated to vivarium conditions for a minimum of 
2 days before behavioral training. All pharmacology experiments 
were conducted at Behavioral Pharma in accordance with the 
National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of 
Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Ani­
mal Care and Use Committee at Behavioral Pharma (Protocol 
#062218).
2.8. Mouse DOI-induced head-twitch response
Mouse DOI head-twitch assays were carried out at Behavioral 
Pharma using male C57BL/6 mice. Head-twitch response (HTR) 
testing was conducted in a quiet, temperature-controlled room 
illuminated by white light on the ﬁrst half of the light cycle. Mice 
were individually placed in a plexiglass testing chamber (Length 
28 cm × Width 18 cm × Height 12 cm) with fresh bedding, and 
video recorded from a top-down perspective for subsequent 
behavioral analysis. On test day, mice were administered subcu­
taneously (SC) (10 mL/kg) a pretreatment of one of several doses 
(0.03, 0.1, or 0.3 mg/kg) of ACP-204 or vehicle (10% DMSO/saline). 
Sixty minutes after pretreatment, mice were administered intra­
peritoneally (10 mL/kg, 2.5 mg/kg) DOI and immediately placed in 
the testing chamber where activity was recorded for a duration of 
8 minutes. After the 8-minute recording session, mice were 
immediately returned to their home cage. Timing and quantiﬁca ca­
tion of HTR in mice was then measured by viewing individual 
video recordings at half speed. Head twitching was deﬁne ned as 
rapid, bidirectional, and rotational head movements (ie, wet dog 
shakes) that were not related to exploration or grooming. At the 
commencement of the experiment and during HTR quantiﬁca cation, 
the tester was blinded to the test doses. Data were recorded as the 
mean number of head twitches and analyzed using appropriate 
ANOVA, with head twitches as dependent variables and drug dose 
as independent variables. Where appropriate, post hoc analysis 
using Dunnett’s multiple-comparisons was performed. The head- 
twitch data were converted to percent inhibition relative to the 
vehicle/DOI control group according to the following equation: 
Percent inhibition = 100 −
([number of head twitches / mean 
number of head twitches in vehicle group] × 100).
2.9. Rat DOI-induced HTR
Rat DOI head-twitch assays were carried out at Behavioral 
Pharma using male Sprague—Dawley rats. HTR testing was con­
ducted identically to the mouse DOI head-twitch assay with the 
following changes: The testing chamber was Length 45 cm ×
Width 24 cm × Height 21 cm and the recording time to count head 
twitches after DOI administration was 15 minutes. ACP-204 was 
administered SC in a volume of 1 mL/kg, in saline vehicle, 60 mi­
nutes before DOI or orally (PO) in a volume of 5 mL/kg, in saline 
vehicle, 165 minutes before DOI, where indicated.
2.10. MK-801 (dizocilpine) induced locomotor activity
Mouse MK-801 locomotor assays were carried out at Behavioral 
Pharma using male BALB/c mice. Mice received a 10 mL/kg pre­
treatment SC injection of vehicle (0.9% saline) or ACP-204 at 0.1, 
0.3, or 1 mg/kg 30 minutes before a 10 mL/kg i.p. injection of MK- 
801 (0.4 mg/kg). M100907 (0.3 mg/kg, administered SC 30 minutes 
before MK-801) was included as a positive control. Locomotor 
activity was measured by an automated infrared photobeam 
monitoring apparatus (Kinder Scientiﬁc) c). Plexiglas cages (46 ×
24 × 21 cm) were placed inside the photobeam system, which 
consisted of 7 beams along the short axis and 15 beams across the 
long axis of the cage. The photobeam system was housed in a 
sound-attenuated room equipped with ventilation fans and a light 
source. On test day, mice were administered one of several doses of 
ACP-204 (time 0 minute) and placed in locomotor chambers and 
habituated for 30-minute habituation period (time −30 to 0 mi­
nutes). After the 30-minute habituation, mice were administered 
MK-801 (time 30 minutes), and locomotor activity was recorded 
for a duration of 90 minutes. Testing was conducted between 
10:00 AM to 1:00 PM. Data were collected online simultaneously 
from multiple photobeam activity chambers. A microcomputer 
connected to the activity boxes recorded X- and Y-ambulations. 
Activity monitor software (Motor Monitor) was used to reduce raw 
data into 10-minute interval “blocks.” The data were expressed as 
mean (± SEM) XY ambulations for the 90-minute activity cycle 
immediately after MK-801 administration). Data were analyzed 
using the GraphPad Prism (v8.3.1) statistical software. Appropriate 
ANOVA were used with treatment groups as between-subjects 
independent factors and total XY-ambulation as the dependent 
variables. Where appropriate, post hoc analysis was conducted 
using Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. The positive control was 
analyzed separately.
2.11. Prepulse inhibition
Rat prepulse inhibition (PPI) assays were carried out at 
Behavioral Pharma using male Sprague—Dawley rats. For control of 
stimuli and monitoring of startle responses, a SR-LAB Startle Sys­
tem (San Diego Instruments) was used. Each test chamber con­
sisted of a sound-attenuated, lighted, and ventilated cabinet that 
held a cylindrical, Plexiglas stabilimeter.
The rats were always tested according to a predetermined 
sequence that counterbalanced groups with respect to the time of 
day and stabilimeter chamber. Two days before drug testing, all 
rats were tested in a brief matching session consisting of 12 pulses 
alone (see below) trials and 3 PPI (12 dB[A]) trials (see below). The 
rats were then grouped into treatment cells (n = 15/cell), with 
approximately equal mean PPI scores across the cells.

On test days, rats were administered vehicle (0.9% saline, SC, 1 mL/ 
kg) or 1 of 3 doses of ACP-204 (0.1, 0.3, or 1.0 mg/kg) 45 minute 
before the administration of vehicle or DOI (1.25 mg/kg). Fifteen 
minutes after the DOI injection, PPI testing began. For testing, the rats 
were placed in a startle chamber for a 5-minute acclimation period 
with 70 dB[A] background noise. This was followed by a series of 
startle trials, consisting of 2 experimental conditions: (1) a 118 dB[A] 
40-ms noise burst presented alone (pulse-alone) or (2) the same 118 
dB[A] 40-ms noise burst preceded 100 ms by 1 of 3 prepulses (20-ms 
noise bursts) that were 3, 6, or 12 dB[A] above background. The 
degree of reﬂe ex inhibition caused by the prepulses was an opera­
tional measure of sensorimotor gating, and PPI was reduced 
signiﬁca cantly by the DOI used in these studies.
Each test session consisted of 4 experimental blocks. The ﬁrst 
and fourth blocks provided data only on the magnitude of the 
normal startle response generated by 4 pulse-alone trials. The 
change in startle magnitude between the ﬁrst and fourth blocks 
served as the main measure of startle habituation. The second and 
third blocks consisted of 8 pulse-alone and 5 prepulse trials of each 
type as described above, presented in pseudorandom order. The 
second and third block trials served as the main measure of PPI and 
startle magnitude. Intertrial intervals averaged 15 seconds (range, 
9—21 seconds). “No stimulus” measurements were made between 
each trial with stabilimeter recordings obtained without stimulus 
delivery. Mean no stimulus values were subtracted from all startle 
values by trial block to eliminate any contribution of nonstartle 
motor artifact to measures of startle magnitude.
The PPI was deﬁne ned as the percent reduction in startle amplitude 
in the presence of the prepulse stimulus compared with the 
amplitude in the absence of the prepulse stimulus for the 3 prepulse 
intensities: %PPI = [100 −
(100 × amplitude on prepulse trial / 
amplitude on pulse-alone trial)]. Thus, a high percent score indi­
cated a high degree of PPI, whereas a lower percent score indicated 
lesser degree of PPI. A 4-way repeated measures ANOVA with 
pretreatment and treatment as between subject factors, and block 
and prepulse intensity as within subjects factors was performed on 
the %PPI data. A similar 2-way ANOVA was performed on the 
average %PPI data from blocks 2 and 3 with pretreatment and 
treatment as between subject factors. The same analyses were 
performed on the acoustic startle data (startle to the pulse-alone 
trials). Post hoc analyses were carried out using Tukey’s test.
2.12. Positron emission tomography studies
Positron emission tomography (PET) studies using the 5-HT2A 
radiotracer [18F]altanserin were facilitated by Invicro using a male 
rhesus monkey (16 kg, 12 years old). Before assignment to the 
study, the subject was examined by a veterinarian and found to be 
healthy. The study was conducted in full compliance with Yale 
University’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee policies 
and procedures. Yale’s animal program is accredited by AAALAC 
International and has an Animal Welfare Assurance statement 
from the National Institutes of Health Ofﬁce ce of Laboratory Animal 
Welfare.
The subject was fasted overnight and anesthetized before each 
imaging event. At least 2 hours before radiotracer administration, 
the subject was induced with alfaxan 2 mg/kg intramuscular (IM), 
dexmedetomidine 0.1 mg/kg IM, and midazolam 0.3 mg/kg IM. 
Once induced, the subject was placed on isoﬂur urane using a mask 
and then administered dexamethasone 0.5 mg/kg IM to prevent 
nausea and emesis during recovery. Before intubation, lidocaine or 
cetacaine was applied topically over larynx to prevent spasm. 
Shortly after intubation, Antisedan 0.1 mg/kg IM was given to 
partially reverse the effects of dexmedetomidine. Sedation was 
maintained with 1.5% to 2.5% isoﬂur urane administered through a 
rebreathing circuit along with oxygen (1.5 L) throughout the PET 
scan. The subject received Lactated Ringer’s Solution plus 5% 
dextrose at 4 to 6 mL/kg/h IV. Body temperature was maintained 
using a heated water blanket. Vitals signs, including heart rate, 
respiratory rate, body temperature, oxygen saturation, and blood 
pressure were measured at least 4 times per hour.
ACP-204 was formulated in 0.9% saline fresh on each study day 
and ﬁltered through a 0.2 μm ﬁlter before use. The animal was 
dosed with vehicle (0.9% saline) followed by [18F]altanserin to 
obtain a baseline PET scan. Subsequently, the indicated doses of 
ACP-204 were administered intravenously (IV) as a bolus over 5 
minutes, followed by [18F]altanserin administered 30 minutes after 
ACP-204 administration start time. The baseline scan and the scans 
at each dose level were conducted at least 2 weeks apart to allow for 
recovery. Venous pharmacokinetic (PK) blood samples were 
collected at predose and 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes postdose 
into K2EDTA tubes, 2 mL per sampling time point following ACP-204 
administration for determination of ACP-204 plasma concentra­
tions. The plasma samples were stored frozen at −80 ◦C shipped on 
dry ice to QPS, LLC, where the bioanalysis was performed.
Dynamic PET images were acquired from the Focus-220 
(Siemens Medical Solutions) over 120 minutes in list mode and 
reconstructed into a series of 33 frames: 6 × 0.5 minutes, 3 × 1 
minutes, 2 × 2 minutes, and 22 × 5 minutes. Standard corrections 
were applied including random, scatter, system dead time, and 
attenuation. Image processing and data analyses were performed 
using PMOD 3.802 (PMOD Technologies). The subject’s PET images 
were coregistered to its magnetic resonance image and subse­
quently normalized to a common rhesus template space, where 
volumes of interest are deﬁne ned. Time-activity curves (kBq/cm3) 
were generated in volumes of interest, including the frontal lobe, 
parietal lobe, occipital lobe, temporal lobe, anterior cingulate, 
posterior cingulate, insula, and cerebellum. The outcome measure 
was binding potential (BPND) using the cerebellum as the non­
displaceable reference region, using a noninvasive modeling 
method applied to the time-activity curves (eg, simpliﬁed ed refer­
ence tissue model).13 Occupancy was computed as % Occupancy =
[1 −
(post-ACP-204 BPND / baseline BPND)] × 100, averaged across 
volumes of interest. The relationship between occupancy at 5- 
HT2A receptors with ACP-204 dose and plasma concentration 
were investigated using the Emax model (Bmax = 100%) to estimate 
ED50 and EC50, respectively.
2.13. Pharmacokinetic studies in rats
Rat PK studies were carried out at Wuxi using male (230—260 g) 
and female (200—230 g) Sprague—Dawley rats from Hilltop Lab 
Animals, Inc. Animals were acclimated for at least 2 days before the 
start of the study. Rats were fasted overnight before drug admin­
istration and fed 4 hours postdosing. Rats had access to water freely. 
ACP-204 was formulated in saline immediately before use. Aliquots 
of dose solution were taken from the dose formulations for the 
determination of dose accuracy by liquid chromatography—tandem 
mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). For IV dosing, the dosing solution 
was administered via the tail vein, 1 mg/kg in a volume of 2 mL/kg. 
For PO dosing, the dose solution was administered by oral gavage, 
delivering 10 mg/kg in a volume of 5 mL/kg. N = 3 rats were used for

via jugular vein or tail vein at 0.167, 0.333, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 4 hours

prechilled EDTA-K2—treated collecting tubes and centrifuged to 
separate plasma, which was stored at −70 ◦C until LC-MS/MS bio­
analysis. Plasma concentration versus time data was analyzed by 
noncompartmental approaches using the Phoenix WinNonlin 6.3 
software program to calculate the indicated PK parameters.

2.15. pKa and logP/logD determination
The pKa and logP/logD of ACP-204 were determined at Lonza 
using the Sirius Analytical Sirius T3. The sample consisted of 2 ex­
periments, one to determine pKa and a second to determine the logP/ 
logD. For the pKa determination, a small amount of the sample was 
placed in a sample vial with 1.5 mL of ionic strength adjusted water 
(0.15 M potassium chloride solution). The sample was then titrated 
from pH 2.0 to pH 12.0 3 times. The Sirius T3 records the pH and 
volume adjustments at each point to develop mass balance equations 
over the data. The data was reﬁned using the Sirius T3 Reﬁne ne soft­
ware to determine the pKa for the sample. Using the pKa, a second 
experiment was set up to determine the logP/logD. A small amount of 
sample was placed in a sample vial with 1.5 mL of ionic strength 
adjusted water and 0.25 mL of water-saturated octanol. The sample 
was then titrated from pH 2.0 to pH 12.0 3 times. Between each 
titration, 0.25 mL of water-saturated octanol was added. The data was 
reﬁne ned to determine the logP and the logD proﬁle le for the sample.

3.1. In vitro profile
To evaluate the in vitro activity of ACP-204, the inverse agonist 
activity (ability to suppress basal or “constitutive” receptor activ­
ity) and functional antagonist activity (ability to block agonist- 
induced receptor activity) was measured at 5-HT2A and 5-HT2C 
receptors (Table 2).14 The novel, cell-based functional assay, 
R-SAT,12 as well as a standard IP1 assay with Homogeneous Time- 
Resolved Fluorescence detection was used for these tests. ACP-204 
had a potency of approximately 0.3 nM as an inverse agonist and 
as a functional antagonist at 5-HT2A receptors in R-SAT assays, 0.5 
nM as a functional antagonist in IP1 assays.
With respect to in vitro activity at 5-HT2C receptors, ACP-204 
had a potency of approximately 37 nM as an inverse agonist and 
17 nM as a functional antagonist at 5-HT2C receptors in R-SAT as­
says and 16 nM as a functional antagonist in IP1 assays.
The agonist, functional antagonist, and inverse agonist activ­
ities of ACP-204 at a panel of receptors other than 5-HT2A or 
5-HT2C receptors spanning the monoamine G protein—coupled 
receptor family were characterized using R-SAT functional 
assays.12 No appreciable agonist activity of ACP-204 was seen at 
any of the receptors tested, including the 5-HT1A, 5-HT1B, 5-HT1D, 
5-HT1E, 5-HT1F, 5-HT2B, and 5-HT7 serotonin receptors; the α1A, 
α2A, α2B, and α2C adrenergic receptors; the D1 and D2 dopamine 
receptor; the H1 histamine receptor; and the M1, M2, M3, M4, and 
M5 muscarinic receptors.
ACP-204 had weak functional antagonist activity at the human 
H3 histamine receptor (200 nM) and the rat α2B adrenergic re­
ceptor (800 nM) and no functional antagonist activity at concen­
trations below 1 μM at the 5-HT1A, 5-HT1B, 5-HT1D, 5-HT1E, 5-HT1F, 
5-HT2B, or 5-HT7 serotonin receptors; the α1a, α2A, or α2C 
adrenergic receptors; the D1 and D2 dopamine receptors; the H1 
histamine receptor; or the M1, M2, M3, M4, or M5 muscarinic re­
ceptors. ACP-204 had weak inverse agonist activity at human 5- 
HT6 receptors (IC50 = 2 μM).
Radioligand binding studies were carried out to directly assess 
binding afﬁni nities of ACP-204 for 5-HT2A, 5-HT2B, and 5-HT2C re­

receptors were 0.14, 372, and 1.86 nanomolar, respectively. Thus, 
ACP-204 has approximately 13-fold selectivity for 5-HT2A relative 
to 5-HT2C receptors and approximately 2500-fold selectivity for 5- 
HT2A relative to 5-HT2B receptors based on radioligand binding.

3.2. In vivo profile
Administration of the 5-HT2A/2C agonist DOI to mice and rats 
elicits a characteristic head-twitch behavior that is mediated by 
central 5-HT2A receptors.15 Reduction of this DOI-induced head- 
twitch by the coadministration of another compound can be used 
to assess the in vivo antagonist activity of that compound at cen­
tral 5-HT2A receptors. As shown in Fig. 1A, ACP-204 signiﬁca cantly 
reduced DOI-induced HTR in a dose-dependent manner in mice. 
Results from mice treated SC with ACP-204 dissolved in 10% 
DMSO/saline demonstrated a signiﬁca cant effect of ACP-204 (F =
22.85; df = 3, 20; P < .0001). Follow-up post hoc analysis revealed 
that all doses of ACP-204 were signiﬁca cantly different from vehicle- 
DOI treated mice. ACP-204 inhibited HTR approximately 32% at 
0.03 mg/kg, 53% at 0.1 mg/kg and approximately 80% at 0.3 mg/kg, 
the highest dose tested. Similar results were observed in rats. 
Results from rats receiving SC ACP-204 pretreatment showed that 
rats treated with 0.3 and 1.0 mg/kg demonstrated a signiﬁca cant 
effect of ACP-204 (F = 8.79; df = 2, 14; P < .004). Follow-up post 
hoc analysis revealed all doses of ACP-204 were signiﬁca cantly 
different from vehicle-DOI treated rats. ACP-204 reduced HTR in a 
dose-dependent manner, with the lowest dose tested inhibiting 
HTR approximately 59% and nearly complete reduction (93%) of 
HTR at a dose of 1 mg/kg (Fig. 1B). Finally, the effects of ACP-204 on 
DOI-induced HTR were assessed after oral administration to rats. 
Results from rats receiving PO ACP-204 pretreatment showed that 
rats treated with 0. 3, 1.0, and 3.0 mg/kg demonstrated a signiﬁca cant 
effect of ACP-204 (F = 5.006; df = 3, 28; P < .007). Follow-up post 
hoc analysis revealed doses of 1.0 and 3.0 ACP-204 were signiﬁ­
cantly different from vehicle-DOI treated rats. There was a dose- 
dependent reduction in HTR, with a 56% reduction at 0.3 mg/kg, 
72% reduction at 1 mg/kg, and complete suppression of HTR at 3 
mg/kg (Fig. 1C). These results demonstrate that ACP-204 potently 
blocks central 5-HT2A receptors in rodents and is orally active.
MK-801 is a noncompetitive N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor 
antagonist that causes psychotic-spectrum reactions in humans 
and is used to mimic psychosis in experimental animals, an effect 
that is quantiﬁed ed by measuring hyperlocomotion. In this study, 
ACP-204 was assessed for its ability to attenuate MK-801—induced 
hyperlocomotion in BALB/c mice. Male BALB/c mice (16/group) each 
received a 10 mL/kg pretreatment SC injection of vehicle (2 groups), 
M100907 0.3 mg/kg (1 group, as an active control), or ACP-204 at 
doses of 0.1, 0.3, or 1 mg/kg (3 groups). Thirty minutes later, a 10 mL/ 
kg intraperitoneal injection of MK-801 at 0.4 mg/kg was given to the 
mice except for one of the vehicle-treated groups, which received 
another injection of vehicle. MK-801 signiﬁca cantly increased loco­
motor activity versus vehicle controls across the 90-minute activity 
session (P < .0001), and M100907 (positive control) ameliorated 
MK-801-induced hyperlocomotion (P =.0008). The ACP-204 dose 
response was also signiﬁca cant (by ANOVA, P < .0001). Follow-up 
post hoc analysis revealed that mice treated with ACP-204 at 
doses of 0.1, 0.3, and 1 mg/kg were signiﬁca cantly improved over 
vehicle-MK-801-treated mice with the minimum effective dose 
equal to 0.1 mg/kg (Fig. 2). Data collected during the baseline 
session, immediately after ACP-204 administration, and 30 
minutes before MK-801 administration, revealed that M100907 
and ACP-204 at all doses tested did not produce any deﬁci cits in 
baseline activity relative to vehicle treated animals (data not 
shown).
ACP-204 was assessed for its ability to reverse DOI-induced 
deﬁci cits in PPI in Sprague—Dawley rats. Rats were pretreated 
with either vehicle or ACP-204 0.1, 0.3, or 1 mg/kg, SC, followed 45 
minutes later by the second treatment with vehicle or DOI (SC). 
Rats were placed into the test chambers 15-minute after DOI 
administration and testing began after a 5-minute habituation 
period. DOI disrupted PPI as indicated by a main effect of drug 
treatment (F[1,112] = 33.37, P < .001); whereas ACP-204 alone had 
no overall effect on PPI (main effect of pretreatment; F < 1, not 
signiﬁca cant). Data were collapsed by prepulse intensity and block 
(block 2 and 3) to evaluate the drug interactions. There were no 
robust effects of DOI or ACP-204 on startle magnitude that would 
confound the interpretation of the PPI data (data not shown). 
Although there was not an overall signiﬁca cant effect of ACP-204 
dose on PPI, exploratory analysis on individual doses of ACP-204 
revealed that ACP-204 at a dose of 1 mg/kg prevented impair­
ment in PPI induced by DOI (Fig. 3, P < .05). No effect of ACP-204 
was observed at lower doses. Thus, ACP-204 blocked DOI- 
induced PPI disruption. Additionally, evaluation of the habitua­
tion data (ie, comparison of startle magnitude from block 1 vs 
block 4) revealed ACP-204 did not disrupt normal habituation to 
startle response (data not shown).

3.3. Cardiovascular safety
In GLP-compliant manual patch clamp assays, ACP-204 
inhibited hERG (human ether-a-go-go-related gene product, 
Kv11.1 potassium channel) current in HEK-293 cells with a con­
centration of drug producing 50% inhibition (IC50) of 1.8 μM 
(Table 4). In contrast, the hERG IC50 for pimavanserin was 0.2 μM. 
The IC50 for the effect on the human L-type calcium current 
(hCav1.2) was 3.5 μM compared with an IC50 of 1.2 μM for pima­
vanserin. ACP-204 did not signiﬁca cantly inhibit the sodium NaV1.5 
ion channel at concentrations up to 3 μM and inhibited the NaV1.5 
ion channel by an average of 43% at 10 μM compared with an IC50 
of 1.2 μM for pimavanserin.
To assess the effects of ACP-204 on cardiovascular function in 
native tissue, a study was conducted using isolated human car­
diomyocytes, which provided further evidence that ACP-204 poses 
low risk of causing arrhythmia. ACP-204 had mild effects on ino­
tropy, decreasing sarcomere shortening on the adult human pri­
mary cardiomyocytes, with an IC50 value of 0.74 μM. ACP-204 had 
minimal effects on all proarrhythmia parameters measured at 
concentrations up to 1 μM and no effects up to 3 μM on the more 
serious parameters (with respect to arrhythmia) of after- 
contraction (deﬁne ned as a spontaneous secondary contraction 
transient of the cardiomyocyte that occurred before the next 
regular contraction leading to an abnormal and unsynchronized 
contraction) and contractility escape (identiﬁed ed when the elec­
trical stimulus did not initiate a contraction). The results on car­
diovascular function seen with ACP-204 on native tissue are 
consistent with its effects on isolated hERG, CaV1.2, and NaV1.5 
channels in that there was little to no impact on contractility or 
rhythm at concentrations up to and including 1 μM.

3.5. Safety and toxicity
The safety and toxicity proﬁle le of ACP-204 was evaluated in a 
series of studies in rats and cynomolgus monkeys, including gen­
otoxicity studies and chronic administration studies of 6- and 9- 
months duration for rats and NHPs, respectively. ACP-204 was 
negative in the Ames mutagenicity test, and in both in vitro and 
in vivo micronucleus tests (Acadia data on ﬁle).
In the 6-month rat toxicity study, doses of 20, 40, and 60 mg/kg/ 
day were administered to the male rats to account for the observed 
sex differences in metabolism and achieve similar exposures as 10, 
20, and 40 mg/kg/day in the female rats (see Table 7). After daily 
oral administration of ACP-204 to male rats, Cmax and AUC0—24hr 
values of ACP-204 were less than dose proportional across the 
dose range studied on days 1 and 182. After daily oral adminis­
tration of ACP-204 to female rats, Cmax values of ACP-204 were less 
than dose proportional and AUC0—24hr values were approximately 
dose proportional across the dose range studied on days 1 and 182.
Based on the absence of adverse antemortem and postmortem 
ﬁndings, the no-observed-adverse-effect level was considered to 
be the highest dose tested in each sex, which was 40 and 60 mg/kg 
in females and male rats, respectively. At the no-observed- 
adverse-effect level, after 26 weeks of treatment, the mean ACP- 
204 Cmax and AUC0—24hr values for females were 1360 ng/mL and 
27,500 ng × h/mL and for males were 1400 ng/mL and 19,400 ng ×
h/mL, respectively. The mean day 182 Cmax value at the 60 mg/kg/ 
day dose in male rats represents a 36-fold safety margin compared 
with the exposure of 39 ng/mL in rats at the maximum effective SC 
dose of 1 mg/kg in the rat DOI head-twitch assay, and a 47-fold 
safety margin compared with the exposure of 30 ng/mL at the 
maximum effective PO dose of 3 mg/kg in the same assay.

40 mg/kg/day for both males and females, as systemic exposure 
appeared independent of sex in monkeys. Drug exposure was 
approximately dose proportional from 10 to 20 mg/kg/day and 
slightly greater than dose proportional between 20 and 40 mg/kg/ 
day (Table 7). Based on the absence of adverse antemortem and

4. Discussion
Alzheimer disease psychosis (ADP) and Lewy body dementia 
psychosis (LBDP) are debilitating conditions for which there are 
currently no approved therapies. Off-label treatment with dopa­
mine blocking antipsychotics is usually ineffective and associated 
with serious adverse effects.17—20 Psychosis is estimated to afﬂic ict 
30% of subjects with dementia21 and 60% or more in subjects with 
Lewy body dementia,22 representing large, unmet medical needs. 
Subjects with ADP or LBD are typically frail, elderly people, often 
with many other comorbidities in addition to their psychotic 
symptoms. Accordingly, a number of considerations went into the 
effort to identify a compound with clear antipsychotic potential 
and an excellent safety proﬁle le that eventually produced ACP-204.
Several lines of evidence implicate 5-HT2A in psychosis, sug­
gesting that it is a good target to treat ADP and LBDP. Visual hal­
lucinations 
are 
a 
common 
symptom 
in 
subjects 
with 
neurodegenerative disease.23 Qualitatively similar symptoms can 
be provoked by 5-HT2A receptor agonists such as lysergic acid 
diethylamide and psilocybin, and are suppressed by coadminis­
tration of drugs such as ketanserin that block 5-HT2ARs.24,25
Secondly, a large class of drugs called atypical antipsychotics, 
exempliﬁed ed by clozapine, feature high afﬁni nity for 5-HT2ARs rela­
tive to D2 dopamine receptors as the most common and deﬁni ning 
feature of their molecular proﬁle le.26 Although used widely to treat 
schizophrenia in the general population, these drugs not approved 
for psychosis in ADP or LBDP. Off-label use of these drugs to treat 
psychosis associated with neurodegenerative disease has had 
mixed success, particularly when the adverse effects of these 
drugs, including motoric dysfunction, sedation, cognitive impair­
ment, and increased risk of mortality are taken into account.17—20 It 
is generally believed that these adverse effects are due to in­
teractions with many other molecular targets such as dopami­
nergic, histaminergic, adrenergic, and muscarinic receptors. In 
addition, all atypical antipsychotic drugs occupy D2 dopamine 
receptors at therapeutic doses, thus their antipsychotic efﬁca cacy 
cannot be entirely ascribed to blockade of 5-HT2ARs.
Several selective 5-HT2AR inverse agonists, including voli­
nanserin, eplivanserin, ritanserin, and lumateperone have 
demonstrated 
clinical 
efﬁca cacy 
in 
patients 
with 
schizo­
phrenia.1,27—31 With the exception of lumateperone, none of these 
drugs are predicted to occupy D2 dopamine receptors at thera­
peutic doses, conﬁrm rming the antipsychotic potential of targeting 5- 
HT2ARs. Tolerability of these more selective agents is generally 
better than the less-selective atypical antipsychotic drugs, 
consistent with the notion that the interactions at other targets 
reduce tolerability.
Pimavanserin is a selective 5-HT2A inverse agonist that is the 
ﬁrst US Food and Drug Administration approved drug to treat 
hallucinations and delusions associated with Parkinson disease,32 
and is the ﬁrst approved antipsychotic drug that does not occupy 
D2 dopamine receptors at therapeutic doses.33 Subsequently, 
pimavanserin was shown to be effective at reducing the frequency 
and/or severity of hallucinations and AD and LBD,5,34 and was well 
tolerated and did not impair cognition or worsen motoric function 
in these patients.34—36
Pimavanserin prolongs the QTc interval at therapeutic doses, 
and the degree of QTc prolongation increases with higher doses.37 
Thus the therapeutic dose is capped at a once-daily dose of 34 mg. 
Exposure-response analysis of pimavanserin across a number of 
clinical studies showed a positive correlation between drug 
exposure and clinical response suggesting that higher doses would 
potentially have been more effective.38,39 Therefore, given the 
evidence that targeting 5-HT2ARs may be a promising strategy for 
treating ADP and LBDP, an effort was launched to identify a potent 
and selective 5-HT2A inverse agonist. This effort culminated in the 
creation of ACP-204.
A comprehensive nonclinical evaluation was conducted on 
ACP-204, including in vitro and in vivo pharmacology, target 
engagement, pharmacokinetics, safety, and toxicity studies. ACP- 
204 showed potent in vitro antagonist and inverse agonist activ­
ity at 5-HT2A receptors, approximately 3- to 6-fold more potent 
than previously reported for pimavanserin.40,41 ACP-204 was 
approximately 30- to 100-fold selective for 5-HT2A over 5-HT2C in 
functional assays, comparable to what was reported previously for 
pimavanserin.40,41 ACP-204 had no appreciable activity over 70 
other targets tested. Overall, the in vitro proﬁle le demonstrates ACP- 
204 is a highly potent and selective antagonist/inverse agonist of 
5-HT2A receptors that lacks appreciable interactions with other 
targets except 5-HT2C receptors.
The antipsychotic potential of ACP-204 was evaluated using 3 
separate experimental animal models, each considered standard 
assays for predicting antipsychotic activity. Administration of the 
hallucinogenic 5-HT2A/2C agonist DOI to mice and rats elicits a 
characteristic head-twitch behavior that is correlated with hallu­
cinogenic activity42 and is mediated by central 5-HT2A receptors.15 
ACP-204 reduced DOI-induced HTR, indicating oral bioavailability 
and potent in vivo blockade of central 5-HT2A receptors.
MK-801 is used in animal models to mimic psychosis for 
experimental purposes and to assess the antipsychotic potential of 
drugs.43 It is a noncompetitive N-methyl-D-aspartate antagonist 
associated with a number of adverse side effects in humans, 
including cognitive disruption and psychotic-spectrum reactions. 
ACP-204 and the highly selective 5-HT2A antagonist/inverse 
agonist volinanserin (M100907) attenuated hyperlocomotion 
provoked by MK-801.
Deﬁci cits in sensorimotor gating are a feature of schizophrenia in 
humans and can be assessed by measuring startle response to an 
auditory stimulus, either with or without a prior lower-intensity 
stimulus (a prepulse) immediately preceding the main stimulus. 
With normal sensorimotor gating mechanisms intact, rodents will 
startle less if a prepulse is presented than if it is not (eg, prepulse 
inhibition or PPI). A similar phenomenon occurs in humans, and 
thus, PPI assays have translational value. Hallucinogenic drugs can 
disrupt PPI, and drugs such as the 5-HT2A agonist DOI disrupt PPI in 
rats.44,45 ACP-204 was shown to reduce PPI in rats, demonstrating 
its potential to ameliorate sensorimotor gating deﬁci cits in humans.
PET studies in rhesus monkeys using the highly selective 5- 
HT2A receptor radioligand [18F]altanserin46—49 demonstrated that 
ACP-204 efﬁci ciently enters the brain and potently engages cortical 
5-HT2A receptors in higher species. The PET data provided a good 
estimate of the relationship between occupancy of central 5-HT2A 
receptors and plasma concentrations of ACP-204. The estimated 
EC50 for ACP-204 was 0.33 ng/mL, approximately half the value 
(0.6 ng/mL) estimated for pimavanserin,50 in agreement with 
ongoing (Burstein unpublished observations) and completed51 
clinical work on these 2 compounds. These data support the sys­
temic administration of ACP-204 to humans to engage central 5- 
HT2A receptors, and suggest it may do so more potently than 
pimavanserin.
Safety is a chief concern among drug candidates intended for 
use in elderly populations. Inhibition of human ether-a-go-go 
(hERG) potassium channels is linked to prolongation of the QTc 
E.S. Burstein, R. Olsson, N. Skold et al. 
The Journal of Pharmacology and Experimental Therapeutics 393 (2026) 104932
10

interval and is common in many central nervous system drugs 
beside pimavanserin, including other selective 5-HT2A inverse 
agonists, many antipsychotics and certain antidepressants.52—55 
The nonclinical proﬁle le of ACP-204 suggests a low risk of causing 
QT prolongation, clinically. The IC50 for inhibition of hERG current 
was 9-fold higher for ACP-204 than pimavanserin, whereas ACP- 
204 engages central 5-HT2A receptors approximately 2-fold more 
potently than pimavanserin (discussed above), effectively a nearly 
20-fold advantage. Other ion channels can also impact cardiovas­
cular function, particularly the L-type calcium current (Cav1.2) and 
the sodium NaV1.5 ion channel. The IC50 values for inhibition of 
these ion channels was 3- and over 8-fold higher, respectively, for 
ACP-204 than pimavanserin. Signiﬁca cantly, the IC50 for ACP-204 at 
Cav1.2 was only 1.9-fold higher than its IC50 for hERG. L-type cal­
cium channels are believed to have opposing effects to hERG on 
heart rhythm. Therefore, drugs that block both hERG and calcium 
channels may have a lower propensity for causing arrhythmic ef­
fects.56 Experiments with primary human cardiomyocytes 
conﬁrm rmed the minimal impact of ACP-204 on cardiovascular 
function, now conﬁrm rmed clinically (Acadia data on ﬁle, discussed 
below). In addition to cardiovascular safety, chronic toxicity 
studies demonstrated wide safety margins for ACP-204.
Initial studies into the PKs of ACP-204 indicated differences in 
metabolism and oral bioavailability between male and female rats. 
Sex differences in ACP-204 PK were not seen in the toxicity studies 
in cynomolgus monkeys (see above), nor were they observed in 
mice or in humans in a phase 1 clinical trial (Acadia data on ﬁle). 
Thus, the sex differences in PK seen in rats are most likely a 
rat-speciﬁc c phenomenon. Rats are known to exhibit large sex 
differences in the expression of cytochrome P450 metabolizing 
enzymes,57 which may explain the rat-speciﬁc c differences in PK 
seen with ACP-204.
In cynomolgus monkeys, the volume of distribution and half- 
life estimates were signiﬁca cantly larger for pimavanserin than for 
ACP-204, whereas their clearance rates were similar. These results 
suggest that the difference in the half-lives of the compounds in 
cynomolgus monkeys is governed primarily by the volume of 
distribution. Estimates for human ACP-204 half-life ranged be­
tween 14.7 and 21.7 hours. These projected ACP-204 half-life 
values are all consistent with once-daily dosing and would allow 
steady state to be achieved much more quickly, potentially 
permitting a faster onset of action than pimavanserin, which has a 
t1/2 of 55—60 hours.16 The safety proﬁle of ACP-204 in rats and 
monkeys was excellent, with a wide safety margin demonstrated 
in long-term toxicity studies.
Supported by robust nonclinical evidence, ACP-204 has 
completed phase 1 clinical studies and recently started phase 2 
trials in ADP and LBDP. The results of the phase 1 studies will be 
presented in detail elsewhere. In brief, ACP-204 was well toler­
ated; had nearly linear pharmacokinetics, with t1/2 values similar 
to those predicted above; and showed no evidence of QT prolon­
gation at doses exceeding those being tested in phase 2. The ACP- 
204 phase 2 programs build on the experience with pimavanserin 
and will evaluate 30- and 60-mg doses versus placebo. This dose 
range will test doses approximately twice the pimavanserin 34-mg 
equivalent. If the results from phase 2 warrant, full phase 3 pro­
grams will be pursued.

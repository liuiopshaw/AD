ABSTRACT: Microglia as an important type of innate immune cell in the brain have been
considered as an eﬀective therapeutic target for the treatment of central nervous degenerative
diseases. Herein, we report cell membrane coated novel biomimetic Cu2−xSe-PVP-Qe
nanoparticles (denoted as CSPQ@CM nanoparticles, where PVP is poly(vinylpyrrolidone), Qe
is quercetin, and CM is the cell membrane of neuron cells) for eﬀectively targeting and
modulating microglia to treat Parkinson’s disease (PD). The CSPQ nanoparticles exhibit
multienzyme activities and could eﬀectively scavenge the reactive oxygen species and promote the
polarization of microglia into the anti-inﬂammatory M2-like phenotype to relieve neuro-
inﬂammation. We reveal that biomimetic CSPQ@CM nanoparticles targeted microglia through
the speciﬁc interactions between the membrane surface vascular cells adhering to molecule-1 and
α4β1 integrin expressed by microglia. They could signiﬁcantly improve the symptoms of PD mice
to result in an excellent therapeutic eﬃcacy, as evidenced by the recovery of their dopamine level
in cerebrospinal ﬂuid, tyrosine hydroxylase, and ionized calcium binding adapter protein 1 to
normal levels. Our work demonstrates the great potential of these robust biomimetic
nanoparticles in the targeted treatment of PD and other central nervous degenerative diseases.
■INTRODUCTION
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common major
central nervous degenerative disease, which is especially
common in elderly people and has become a serious economic
and social burden.1 The current clinical treatment of PD is
strongly reliant on chemical drugs (e.g., dopaminergic-type
drugs, anticholinergics, glutamate antagonist). The chemo-
therapy of PD faces the challenge of eﬀective targeted delivery
of robust drugs to substantia nigra and striatum of the brain,
however, due to the lack of targeting capability of drugs and
the protection of the blood brain barrier (BBB). Moreover, the
chemotherapy only relieves the patient’s symptoms and cannot
completely stop the progress of the disease or reverse the
existing disabilities.2 An alternative solution is surgical
treatment, such as the neuronuclear destruction and deep
brain stimulation (DBS).3 The implantation of electrodes is
invasive, however, with risks of inﬂammation and brain
trauma.4 It is of great importance to develop advanced
noninvasive treatments for PD patients.
From a pathogenetic perspective, PD is usually characterized
by the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and
striatum and the intracellular accumulation of α-synuclein,5
both which involve multiple pathways and mechanisms,
including oxidative stress, neuroinﬂammation, mitochondrial
dysfunction, formation of Lewy bodies, and so on.6−8 Under
normal conditions, the homeostasis between oxidation and
antioxidation and the resulting microenvironment of the brain
are ﬁnely supervised and maintained by microglia.9 Breaking
such homeostasis in the brain could lead to an accumulation of
excessive ROS to cause neuronal death and neural
dysregulation.10 Microglia usually participate in ROS gen-
eration, and ROS in turn can activate microglia to secrete
proinﬂammatory factors and exacerbate inﬂammation, even-
tually leading to the apoptosis of neuronal cells.11,12 In
addition, excessive activation of microglia can cause astrocytes
to convert to the pathological A1 morphology, which also
results in various neurodegenerative diseases, including PD.13
The above statements demonstrate that microglia could be
an important target for treating various neurodegenerative
diseases. The interrelationship between microglia and ROS
suggests that the elimination of excess ROS could signiﬁcantly
modulate the overactivation of microglia and alleviate the
oxidative damage to neurons to improve the eﬃcacy of PD
treatment. There are two types of antioxidants for the
elimination of ROS in the brain, i.e., enzymatic antioxidants,
such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and
glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and nonenzymatic antioxidants,
including vitamin C, vitamin E, reduced glutathione,
carotenoids, trace elements, etc.14 The amount and activity
of endogenous antioxidant enzymes are decreased and

substantia nigra and striatum of the brain for the elimination of
ROS, which is also very challenging due to the low targeting
capability of exogenous enzymes, the instability of enzymatic
activity, and the protection of the BBB. In addition, the
exogenous enzymes delivered to the brain can be rapidly
cleared by the glymphatic system, in which the resident
immune cells such as microglia play a pivotal role.16 Microglia
can remove them through the paravascular route,17 which
results in low accumulation and greatly reduces their
therapeutic eﬃcacy. Therefore, targeting microglia for
improving their endocytosis of enzymes is crucial for
improving the therapeutic eﬃcacy.
As an complementation of native enzymes, there are a
number of synthetic nanoparticles that have been recently
demonstrated to show enzymelike activities for the potential
therapy of cerebral diseases, e.g., cerium oxide (CeO2),18
manganese oxide (Mn3O4),19 copper oxide (CuxO),20 and iron
oxide (Fe3O4),21 which exhibit the advantages of stable
enzyme activity and low cost.22−24 To further boost the
enzymelike activity of nanoparticles, they are usually modiﬁed
with speciﬁc functional organic molecules. One typical
modiﬁer is quercetin (Qe), which is a ﬂavonoid mainly
found in fruits, vegetables, and medicinal botanicals25 and has
shown plenty of beneﬁts for health, such as antioxidation,26
antitumor activity,27 radio sensitization,28 and tyrosine kinase
inhibition.29 Qe can be coordinated with the surface atoms of
nanoparticles not only to increase the performance of
nanoparticles but also to improve its water solubility and
pharmacological activity.
Since the enzymelike activity of nanoparticles is strongly
dependent on the particle size due to the dominant ratio of
surface atoms, it is a signiﬁcant advance to prepare
biocompatible ultrasmall nanoparticles with excellent enzyme-
like activity for PD treatment. In this context, ultrasmall copper
chalcogenide nanoparticles, such as Cu2−xSe,30,31 could be an
ideal candidate. They are expected to show excellent
performance because they can eﬃciently degrade H2O2 into
H2O through the redox reaction. More importantly, ultrasmall
Cu2−xSe nanoparticles show advantages in crossing the BBB in
comparison with their larger counterparts.32 They could be
eﬃciently delivered to the brain tissue under the assistance of
focused ultrasound and rapidly degraded in the brain and other
major organs to reduce the potential side eﬀects. They also
show excellent performance in multimodal imaging and
therapy of tumors, including orthotopic glioma.33,34 In
addition, copper is a crucial cofactor of many enzymes, such
as cytochrome c oxidase35 and copper−zinc superoxide
dismutase,36 which are essential for maintaining healthy
neurons.37 Interestingly, it has been demonstrated that the
Cu2+−Qe complex exhibited better enzymelike activity and
stability than Qe alone.38 Therefore, the modiﬁcation of
ultrasmall Cu2−xSe nanoparticles with Qe would signiﬁcantly
increase the therapeutic performance of nanoparticles in PD
treatment, which has not been investigated.
As mentioned previously, the main challenge of PD
treatment includes the low accumulation of therapeutic agents
in the substantia nigra and striatum of the brain due to the
protective BBB, the lack of targeting capability, and the
clearance by immune cells. We hypothesize that coating
nanoparticles with the proper type of cell membrane could
enable them to eﬀectively target microglia and modulate their
overactivation for improving the treatment of PD. As a matter
of fact, diﬀerent types of cell membranes (e.g., red blood cell
membrane, cancer cell membrane, neutrophil membrane, and
platelet membrane) have been used to camouﬂage the
nanoparticles for tumor therapy and ischemic stroke.39−41
The resultant biomimetic nanoparticles possess some favorable
functions of the source cells, such as immune evasion, long
blood circulation time, and tumor targeting capability.
However, there is no report on their applications in PD
treatment.
In this work, we propose that neuronal cell membrane (CM,
i.e., MES23.5 cells) coated Cu2−xSe-PVP-Qe biomimetic
nanoparticles (abbreviated as CSPQ@CM) could target
microglia and manipulate their polarization for improving the
eﬃcacy of PD therapy (Scheme 1). These biomimetic

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Characterization of CSPQ Nanoparticles and Assess-
ment of Excellent Multiple Enzyme Activities. The
poly(vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP) modiﬁed ultrasmall Cu2−xSe
nanoparticles (abbreviated as CSP) were prepared by an
aqueous route under ambient conditions as described else-
where.43 The obtained Cu2−xSe nanoparticles were 3.0 ± 0.3
nm in size (Figure S1a−b of the Supporting Information) and
exhibited excellent colloidal stability. The abundant copper
ions on the surfaces of the ultrasmall nanoparticles were used
to coordinate with quercetin (Qe) for functionalization. The
nanoparticle solution turned brownish yellow after modiﬁca-
tion (Figure 1a). The morphology and size of the modiﬁed
nanoparticles (CSPQ) were well maintained, as characterized
by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Figure 1b and
Figure S1c). The Qe loaded on the surface of CSP
nanoparticles was estimated to be 17.6 wt % by thermogravi-
metric analysis (TGA) (Figure S1d). To demonstrate the
properties and better performance of CSPQ nanoparticles
compared to CSP nanoparticles and Qe alone, we prepared
PVP-modiﬁed Qe (abbreviated as QP) solution for compar-
ison. The red shift of the characteristic absorption of Qe
(shown in Figure 1c) from 258 nm (QP) to 293 nm (CSPQ)
and the new absorption at 436 nm (CSPQ) demonstrate the
successful modiﬁcation of Cu2−xSe nanoparticles through the
coordination of surface copper ions with Qe (Figure S2).44

CSPQ nanoparticles to eliminate overall ROS by using 2,7-
dichloroﬂuorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) as a probe, which
can react with ROS to show strong ﬂuorescence.30 The lowest

Microenvironment Regulation and Cytoprotection
Mechanism of CSPQ Nanoparticles. The above results
show that we are able to obtain robust nanoparticles with
multienzyme-like property by proper surface modiﬁcation.
Based on these results, we investigated the regulation of the
microenvironment by BV2 cells and the cytoprotection of SH-
SY5Y cells provided by CSPQ nanoparticles. The cell
endocytosis of CSPQ nanoparticles is schematically shown in
Figure 2a. We ﬁrst used the classic CCK-8 assay to assess the
cell viability of BV2 cells, SH-SY5Y cells, and NIH-3T3 cells
after they were cultured with diﬀerent concentrations of CSPQ
nanoparticles. The results show that CSPQ nanoparticles had
no obvious cytotoxicity to either cell line with a copper
concentration below 0.8 mM (Figure 2b). We then evaluated
the ROS scavenging ability in BV2 cells by monitoring the
ﬂuorescence of DCF. As shown in Figure 2c and Figure S7,
incubation of pretreated BV2 cells (by 0.1 mM H2O2) with
CSPQ nanoparticles drastically reduced the ROS to similar
levels of normal control cells compared to that of CSP
nanoparticles and QP complex. The above results demonstrate
that our CSPQ nanoparticles could eﬀectively relieve oxidative
stress in cells by multienzyme-like property.
In addition, the protection of neurons by CSPQ nano-
particles was examined by using SH-SY5Y cells to mimic
neuronal cells, which were treated with 1-methyl-4-phenyl-
pyridinium (MPP+) to induce classic oxidative stress in PD.45
As shown in Figure S8, the cell viability was gradually
decreased with MPP+ concentration increasing from 0 to 10
mM. In the subsequent experiments, we selected 3 mM MPP+
to induce cell neurotoxicity. The regulation of the micro-
environment and protection of CSPQ nanoparticles for
neuronal cells are attributed to their capability of scavenging
ROS, which is further proved by the change in intracellular
ROS. Incubation of pretreated cells with CSPQ nanoparticles
drastically reduced the ROS of MPP+-treated SH-SY5Y cells to
similar levels of normal cells (Figure S9a), and the
corresponding ﬂow cytometry assay in Figure S9b also
indicates that CSPQ nanoparticles could more eﬀectively
eliminate ROS than QP complex and CSP nanoparticles.
The excess ROS could lead to mitochondrial dysfunction
and apoptosis of cells. Therefore, we further used double-
ﬂuorescence 5,5′,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethylimidacar-
bocyanine iodide (JC-1) to explore the depolarization of
mitochondrial membranes of SH-SY5Y cells. The red
ﬂuorescence of the probe in the normal SH-SY5Y cells
indicates a high mitochondrial potential, but notable green
ﬂuorescence observed in the cells treated with MPP+ (Figure
2d) suggests a dramatic decrease in mitochondrial membranes
potential. Pretreatment of SH-SY5Y cells with CSPQ nano-
particles can protect cells against depolarization of their
mitochondrial membranes, in comparison with cells treated
with CSP nanoparticles and QP complex, respectively (Figure
2d and Figure S10). The protection of CSPQ nanoparticles
against the oxidative stress of SH-SY5Y cells is also proved by
their morphological characteristics (Figure 2e). Compared
with normal SH-SY5Y cells, they became elongated after
treatment with 3 mM MPP+ for 24 h, but these pretreated
fusiform cells changed back to normal morphology after the
addition of 0.2 mM CSPQ nanoparticles.
It is well-known that M2-like phenotype microglia exhibit
anti-inﬂammatory properties and hold potential for the
treatment of central nervous degenerative diseases by releasing
anti-inﬂammatory cytokines.10 We used ﬂow cytometry to
determine the variation of expressed speciﬁc markers of cells
treated with or without CSPQ nanoparticles. The M2-like
phenotype marker CD20642 was enhanced after treatment with
CSPQ nanoparticles for 24 h (Figure 2f), which means that
CSPQ nanoparticles notably increased the ratio of M2-like
phenotype in BV2 cells in comparison with untreated cells.
Furthermore, the immunoﬂuorescence images of biomarker
CD206 expressed by BV2 cells after diﬀerent treatments were
collected and are shown in Figure 2g. Obviously, the
expression of CD206 in BV2 cells after treatment with
CSPQ nanoparticles was signiﬁcantly enhanced in comparison
with the cells treated with H2O2 or IL-4 (25 ng/mL). The
above results demonstrate the superiority of CSPQ nano-
particles in modulating the polarization of microglia into M2-
like phenotype by eliminating ROS in the microenvironment.
Since excess ROS could lead to the imbalance of the
microenvironment, which is strictly surveilled by microglia to
protect neurons from damage, we investigated the inﬂamma-
tory response of microglia after treatment with CSPQ
nanoparticles by quantifying the expression of cytokines in
the conditioned media. The standard curves of interleukin-10
(IL-10) and interleukin 6 (IL-6) are shown in Figure S11. A
signiﬁcant reduction of the proinﬂammatory cytokine IL-6 was
obtained (Figure 2h). In contrast, the expression of IL-10 in
BV2 cells, which were treated with 0.2 mM CSPQ nano-
particles (Figure 2i), was about twice that in untreated cells.
Further increasing the concentration of CSPQ nanoparticles to
0.4 mM did not lead to a notable change.
To further address the protective eﬀects of CSPQ
nanoparticles on SH-SY5Y cells, we examined the expression
of antiapoptotic Bcl-2 and proapoptotic caspase-3 proteins,
which are associated with the apoptotic pathway of cells and
play an important role in neuronal degeneration.46,47 When the
SH-SY5Y cells were incubated with MPP+, their expression of
proapoptotic caspase-3 proteins was obviously upregulated,
and the variation of antiapoptotic Bcl-2 was negligible in
comparison with normal healthy cells (Figure 2j−k). In
contrast, incubation of SH-SY5Y cells with CSPQ nano-
particles led to drastic upregulation of Bcl-2 protein and
obvious downregulation of caspase-3 protein, which demon-
strates that CSPQ nanoparticles could signiﬁcantly boost the

Targeted Delivery of CSPQ@CM Nanoparticles by
Opening the Blood−Brain Barrier with Focused Ultra-
sound. The in vitro results demonstrate that CSPQ@CM
nanoparticles exhibited excellent targeting capability and could
be used to treat ROS-related PD if they were eﬃciently
delivered to the brain. As reported previously, focused
ultrasound can temporarily and reversibly open the BBB to
realize the eﬃcient accumulation of nanoparticles in the brain
through diﬀusion.49,50 Modiﬁcation of nanoparticles with a
targeting moiety can further enhance the accumulation of
nanoparticles delivered by focused ultrasound. Therefore, we
chose focused ultrasound to assist the delivery of our CSPQ@
CM nanoparticle for treatment of PD (Figure 4a). Since the
Cu2−xSe nanoparticles exhibited excellent photothermal
conversion for photoacoustic (PA) imaging,51 which can be
used to monitor the opening of the BBB and the accumulation
of as-prepared CSPQ@CM nanoparticles, we tested the PA
performance of our nanoparticles in vitro and in vivo. As shown
in Figure 4b, the PA signal was signiﬁcantly enhanced by
CSPQ@CM nanoparticles and increased with their concen-
tration. We then sonicated the right brain of a nude mouse
with 0.5 MHz ultrasound for 90 s, followed by intravenous
injection of CSPQ@CM solution (0.6 mg/mL, 200 μL) and
collection of PA images of the brain at diﬀerent points (Figure
4c). The PA signals in the right brain were gradually increased
after injection of CSPQ@CM nanoparticles and reached their
maximum at 2 h postinjection, followed by a slight decrease
(Figure S15a). There was no obvious enhancement in the PA
signal in the mice without sonication after CSPQ@CM
nanoparticles were injected (Figure 4d and Figure S15b),
and no blue EB was observed in the brain tissue, which
supports the important role of focused ultrasound in opening
the BBB for accumulation of nanoparticles in the brain. The
successful opening of the BBB by ultrasound (US) is further
conﬁrmed by staining of brain slices with rubeanic acid for the
detection of copper ions (Figure S16). Both staining results
also demonstrate the deep penetration of focused ultrasound
and the accumulation of nanoparticles in the substantia nigra
and striatum of the brain.
To further show the targeting capability of CSPQ@CM
nanoparticles, we did similar PA imaging experiments by
intravenously injecting CSPQ@3T3CM nanoparticles (CSPQ
nanoparticles coated with NIH-3T3 cell membrane) after
sonication. In the case of injection of CSPQ@3T3CM
nanoparticles after sonication, there was slight enhancement
in the PA signal of the right brain due to the diﬀusion of
nanoparticles after the opening of the BBB with focused
ultrasound (Figure S17), which indirectly demonstrates the
excellent targeting capability of CSPQ nanoparticles after
coating with MES23.5 cell membrane rather than NIH-3T3
cell membrane.
Therapy of PD Mice with CSPQ@CM Nanoparticles.
The in vivo imaging results demonstrate that CSPQ@CM
nanoparticles can be eﬃciently delivered to the substantia nigra
and striatum of the brain. To investigate their neuroprotective
eﬀect, we used 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine
(MPTP, 30 mg/kg) to induce PD in mice for therapy (Figure
5a).20 Speciﬁcally, the healthy and PD mice were randomly
divided into four groups: (1) normal healthy mice (normal
group); (2) PD mice intravenously injected with phosphate-
buﬀered saline (PBS) after ultrasound treatment (PD+US
+PBS group); (3) PD mice intravenously injected with
CSPQ@CM nanoparticles without ultrasound treatment (PD
+CSPQ@CM group); (4) PD mice intravenously injected
with CSPQ@CM nanoparticles after ultrasound treatment
(PD+US+CSPQ@CM group).
Since PD is well-known for dyskinesia, autonomic
dysfunction, and cognition impairment,52 the behaviors of
PD mice were recorded through the rotatory-rod test and the
Morris water maze (MWM) test. Rotatory-rod tests were
conducted every other day for 2 weeks (Figure 5b). Compared
to normal healthy mice, the average time of stay on the
rotatory for mice from the PD+US+PBS group was about
125−180 s, which is signiﬁcantly shorter than for normal mice
(200−225 s). Obvious therapy eﬃcacy was observed, however,
for the mice in the PD+US+CSPQ@CM group, as evidenced
by how long they stayed on the rotatory rod, which changed
from the initial 125 s before treatment to 225 s after treatment,
close to the performance of normal mice. Their swimming
speeds and escape latencies are compared in Figure 5c. Faster
swimming and shorter latency of PD mice treated with
CSPQ@CM nanoparticles indicate that our nanoparticles
could immensely ameliorate their spatial memory. Further-
more, the therapy eﬃcacy is also conﬁrmed by the frequency
across the target quadrant (Figure 5d). The PD mice after
treatment with CSPQ@CM nanoparticles crossed the target
quadrant more often in comparison with untreated mice,
which suggests obvious improvement in their PD symptoms.
Their motion along paths in the space probe trial for memory
assessment in Figure 5e was mainly focused on the target
quadrant, indicating that CSPQ@CM nanoparticles could
induce a remarkable recovery from the spatial memory
disorder of PD mice. Since the abnormal variation of dopamine
(DA) concentration is strongly associated with neural
disorders and can cause neurodegenerative diseases, the
concentrations of DA in the cerebrospinal ﬂuid of these mice
were evaluated through an enzyme-linked immuno sorbent
assay to further prove the therapeutic eﬀect of CSPQ@CM
nanoparticles. The signiﬁcant increase of DA concentration in
the mice from the PD+US+CSPQ@CM group (Figure 5f)
compared to that of healthy mice demonstrates the remission
of PD symptoms, in comparison with the mice from the PD
+US+PBS group and PD+CSPQ@CM group.
To further reveal the therapy eﬃcacy, we analyzed the
neuronal injury through Nissl body staining (Figure 5g).
Because tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) is a rate-limiting enzyme
during neurotransmitter synthesis, we compared the variation
of TH in the substantia nigra of brains from the four groups of
mice, among which the mice from the PD+US+CSPQ@CM
group had similar TH to those of normal healthy mice. The
mice from the PD+US+PBS group had the lowest TH in
comparison with other mice. In addition, we also characterized
the upregulation of their ionized calcium binding adapter
protein 1 (IBA-1), which usually serves as a marker of
microglial activation and reﬂects neuroinﬂammation.20 The
results show that the expression of IBA-1 in the substantia
nigra of brains from the mice in the PD+US+CSPQ@CM
group was similar to that in the healthy mice (normal group).

To further demonstrate the accumulation of CSPQ@CM

microglia, we labeled CSPQ@CM nanoparticles with FITC

of the blood−brain barrier and the accumulation of nano-
particles in the brain. These results are consistent with the
results in Figure 3, which demonstrate that our CSPQ
nanoparticles could promote the polarization of microglia
into the neuroprotective M2-like phenotype.
The proportions of M2-like microglia in the brains of the
four groups of mice were quantiﬁed by ﬂow cytometry through
detection of the M2 biomarker CD206 and the M1 biomarker
CD86 (Figure 6b). The PD mice treated with ultrasound
collaborated with the CSPQ@CM nanoparticles exhibiting
more M2-like microglial cells (14.8%) than those of mice from
the PD+US+PBS group (11.1%) and the PD+CSPQ@CM
group (10.8%). Meanwhile, the mice from the PD+US+PBS
group showed more M1-like microglia than those from the PD
+US+CSPQ@CM group (20.9% vs 14.7%). These results
demonstrate the excellence of our CSPQ@CM nanoparticles
in polarizing proinﬂammatory M1-like phenotype microglia
into anti-inﬂammatory M2-like phenotype microglial cells for
PD treatment.
In addition, the Cu concentrations in the major organs (i.e.,
heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, brain) and in the blood were
measured by the ICP-MS method after the end of the MWM
test. The very similar copper concentration (Figure 6c−d) in
the major organs from diﬀerent groups of mice indicates that
the CSPQ@CM nanoparticles can be completely degraded and
metabolized through liver and renal clearance, which
eventually reached that of healthy mice. It should be noted
that the copper concentration in the brain was increased from
2.6 to 3.6 μg/g at 2 h postsonication for the opening of the
BBB and then decreased to 3.0 μg/g at 12 h postsonication.
After the end of the MWM test, the copper concentration in all
mice was very similar to that of healthy mice and around 2.5
μg/g.
To assess the biosafety of our nanoparticles, the major
organs (e.g., heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) of mice from the
four groups were collected for hematoxylin/eosin (HE)
staining (Figure S18). No obvious histopathological lesions
were observed in any of the tissues from all the mice. All these
results demonstrate that our CSPQ@CM nanoparticles not
only could drastically alleviate the pathological damage of ROS
to the brain but also exhibit excellent biocompatibility and
biosafety.

for targeting and modulating microglia to improve the therapy

multienzyme-like activities, followed by wrapping with neuro-
nal cell membrane (MES23.5 cells, referred as CSPQ@CM) to

We reveal that the high expression of VCAM-1 on the
membrane of MES23.5 cells allowed the CSPQ@CM
nanoparticles to target α4β1 integrin of microglia through
the speciﬁc and strong α4β1-VCAM-1 interactions. We also
reveal that these nanoparticles promoted polarization of
microglia toward the M2-like phenotype and played an anti-
inﬂammatory role by downregulation of neurotoxic IL-6 and
upregulation of neuroprotective IL-10. Our CSPQ@CM
nanoparticles could be eﬃciently and targetedly delivered to
the substantia nigra and striatum of the brain with the
assistance of focused ultrasound to eﬀectively modulate the
microglia and microenvironment by eliminating ROS in the
brain, which led to increased dopamine in the cerebrospinal
ﬂuid, high expression of tyrosine hydroxylase, and low
expression of IBA-1. The drastic relief of symptoms of PD
mice and improvement of their dyskinesia and cognition
impairment highlight the great potential of CSPQ@CM
nanoparticles for targeted therapy of PD. Our work provides
new guidance in the search for biomimetic nanoparticles for
manipulating the microenvironment in the brain for targeted
therapy of PD and other neurodegenerative diseases.

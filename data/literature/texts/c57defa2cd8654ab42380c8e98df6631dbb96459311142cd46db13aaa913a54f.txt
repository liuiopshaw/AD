A B S T R A C T
Organic pollutants such as nitroaromatic compounds (NACs) have extensively contaminated aquatic environ­
ments. This research proposes a composite based on paper, beta-cyclodextrin (βCD), and metallic nanoparticles 
(MNPs) for the effective removal of NACs from water. The synthesis involved a green and sustainable approach, 
utilising sonication to fabricate paper-AuNPs/βCD, paper-AgNPs/βCD, and paper-CuNPs/βCD (ternary systems). 
Physicochemical and mechanical characterization confirmed the successful formation of the paper-MNPs/βCD 
composites. The results demonstrated that the ternary systems exhibited superior adsorption capacity for three 
NACs: 4-nitrobenzoic acid, 4-nitroaniline, and 4-nitrothiophenol, compared to the individual components. The 
interaction between NACs and the ternary system was evaluated using kinetic and adsorption isotherm models. 
The interaction process followed pseudo-second-order kinetics and was best described by the Freundlich 
isotherm, confirming the adsorption behavior. All paper-MNPs/βCD composites subjected to remediation ex­
periments in wastewater samples achieved up to 90 % removal for NACs after 20 min, demonstrating their high 
efficiency. Additionally, the recyclability of the ternary systems was evaluated through adsorption/desorption 
cycles. The NAC removal percentages remained above 50 % over three consecutive reuses. Finally, when tested 
in real water samples from the Mapocho River, all composites achieved up to 80 % NAC removal within 20 min, 
demonstrating consistent performance even in complex environmental matrices. These findings highlight the 
efficiency, reusability, and potential application of paper-MNPs/βCD composites for the removal of NACs from 
aqueous environments.
1. Introduction
Water is an essential but increasingly scarce resource. The continued 
supply of clean water is increasingly at risk due to rapid global popu­
lation growth and the proliferation of anthropogenic pollutants 
(Adyasari et al., 2021; Anh et al., 2023). Currently, researchers are 
focusing on improving water remediation technologies to address the 
presence of various organic pollutants (OPs), including dyes, pharma­
ceuticals, hydrocarbon derivatives, and pesticides within aquatic 
environments (Adithya et al., 2021; Olisah et al., 2021; Vasseghian 
et al., 2021). OPs are classified as persistent (POPs) or emergent (EOPs) 
depending on the degradation rate. POPs are resistant to environmental 
degradation and lead to immediate or chronic health problems (Jones, 
2021).
EOPs are compounds with several origins and chemical natures such 
as pharmaceutical products, illicit drugs, steroid hormones, personal 
cleaning materials, and others (L. Chen et al., 2021). Their presence in 
water sources has serious and adverse impacts on human health and the

environment. Among EOPs, nitroaromatic compounds (NACs) are of

NACs originate primarily from industrial processes including the 
manufacturing of explosives, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, and synthetic 
dyes (Bilal et al., 2021a, 2021b; Galmiche et al., 2021; Niyirora & 
Cyganowski, 2025). Their environmental persistence stems from the 
electron-withdrawing nitro groups that resist degradation (Tiwari et al., 
2019; Zhu et al., 2022), while their toxicity manifests through carcino­
genic, mutagenic, and cytotoxic effects even at parts per billion (ppb) 
concentrations, making their remediation a critical environmental pri­
ority (Bilal et al., 2021a, 2021b). The development of a system that can 
remediate NACs at a low cost, and that is simple, fast, and highly 
effective is a global challenge(Bas¸er et al., 2021; Gadore & Ahmar­
uzzaman, 2021). Among the various remediation techniques such as 
chemical precipitation(Keerthana et al., 2023), bioremediation (Kalra 
et al., 2021), and photocatalysis(Kumari et al., 2023), filtration is a 
simple, efficient, and cost-effective strategy for NAC removal in water 
treatment(Li et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021; Sanchis-Perucho et al., 2024). 
Continuous innovation for water remediation is imperative owing to the 
persistent generation of pollutants. The use of sustainable materials like 
biopolymers derived from renewable or recycled sources can serve as an 
environmentally-friendly approach for NAC remediation(Chakravarty & 
Edwards, 2022; Russo et al., 2021).
Oligosaccharides and polysaccharides, particularly those composed 
of glycosidic units like cellulose, exhibit biodegradability, low toxicity, 
and high functionalization capacity, making them popular solutions for 
pollutant remediation(Akter et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2021; Rong et al., 
2021). Cellulose is a linear polysaccharide, with its structural unit being 
cellobiose, composed of two units of anhydrous D-glucopyranose linked 
by β1–4 glycosidic bonds. Its hydroxyl groups confer reactivity, allowing 
the formation of covalent bonds between the adsorbent and the sub­
strate, in addition to facilitating adsorption processes(Mishra et al., 
2024; Rodrigues et al., 2024). Since cellulose is considered a green, non- 
toxic, and economical material, it is popularly used as a support for 
adsorbent materials, either as a powder or paper. The paper form of 
cellulose is obtained from the dehydration of a cellulose fiber suspen­
sion. Similarly, cyclodextrins (CDs) are promising as they can form in­
clusion complexes with guest molecules. Among CDs, βCD is noteworthy 
owing to the dimensions of its cavity (7.8 Å), which allow it to complex 
with a wide range of molecules, including those with benzene groups 
(K¨ose et al., 2022; Syeda et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2021). As such, βCD 
offers unique advantages for NAC capture through its toroidal structure, 
enabling the selective encapsulation of NAC aromatic rings via two 
synergistic interactions: hydrophobic-driven inclusion and hydrogen 
bonding(Ahmadi et al., 2025; Sangwan & Poursattar Marjani, 2023; 
Shahzaib, Shaily, Ahmad, Singh, et al., 2023).
Metallic nanoparticles (MNPs) exhibit remarkable potential for 
pollutant removal, especially when combined with glucopyranose-based 
materials like paper or βCD, enhancing the adsorption capacity syner­
gistically(Almutairi, 2024; Bindhu et al., 2021; Lighvan et al., 2021). 
While there are reports on the use of various MNPs, studies using gold 
(AuNPs)(Nwaji et al., 2021; Xiong et al., 2021), silver (AgNPs) 
(Prosposito et al., 2020; Schiesaro et al., 2020), and copper nano­
particles (CuNPs)(Alothman et al., 2021; Laghari et al., 2019; Mahmoud 
et al., 2021) are notable.
The intrinsic properties of Au, Ag, and Cu-based materials make 
them promising candidates for pollutant removal, especially when 
synthesized using sustainable methods such as sonication(Ali Dheyab 
et al., 2021; X. Zhang et al., 2024). The chemical reactivity of MNPs 
allows for their assembly with OPs, further emphasizing their potential 
in NAC remediation. The inclusion compounds of CDs-MNPs systems can 
stabilize and nucleate with guest functional groups, providing a pathway 
for efficient organic pollutant removal while complying with the sus­
tainability aspect of the research(Salazar et al., 2021; Silva et al., 2016). 
Further, CDs can act as stabilizing and reducing agents for the formation 
of MNPs.
Despite significant advancements in water remediation systems, the 
removal of NACs using adsorbents comprising paper-βCD-MNPs is 
under-reported in the literature. Therefore, this work explores a paper- 
based composite consisting of paper, βCD, and MNPs. Paper was selected 
as a substrate owing to its low price as well as high porosity and surface 
area, βCD enhanced pollutant adsorption through molecular recogni­
tion. MNPs contributed to improving water remediation efficiency 
owing to their compatible properties with NAC functional groups. In this 
study, Cu, Au, and AgNPs were synthesized using βCD as a reducing 
agent to develop MNPs/βCD (binary systems). Further, the paper sub­
strate was modified with CuNPs/βCD, AuNPs/βCD, and AgNPs/βCD 
(ternary systems). The resulting composites were evaluated for their 
efficiencies in the removal of 4-nitroaniline (4-NA), 4-nitrothiophenol 
(4-NTP), and 4-nitrobenzoic acid (4-NBA) pollutants (Fig. 1). The 
MNPs were selected following Pearson's hard-soft acid-base (HSAB) 
principle, where each metal preferentially interacts with complemen­
tary NAC functional groups: AuNPs with 4-NTP (Au-SH), AgNPs with 4- 
NA (Ag-NH2), and CuNPs with 4-NBA (Cu-COOH), creating comple­
mentary adsorption sites that enhance the aromatic encapsulation of 
βCD. The feasibility of NAC removal was assessed as a function of the 
contact time and adsorbate concentration. Additionally, we evaluated 
the interaction between NACs and the paper-MNPs/βCD composites 
through different kinetic and isotherm models. To the best of our 
knowledge, there have been no reports thus far on the removal of NACs 
from wastewater using a paper-MNPs/βCD composite. Thus, this 
research offers a promising technology for water remediation.
2. Experimental section
2.1. Materials/chemicals and reagents
Anhydrous β-CD, C42H70O35, 97 %, 1134.98 g/mol; copper sulfate 
pentahydrate, CuSO4⋅5H2O, 98 %, 249.7 g/mol; tetrachloroauric acid, 
HAuCl4, 99 %, 393.8 g/mol; silver nitrate, AgNO3, 99 %, 169.8 g/mol; 
ascorbic acid, C6H8O6, 99 %, 176.1 g/mol; 4-NA, C6H6N2O2, 99 %, 
138.1 g/mol; 4-NTP, C6H5SNO2, 80 %, 155.17 g/mol; and 4-NBA, 
O2NC6H4CO2H, 98 %, 167.1 g/mol were all sourced from Merck, 
Darmstadt, Germany and used as received. A qualitative filter paper (CL- 
506) with a 16 cm diameter and grammage of 85 g/m2 was used as a 
substrate for the formation of the paper-MNPs/βCD composite. Ethanol, 
distilled water, and NaOH (Winkler, Santiago, Chile) were used as 
received.
2.2. Preparation of MNPs/βCD
Three different MNPs/βCD (labelled as CuNPs/βCD, AuNPs/βCD, 
and AgNPs/βCD) were synthesized using βCD as the stabilizing agent. 
All MNPs were synthesized via chemical reduction under agitation. In 
each synthetic route, a 10 mL aliquot of 7 mM βCD was added to a flask 
and heated to 60 ◦C. The CuNPs were synthesized using CuSO4 as the 
metallic salt, with ascorbic acid (AA) as reducing agent. A 500 μL aliquot 
of 0.1 M NaOH was added to the βCD solution. Then, a 500 μL aliquot of 
a 1 M CuSO₄ solution (prepared in 0.4 M NaOH) was added to the βCD/ 
NaOH mixture, followed by the addition of 1 mL of 1 M AA. A brownish 
colloid was then formed corresponding to CuNPs. The AuNPs were 
synthesized using HAuCl₄ as the precursor. First, a 500 μL aliquot of 0.1 
M NaOH was added to a βCD solution. Subsequently, a 500 μL aliquot of 
0.01 M HAuCl₄ was added to the βCD/NaOH mixture. After 20 min, a 
reddish colloid formed, corresponding to AuNPs.
The synthesis of AgNPs followed the same route, except that AgNO₃ 
was used as the precursor instead of HAuCl₄. After 20 min, a brownish- 
yellow colloid was observed, indicating the formation of AgNPs. All 
samples were stored at 4 ◦C for further analysis.

2.3. Impregnation of MNPs/βCD on the paper substrate
The ternary system was prepared through modification of quantita­
tive paper with a binary system of MNPs/βCD. Briefly, the paper sub­
strate was submerged in a beaker containing 50 mL of the MNPs/βCD 
binary system. The samples were then subjected to homogenization in 
an ultrasonic bath at 25 ◦C for 20 min. (IsoLab Laborgerate GMBH, 40 
Hz). After sonication, the paper-MNPs/βCD composites were dried in an 
oven at 60 ◦C for 30 min.
2.4. Characterization methods
The optical properties of MNPs/βCD impregnated in paper matrices 
were characterized using UV–Visible spectroscopy. Measurements were 
performed on a Shimadzu UV-1280 spectrophotometer following a 
previously reported procedure(Araya-Hermosilla et al., 2023). UV–Vi­
sible spectra were recorded over a wavelength range of 200–700 nm.
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to ascertain 
the morphology and average size of the MNPs samples. The analysis was 
performed using a Hitachi model HT-7700 instrument, operated at 120 
kV. For the TEM analysis, the MNPs were dispersed on ultrapure water 
(18 MΩ⋅cm), sonicated for 10 min, and deposited on Formvar-coated Cu 
grids. Micrograph analysis was conducted using ImageJ Software 
(version 1.53) to acquire the particle distribution data. Size distribution 
histograms were generated based on populations of 100 particles.
X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis was performed to determine the 
concentration of each metal in the composite. Circular samples of 2.5 cm 
in diameter were collected and measured in the Thermo Scientific™ 
Niton™ XL3t analyzer.
The instrument was calibrated prior to analysis using certified 
reference materials (XRF standard, Cu 9.5 %, Au 78.8 %, Ag 14.5 %, XRF 
Company, Texas, USA) matching the target metal composition.
The metal concentrations (Cu, Ag, and Au) in the paper-MNP/βCD 
composites were quantified using a PerkinElmer 5100 atomic absorption 
spectrometer (AAS). Calibration standards were prepared from certified 
1 g/L Trace-CERT reference materials (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) for 
each metal. Prior to analysis, samples were digested in 1 mL of 0.25 M 
HNO₃. Measurements were performed in triplicate.
For the paper-MNPs/βCD samples, diffuse reflectance spectra were 
acquired using a Shimadzu UV-2101 spectrophotometer, with BaSO4 as 
the baseline reference. The measurements were acquired in the same 
wavelength range (200–700 nm). The diffuse reflectance data were 
subsequently converted to absorbance data using the Kubelka-Munk 
function.
The paper-MNPs/βCD samples were also characterized using a 
Gemini 360 ZEISS (Oberkochen, Germany) field emission scanning 
electron microscope (FE-SEM) provided with a FE-gun, an Inlens de­
tector, and operated at an acceleration voltage of 20 kV with variable 
magnification.

2.6. pH-dependent sorption studies
Sorption efficiency of 4-NA, 4-NTP, and 4-NBA by paper-MNP/βCD 
composites was tested under three different pH conditions: pH 3.0, pH 
7.0, and pH 9.0. The pH of the NAC solutions was adjusted using 0.1 M 
HCl or 0.1 M NaOH(Hao et al., 2019). For sorption experiments, 20 mL 
of each NAC solution (50 ppm) was added to the paper-MNP/βCD. The 
mixtures were then shaken at 200 rpm at room temperature, following 
which the residual NAC concentrations were determined through 
UV–Visible spectroscopy at specific wavelengths (4-NA:380 nm, 4- 
NTP:293 nm, 4-NBA:259 nm).

2.9. Reusability of the ternary system
The reusability of the ternary system was evaluated over three 
sorption/desorption cycles, using the same experimental conditions as 
described for the sorption studies. The reusability of paper-MNPs/βCD 
was evaluated by measuring the equilibrium concentrations of adsorbed 
and desorbed NAC in each reuse cycle. The desorption and regeneration 
of the substrate with NAC were achieved by placing the ternary system 
into 30 mL of 0.1 M NaOH shaken at 25 ◦C for 1 h. The regenerated 
substrate was used for another serial filtration of NACs. After filtration, 
each NAC was quantified using UV–Visible spectroscopy.
2.10. Application of the ternary system in real water samples
Real water samples were collected from two locations along the 
Mapocho River in Santiago, Chile (Regi´on Metropolitana): an up-river 
site (coordinates: N: 51◦30′28″; W: 11◦57′67″) and a down-river site 
near Brachwitz (coordinates: N: 51◦30′28″; W: 11◦52′90″) in November 
2024. The samples were collected in pre-washed amber bottles and 
transported to the laboratory in cooled containers to maintain their 
integrity. Upon arrival, the samples were filtered through 0.45 μm cel­

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Synthesis of MNPs/βCD
The synthesis of MNPs/βCD in this study was aimed at enhancing the 
water remediation efficiency by introducing MNPs that provide addi­
tional active sites and enable affinity-driven interactions for the 
adsorption of NACs. Figs. 2(a), (b), and (c) display images of colloidal 
suspensions containing CuNPs, AuNPs, and AgNPs, respectively, each 
exhibiting distinct colors due to their unique localized surface plasmon 
resonance (SPR) bands. The CuNPs/βCD suspension (a) shows a char­
acteristic brownish colour, the AuNPs/βCD suspension (b) appears deep 
red, and the AgNPs/βCD suspension (c) displays a brown-yellowish hue. 
UV–Visible spectroscopy was employed to confirm the formation of 
MNPs/βCD, as indicated by the SPR bands observed in the spectra (Fig. 2
(d)). The position of the SPR bands provides valuable information about 
the size and oxidation state of the MNPs. CuNPs typically exhibit SPR 
bands in the range of 560–580 nm(Yimeng et al., 2022), however, the 
observed peak at 491 nm suggests partial oxidation of the CuNPs, 
potentially forming Cu2O NPs, which are known to display SPR peaks 
around 450–500 nm, depending on their size(Asmat-Campos et al., 
2023).
This result aligns with the well-known tendency of CuNPs to oxidize 
rapidly under ambient conditions, even when stabilized by organic 
surfactants such as βCD. For AuNPs/βCD, a strong SPR band with a 
maximum at 520 nm was observed, which is characteristic of nano­
metric gold particles in the size range of 7–20 nm. The intensity and 
sharpness of the SPR band suggest that the AuNPs are well-dispersed, 
with minimal aggregation or oxidation(Sangwan & Seth, 2022; Z. 
Tang, Wu, et al., 2021). Similarly, AgNPs/βCD exhibited an SPR band at 
406 nm, consistent with previously reported values for nanometric silver 
colloids(Zaheer et al., 2023). This peak position indicates that the AgNPs 
likely have sizes within the 10–20 nm range(Kaur et al., 2021). The 
sharpness of the SPR band further confirms that the AgNPs are mono­
dispersed and exhibit negligible oxidation.
TEM images and particle size distribution histograms (Fig. 3a–c) 
offer detailed insights into the morphology and size distribution of the 
MNPs/βCD binary systems. The TEM image of CuNPs/βCD (Fig. 3a) 
reveals relatively spherical NPs with a high degree of monodispersity in 
both shape and size. The corresponding particle size distribution histo­
gram indicates an average diameter of 12.21 ± 0.33 nm. The narrow 
size range and distribution suggest that βCD effectively stabilizes the 
CuNPs, preventing agglomeration. Notably, the nanoparticle size falls 
within the range typically associated with CuNPs that exhibit SPR bands 
in the visible spectrum. Similarly, AuNPs/βCD (Fig. 3b) display well- 
dispersed, spherical nanoparticles with high monodispersity and mini­
mal variation in size and shape. The size distribution histogram reveals 
an average diameter of 7.86 ± 0.16 nm, reflecting a narrow size distri­
bution that indicates uniform and controlled growth during synthesis 
(Yasuda et al., 2020). This uniformity aligns with the sharp SPR band 
observed at 520 nm in the UV–Vis spectrum. In contrast, the TEM image 
of AgNPs/βCD (Fig. 3c) shows nanoparticles with greater size variation 
compared to AuNPs and CuNPs. The histogram indicates an average 
diameter of 29.43 ± 1.26 nm, suggesting a higher degree of poly­
dispersity. This larger average size and broader size distribution are 
consistent with the broader SPR band observed at 406 nm in the UV–Vis 
spectrum of AgNPs. Overall, βCD plays a critical role in stabilizing the 
MNPs, preventing agglomeration, and providing control over size and 
shape, particularly in the case of AuNPs and CuNPs.
3.2. Characterization of paper-MNPs/βCD composites
The paper-MNPs/βCD was synthesized with the aim to develop a 
green and sustainable composite for the efficient removal of NACs from 
water sources. The process employed sonication to ensure an eco- 
friendly approach. The association of MNPs/βCD with the paper ma­
trix was initially evaluated through colorimetric changes in the paper 
substrate. Fig. 4 shows photographs of unmodified paper (a) and papers 
modified with MNPs (b-d). The unmodified paper (a) appears white,

According to Pearson acid-base theory, AuNPs tend to exhibit a 
higher affinity towards thiol groups(Mironov & Kharlamova, n.d.; 
Okyem et al., 2021). In this theory, Lewis's acids species such as Au, are 
electron acceptors (soft Lewis's acid), and Lewis bases, like thiol groups, 
are electron donors. The interaction between a Lewis acid and a Lewis 
bond is often described as a coordination or chemical bonding interac­
tion. Thiol groups (-SH), which is present in the 4-NTP structure, have a 
strong affinity towards gold surface due to soft-soft acid-base interac­
tion. The soft-soft interaction principle in Pearson theory predicts a 
stronger bonding affinity between Au-SH groups compared to other 
functional groups like amines or carboxylic acids(Çifteci et al., 2022). So 
that, -SH groups most probably form strong chemisorption bonds with 
AuNPs, which is why a higher RE% is observed in paper-AuNPs/βCD 
after contact with 4-NTP (80 %) when compared to AgNPs (51 %) or 
CuNPs (45 %).
Ag is also considered a soft Lewis acid. Therefore, it tends to exhibit a 
higher affinity for soft Lewis bases. Amine groups (-NH) are expected to 
have a strong affinity for AgNPs due to soft-soft acid-based interaction 
(Gonz´alez-Pedroza et al., 2023; Han et al., 2021). However, the differ­
ences in the removal efficiencies of 4-NA using AgNPs (75 %) compared 
to AuNPs (65 %) and CuNPs (50 %) were less pronounced than those 
observed for thiol groups, where the strong affinity of 4-NTP for Au 
clearly predominates over the other MNPs. Copper, on the other hand, is 
often categorized as a “hard” Lewis acid. According to Pearson's theory, 
hard acids preferentially interact with hard bases. Chemical functional 
groups bearing oxygen atoms, such as carboxylic acids (-COOH), may 
exhibit a stronger affinity for CuNPs(Noman et al., 2023; Tokura et al., 
2022). However, this affinity was less prominent than those observed for 
Au-SH. All RE% ranged within 45–57 %, with CuNPs exhibiting the 
highest removal percentage towards 4-NBA.

3.4. pH-dependent sorption behavior
Based on the observed affinity trends (paper-AuNPs/βCD towards 4- 
NTP; paper-AgNPs/βCD towards 4-NA; paper-CuNPs/βCD towards 4- 
NBA) we evaluated the effect of pH in the adsorption of each NAC. 
UV–Vis spectral analysis revealed distinct pH-response profiles for each 
optimal MNP-NAC interaction, as summarized on Fig. 10.
The paper-MNPs/βCD achieved maximal removal at pH 3.0 for most 
NACs (4-NA: 85 %; 4-NBA: 81 %), which are comparable to benchmark 
materials (see Table 3), although some required longer contact times (20 
vs. 120 min.). 4-NTP exhibited a different behavior, with sustained high 
removal (93 % at pH 9.0), attributable to the gold-thiolate (Au–S) 
chemistry. Under alkaline conditions, thiol deprotonation enables direct 
covalent Au–S bond formation, while acidic pH facilitates coordinate- 
type bonding(Fotoohi & Mercier, 2015; Xue et al., 2014). All compos­
ites, including those observed in the literature, exhibited declining 4-NA 
and 4-NBA removal performance with increasing pH, while 4-NTP 
removal performance showed an opposite trend, consistent with pH 
speciation of NAC functional groups. For instance, in the case of 4-NA, at 
pH < pKa (1.0), the amine group remains protonated, which enables 
electrostatic attraction to negatively charged surfaces or adsorbents 
(Gharaghani et al., 2024; Li et al., 2009; Mei et al., 2020) and interaction 
with βCD hydroxyl groups. Acidic environments promote the formation 
of hydrogen bonds between protonated anilines and hydroxyl groups of 
CDs, which further stabilize the βCD/4-NA inclusion complex(Lo Meo 
et al., 2009a, 2009b; Marei et al., 2022). On the other hand, 4-NBA, at 
pH < pKa (3.4), exhibits greater hydrophobicity compared to its 
deprotonated benzoate counterpart (-COO−). This enhanced hydro­
phobicity promotes 4-NBA inclusion within the cavity of βCD(Terekhova 
et al., 2014; Usacheva et al., 2021) and coordination with negatively 
charged surfaces via carboxyl oxygen lone pairs(Chong et al., 2016; 
Wasilewska et al., 2024). Finally, for 4-NTP, alkaline conditions 
generate thiolate species (–S), which enables direct covalent bonding to 
AuNPs(Steen et al., 2022). Furthermore, βCD contributes to 4-NTP 
removal through the inclusion of the nitroaromatic ring(Xu et al., 
2020; X. Zhang et al., 2007).
However, environmentally realistic conditions (pH 5.5–8.5) still 
yielded substantial removal efficiencies for all studied NACs (4-NA: 
75–85 %; 4-NBA: 60–81 %; 4-NTP: 80–93 %) demonstrating practical 
application in natural aquatic environments. This pH resilience, com­
bined with high removal efficiencies achieved at low contact times, 
suggest that the paper-MNPs/βCD composites might outperform con­
ventional adsorbents in real world applications, where pH adjustment is 
impractical and rapid treatment is required.

of the ternary systems, FT-IR and Raman spectroscopies were per­
formed. The FT-IR and Raman spectra of all NACs and the paper-MNPs/ 
βCD systems are summarized on Fig. 11.

The spectroscopic analysis suggests that βCD promotes non-covalent 
complexation primarily driven by van der Waals forces and hydrophobic 
interactions within its apolar cavity. Raman shifts, particularly in the 
NO2 stretching and aromatic ring vibrations, indicate that the aromatic 
moiety of the NACs are effectively encapsulated by βCD, which is 
consistent with the hydrophobic nature of these molecules and previ­
ously reported host-guest behavior in βCD/NAC systems(Di et al., 2025; 
Lo Meo et al., 2009a, 2009b; Rajamanikandan et al., 2023; Shahzaib, 
Shaily, Ahmad, Zafar, et al., 2023; Shukla et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022). 
Moreover, the polar functional groups of the NACs (NH2, COOH, and 
SH) are not involved in the inclusion process and are thus expected to 
remain accessible after complexation(Chen et al., 2012; Lin et al., 2006; 
Ramaraj et al., 2001; Shahat et al., 2025). This spatial orientation en­
ables another interaction, where these functional groups can bind to the 
surface of the MNPs embedded in the composite system. These in­
teractions occur through a combination of electrostatic forces, phys­
isorption, and, in specific cases, covalent bonding, which is consistent 
with HSAB theory(Abdelaziz et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2022; Rodríguez- 
Zamora et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). Among these, Au–S in­
teractions are particularly known for their strength and specificity. In 
the case of 4-NTP, the sulfur atom forms strong covalent bonds with the 
gold surface, resulting in the formation of thiolate linkages(Dileseigres 
et al., 2022; Grimm et al., 2021). This is further favored by the particle 
size of the synthesized AuNPs (within 10–20 nm range), which provides 
a high surface area to volume ratio and increased availability of reactive 
surface atoms(Farcas et al., 2022; Hong & Ha, 2023). Similarly, the 
amine group in 4-NA can interact with AgNPs through electrostatic 
attraction and surface coordination(Rodríguez-Otamendi et al., 2021; 
Xiao et al., 2022), while the carboxyl group of 4-NBA can form surface 
complexes with CuNPs through inner-sphere coordination mechanisms 
(Li et al., 2024; Mao et al., 2021). These proposed interaction mecha­
nisms are supported by specific functional group perturbations, which 
were evident for each compound: 4-NA exhibited shifts in its N–H 
wagging mode (1281 cm−1), 4-NBA showed altered C––O stretching 
(1680 cm−1), and 4-NTP displayed reduced intensity in its C–S 
stretching band (839 cm−1) also suggesting a thiolate bond formation 
with AuNPs(Nyamekye et al., 2021). While the primary mechanism of 
NAC-MNPs interaction is surface adsorption, literature reports that 
metallic surfaces can catalyze the reduction of nitro groups under spe­
cific conditions, such as in the presence of reducing agents (NaBH4) or 
light activation(Deokar & Ingale, 2023; Dong et al., 2021; Ravichandran 
et al., 2023; Tang, Chen, et al., 2021). However, under the experimental 
conditions used in this work, no spectral evidence of nitro to amine 
transformation was observed, suggesting that redox activity does not 
play a significant role in the remediation process. These findings support 
a synergistic mechanism wherein βCD encapsulates the aromatic moiety 
of NACs, positioning the polar functional groups outward for subsequent 
interaction with MNPs surfaces. This combination of molecular recog­
nition and surface adsorption underpins the enhanced removal effi­
ciency observed in the ternary composite systems.

interactions.
The adsorption isotherm data, which are concentration-dependent, 
were analyzed using linear fittings to identify the most suitable model. 
Isotherm models provide valuable insights into the adsorption mecha­
nism, and their interpretation will be discussed in detail in the following 
section, based on the results presented in Fig. 14.
According to the fitting results shown in Fig. 13, the R2 values for the 
Freundlich model (0.937 for 4-NA; 0.993 for 4-NBA; 0.981 for 4-NTP) 
are higher than those for the Langmuir model (0.713 for 4-NA; 0.899 
for 4-NBA; 0.955 for 4-NTP) across all NACs. This suggests that the 
Freundlich model is more suitable for describing the adsorption process.

The preference for the Freundlich model over the Langmuir model is 
consistent with the heterogeneous nature of the ternary system. The 
paper-MNPs/βCD composites create a non-uniform surface with multi­
ple adsorption sites, favoring multilayer adsorption rather than the 
monolayer adsorption typically described by the Langmuir model. This 
heterogeneity is further supported by the presence of diverse functional 
groups and interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic in­
teractions, and π-π stacking) within the ternary system, which enhance 
the adsorption capacity for NACs. This is consistent with previous 
studies reporting bimolecular layer adsorption for βCD and paper-based 
adsorbents (Hemine et al., 2021a, 2021b, 2021c; Liu et al., 2017).
3.6. Serial filtration tests on nitroaromatic compounds
The ternary paper-MNPs/βCD systems were further evaluated 
through a serial filtration test to investigate their selectivity towards a 
mixture of NACs. The mixture contained equal concentrations of 4-NA, 
4-NBA, and 4-NTP (50 ppm each), and the filtration sequence was as 
follows: paper-CuNPs/βCD (1), paper-AgNPs/βCD (2), and finally paper- 
AuNPs/βCD (3). The absorbance changes of the mixture after each 
filtration step were monitored, as shown in Fig. 15.
Upon initial filtration through paper-CuNPs/βCD, the maximum 
absorbance of the NAC mixture decreased significantly, indicating the 
effective removal of one or more components. This initial filtration step 
is probably ascribed to the removal of 4-NBA, as CuNPs are expected to 
interact prominently with the carboxylic acid group according to the 
removal efficiency results (see Fig. 8 for reference). After the second 
filtration step with paper-AgNPs/βCD, a slight decrease in absorbance is 
observed, suggesting that AgNPs continue to adsorb NACs but a signif­
icant portion of the mixture had already been removed by the previous 
filtration. The fact that two absorbance bands are visible in the NAC 
mixture implies that 4-NTP and 4-NBA might overlap, as both contribute 
to the peak at approximately 290 nm(Cerd`a et al., 2022). Filtration 
through paper-AuNPs/βCD results in the complete disappearance of the 
absorbance band at 290 nm, which corresponds to 4-NTP. This suggests 
that AuNPs preferentially remove 4-NTP due to the strong affinity be­
tween SH-Au. The fact that 4-NTP is fully removed while the other NAC 
bands remain present, albeit at lower concentrations, highlights the 
selectivity of the AuNPs system towards thiol-containing compounds. 
The band at 375 nm attributed to 4-NA remains visible after the final 
filtration step suggesting that 4-NA is less effectively removed by AuNPs 
and primary adsorbed by βCD, the cellulose paper matrix, or AgNPs in 
previous filtration steps.

The mechanical properties of all paper-MNPs/βCD materials were 
evaluated by using stress-strain mechanical response before and after

observed at around 4 MPa across all paper samples, including unmodi­

Despite the incorporation of βCD and MNPs, the fundamental structural 
integrity of the cellulose matrix remains dominant, dictating the tran­
sition from elastic to plastic behavior. The applied mechanical stress is 
distributed through the hydrogen bonding interactions between cellu­

point across all samples. While βCD increases water absorption and in­

modifications do not significantly alter the overall mechanical transi­

the cellulose. So that, pristine paper exhibited the highest stress at

superior load-bearing capacity even as their deformation capacity 
diminishes.

tible to structural deterioration, possibly due to higher oxidation 
tendencies, thereby increasing the brittleness of the paper.

4. Conclusions
The synthesis of three MNPs, namely AuNPs, AgNPs, and CuNPs, was 
successfully achieved using βCD as a reducing agent. These MNPs were 
immobilized onto a paper substrate to create a ternary system with 
multiple adsorption, inclusion, and interaction sites for the removal of 
NACs from aqueous matrices. Characterization of the paper-MNPs/βCD 
composite confirmed that the MNPs retained their plasmonic properties 
and nanometric integrity upon immobilization. NAC removal tests 
demonstrated that the ternary systems significantly outperformed both 
unmodified paper and paper-βCD in terms of removal efficiency. The 
removal experiments using three different NACs revealed that each type 
of MNP exhibited a distinct affinity for a specific pollutant: AuNPs 
showed the highest sorption capacity for 4-NTP, AgNPs for 4-NA, and 
CuNPs for 4-NBA. These findings align with the Pearson soft-hard acid- 
base criterion, highlighting the selective interactions between the MNPs 
and NACs, which enables the separation of contaminants based on their 
specific affinities for different nanoparticles. Post-remediation analyses 
revealed minimal metal loss from the paper substrates, as evidenced by a 
slight decrease in colour intensity, quantified using the colorimetric ΔE* 
value. This observation aligns with a minor reduction in metallic con­
tent, as confirmed by XRF measurements. The adsorption process is best 
described by pseudo-second-order kinetics, indicating that chemisorp­
tion is the primary mechanism governing NAC removal from water. The 
adsorption isotherms also demonstrated that the Freundlich model is 
more suitable to describe the NAC removal process, given the hetero­
geneous nature of the paper-MNPs/βCD composites. Additionally, me­
chanical property evaluations indicated that AuNPs and AgNPs 
maintained their structural integrity over multiple cycles, while CuNPs 
exhibited brittleness over time, likely due to oxidation or agglomeration. 
The removal experiments conducted in real water samples demonstrated 
adsorption efficiencies of up to 80 % for all NACs, confirming the high 
affinity of the ternary systems for the target pollutants even in the 
presence of multiple competing compounds. These results underscore 
the potential of AuNPs and AgNPs for long-term applications, while 
remarking the need for further optimization of CuNPs-based systems to 
enhance their stability.

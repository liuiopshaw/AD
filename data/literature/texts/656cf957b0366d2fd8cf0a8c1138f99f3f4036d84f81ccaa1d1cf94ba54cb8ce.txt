G-Quadruplex/Hemin DNAzyme-Functionalized Silver
Nanoclusters with Synergistic Antibacterial and Wound
Healing Capabilities for Infected Wound Management
Jiaojiao Zhu, Tiao Wen, Shuangquan Qu, Qingnian Li, Biwu Liu, and Wenhu Zhou*
Systematic management of infected wounds requires simultaneous
antiinfection and wound healing, which has become the current treatment
dilemma. Recently, a multifunctional silver nanoclusters (AgNCs)-based
hydrogel dressing to meet these demands is developed. Here a diblock DNA
with a cytosine-rich fragment (as AgNCs template) and a guanine-rich
fragment (to form G-quadruplex/hemin DNAzyme, termed G4/hemin) is
designed, for G4/hemin functionalization of AgNCs. Inside bacteria,
G4/hemin can not only accelerate the oxidative release of Ag+ from AgNCs
but also generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) via catalase- and
peroxidase-mimic activities, which enhance the antibacterial eﬀect. On the
other hand, the AgNCs exhibit robust anti-inﬂammatory and antioxidative
activities to switch M1 macrophages into M2 phenotype, which promotes
wound healing. Moreover, the hemin is released to upregulate the heme
oxygenase-1, an intracellular enzyme that can relieve oxidative stress, which
signiﬁcantly alleviates the cytotoxicity of silver. As a result, such silver-based
dressing achieves potent therapeutic eﬃcacy on infected wounds with
excellent biosafety.

Compared to free Ag+, silver-based nanoparticles could release
Ag+ in a sustained proﬁle to exert a long-lasting anti-bacterial
eﬀect.[18,19] Therefore, a series of silver-based nanoparticles have
been applied as wound dressings, such as silver nanoparticles
(AgNPs) and silver nanoclusters (AgNCs).[17,20–22] For clinical ap-
plications, however, the biggest concern of these silver-based
dressings is the hazard of heavy metal to human health, which
only causes potential side eﬀects but also delays wound healing
when accumulating in wounds with high concentration.[23,24]
To simultaneously improve the antibacterial activity and bio-
compatibility, herein we reported a novel type of DNA-templated
AgNCs with Carbopol 941 as the matrix for better manage-
ment of infected wounds.[25] By virtue of the strong metal co-
ordination, DNA has been widely used as the building block
to synthesize metal nanostructures. Compared to other bioma-
terials, DNA possesses several unique merits, such as molecu-
lar recognition capability, programmability, and multifunction-
alities. For example, cytosine- and thymine-rich DNA could
speciﬁcally recognize Ag+ and Hg2+, respectively.[26,27] More-
over, various functional nucleic acids such as DNAzymes and
aptamers have been incorporated into DNA-templated metal
nanoparticles for theranostic applications.[28,29] Capitalized on
these facts, we designed a diblock DNA sequence (Scheme 1),
where the cytosine-rich fragment could eﬀectively bind Ag+ as
the template for AgNCs synthesis, and the guanine-rich fragment
formed a G-quadruplex/hemin (G4/hemin) DNAzyme.[30–32]
We found that G4/hemin displayed multiple catalytic activi-
ties to synergize the antibacterial eﬀect of AgNCs. Speciﬁcally,
G4/hemin with catalase-like activity could promote the oxida-
tive release of Ag+ via in-situ oxygenation, while its peroxidase-
like activity could generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) to
directly damage bacteria.[33–35] In macrophages, on the other
hand, such nanoclusters showed anti-inﬂammatory and anti-
oxidative activities to promote M1-to-M2 repolarization, which
provides a suitable microenvironment to accelerate wound heal-
ing. In addition, the cytotoxicity of silver can be eﬀectively res-
cued by virtue of hemin-induced upregulation of heme oxy-
genase (HO-1) inside cells.[36,37] With these properties, such
AgNCs-based Carbopol dressing has been applied in infected
mouse wound models, showing potent eﬃcacy and excellent
biocompatibility.
2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Preparation and Characterization Ag-G4/Hemin
To prepare Ag-G4/hemin, a diblock DNA template was designed
(Figure 1A), in which the cytosine-rich block (highlighted in blue)
was employed to coordinate with Ag+ for AgNCs formation by
using NaBH4 as reductant (Figure S1, Supporting Information),
while G-quadruplex (G4, the red region) could intercalate hemin
to form G4/hemin DNAzyme. Upon AgNCs part formation, a
light brownish-yellow solution was observed, and a strong red
emission with nanocluster characteristics was seen under UV
irradiation (Figure 1B,D inset). From ﬂuorescent spectra, such
nanocluster displayed the maximum emission at 620 nm with
excitation at 540 nm (Figure 1C). Notably, the ﬂuorescence of
the nanocluster was highly stable under ambient conditions for
at least three months (Figure S2, Supporting Information), in-
dicating that the cytosine-rich DNA can protect the silver core
from oxidation and photobleaching. Then, AgNCs were added
with K+ and hemin to allow the formation of Ag-G4/hemin.
Upon incorporation of hemin, a remarkable change in the UV-
vis spectrum was noticed (Figure 1D), and interestingly, the ﬂu-
orescence was strongly quenched with a darkened solution color
(Figure 1B,D inset). This can be ascribed to the photoinduced
electron transfer (PET) that occurs in the ﬂuorescence quench-
ing process, where the photoexcited electrons are transferred to
the hemin.[30,38] From FT-IR spectra, typical stretching vibration
peaks of free O-H/N-H were observed at 3500∼3000 cm−1 in Ag-
NCs, which was narrow and sharp. However, when the G-rich
sequence formed a tetrahedral structure through hydrogen bond-
ing, the stretching vibration peak of O–H/N–H was aﬀected, re-
sulting in a broad and blunt peak shape in Ag-G4/hemin and
G4/hemin. In addition, a typical peak at ≈1420 cm−1 was ob-
served in Ag-G4/hemin and G4/hemin, which can be attributed

2.2. Antibacterial Activity of Ag-G4/Hemin In Vitro
To measure the antibacterial activity of Ag-G4/hemin, the
representative Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus),
Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and Gram-
negative Escherichia coli (E. coli) were employed as they are the
commonly tested bacteria in wound infection. The agar disk dif-
fusion test was ﬁrst conducted to test the sensitivity of the bacteria
toward various treatments by measuring the diameters of inhi-
bition haloes (Figure 2A,B). Without Ag loading, the G4/hemin
DNAzyme showed marginal antibacterial activity as compared to
the H2O2 control, which could be ascribed to its POD-like catal-
ysis to generate hydroxyl radicals for bacteria-killing (Figure S4,
Supporting Information). Upon Ag incorporation, the antibac-
terial activity was strongly enhanced. Speciﬁcally, Ag-G4/hemin
showed signiﬁcantly better eﬃcacy than AgNCs. For more direct
comparison, Ag-G4/hemin and AgNCs were co-incubated with
the tested bacteria suspension to determine the minimum in-
hibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concen-
tration (MBC) (Figure 2C,D; Figure S5, Supporting Information).
Both Ag-G4/hemin and AgNCs exhibited a dose-dependent an-
tibacterial activity, while Ag-G4/hemin achieved one-fold lower
MIC and MBC for each type of bacteria, suggesting a sen-
sitization eﬀect of G4/hemin towards AgNCs. Moreover, Ag-
G4/hemin could completely eradicate bacteria at high concen-
trations, which is also better than AgNCs. Interestingly, both
types of AgNCs showed relatively lower antibacterial eﬀect for
E. coli than SA. This might be due to the secretion of ﬂagellin on
outer membrane of E. coli to induce coagulation of nanoparticles
and reduce their antibacterial activity.[39,40] The live/dead bacte-
ria staining was further performed to provide more supportive
evidence of the antibacterial activity (Figure 2E,F). The control
bacteria emitted bright green ﬂuorescence, indicating high via-
bility. With G4/hemin treatment, red ﬂuorescence can be seen
in the ﬁeld, conﬁrming its bacteria damage eﬀect. Notably, the
red signal was signiﬁcantly intensiﬁed for the Ag-G4/hemin and
AgNCs groups. We further measured the ﬂuorescence intensity
to quantify the live/dead bacteria, based on which Ag-G4/hemin
displayed the best eﬃcacy against all three types of bacteria. The
consistent results were also obtained from the bacterial colonies
experiment, in which almost no colony was observed after Ag-
G4/hemin treatment (Figure S6, Supporting Information).
2.3. Mechanism of Ag-G4/Hemin for Enhanced Antibacterial
Activity
It is well-known that the main antibacterial mechanism of silver-
based nanoparticles is the oxidation and release of Ag+, which
could interfere with bacterial homeostasis and further produce
a mass of ROS to kill bacteria.[18,19] To this end, the ROS level
inside bacteria was measured by using a DCFH-DA probe. Com-
pared to the control, weak green ﬂuorescence was noticed upon
G4/hemin treatment (Figure 3B,C), due to its POD-like activity
for intracellular ROS generation (Figure S4, Supporting Infor-
mation). For AgNCs, ﬂuorescence was also noticed, which can
be ascribed to the release of Ag+ to produce ROS. Strikingly, Ag-
G4/hemin displayed signiﬁcantly stronger green ﬂuorescence in
all types of bacteria. The ﬂuorescence was then quantiﬁed, and
the level was in the order of Ag-G4/hemin > AgNCs > G4/hemin.
Such order was highly consistent with their antibacterial activity
as described above. Therefore, the incorporation of G4/hemin
DNAzyme could synergize AgNCs via promoting ROS genera-
tion, and the mechanisms can be explained by the following two
aspects (Figure 3A2). First, G4/hemin could directly synergize
with AgNCs by virtue of its POD-like activity to generate ROS as
shown in Figure S4 (Supporting Information). Second, the CAT-
like activity of G4/hemin generates a locally high concentration
of molecular oxygen, which facilitates the oxidation of AgNCs to
release Ag+ for enhanced eﬃcacy.
To conﬁrm such combinatorial eﬀects, a series of di-block
DNAs were designed with diﬀerent lengths of the poly-thymine
(T) linker to tether the G4 block and cytosine-rich block
(Figure 3A1). Through this design, the spatial distance between
G4/hemin and AgNCs was rationally controlled, and thus their
synergistic eﬀect can be probed. All these DNAs were used to syn-
thesize AgNCs and Ag-G4/hemin, and a series of experiments
were performed. First, the oxidative release of Ag+ was stud-
ied in an H2O2-containing PBS buﬀer. For AgNCs with diﬀer-
ent lengths of T linker, only an initial burst release was observed
(likely originated from the weakly bound Ag+ on particle surface),
followed by a leveling-oﬀ(Figure S7, Supporting Information).
The accumulative Ag+ release was only ≈20% over 24 h, indi-
cating high stability of the nanostructure. For Ag-G4/hemin, by

2.4. Intracellular Delivery of Ag-G4/Hemin with Low Cytotoxicity
by Virtue of Hemin-Mediated Upregulation of Heme
Oxygenase-1
Having conﬁrmed the antibacterial activity of Ag-G4/hemin, we
next studied its eﬀect on skin wounds at the cellular level. By
virtue of the intrinsic ﬂuorescence, the uptake of AgNCs was con-
veniently visualized by CLSM, and the cell nuclei were stained
blue by DAPI (Figure 4A). A bright red ﬂuorescence was observed
inside cells after 0.5 h incubation, indicating eﬀective internaliza-
tion of AgNCs. Meanwhile, we also noticed green ﬂuorescence,
attributable to the release of Ag+ to blue-shift the ﬂuorescence
(Figure S11, Supporting Information). The green ﬂuorescence
was further intensiﬁed with attenuation of red ﬂuorescence at
1 h, indicating more Ag+ release. For Ag-G4/hemin, by contrast,
minimal ﬂuorescence was observed inside cells at 0.5 h because
of the ﬂuorescence quenching eﬀect of the G4-hemin fragment.
After 1 h incubation, both red and green ﬂuorescence was ob-
served, suggesting the release of hemin to recover the ﬂuores-
cence, and also the release of Ag+ to shift the ﬂuorescence. To
quantify the cellular uptake, Ag content was further measured
by ICP-MS, and notably, both Ag-G4/hemin and AgNCs showed
comparable intracellular Ag levels at each time point, indicating
eﬀective internalization of both nanostructures (Figure 4B).
We next studied their intracellular performances. The wound-
healing process involves various types of cells, such as ker-
atinocytes, ﬁbroblasts, endothelial cells, and macrophages. To
this end, the cytotoxicity of the nanoclusters was evaluated us-
ing their respective cell lines, e.g., HaCaT, NIH3T3, HUVEC, and
RAW264.7. Both AgNCs and Ag-G4/hemin were biocompatible
without strong cell damage eﬀects (Figure 4C–F). Intriguingly,
we noticed that Ag-G4/hemin was even safer than AgNCs, in
which ≈90% of cells were still alive upon treatment with 36 μg
mL−1 Ag-G4/hemin (its MBC value). Although Ag-G4/hemin
showed a much faster Ag+ release rate, it had better compatibil-
ity than AgNCs. We then speculated that the G4/hemin fragment
in the Ag-G4/hemin structure might provide a protective eﬀect
against the silver-induced cell damage, and this might be due to
the incorporation of hemin (Figure 4G). Hemin is a strong in-
ducer of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), an intracellular enzyme with
antioxidative stress capability, which might rescue Ag+-induced
oxidative cell damage. To conﬁrm this speculation, the expression
of HO-1 was measured (Figure 4H,I). We indeed observed sig-
niﬁcantly stronger upregulation of HO-1 protein after G4/hemin
and Ag-G4/hemin treatments. The HO-1 level was in the order of
Ag-G4/hemin > G4/hemin > AgNCs. Therefore, the G4/hemin
fragment improves the biocompatibility of AgNCs via the HO-1
signaling pathway.
2.5. Anti-Inﬂammatory Activity of Ag-G4/Hemin to Enable
M1-to-M2 Phenotypic Transition of Macrophages
During bacterial infection and cell injury, a large amount of
ROS was produced inside cells to cause intracellular antiox-
idant imbalance and cell dysfunctions, which is one critical
pathological feature of the infected wound. To mimic such a
state, the macrophages were stimulated with LPS, and the in-
tracellular ROS level was monitored by a DCFH-DA probe with
green ﬂuorescence. Upon LPS treatment, bright green ﬂuores-
cence was detected inside cells, indicating a high ROS level
(Figure 4J,K). With AgNCs coincubation, by contrast, the ﬂuores-
cence markedly decreased, indicating ROS scavenging activity.
Interestingly, AgNCs could generate ROS in bacteria, while scav-
enging ROS in M1-polarized macrophages. Actually, this phe-
nomenon has already been noticed for AgNPs. For example, Ag-
NPs could kill cancer cells by producing ROS to damage intra-
cellular macromolecules[41], while acting as ROS scavengers in
M1 macrophages.[42] These discrepancy properties might be orig-
inated from the diﬀerent intracellular environments, in which
the M1 macrophages are activated with high oxidative pressure.
Such environment-dependent functionalities of nanomaterials
have already been reported, such as iron oxide nanoparticles and
nanoceria.[29,43] In terms of Ag-based nanomaterials, the ROS
regulation activity is partially controlled by intracellular ROS
level, and the ROS scavenging activity was mainly ascribed to the
release of Ag+.[42]
For G4/hemin without Ag loading, an even stronger ROS scav-
enging activity was observed than AgNCs. This result was also
interesting. The use of G4/hemin DNAzyme to regulate oxygen
species has been reported previously. For instance, we previously
employed the CAT-like activity of G4/hemin to relieve tumor hy-
poxia for enhanced photodynamic therapy[33,35], and the POD-like
activity of G4/hemin was also been applied to generate hydroxyl
radicals for tumor eradication.[44] However, the ROS scavenging
activity of G4/hemin inside cells has not been mentioned pre-
viously. We reason that this might be due to the containing of
hemin in its structure. Hemin has been reported to play vari-
ous roles in inﬂammatory processes and wound healing, and its
functions are mainly realized via the stimulation of HO-1 expres-
sion, the eﬀector molecules to defend against cellular stress by
maintaining oxidant homeostasis. As a strong inducer of HO-
1, we indeed observed a signiﬁcant upregulation of HO-1 pro-
tein after G4/hemin treatment (Figure 4H,I). The expression of
HO-1 could degrade hemin to produce carbon monoxide (CO),
biliverdin, and iron, all of which are responsible for ROS elimi-
nation. On the one hand, biliverdin could function as a potent an-
tioxidant system. The free form of iron, on the other hand, could
promote the expression of ferritin, which is a type of antioxidant
protein to scavenges ROS. For Ag-G4/hemin that combined Ag-
NCs and G4/hemin, it displayed the strongest ROS scavenging
activity, and the green ﬂuorescence was almost unnoticed. The
ﬂuorescence was further quantiﬁed, and a consistent result was
obtained with ROS scavenging activity in order of Ag-G4/hemin
> G4/hemin > AgNCs, and the best eﬃcacy of Ag-G4/hemin was
partially attributable to its strongest HO-1 upregulation eﬀect.
In the infected wound with bacteria and tissue injury stim-
ulation, the macrophages are abnormally activated into the
M1 phenotype, which leads to excessive inﬂammation and
delayed wound healing. However, during the wound remodeling
period, M2 phenotype macrophages promote wound healing
and remodeling by releasing various cytokines. As such, repro-
gramming the inﬂammatory macrophages from M1 to M2 has
become one of the common strategies to promote wound heal-
ing. Encouraged by high ROS scavenging and HO-1-inducing
capabilities of Ag-G4/hemin, we then explored its activity to
re-educate macrophage. To do this, the typical biomarkers of
macrophages, e.g., CD206 (M2 marker) and CD80 (M1 marker),

2.6. In Vivo Application of Ag-G4/Hemin Hydrogels to Treat a
Bacteria-Infected Skin Wound
Having demonstrated the great eﬃcacy of Ag-G4/hemin to
kill various bacteria with high biocompatibility and anti-
inﬂammatory activity, we then tested its vivo application for
bacteria-infected skin wound healing (Figure 5A). To allow con-
venient administration at wound skin, Ag-G4/hemin hydrogels
were prepared by using carbomer as a matrix considering its
low toxicity and acceptable feeling and smell. The hydrogels
were in a semisolid state with a transparent appearance and ﬁne
texture. Under ultraviolet light with a wavelength of 254 nm,
AgNCs gel showed strong red ﬂuorescence while the ﬂuores-
cence of Ag-G4/hemin was quenched due to hemin incorpora-
tion (Figure 5B). The rheological properties of the hydrogels were
characterized by a rheometer (Figure 5C,D). With ﬁxed scanning
frequency at 1 Hz, the linear viscoelastic region of Ag-G4/hemin
hydrogel was determined to be below ≈3.8%, and elastic mod-
uli (G′) was higher than viscous moduli (G″) when oscillation
strain <29.2%, suggesting an elastic solid-like viscoelastic behav-
ior of the hydrogel. The frequency sweep test showed elastic prop-
erties of hydrogels in the range of 0.1–10 Hz, and composite
viscosity (𝜂*) declined along with the increase in frequency. All
these parameters demonstrated that the hydrogels had a shear-
thinning behavior, which can be applied evenly to the skin. The
functions of Ag-G4/hemin were also studied after loading into
hydrogel. Ag-G4/hemin still maintained its CAT-like and POD-
like catalysis (Figure S12A,B, Supporting Information), while the
overall activity was much lower, likely due to the relatively lower
diﬀusion rate of the substrates within the hydrogel. For the an-
tibacterial eﬀect, Ag-G4/hemin gel could kill ≈80% of the bac-
teria, which was slightly lower than the Ag-G4/hemin solution
(Figure S12C,D, Supporting Information). In addition, the re-
lease of Ag (including Ag+ and Ag nanoclusters) from hydro-
gels was also tested, in which a rapid Ag release was observed
when buﬀering in physiological conditions, reaching 52% at 12 h
(Figure 5E). Therefore, Ag-G4/hemin could rapidly diﬀuse from
hydrogel matrixes to perform its therapeutic functions.
To evaluate the therapeutic eﬃcacy, an MRSA-infectious skin
model was established, and the therapeutic regime was presented
in Figure 5A. The motility of the model mice decreased, and the
infected wound emerged as yellowish pus, indicating the success-
ful establishment of the infected wound. The mice were treated
with blank gel, AgNCs gel, or Ag-G4/hemin gel, respectively, and
the wound without MRSA infection was applied with PBS as a
normal control. The wound was dynamically monitored every
other day, through which relative wound area was recorded to
evaluate the treatment eﬃcacy, and the process of wound healing
was simulated graphically (Figure 5F,G and Figure S13, Support-
ing Information). Because of the self-healing activity, the wound
area gradually decreased over time for the control group. For the
model mice with blank gel control, the healing rate was much
slower, suggesting a negative eﬀect of bacterial infection. How-
ever, this bacteria-infected wound could be healed by both AgNCs
and Ag-G4/hemin gels. At 10 d after treatment, the wound clo-
sure ratio reached 95.2%, 86.2%, and 69.2% for Ag-G4/hemin,
AgNCs, and blank gel, respectively, where the best therapeutic
outcome was achieved for Ag-G4/hemin gel.
We next evaluated the granulation formation and matura-
tion of skin by H&E staining and Masson staining on day 10
(Figure 5H). For the control and blank gel groups, the obvious
scar was formed (black dashed line and black arrow: the bound-
ary between epithelium and dermis; red arrow: scar). In addition,
signiﬁcant inﬂammatory cell inﬁltration was also observed (blue
arrow: inﬂammatory cells). These results suggested local inﬂam-
mation and incomplete wound healing. These symptoms were
well relieved after treatments with both Ag-G4/hemin and Ag-
NCs. Speciﬁcally, Ag-G4/hemin promoted the highest level of re-
epithelialization and dermis regeneration and the lowest degree
of scar formation. The inﬂammatory cells were also the fewest
with more hair follicles and blood vessels for Ag-G4/hemin treat-
ment. Moreover, the highest levels of collagen deposition and
skin maturation were evidenced by Masson staining, indicat-
ing complete wound healing. All these results demonstrated
the potent eﬃcacy of Ag-G4/hemin to manage the infected
wound.
2.7. Mechanisms of Ag-G4/Hemin to Manage the Infected
Wound
Having conﬁrmed the multifunctionalities of Ag-G4/hemin to
manage the infected wound, we next explored the underlying
mechanisms at the animal level. To explore the antibacterial ef-
ﬁcacy, the wound tissue was collected, and the bacteria coloniza-
tion was analyzed by colony formation assay after homogeniza-
tion and dilution (Figure 6A,B and Figure S14, Supporting In-
formation). Compared to the control, the model mice with blank
hydrogel treatment exhibited a high level of bacterial infection on
day 3. Both Ag-G4/hemin and AgNCs could eﬀectively suppress
bacteria, and obviously, Ag-G4/hemin achieved signiﬁcantly bet-
ter eﬃcacy, consistent with the synergistic eﬀect between Ag-
NCs and G4/hemin for bactericidal activity. At day 10, abundant
bacteria were still detected for the model mice with blank gel
treatment, while bacteria were completely eliminated upon Ag-
G4/hemin and AgNCs therapies, demonstrating their capability
to control bacterial infection.

3. Conclusion
In summary, a multifunctional AgNCs-based hydrogel dress-
ing was developed to manage infected wounds. The G4/hemin
DNAzyme-functionalized AgNCs were facilely prepared by using
a di-block DNA as a template and systematically characterized
with intrinsic ﬂuorescence and multiple catalytic activities. Im-
portantly, the G4/hemin fragment endowed the AgNCs with en-
hanced eﬃcacy for bacteria killing and wound healing, as well as
better biocompatibility via reducing silver-induced toxicity. For in
vivo application, the AgNCs were dispersed into carbomer hydro-
gel with suitable rheological properties, which showed a robust
therapeutic eﬀect to heal the bacteria-infected wound, resulting
in signiﬁcant re-epithelialization, collagen deposition, and scar-
less skin regeneration. Given the simple and cost-eﬀective prepa-
ration, potent therapeutic eﬃcacy, and excellent biocompatibility,
such silver-based wound dressing holds great potential as an in-
novative medical apparatus for clinical translation.

Synthesis and Characterization of Silver Nanoclusters:
To synthe-
size silver nanoclusters (AgNCs) and hemin-loaded nanoclusters (Ag-
G4/hemin), 5 × 10−6 m DNA (C-G4) was dispersed in 10 × 10−3 m phos-
phate buﬀer (0.5 × 10−3 m MgCl2, pH 7.4), and then 50 × 10−6 m AgNO3
was added. After heating for 10 min at 88 °C, the mixture was gradually
cooled to room temperature and further incubated for 1 h. Subsequently,
50 × 10−6 m NaBH4 was added to the mixture and incubated for 4 h in
the dark to obtain the AgNCs. Then, 10 × 10−6 m hemin and 50 × 10−3
m KCl were added and incubated for another 2 h to further obtain Ag-
G4/hemin. The synthesized AgNCs and Ag-G4/hemin were centrifuged by
using Nanosep Centrifugal Devices (30 K, molecular weight cutoﬀs) with
5000 rpm for 10 min, respectively.
The silver contents were quantiﬁed by ICP-MS (Agilent 7500C). The
morphology was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
(Titan G2-F20, FEI, USA). The ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectra were
obtained by UV-2600 spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Shimadzu). The size
distribution and zeta potential were determined by the Malvern Zeta Sizer
Nano series (Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments, UK). The Infrared absorp-
tion spectra were obtained by Fourier near-infrared spectrometer (Bruker,
Germany). The ﬂuorescence intensity was measured by the F-7000 Fluo-
rescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan).
Peroxidase-Like (POD) Activity and Catalase-Like (CAT) Activity:
All the
POD activities were determined by UV spectrophotometry in a wavelength
scan mode from 800-400 nm at room temperature. In a typical experiment,
diﬀerent solutions (in terms of 1000 × 10−9 m DNA) were added and re-
acted in a total volume of 1 mL of NaAc buﬀer (10 × 10−3 m, pH 5.5) with
TMB (0.5 × 10−3 m) as a substrate under 1.2 × 10−3 m H2O2 for 10 min
and then scan the absorbance of the reaction solution. For concentration-
dependent activity, all Ag-G4/hemin concentrations were kept the same as
described above, and the reaction solution was then diluted twice before
UV analysis.
The CAT activity was determined by measuring the oxygen generation
using the portable dissolved oxygen meter (JPBJ-609L, INESA Scientiﬁc
Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Brieﬂy, diﬀerent solutions (in term of 1 ×
10−6 m DNA) was added to an equal volume of H2O2 (100 × 10−3 m), and
the oxygen generation was monitored every 10 s for 600 s.
Antibacterial Assay of the AgNCs and Ag-G4/Hemin In Vitro:

E. coli, and MRSA as typical Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and drug-
resistant bacteria. The antibacterial assay included disk diﬀusion assay,
MIC/MBC measurements, and live/dead bacteria staining.
In disk diﬀusion assay, MH agar solution was sterilized, poured into
peri dishes, and allowed to solidify. The bacterial suspensions (1 × 108
CFU mL−1, 200 μL) were swabbed onto the agar surface. Then, 50 μL
G4/hemin, AgNCs, and Ag-G4/hemin (in terms of 36 μg mL−1 Ag) were
dropped on 6-mm ﬁlter paper disks for 30 min, and then H2O2 (10 × 10−3
m) was added. Subsequently, the disks and the disks loaded with ultrapure
water were placed onto the agar plates and incubated in static conditions
at 37°C for 12 h. Following, the inhibition zones were measured at three
random positions and photographed.
For MIC/MBC measurements, Ag-G4/hemin solution was serially di-
luted by ultrapure water and was added to the bacterial suspensions (2 ×
106 CFU mL−1) with a ratio of 1:5. Then, the bacterial suspensions were
incubated at 37 °C, 180 rpm for another 24 h, and measured the bacteria
turbidity at 600 nm. The lowest dose that could reduce 90% of the bac-
terial cell population was deﬁned as minimum inhibitory concentration
(MIC). For minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) testing, the bacte-
rial suspensions were swabbed on LB agar plates and incubated in static
conditions at 37 °C for 24 h, and the lowest dose of no bacteria growth
was deﬁned as MBC.
In live/dead bacteria staining, G4/hemin, AgNCs, and Ag-G4/hemin so-
lutions were added into the bacterial suspensions (5 × 105 CFU mL−1)
at a ratio of 1:5, and then added H2O2 (10 × 10−6 m) respectively. After
incubation at 37°C, 180 rpm for 24 h, the bacterial suspensions were cen-
trifuged at 3000 rpm and washed three times with PBS, then the SYTO 9

Ag+ Release Behaviors of AgNCs and Ag-G4/Hemin:
As described
above, C-Tn-G4 (n = 0, 3, 5, 9, DNA sequence shown in Table S1 in the Sup-
porting Information) were used as DNA templates to synthesize diﬀerent
AgNCs and Ag-G4/hemin. Subsequently, diﬀerent nanoclusters (0.5 mL)

pH7.4 PBS). The supernatant was collected and an equal buﬀer was added
at diﬀerent time points. Then, the Ag contents in the supernatants were
analyzed by ICP-MS, and the cumulative amounts of released Ag were cal-
culated.
Cytotoxicity Evaluation:
In vitro, cytotoxicity was evaluated using the
MTT assay. HaCaTs, NIH3T3, HUVEC, and RAW 264.7 cells were seeded
into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 per well and cultured for 12 h, re-
spectively. Then, gradient concentrations of AgNCs or Ag-G4/hemin were
added for 24 h incubation; subsequently, the cell viability was evaluated by
MTT assay.
ROS-Scavenging Activity:
RAW 264.7 cells were pretreated with LPS
(10 μg mL−1) for 24 h and incubated with G4/hemin, AgNCs, and Ag-
G4/hemin for 24 h. Then, the cells were washed three times with PBS and
co-incubated with DCFH-DA (10 μM) for 30 min and observed under ﬂu-
orescence microscopy.
Cellular Uptake:
RAW 264.7 cells were seeded into confocal dishes at
a density of 5 × 103 per dish and cultured overnight. After replacing the
medium with serum-free RIPM 1640, the cells were treated with AgNCs or
Ag-G4/hemin (equal to the same Ag content) at 37 °C for diﬀerent times
(0.5, 1 h), respectively. Then, the cells were washed and observed by the
confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).
As above mentioned, the cells were treated with AgNCs or Ag-
G4/hemin (equal to the same Ag content) at 37 °C for diﬀerent times (0.5,
1, 2, 4, 8 h), respectively. Then, the cells were collected by scraping and
added to 100 μL ddH2O. Subsequently, the cell suspension was digested
overnight at 60°C with aqua regia and the Ag contents in the supernatants
were analyzed by ICP-MS.
Eﬀect of Hemin up-Regulating HO-1 in Inﬂammatory Macrophage:
RAW
264.7 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 per well
and cultured overnight. Then, the cells were pretreated with LPS (10 μg
mL−1) for 24 h and incubated with G4/hemin, AgNCs, and Ag-G4/hemin
for 24 h. The level of HO-1 was determined by western blot. Subsequently,
to explore the phenotype transition, the mRNA levels as well as protein ex-
pression of the macrophage biomarkers were measured by RT-PCR (CFX-
Connect, BIO-RAD, USA) and western blot.
In Vitro HUVEC Angiogenesis Assay and HaCaT Migration:
To deter-
mine the crosstalk between macrophages and skin cells, such as HUVEC
and HaCaT, a co-culture assay with macrophages was developed.
In in vitro tube formation assay, in precooled 12-well Transwell plates,
500 μL of Matrigel (Growth factor reduced, BD, USA) was added to the
lower chambers and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, HU-
VECs were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 per well, and 1 × 105 cells mL−1
RAW 264.7 cells were seeded to the upper 0.4 μm-sized Transwell chamber
and co-cultured overnight. Subsequently, fresh medium with G4/hemin,
AgNCs, and Ag-G4/hemin was added into the upper chamber, respec-
tively. After coculture for 12 h, HUVECs were washed and co-incubated
with calcein-AM (Beyotime, China), then the tube length was captured by
inverted ﬂuorescence microscopy and analyzed by ImageJ software.
In in vitro scratch assay, Hacat cells were seeded into the 12-well plate
at a density of 2 × 106 per well, and 1 × 105 cells/mL RAW 264.7 cells
were seeded to the upper 0.4 μm-sized Transwell chamber and co-cultured
overnight. Subsequently, a sterile P-200 pipette tip was used to scratch
the HaCaTs and washed three times with PBS to remove unattached cells,
then fresh DMEM containing 2% FBS was added, and G4/hemin, AgNCs,
and Ag-G4/hemin were added into the upper chamber, respectively. The
migration of HaCaTs was observed by inverted ﬂuorescence microscopy
after 24 h. The percentage of the migration area was calculated by the ratio
of the cell-free area to the initial scratch width.
Physicochemical Characterization and Controllable Release Behavior of the
Hydrogels:
To prepare AgNCs and Ag-G4/hemin contained gel, 2% Car-
bopol 941 hydrogel was swelled overnight in advance, and added mod-
erate NaHCO3 to adjust pH to ≈7.4; then, AgNCs and Ag-G4/hemin so-
lution was added at a ratio of 1:3, and stir gently for 10 min to get Ag-
NCs gel or Ag-G4/hemin gel. The rheological properties of hydrogels were
analyzed by Discovery HR-2 (TA, US), including amplitude and frequency
sweep tests. The strain amplitude sweep test was conducted at a constant
frequency of 1 Hz with strain amplitude ranging from 0.01 to 100%. Fre-
quency sweeps were taken at a constant strain of 1.5 Pa from 0.1 to 10 Hz.
To simulate the Ag-G4/hemin release behavior of the hydrogel in the
wound, Ag-G4/hemin gel (1 mL) was packaged into the dialysis membrane
(MWCO = 1000 Da), and immersed in PBS (10 mL, 10 × 10−3 m, pH 7.4).
The supernatant was collected and an equal fresh PBS was added at diﬀer-
ent time points. Then, the Ag contents in the supernatants were analyzed
by ICP-MS, and the cumulative amounts of released Ag were calculated.
Animal Experiments:
Mice were anesthetized until the disappear-
ance of the plantar reﬂex, and square full-thickness excisional cutaneous
wounds (1 cm × 1 cm) were created on the dorsal of mice. Then, the in-
fected wound was established by covering it with 100 μL of MRSA suspen-
sion (1 × 106 CFU mL−1) and randomly dividing it into four groups (n =
6). The four groups were treated with PBS, blank gel, AgNCs gel, and Ag-
G4/hemin gel (in terms of 36 μg mL−1, 100 μL) three times every two days,
respectively. Subsequently, the mice were individually housed and allowed
to heal for 13 days. The wound area was recorded on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and
10. The tissues were collected on days 3 and 10 for evaluating antibacte-
rial activity in vivo, which were spread on the LB agar plates and incubated
in static conditions at 37 °C for 12 h. The mice were sacriﬁced after 10-
day and granulation tissues over the wound bed were excised. One-half of
the wound skins were immersed in neutral formaldehyde for further his-
tology and immunoﬂuorescent analysis, the other was prepared tissues
homogenate to detect cytokines levels.
Statistical Analysis:
All the quantitative data were expressed as mean ±
standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using Graph-
Pad Prism 6.01 software. Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of vari-
ance (ANOVA) were used to assess the signiﬁcance of diﬀerences among
groups, respectively. The signiﬁcance was deﬁned as follows: *P < 0.05,
**P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

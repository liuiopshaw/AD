Abstract
Advanced sustainable biomedical materials are urgently needed for clinical
applications; however, developing biomedical materials with exceptional
mechanical and bactericidal properties as well as removable functionalities
to reduce unintended secondary injury remains a challenge. Here, we report
a biomass‐derived composite consisting of water‐soluble fish gelatin (FG)
and antibacterial ZnO@silk fibroin (ZSF) microspheres for potential appli-
cation as the wound dressing. The ZSF microspheres are embedded in a FG
matrix to realize the stretchable, antibacterial, and removable ZSF/FG
composites. By introducing glycerin as the plasticizer, ZSF/FG composites
deliver a tensile strength of 4.5 MPa and stretchability of 550%. Acting as
both the germicide and hydrophile components, ZSF microspheres endow
the composites with excellent antibacterial capacity and water solubility. To
prevent secondary injury, the ZSF/FG composites can be easily removed
from the wounds by simply exposing them to excess water. Additionally, the
ZSF/FG composites exhibit favorable biocompatibility and sustain high cell
viability of over 100%. The full‐thickness skin wound model on infected
mice demonstrated an efficient rate of wound closure and a reduced in-
flammatory response. The ZSF/FG composite shows promise to hasten the
healing of infected wounds and is expected a promising candidate as wound
dressing for clinical therapy.

INTRODUCTION
Being the largest organ in the body, the skin is vulnerable
to bacterial infections that can cause uncomfortable
symptoms or even serious threats to human health.[1,2] In
real life, scratches, abrasion, and surgical trauma can
readily harm the skin. Due to the prevalence of traumatic
injuries and their potentially devastating effects, wound

management remains the main focus of clinical care.[3] In
clinical wound therapy nowadays, cleaning with alcohol
or iodophor is usually followed by bandaging the wound,
which is quite inconvenient for wounds in specific re-
gions like joints due to its poor adherence and insuffi-
cient coverage.[4] In addition, frequent cleaning and
replacement as well as the usage of tape materials provide
hazards for secondary harm and sensitization. Therefore,
it is urgently needed to develop multifunctional wound
dressings to hasten the wound healing process.[5,6] Better
adhesion and stretchability that conform to different
body parts, excellent antibacterial performance to prevent
or reduce the infection, and the ability to remove dress-
ings on demand to minimize injury during removal are
all expected characteristics of the ideal wound dressings.
Wound healing, as a complexed multi‐stage process,
necessitates the early inhibition of bacterial infection and
the subsequent process of promoted tissue reconstruc-
tion. Given the risk of bacterial resistance caused by
broad‐spectrum antibiotics, various novel antibacterial
strategies have been developed for the early treatment of
wounds, including photothermal,[7,8] reactive oxygen
species (ROS),[9] piezoelectrically active[10,11] and cationic
polymer antibacterial.[12] Silver nanoparticles were pro-
posed to improve the synergistic antibacterial properties
of Gel‐DA/GG@Ag hydrogels,[13] but higher doses or
longer periods of application will increase the risk of
cumulative toxic heavy metals in living organisms.[14]
Zhao et al. demonstrated a kind of perilla‐derived carbon
nanodots (CNDs) that generate ROS in situ onto bacterial
membranes when exposed to 660 nm light and achieve
99.99%
inactivation
efficiency
against
Gram‐positive
bacteria within 5 min, indicating that ROS can be used
as a high‐efficiency antibacterial agent.[9] However, pro-
ducing ROS at CNDs necessitates an additional infrared
excitation process, which limits its application. Despite
extensive researches, these strategies are unreliable in
clinical application due to issues such as complex syn-
thesis, unstable structure, additional activation process,
and potential cytotoxicity. It is still crucial to develop safe
and effective antibacterial agents that do not cause
resistance in clinical application.
In addition to the materials with superior antibacte-
rial activity, appropriate structures and mechanical
properties are important in the preparation of wound
dressings.[15,16]
Various
types
of
wound
dressings,
including films,[17,18] nanofibrous,[19] microneedles[20]
and microparticles,[21,22] have been developed to achieve
effective wound management and accelerate wound
reconstruction. Hydrogels, which have a structure similar
to soft tissue, are commonly employed among them. As
for the synthesis of hydrogels, biomass materials such as
silk
fibroin
(SF),[23,24]
cellulose,[25,26]
gelatin,[27]
hyaluronic acid[7,28] and amylopectin[29,30] are more
likely to be utilized due to their inherent biocompatibility
and degradability.[31–34] Nevertheless, the application of
biomass gels is limited by their poor mechanical prop-
erties, inability to match skin deformation, and difficulty
in maintaining the wound healing microenvironment.
Hence, it remains a challenge to create stretchable, flex-
ible, and stable biomass hydrogels. Furthermore, a
removable on‐demand feature is required for improved
wound dressing since it can reduce patient discomfort
during stripping.[35,36]
Herein, biomass‐derived fish gelatin (FG) based
composites with stretchable, antibacterial, and removable
properties were designed and prepared by embedding the
ZnO@silk (ZSF) microspheres in the FG matrix as wound
dressings. The excellent stretchability can be attributed to
the hydrogen bonding between the FG matrix and glyc-
erin. Glycerin was added to support the desired stretch-
ability by breaking the hydrogen bonds between FG
molecules and inducing a disordered structure. The ZSF
microspheres were synthesized and embedded in the FG
matrix to act as an antibacterial agent to interfere with
bacterial infecting wounds. ZnO NPs and SF endowed
ZSF microspheres with excellent antibacterial ability and
biocompatibility.
Simultaneously,
ZSF
microspheres
enhanced the surface chemical bonding of ZSF/FG
composites, resulting in better water solubility and
opening the door for future applications in multifunc-
tional dressings. The ZSF/FG composites can be easily
removed from wounds by exposing them to excess water
without causing further damage. The ZSF/FG composites
effectively stimulated wound healing in the mouse
infected full‐thickness skin wound model, demonstrating
tremendous promise in skin tissue restorations.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
To realize the stretchable and removable antibacterial
wound dressing, ZSF microspheres were synthesized and
embedded in FG matrix to obtain the ZSF/FG composites
(Figure 1). The antibacterial composite consists of the
antibacterial component, the sustained‐release element,
and the hydrophilic constituent. ZnO NPs are broad‐
spectrum antibacterial components that can eliminate
germs without developing resistance. We obtained ZSF
microspheres from ZnO NPs and silk protein for their
excellent biocompatibility and antibacterial ability. The
ZSF microspheres were synthesized via spontaneous
phase separation of SF and PVA. Fish gelatin extracted
from fish scales was employed as the composite matrix
that can be cast into a self‐standing film at room tem-
perature. Hydrogen bonds related to hydroxyl and amino

Four different ZSF microspheres were synthesized ac-

ZSF01 (ZnO 0.1 g/L), ZSF05 (ZnO 0.5 g/L), and ZSF20
(ZnO 2 g/L). As shown in Figure 2a, the ZSF micro-

The

Consequently,
the

adjusting the content of ZnO NPs, presumably in relation
to potential ZSF microsphere applications. In order to
sustain the antimicrobial activity of ZnO NPs and the
binding of ZSF microspheres to biomolecules, SF is
employed as a crucial controlled release agent. The me-
chanical properties of ZSF microspheres are governed by
the primary units in the secondary structure of silk,
which are the β‐sheet, α‐helix, and random coil confor-
mations. In terms of the correlation between structure
and properties, α‐helical and random coil conformations
were considered to cause silk flexibility, whereas β‐crys-
tallization contributed to the materials' excellent me-
chanical stability. FTIR was utilized to investigate the
influence of ZnO on the secondary structure of ZSF mi-
crospheres. The characteristic vibration bands of ZSF
microspheres at 1580‐1730 cm−1 for the amide I region
are evidenced in Figure 2b. Notably, the secondary
structure composition of ZSF microspheres was altered
by ZnO NPs. According to Figure 2c, ZSF microspheres
have more β‐sheets and random coil/α‐helical contents
when ZnO NPs concentration rises. For instance, it is
estimated that ZSF05 has more β‐sheets and random coil/
α‐helical contents than SF microspheres (53.1% and
29.6%, respectively). It should be noted that excessive
ZnO NPs may destroy the original structure of silk pro-
tein and reduce the random coil/α‐helical similar to that
exhibited in ZSF20.

The crystal composition of ZSF composite micro-
spheres was further investigated by using X‐ray diffrac-
tion (XRD). The diffraction peaks were all assigned to
either SF or ZnO (Figure 2d), indicating the formation of
ZnO/SF hybrids in ZSF microspheres. The existence of
diffraction peaks of ZnO in ZSF microspheres implies
that no obvious chemical reaction has taken place in this
case. Compositional analysis of ZSF microspheres by X‐
ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) also verified the
presence of ZnO (Figure S4). According to the XPS
spectra, adding more ZnO NPs to the ZSF microspheres
greatly enhanced the peak intensity of the Zn2P at 1022
and 1044 eV (Figure 2e), which is consistent with the
FTIR and XRD results. Besides, the peak of Zn2P at
around 1022 eV shifted abruptly (~0.5 eV) towards low
energy probably due to the formation of hydrogen
bonding between hydroxyl groups of silk and amino
groups of hydrophilic ZnO NPs. The extensive hydrogen
bonds formed between silk and ZnO are suggested to
restrict the structural transition from silk I to amorphous.
Meanwhile, the electron migration between Zn2þ and SF
may induce the formation of β‐sheets. In general, the
degradation rate decreased with the increased β‐sheets
content. Thus, it is possible to efficiently control the
degradation rate of ZSF microspheres.

As one of the major obstacles to wound healing,
pathogenic bacteria infection can readily threaten the
health of patients. The design of dressings with excep-
tional antibacterial activity remains a challenge. The
antibacterial properties of the composites against Gram‐
positive S. aureus were evaluated by surface antibacte-
rial activity tests. The case of S. aureus after cultivation on
an agar plate at 37°C for 24 h is shown in Figure 4a. It is
evident that ZnO NPs perform a significant antibacterial
impact because the number of surviving S. aureus de-
creases
sharply
with
the
ZnO
NPs
concentration.
Compared with the control group, the ZSF20/FG‐treated
group showed almost no S. aureus colonies. The corre-
sponding
number
of
colony
forming
units
(CFUs)
exhibited a regressive tendency along with the addition of
ZnO NPs (Figure 4b). The initial concentration of S.
aureus on the plants is 108 CFU/ml, which decreases to
106 CFU/ml after being treated by ZSF20/FG composites.
The calculated bacterial mortality of ZSF20/FG compos-
ites against S. aureus was 98.81%, reflecting their excel-
lent antibacterial efficacy (Figure 4b). However, the direct
incorporation of ZnO NPs into FG achieved an undesir-
able lethality (less than 85.2%), as shown in Figure S6,
which may be attributed to the antioxidant activity of FG.
Noticeably, the SF/FG composites also exhibit moderate
antibacterial activity, with a bacterial inactivation rate of
9.63%, which can be attributed to the antimicrobial ac-
tivity of the abundant seroin in silk.[38] The above results
reveal that the synergetic antibacterial activity of ZnO
NPs and SF microspheres is what endows the ZSF/FG
composites with remarkable antibacterial efficacy.

Owing to their antibacterial and non‐cytotoxic prop-
erties, the ZSF/FG composites exhibit great potential in
wound healing. The antibacterial effect and wound
healing performance of ZSF/FG composites in vivo were
evaluated using a mouse full‐thickness S. aureus infection
wound model as shown in Figure 5a. Skin wounds were
treated with SF/FG composites and ZSF/FG composites
while taking the untreated wounds as the control group.
Wound healing was assessed on days 0, 4, 8 and 12. As
depicted in Figure 5b, wound closure was significantly
faster in the treatment group than in the control group
after infection during the recovery process. The quanti-
tative assessment and trace tracking of wound closure in
different treatments are shown in Figure 5c,d. After
healing for 4 days, the wounds treated by ZSF05/FG
composites showed more contraction than that of control
groups (p < 0.05), and the wound area ratio of the ZSF05/
FG group is reduced to 29.19%, indicating a fast wound
healing rate. On day 8, all the wounds treated by the ZSF/
FG composites were superior to the control group
(p < 0.05); additionally, the ZSF05/FG group's wound
healing was significantly better than that of the control
group (p < 0.001). After being treated for 12 days, the
wound of the ZSF05/FG group healed almost completely,
leaving only faint scars, while the wounds of other groups
were clearly visible. Compared to physical hydrogels of
pure chitosan, which are also considered promising
candidates for wound dressing applications, the rate of
wound closure using ZSF/FG composites was even faster.
The inherent antibacterial activity of ZnO NPs and SF
synergistically enhances the antibacterial capacity of the
composites and accelerates the wound‐healing process. It
is worth noting that the healing rate of ZSF05/FG com-
posites is even higher than that of ZSF20/FG composites.
This optimized condition at a relatively low ZnO con-
centration may be attributed to the oxidative stress and
retard skin tissue repair due to the overproduced ROS
produced by excessive ZnO NPs.[19,41]
Infected wound healing involves a number of pro-
cesses, including inflammation, angiogenesis, fibroblast
proliferation, and collagen deposition. To evaluate the
quality of the regeneration epidermal from histological
analysis, H&E, Masson trichrome and CD 31 immuno-
histochemical
staining
were
performed
on
tissues
retrieved 15 days post‐surgery. As shown in Figure 5e, all
the wound tissues were covered with a new layer of
epidermis and the thickness of the epidermis in the
ZSF05/FG group was obviously lower than that in other
groups, suggesting that the ZSF05/FG group is at a more
mature stage of wound repair. Compared to the ZSF05/
FG groups, the control group still rendered a slightly
inflammatory response and new capillaries in H&E
strained
wound
sections.
In
addition,
more
skin
appendages such as blood vessels can be observed, which
are primary indicators to evaluate the healing process.
The Masson strain results indicated that collagen fibers
were well arranged and denser in the ZSF05/FG group,
whereas those in the control group were chaotic and
sparser. Meanwhile, the wound depth of the ZSF05/FG
group was smaller than that of the control group
(Figure S8). Actually, the wound depth reduced along
with the antibacterial capacity. Note that for the SF/FG
group, some hair follicles and blood vessels could be
observed in H&E strained wound sections, while denser
granulation tissue deposition and better collagen bundles
than the control group were shown in the Masson strain
result, indicating that the SF/FG composites possessed
certain skin repair ability. Further quantification of ves-
sels by CD31 immunohistochemical staining confirmed
that the mean diameter of newly formed vessels was
significantly larger in the ZSF05/FG group than in the
control group. The above results verify that the ZSF/FG
composites with good adhesive and antibacterial capacity
are suitable as wound dressings to accelerate wound
closure. Only the ZSF/FG composites' mechanical per-
formance and their capacity to promote wound closure in
mice have been studied so far. It is still significant to
explore more applications of ZSF/FG composites, such as
bioabsorbable stents and bio‐electronic systems.[42–46]
3
|
CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we have successfully prepared a sustainable
biomass composite with exceptional mechanical proper-
ties, biocompatibility, antibacterial activity, and on‐
demand removal performance for infected wound heal-
ing. We synthesized ZSF microspheres by combining
ZnO NPs with silk fibroin, and then embedded the ZSF
microspheres in FG matrix to form ZSF/FG composites.
ZSF microspheres deliver remarkably enhanced antibac-
terial activity against S. aureus as well as improved
biocompatibility. The full‐thickness skin wound model
on infected mice demonstrated an efficient rate of wound
closure and a reduced inflammatory response. Besides,
ZSF/FG composites exhibit excellent solubility, making
them simple to be removed by rinsing with water, which
helps to prevent unintended operation injury.

4.2

Preparation of SF solution
Bombyx mori cocoons (7 g) were cut into small pieces and
boiled in 0.01 M Na2CO3 aqueous solution for 30 min for
degumming. The degummed silk fibers were washed
with deionized water and dried in an oven at 60°C. The
SF solution was obtained by dissolving the degummed
silk fibers in 9.3 mol/L LiBr solution at 60°C for 2h and
followed by dialyzing with distilled water for 3 days. The
SF solution was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15 min and
diluted to about 5 wt.% for the following process.
4.3
|
Preparation of ZSF/FG composites
Hydrophilic ZnO nanoparticles were synthesized as in
our previous work.[47] Briefly, Zn (Ac)2•H2O (25 mM)
was dissolved in ethanol (150 mL) under stirring for 2 h
at 80°C and followed by dropping 2 mL KOH ethanol
solution (17.5 mM/mL) and continuous stirring for
30 min then 500 μL of 3‐aminopropyltriethoxysilane
(APTES) was added to the above solution under contin-
uous stirring for 1 h. the obtained precipitates were
washed using ethanol several times and dried in an oven
at 60°C for 6 h to obtain ZnO NPs.
The FG solution was obtained by placing fish scales
(200 g) in deionized water (300 mL) at 85°C for 8h and
followed by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 20 min. Then,
the FG solution was freeze‐dried for 48h to gain FG foam.
FG foam (1g) was dissolved in deionized water (9 mL) to
obtain a 10% FG solution.
Different amounts of ZnO NP powders (0, 0.001,
0.005, and 0.02 g) were added to 10 mL of SF solution
under ultrasonic stirring. Then, 2 mL of ZnO/SF solution
was dropped into 8 mL of 5 wt.% PVA aqueous solution
with magnetic stirring at 200 rpm for 1h. The mixed so-
lution was poured into a plastic Petri dish and dried for
24 h. After that, the obtained films were dissolved in
deionized water and washed several times to completely
remove all PVA. Finally, the freeze‐drying process was
adopted at −55°C for 24 h to obtain ZSF microspheres.
ZSF microspheres were dispersed into simulated body
fluids incubated on a shaker at 37°C for different times
used to test the ion release profile. Then, the ZSF mi-
crospheres (0.04 g) were mixed into 10 mL of FG solution
(10 wt.%) with glycerol (5 wt.%) and then poured into the
acrylic mold to dry at room temperature.

In vivo wound healing
A mouse full‐thickness S. aureus infected wound model

composites on wound healing. Kunming mice (6 weeks
old) were purchased from the Experimental Animal
Center of Zhengzhou University (Henan, China). All

the guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory An-
imals at Zhengzhou University (No. U2004168). The
hind hairs of the mice were shaved after anaesthetiza-
tion with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pento-
barbital (40 mg/kg body weight). A full‐thickness skin
wound (Approx. 10  10 mm) (n = 5) was created by
excising the back skin of mice and the suspension of S.
aureus (10 μL, 108 CFU/mL) was dropped onto the
wound. After 24 h, the wound was treated by the ZSF/
FG composites, while the control group was without
any treatment. The mice were housed in individual
cages and the wound‐healing process was recorded
daily by smartphone. After 15 days of treatment, all
mice
were
euthanized.
Subsequently,
the
tissue
collected from the infected wound was fixed in 4%
paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into
sections. The samples were stained with hematoxylin‐
eosin (H&E), Masson and CD 31 and observed via a
brightfield microscope.

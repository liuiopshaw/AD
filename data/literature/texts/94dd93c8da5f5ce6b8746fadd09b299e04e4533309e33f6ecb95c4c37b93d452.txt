A B S T R A C T
The rational use of carbohydrate polymers as functional matrices for integrating inorganic and organic com­
ponents remains a key challenge in developing sustainable multifunctional materials. Here, a process-oriented, 
bio-inspired strategy for fabricating a chitosan-centred multifunctional composite coating is presented. This 
approach uniquely combines plasma-assisted activation of the silk surface, chitosan immobilisation, and sub­
sequent controlled in situ generation of TiO2 nanoparticles in the presence of curcumin, a naturally derived 
polyphenolic compound. The resulting chitosan/TiO2/curcumin composite system simultaneously imparts 
antibacterial, UV-shielding, and photocatalytic self-cleaning functions to the silk. Chitosan provides strong 
antimicrobial activity, maintaining robust bio-barrier antibacterial protection in the composite system and 
achieving over 99.5% inhibition of Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli growth. Curcumin acts as a TiO2 
photosensitiser and charge-transfer mediator, suppressing electron-hole recombination and enabling efficient 
visible-light-driven photocatalytic activity, as confirmed by accelerated Rhodamine B dye degradation and 
effective coffee stain removal. Complementary UV absorption by TiO2 (UV-B) and curcumin (UV-A) delivers 
broad-spectrum UV protection with a UV protection factor of 32.1. Overall, this work demonstrates a distinct 
carbohydrate polymer-driven fabrication paradigm for engineering high-performance textiles with integrated 
multifunctional protective properties.
1. Introduction
Conventional textile functionalisation relies heavily on synthetic 
chemicals and non-biodegradable additives, resulting in environmental 
persistence, toxicity concerns, and limited compatibility between indi­
vidual functional components. Additionally, multistep, function-specific 
finishing methods often compromise durability and hinder efficient 
integration of functionalities. As a result, bio-inspired strategies that 
enable multifunctionality through environmentally benign coating 
processes have attracted increasing attention (Raj et al., 2022), with 
naturally derived biopolymers emerging as promising platforms for 
sustainable textile functionalisation (Daget et al., 2025).
Among biopolymers, chitosan—a linear polysaccharide obtained by 
partial deacetylation of chitin—has been extensively investigated as a 
versatile functional material. Its degree of deacetylation (typically 
70–95%) and molecular weight (4–300 kDa) govern key physicochem­
ical properties, including solubility, viscosity, and film-forming ability, 
thereby enabling tailored functionality for specific applications (Kou 
et al., 2022). Chitosan's high density of reactive amino (pKa ~6.5) and 
hydroxyl groups facilitates chemical modification and nanoparticle 
stabilisation, while its polycationic nature under acidic conditions 
confers strong adsorption capacity and intrinsic antimicrobial activity 
through electrostatic interactions with negatively charged bacterial 
membranes (Sahariah et al., 2023). These characteristics, along with its 
biocompatibility, biodegradability, and cost-effectiveness, make chito­
san an attractive platform for developing multifunctional composite 
systems in biomedical (Iqbal et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2023; Yu et al., 
2025), food packaging (Wang et al., 2018), environmental remediation 
(Pal et al., 2021) and textile applications (Singh et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 
2019).

In technical textiles, chitosan-based coatings have been widely used 
for medical, healthcare, and protective applications (Li et al., 2021; Liu 
et al., 2025; Massella et al., 2019; Vicente et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025), 
employing chitosan-based micro- and nanostructured systems with or 
without incorporated bioactive compounds, metals, or metal oxides (Li 
et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025; Massella et al., 2019; Shaheen et al., 2022; 
Singh & Sheikh, 2022; Suryaprabha et al., 2023; Valle et al., 2021; 
Vicente et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2022).
Among these, chitosan/TiO2 nanocomposites have attracted partic­
ular attention due to the compatibility of both components and the 
multifunctionality provided by TiO2, including photocatalytic activity, 
UV absorption, and thermal stability (Rashid et al., 2021). These systems 
have been applied primarily to cotton (Becenen & Erdogan, 2022; Bukit 
et al., 2022; Refaee et al., 2022; Rilda et al., 2021; Tomˇsiˇc et al., 2023; 
Xu et al., 2022; Xue et al., 2024; Zydan et al., 2024) and, to a lesser 
extent, to synthetic fibres (Bouaziz et al., 2020; Kundu et al., 2021). In 
these systems, previously synthesised composites were applied using 
conventional pad-dry-cure or ultrasonic-assisted pad-dry-cure pro­
cesses, dip and dip-spin coatings, or layer-by-layer deposition, resulting 
in antimicrobial activity, photocatalytic self-cleaning, UV protection, 
hydrophobicity, and flame retardancy. However, a persistent challenge 
in textile functionalisation is maintaining coating integrity and func­
tional performance after repeated laundering cycles. Furthermore, the 
practical photocatalytic performance of TiO2 is limited by charge 
recombination and restricted UV light absorption.
To enhance the photocatalytic activity of TiO2 under visible light, 
dye sensitisation is an effective strategy (Etacheri et al., 2015). In this 
context, curcumin—a naturally occurring polyphenolic pigment and the 
principal bioactive component of turmeric (Curcuma longa L.) extract 
(Kocaadam & S¸anlier, 2017)—has emerged as a sustainable alternative 
to synthetic dyes (Abou-Gamra & Ahmed, 2016; Buddee et al., 2014; 
Tomˇsiˇc et al., 2025). In addition to extending light absorption into the 
visible region, curcumin has also been reported to act as a stabilising 
agent during nanoparticle synthesis, influencing particle size, disper­
sion, and interfacial interactions (Tomˇsiˇc et al., 2025).
Consequently, ternary chitosan/TiO2/curcumin hybrid nano­
composites have been successfully investigated in biomedical applica­
tions, including wound dressing with antibacterial and drug-release 
functionality (Marulasiddeshwara et al., 2020; Motasadizadeh et al., 
2022; Venkatasubbu et al., 2018), nanocomposite powders exhibiting 
anticancer activity (Deshpande et al., 2023), antibacterial and antioxi­
dant therapeutic systems (Karthikeyan et al., 2023), and biocompatible 
surgical suture monofilaments (Demirci, 2025). However, despite the 
demonstrated multifunctionality of these ternary systems, there are 
currently no reports on the application of chitosan/TiO2/curcumin 
nanocomposites for the chemical modification of textile substrates 
aimed at developing multifunctional protective fabrics. This represents a 
clear and unexplored research gap.
Based on recent advances in sustainable textile coatings, this study 
utilises chitosan as a functional polysaccharide scaffold to mediate 
nanoparticle nucleation, bioactive stabilisation, and multifunctional 
performance at the textile interface. We hypothesise that chito­
san—owing to its renewable origin, intrinsic antimicrobial activity, and 
excellent interfacial compatibility—can serve as a bio-inspired platform 
for integrating TiO2 and curcumin into a multifunctional hybrid coating. 
In this system, chitosan is expected to provide antimicrobial efficacy, 
while TiO2 nanoparticles contribute UV shielding and photocatalytic 
activity. Curcumin is anticipated to extend light absorption into the 
visible region, enhance photocatalytic efficiency through dye sensitisa­
tion, and impart natural yellow colouration.
To test this hypothesis, a facile and sustainable strategy was devel­
oped to fabricate chitosan/TiO2/curcumin composite coatings on silk, 
selected as a model substrate due to its biocompatibility, biodegrad­
ability, and relevance in medical textiles (Babu, 2013; Holland et al., 
2019). Silk was plasma-activated to introduce reactive hydroxyl and 
carboxyl groups into the fibroin backbone, enabling covalent chitosan 
immobilisation using 1,2,3,4-butanetetracarboxylic acid (BTCA) as a 
non-toxic alternative to glutaraldehyde crosslinking agent. TiO2 nano­
particles were subsequently synthesised in situ in the presence of 
turmeric extract on the chitosan-functionalised silk, providing simulta­
neous colouration, UV protection, and photocatalytic self-cleaning 
functionality. The in situ approach promoted uniform nanoparticle dis­
tribution and strong interfacial interactions compared with conven­
tional pad-dry-cure deposition of pre-formed nanoparticles. To elucidate 
individual and synergistic effects, single- and binary-component coat­
ings were prepared under identical conditions and systematically 
compared with the ternary composite using complementary structural, 
spectroscopic, and functional characterisation.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Materials and chemicals
Optically bleached 100% silk fabric with an Atlas weave pattern and 
an areal density of 70 g m−2 was supplied by Luma d.o.o. (Slovenia). 
Titanium(IV) isopropoxide (TTIP, ≥97%, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany, 
CAS–No. 546–68-9) was employed as the precursor for TiO2 synthesis. 
The biopolymer used was chitosan (Chitoclear® 43,010, Primex, Ice­
land) with a purity of >95% (dry weight basis; food and pharmaceutical 
grade), inorganic residues ≤1%, a degree of deacetylation (DD) of 
84.53% (Eq. S1, Fig. S1, Supplementary Material), a weight-average 
molecular weight (Mw) of 324 kDa, a number-average molecular 
weight (Mn) of 101 kDa and a dispersity (Đ) of 3.21 (Fig. S2, Table S1, 
Supplementary Material). Turmeric powder (Maestro®, finely ground, 
Podravka, Croatia) was purchased from a local supplier and used as the 
source of curcumin. Additional reagents included 1,2,3,4-butanetetra­
carboxylic acid (BTCA, 99%, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany, CAS No. 1703- 
58-8), sodium hypophosphite monohydrate (≥99%, Sigma-Aldrich, 
Germany, CAS No. 100039–56-2), acetic acid glacial (99%, Carlo Erba 
Reagents S.A.S., Spain, CAS No. 64–19-7), and 2-propanol (≥99.8%, 
Honeywell Riedel–de Ha¨en™, Germany, CAS No. 67–63-0). Distilled 
water was used in all experimental procedures.
2.2. Turmeric extract preparation
An aqueous turmeric extract was prepared by dispersing 21.15 g of 
finely ground turmeric powder in 4230 mL of distilled water, yielding a 
concentration of 5 g/L. The suspension was heated to boiling and 
maintained for 5 min, followed by cooling to ambient temperature. The 
cooled suspension was subsequently filtered through Whatman® qual­
itative cellulose filter paper, Grade 1 (Sigma Aldrich, Germany, Product 
No. WHA1001090) with a pore size of 11 μm (particle retention) to 
remove insoluble residues. The obtained filtrate was used immediately 
for further processing.
2.3. Functionalisation of silk fabric
2.3.1. Plasma treatment
Silk fabric specimens (15 × 10 cm) were subjected to oxygen (O2) 
plasma treatment to activate the surface prior to chemical functionali­
sation. The treatment was conducted in an inductively coupled low- 
pressure plasma chamber (pressure: 25 ± 2 Pa; applied voltage: 1800 
V) for 5 s per sample.
2.3.2. Chitosan application
A chitosan-based finishing bath was prepared by dissolving chitosan 
(1 wt%) in distilled water containing 1 wt% glacial acetic acid to achieve 
pH 4.5, followed by the addition of 2 wt% 1,2,3,4-butanetetracarboxylic 
acid (BTCA) as a crosslinking agent and 2 wt% sodium hypophosphite 
monohydrate (NaH2PO2⋅H2O) as a catalyst. Plasma-treated fabrics were 
impregnated with the solution using a pad–dry–cure system (Mathis, 
Switzerland), including immersion in the finishing bath and passage

2.3.3. In situ synthesis of TiO2
A TiO2 precursor bath was prepared by dissolving titanium(IV) iso­
propoxide (TTIP, 16 g) in isopropanol (364 g) with acetic acid (20 g), 
yielding a 4 wt% TTIP concentration. The solution was magnetically 
stirred for 20 min to obtain homogenic sol. Silk specimens were 
impregnated with the sol using the pad–dry–cure system (Mathis, 
Switzerland), dried at 100 ◦C for 90 s, and subsequently immersed in 
either 400 mL turmeric extract or distilled water. The samples then 
underwent hydrothermal treatment in an IR dyeing machine Sarlet-2 DL 
600 Plus (Komachine Inc., Korea) at 120 ◦C for 3 h.

2.4. Structural characterisation
2.4.1. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray 
spectroscopy (EDS)
SEM analysis was conducted with the Scanning electron microscope 
JSM-6060 LV (Jeol, Japan) operated with a primary electron beam 
accelerated to 10 kV. The surface of the samples was previously coated 
with gold to provide conductivity and improve the quality of the images. 
The images were taken at a magnification of 3000×. EDS analysis was 
performed using a field emission scanning electron microscope FEG- 
SEM Thermo Scientific Quattro S (ThermoFischer Scientific, USA). 
Sample analysis was performed using an Oxford Instruments Ultim Max 
65 energy-dispersive detector (EDS) and AZtec software Ver 6.0 (Oxford 
Instruments, USA). Samples were coated with a thin carbon layer before 
analysis to provide conductivity and thus improve the image quality.
2.4.2. Inductively coupled plasma-mass spectroscopy (ICP-MS)
The concentration of TiO2 on the functionalised SE samples before 
and after repeated washes was determined by ICP-MS using a Perki­
nElmer SCIED Elan DRC spectrophotometer. A sample of 0.5 g was 
prepared in a Milestone microwave system by acid decomposition with 
65% HNO3 and 30% H2O2. The concentration of Ti was given as the 
mean ± standard deviation of the two independent measurements per 
sample.
2.4.3. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)
FTIR analysis was performed using an FTIR spectrometer Spectrum 3 
(Perkin Elmer, UK). ATR-IR spectra of the chitosan powder in the KBr 
pellet and the untreated and functionalised SE samples were recorded 
between 4000 cm−1 and 500 cm−1 with a 4 cm−1 resolution and an 
average of 120 spectra per sample.
2.4.4. X-ray diffraction (XRD)
XRD analysis of the powder and SE samples was conducted using a 
PANalyticalX'Pert PRO X-ray diffractometer (XRD) (CuK α 1 = 1.5406 Å) 
with a fully open X'Celerator detector (2.1225◦2θ). The XRD pattern 
was obtained by measuring the 2θ angle from 10 to 70◦with a step size

2.7. Photocatalytic self-cleaning
The photocatalytic activity of the SE samples was assessed by eval­
uating the degradation of 0.02 mM Rhodamine B (RhB) dye solution 
under solar light irradiation using a Q-SUN Xe-3 xenon arc test chamber 
(Q-LAB, United Kingdom), equipped with 3 (radiation settings, 42–75 
W/m2), and optical filter Daylight-Q, which replicate the full outdoor 
solar spectrum from 295 to 3000 nm. The SE samples (0.8 cm × 4.1 cm) 
were immersed in 3 mL of the RhB solution in cuvettes and kept in the 
dark for 30 min to achieve adsorption–desorption equilibrium. The 
samples were then irradiated for 180 min, and the absorbance was 
measured every 30 min at the maximum absorption wavelength equal to 
552.93 nm using a Lambda 850 + UV/Vis spectrophotometer (Perkin 
Elmer, Great Britain). The dye concentration at each time interval was 
calculated using previously established calibration curves. The photo­
catalytic degradation of RhB dye was determined by calculating the 
concentration ratio of Ct to C0, where Ct represents the dye concentra­
tion at a given time of irradiation and C0 is the initial dye concentration 
after the adsorption–desorption equilibrium.
To study the photocatalytic self-cleaning performance, the samples 
were separately immersed in the RhB dye solution and in decanted 
Turkish coffee (5 g of ground coffee/100 mL water) for 30 s and then air 
dried and irradiated in a Q-SUN Xe-3 xenon arc test chamber. Before and 
after the irradiation, the colour coordinates L*, a* and b* in the CIELAB 
colour space were determined for the samples using a Datacolor Spectro 
1050 spectrophotometer (Datacolor, USA). Measurements were per­
formed with a 9 mm aperture under D65 illumination and an observation 
angle of 10◦. Ten measurements were performed for each sample, and 
the differences in the colour coordinates before and after irradiation 
were calculated from the mean values.

2.8.3. Abrasion resistance
The abrasion resistance of the textile materials was evaluated using 
the Martindale abrasion tester in accordance with ISO 12947-2 (Textiles 
— Determination of the abrasion resistance of fabrics — Part 2: Deter­
mination of specimen breakdown). Before testing, all samples were 
conditioned under standard atmospheric conditions (20 ± 2 ◦C and 65 
± 4% RH). Circular specimens were mounted in the Martindale Wear & 
Abrasion Tester M235 (SDL International, UK) and subjected to multi­
directional rubbing against a standard abrasive fabric under a pressure 
of 9 kPa. The test was conducted in intervals of 200 rubs until specimen 
breakdown occurred, defined as the formation of a hole or rupture of 
yarns. Abrasion resistance was expressed as the number of cycles to 
breakdown and reported as the mean ± standard deviation of four 
measurements per sample.
2.9. Wash durability
The wash durability of the functionalised SE/P(Ch + T + C) sample 
was evaluated to determine the retention of its functional properties 
after one (1 W) and five (5 W) repeated launderings using a Gyrowash 
815 apparatus (James Heal, UK). Laundering was performed in accor­
dance with SIST EN 17658:2021 (Chemical disinfectants and antiseptics 
– Chemical textile disinfection for the domestic area, Phase 2, Step 2). 
Samples were immersed in a 2 g/L solution of the non-ionic surfactant 
ETOLAT, with three stainless steel balls, at a material-to-liquid ratio of 
1:10 for 30 min at 40 ◦C. After each wash, the fabrics were rinsed twice 
with distilled water and air-dried at room temperature. The antibacterial 
activity, UV protection and photocatalytic self-cleaning of the samples 
were then assessed after the wash cycles.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Morphological and structural characteristics
The combined SEM, EDS, ICP–MS, FTIR, and XRD results provide a 
coherent understanding of the incorporation, distribution, and struc­
tural effect of TiO2, curcumin, an active ingredient of turmeric extract, 
and chitosan in the silk fibroin matrix. SEM analysis shows that the 
untreated fibres (the SE(UN) sample) have a smooth surface (SEM image 
1 in Fig. 2a). Functionalisation with turmeric extract and plasma treat­
ment with subsequent chitosan application did not lead to any signifi­
cant morphological changes in the fibre morphology (the SE(C), SE/P 
(Ch), and SE/P(Ch + C) samples). In contrast, the in situ synthesis of TiO2 
on the silk fibres led to a significant increase in surface roughness, 
accompanied by the deposition of TiO2 nanoparticles and a continuous 
polymer film on the fibre surface (SE(T), SE(T + C), SE/P(Ch + T), and 
SE/P(Ch + T + C) samples). The latter exhibited visible cracks in the 
porous areas between the fibres.
The EDS analysis confirmed the presence of TiO2 in the SE/P(Ch + T 
+ C) sample (Fig. 2b), as evidenced by the clear Ti peaks in the spectrum. 
The N signal cannot be clearly assigned to the amino groups of chitosan 
as silk intrinsically contains both amino and amide functionalities. 
Similarly, the C and O peaks cannot serve as clear markers for curcumin, 
as these elements are abundant in the silk matrix.
ICP–MS measurements demonstrate that incorporating of curcumin 
and chitosan during synthesis significantly increased TiO2 loading 
(Fig. 2a). The maximum Ti concentration, 16000 mg/kg (corresponding 
to 26,720 mg/kg TiO2), was reached in the SE/P(Ch + T + C) sample, 
which underwent plasma and chitosan pretreatment followed by in situ 
TiO2 synthesis in the presence of turmeric extract. Elemental mapping 
also confirmed the homogeneous spatial distribution of TiO2 on the

polymorph, both in the absence and presence of the turmeric extract. 
However, the lower intensity of (101) reflection in the (T + C) powder 
pattern compared to the (T) powder suggests that curcumin might 
inhibit crystal growth and thus reduce the crystallinity of TiO2.
In addition, the appearance of a weak, broad feature near 2θ ≈43◦in 
the (T + C) powder pattern could originate from amorphous organic 
residues or surface-bound species derived from curcumin, suggesting 
subtle TiO2 surface modification and morphological changes induced by 
turmeric extract. In contrast, the characteristic TiO2 diffraction peaks 
were not evident in the XRD patterns of the SE(T), SE(T + C), SE/P(Ch +
T) and SE/P(Ch + T + C) samples (Fig. 3b). In these samples, the TiO2 
signals are obscured by dominant peaks at 2θ = 20.2◦and 24.5◦, which 
correspond to the crystalline β-sheet structure of the silk II protein 
(Cheng et al., 2022).

TiO2/curcumin composite, Tauc plots were generated for the SE(T), SE

(Liu et al., 2014; Nam et al., 2019), indicating the coexistence of 
amorphous TiO2 with the anatase phase, which is confirmed by XRD 
analysis.
The structural disorder of TiO2 was further evidenced by the Urbach 
tail, which is clearly visible in the Tauc plots of the SE(T) and SE/P(Ch +
T) samples as a curvature below the Eg threshold. In the curcumin- 
containing SE(T + C) and SE/P(Ch + T + C) samples, both the size 
and width of the Urbach tail increased compared to the TiO2-containing 
SE(T) and SE/P(Ch + T) samples, indicating increased structural and 
electronic disorder and the formation of additional intraband gap states 
at the TiO2/curcumin interface (Makuła et al., 2018). These features are 
consistent with the formation of surface-bound complexes between 
curcumin molecules and TiO2 nanoparticles, which locally alter the 
electronic structure. At the same time, curcumin can act as a photo­
sensitizer and electron donor due to its strong absorption of visible light, 
which extends the photoreaction of the composite into the visible range 
and creates a new pathway for charge generation. The pronounced 
Urbach tail in the curcumin-containing samples leads to an apparent 
narrowing of the band gap, resulting in Eg,app values of 3.2 for SE(T + C) 
and 3.0 eV for SE/P(Ch + T + C). This optical effect occurs despite the 
absence of a measurable change in TiO2 crystal structure or intrinsic Eg, 
suggesting that curcumin affects the electronic states of the TiO2 inter­
face without altering its fundamental band structure.
To investigate the effects of chitosan, the plasma treatment and 
curcumin on the electronic properties of TiO2 and the electron–hole 
recombination dynamics, PL spectra were recorded for the functional­
ised SE samples, and the results are shown in Fig. 8. The SE(T) sample 
showed two prominent fluorescence peaks: a weaker peak at 340 nm, 
which is attributed to excitonic recombination and surface defect cen­
tres in TiO2, and a strong, broad peak around 425 nm, which is related to 
electron–hole recombination at surface defects (Fig. 8a) (Diebold, 
2003). This spectrum represents the basic fluorescence properties of 
pure TiO2 within the composite. The introduction of curcumin into the 
SE(T + C) sample significantly altered the fluorescence response. 
Although the 340 nm peak remained, the intensity of the broad 425 nm 
emission was reduced approximately fourfold. This peak appeared 
broadened and split into two components at 405 and 430 nm. These 
features indicate the presence of additional fluorescent centres involving 
both TiO2 and curcumin molecules. Importantly, curcumin acts as a 
photosensitizer and charge carrier mediator, facilitating electron 
transfer from TiO2 and thereby suppressing electron–hole recombina­
tion (Buddee et al., 2014). This charge transfer effect leads to reduced 
fluorescence intensity, indicating improved separation of charge car­
riers, which has a positive effect on photocatalytic performance. The SE/ 
P(Ch + T) sample showed a similar fluorescence profile to SE(T), with 
the intensity of the peak at 425 nm decreasing by about 20%. This in­
dicates that the plasma treatment and chitosan moderately passivate 
surface defects and slightly reduce recombination without significantly 
altering the intrinsic electronic structure of TiO2 (Chen et al., 2009). The 
SE/P(Ch + T + C) sample exhibited the lowest total fluorescence 
emission. The 340 nm peak was almost extinguished, and the broad 425 
nm emission decreased further to about 12% of the intensity observed in 
SE(T). This strongly suggests synergistic interactions between plasma 
treatment, chitosan and curcumin leading to effective passivation of 
surface defects and efficient charge transfer pathways that significantly 
suppress electron-hole recombination.

3.3. Antibacterial properties
The antibacterial activity of untreated and functionalised SE samples 
against S. aureus (Gram-positive bacteria) and E. coli (Gram-negative 
bacteria), with the SE(UN) sample serving as a reference, is presented in 
Fig. 9. The inclusion of TiO2 in the SE(T) sample resulted in measurable 
photocatalytic antibacterial activity, yielding a bacterial reduction of 
0.40 log units for S. aureus and 2.05 log units for E. coli (Fig. 9a,b). This 
effect is attributed to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), 
which trigger oxidative stress, disrupt the morphology of the cell 
membrane and cause cell destruction (Yong et al., 2023). The differ­
ences in the efficiency of TiO2 against S. aureus and E. coli are consistent 
with the differences in bacterial cell wall structure and intrinsic sus­
ceptibility to ROS-mediated attack.
The presence of curcumin in the SE(C) sample unexpectedly pro­
moted bacterial growth compared to SE(UN), despite the well- 
established antibacterial properties of curcuminoids as bioactive phe­
nols (Palaskar et al., 2021). This apparent paradox is most likely due to

the antibacterial performance was expressed as a percentage reduction

detectable CFU and a 99.99% reduction for E. coli, while the SE/P(Ch +
T), SE/P(Ch + C) and SE/P(Ch + T + C) samples achieved a reduction of 
over 99.5% for both stains. These results demonstrate that the combi­
nation of chitosan with TiO2 and/or curcumin retains high antibacterial 
efficacy and represents a robust multimechanistic strategy for the anti­
bacterial functionalisation of silk.
3.4. UV protection properties

samples under UV-A (315–400 nm) and UV-B (290–315 nm) irradiation 
is shown in Fig. 10. The untreated silk fibres (SE(UN)) exhibited poor UV 
attenuation, allowing for a considerable amount of incident radiation to 
pass through and blocking only 60.7% UV-A and 76.0% of UV-B. This 
corresponds to a UPF of only 3.3. Such low protection is not sufficient to 
protect the wearer from harmful UV irradiation, which can lead to 
photo-aging of the skin, erythema, and can increase the risk of skin 
cancer development.

improve the UV-blocking effect due to its chemical structure, which does 
not contain any UV-absorbing components. In contrast, the application

(Ch + C) samples) markedly reduced UV transmission, and the effect 
was even more pronounced when both substances were combined (SE(T 
+ C) and SE/P(Ch + T + C) samples) (Fig. 10a). TiO2, an inorganic

to TiO2. Even without TiO2, the SE(C) and SE/P(Ch + C) samples 
exceeded the minimum protection threshold, with UPF values of 17.1 
and 18.2, respectively.
The co-deposition of TiO2 and curcumin led to a clear synergistic 
improvement by utilising the complementary absorption profiles of both 
active materials. Accordingly, the SE(T + C) and SE/P(Ch + T + C) 
samples achieved UPF values of 32.9 and 32.1, respectively, which are 
categorised as “good” UV protection. This synergistic effect underlines 
the effectiveness of the binary-component functionalisation in optimis­
ing the UV protection performance of the SE fabric.
3.5. Photocatalytic self-cleaning properties
The photocatalytic self-cleaning performance of the functionalised 
SE samples was evaluated using two complementary approaches: (i) 
monitoring the decolourisation of RhB dye in aqueous solution under 
simulated solar light irradiation and (ii) assessing the visible fading of 
RhB and coffee stains deposited directly on the SE fabric surface. The 
corresponding results are shown in Fig. 11. For comparison, control tests 
were carried out with the untreated SE(UN) sample.
In the photodegradation test of the RhB dye solution, a rapid 
decrease in RhB concentration was observed in all SE samples, including 
the SE(UN) control (Fig. 11a). However, the untreated fabric exhibited 
no intrinsic photocatalytic activity, and the apparent decolourisation 
was solely attributed to the high adsorption capacity of the silk fibres for 
RhB, which removed the dye molecules from the solution without 
chemical decomposition. A similar effect dominated by adsorption was 
observed for the MB dye (Fig. S4, Supplementary Material). The decol­
ourisation rate of the dye in solution was therefore not a reliable mea­
sure of the photocatalytic performance of the functionalised SE samples 
as it was dominated by adsorption rather than photocatalytic oxidation.
A more representative evaluation was therefore obtained by tracking 
the colour fading of RhB and coffee stains that had settled on the fibre 
surface when exposed to solar light irradiation (Fig. 11b-d). As expected, 
the SE(UN) control showed no photocatalytic response; in fact, the RhB 
stain darkened even during the first hour of irradiation and faded only 
slightly with prolonged irradiation, as reflected by a slight increase in 
the ΔL* value (Fig. 11b). In contrast, there was an immediate and pro­
gressive fading of RhB colouration in all of the TiO2-containing samples 
with the photocatalytic performance increasing in the following order: 
SE(T) < SE/P(Ch + T) < SE(T + C) < SE/P(Ch + T + C). These results 
highlight the key role of curcumin as a photosensitiser within the TiO2/ 
curcumin composite, which significantly enhances the photocatalytic 
activity of TiO2 under solar light irradiation. A similar trend was 
observed for the SE samples stained with coffee (Fig. 11c,d). In the SE 
samples without TiO2, the coffee stains initially darkened under irradi­
ation, indicating a lack of photocatalytic degradation. In contrast, TiO2- 
containing SE samples already showed a visible fading of the coffee stain 
within the first hour of exposure to sunlight. The presence of curcumin 
further enhanced the self-cleaning effect of TiO2 and led to significantly

Analysis of colour changes revealed that the b* coordinate (yellow-

than the a* coordinate (red-green axis) (Fig. 11d). For SE(UN) and the 
TiO2-free functionalised SE samples (SE(C), SE/P(Ch), and SE/P(Ch +

functionalised SE samples (SE(T + C), SE/P(Ch + T) and SE/P(Ch + T +
C)) exhibited a decrease in both a* and b* coordinates, consistent with 
the simultaneous loss of the red and yellow colour components during 
photocatalytic oxidation. Remarkably, SE(T + C) achieved the most 
pronounced stain removal, narrowly outperforming SE/P(Ch + T + C). 
This shows that the photocatalytic efficiency depends not only on the 
photoactive surface chemistry but also on the intrinsic molecular 
structure of the target stain.
3.6. Multifunctional performance
To evaluate the effect of single active compounds and their combi­
nations on the multifunctional performance of the SE samples, each 
functionality was normalised by dividing the measured value of a 
sample by the maximum value observed across all samples, enabling 
relative comparison (Fig. 12).
The diagram demonstrates that the incorporation of a single active 
compound (SE(T), SE(C), and SE/P(Ch) samples) did not confer 
multifunctional properties. In contrast, binary-component systems (SE 
(T + C), SE/P(Ch + T), and SE/P(Ch + C) samples) considerably 
broadened the functional profile, and improved performance in the 
domains most strongly associated with the incorporated components. 
However, none of these binary formulations simultaneously achieved 
effective antibacterial properties, self-cleaning, and UV protection; at 
least one property, typically governed by the missing component, 
remained compromised. This finding underlines the need to include all 
three active compounds in the functionalisation process and to condition 
their mutual compatibility. This behaviour is clearly evident in the SE/P 
(Ch + T + C) sample, which exhibited the broadest multifunctional 
profile of all the studied SE samples.

with increases of approximately 20%–35%. This behaviour contrasts 
with reports in which chitosan- and inorganic nanoparticle-based sur­

formation of continuous surface coatings (Dong et al., 2026; Trad et al., 
2018; Xu et al., 2025). However, air permeability is influenced not only 
by overall porosity but also by pore size distribution and pore inter­
connectivity. In the present system, chitosan interacts with fibrillated

In contrast to the air permeability results, functionalisation of the SE

Furthermore, the incorporation of TiO2 nanoparticles within the chito­

functionalised SE samples to maintain adequate moisture management 
performance.
Functionalisation with chitosan, either alone or combined with 
curcumin (SE/P(Ch) and SE/P(Ch + C)), resulted in a significant in­
crease in abrasion resistance (Fig. 13c), reaching approximately twice 
that of the untreated control (SE(UN)). This improvement is attributed 
to the formation of a cohesive chitosan coating on the fibre surface, 
which stabilises fibrillated silk fibres and enhance inter-fibre adhesion, 
thereby reducing fibre pull-out and damage during mechanical rubbing. 
In contrast, the incorporation of TiO2 nanoparticles (SE/P(Ch + T) and 
SE/P(Ch + T + C)) led to a decrease in abrasion resistance, as the rigid 
inorganic particles do not contribute to fibre bonding and may create 
localised stress concentrations that promote fibre damage. Nevertheless, 
the chitosan/TiO2 coating maintained abrasion resistance comparable to 
that of the untreated SE(UN) sample while simultaneously providing 
additional multifunctional properties to the fabric.
3.8. Washing durability of the coating
The laundering durability of the SE/P(Ch + T + C) coating was 
systematically evaluated following one and five laundering cycles by 
quantifying the residual TiO2 content, alongside assessments of anti­
bacterial performance, UV-shielding capacity, photocatalytic self- 
cleaning and colour stability (Fig. 14). ICP-MS analysis indicated a 
reduction in TiO2 content after the initial washing cycle, with 52.5% of 
the original loading retained. Notably, the remaining TiO2 exhibited 
high stability during subsequent laundering cycles, with its content 
remaining essentially unchanged and reaching a plateau of 51.9% 
retention after five washing cycles (Fig. 14a). These results, further 
supported by FTIR analysis, indicate a dual-mode immobilisation 
mechanism of TiO2 nanoparticles within the chitosan matrix, involving 
both non-covalent, predominantly hydrogen-bonding interactions, and 
covalent bonding. Nanoparticles associated through weaker, non- 
covalent interactions are preferentially removed during the first 
washing cycle due to mechanical agitation and frictional forces. In 
contrast, the fraction of TiO2 nanoparticles covalently bound to the 
polymeric network demonstrates high resistance to repeated laundering. 
The inclusion of stainless-steel balls in the washing protocol, used to 
simulate enhanced mechanical abrasion, further highlights the me­
chanical and chemical stability of the covalently crosslinked network.
SEM confirmed the persistence of the coating after both one and five 
washing cycles (Fig. S5, Supplementary Material). However, localised 
microcracking was observed, likely resulting from the mechanical 
stresses imposed during laundering. This partial disruption of film 
integrity compromises the continuity of the coating, contributing to the 
gradual decline in its functional performance upon repeated washing.
Following the initial laundering cycle, the antibacterial activity 
value (A) decreased from 3.95 to 1.37 log units for S. aureus and from 
3.45 to 2.07 log units for E. coli relative to the unwashed sample (Fig. 9a, 
Fig. 14b). As the antibacterial efficacy is predominantly imparted by 
chitosan, this attenuation implies a dual-binding mechanism; the chi­
tosan was likely immobilized onto the silk protein matrix via a combi­
nation of robust covalent crosslinks and weaker, non-covalent hydrogen 
bonds. These relatively labile non-covalent interactions are insufficient 
to withstand the hydrolytic and mechanical stresses of laundering, 
leading to partial chitosan desorption. Nevertheless, a substantial frac­
tion of chitosan remained stably anchored on the SE/P(Ch + T + C) 
surface, resulting in bacterial reductions exceeding 97% for S. aureus 
and 99% for E. coli compared to the untreated SE(UN) sample (Fig. 14c).

The UV protection performance of the SE/P(Ch + T + C) sample 
decreased progressively with increasing washing cycles, as reflected by a 
reduction in UPF values, which can be attributed to localised coating 
damage and partial loss of TiO2. Nevertheless, the UPF values remained 
within a functional range, indicating sufficient UV-shielding capability 
due to the stable incorporation of TiO2 within the coating matrix.
Notably, the photocatalytic self-cleaning performance of the SE/P

(ΔL*) of the coffee stain and a pronounced decrease in the red chromatic

not adversely affect photocatalytic activity, implying that the remaining 
TiO2 loading is above the threshold required to sustain efficient photo­
catalytic performance within the nanocomposite coating. After five 
washing cycles, only a marginal reduction in photocatalytic efficiency 
was observed, confirming the durability of the functional coating. 
Furthermore, the negligible colour changes of the SE/P(Ch + T + C) 
sample after repeated washing suggest effective retention of curcumin 
within the coating, thereby preserving its role as a photosensitiser and 
maintaining the overall photocatalytic response.
Overall, the results demonstrate that the chitosan/TiO2/curcumin- 
based coating exhibits semi-durable performance, maintaining a sub­
stantial proportion of its antimicrobial and UV-protective functionalities 
after one washing cycle, followed by a gradual decline after five cycles. 
The findings underscore the potential of this multifunctional coating 
system for practical textile applications, while also highlighting the need 
for further optimisation to enhance long-term washing durability by 
increasing the interfacial bonding and improve coating flexibility and 
stability. The integration of ultrasound-assisted hydrothermal synthesis 
provides a highly efficient method for enhancing TiO2 immobilisation 
and photoprotective performance; specifically, acoustic cavitation ac­
celerates nucleation kinetics and long-range structural ordering of TiO2. 
At the same time, interfacial adhesion between the inorganic phase and 
the substrate can be further strengthened by incorporating silane 
coupling agents, such as 3-glycidyloxypropyltriethoxysilane, which 
promote the formation of robust covalent linkages within the polymeric 
matrix. To broaden UV absorption and improve photoprotective per­
formance, bio-derived chromophores can be added, offering synergistic 
shielding efficiency. Furthermore, the viscoelastic properties of the 
chitosan network can be adjusted by regulating crosslinking density and 
incorporating compatible plasticisers to enhance segmental chain 
mobility. This approach effectively dissipates internal localised stresses, 
thereby suppressing micro-fissure propagation and ensuring mechanical 
durability through repeated hydrodynamic laundering cycles.
4. Conclusions
We present a sustainable, bio-inspired, process-oriented surface en­
gineering strategy for fabricating of a carbohydrate polymer-based 
hybrid nanocomposite coating comprising chitosan, TiO2 and curcu­
min on silk fabric. The resulting system functions as an integrated 
platform exhibiting synergistic antibacterial activity, UV shielding, and 
visible-light-driven photocatalytic self-cleaning within a single, 
coherent coating architecture. The novelty lies in the deliberate design 
of a hybrid organic–inorganic nanocomposite in which each component 
plays an active and cooperative role. Chitosan is not merely a passive 
binder but a structurally and functionally indispensable matrix that di­
rects in situ TiO2 formation, enhances interfacial coupling, and provides 
intrinsic antibacterial activity. Simultaneously, curcumin is elevated

controlled chitosan immobilisation, and in situ nanoparticle synthesis, 
enabling a level of interfacial control, coating uniformity, and functional 
integration not achieved in previous studies. Furthermore, the use of 
bio-based components reinforces the sustainability of the approach 
without compromising performance. Collectively, these features do not 
represent an incremental improvement but rather establish a new design 
framework for carbohydrate polymer-based functional coatings, posi­
tioning the developed system beyond the current state of the art in 
multifunctional textile materials.
From an application-oriented perspective, the proposed coating 
strategy demonstrates promising feasibility. Chitosan and curcumin are 
renewable and widely available biopolymers, while TiO2 is already 
established in industrial textile finishing. The substitution of conven­
tionally used glutaraldehyde with the non-toxic crosslinking agent BTCA 
for chitosan grafting onto the substrate constitutes a greener coating 
design. Moreover, in situ TiO2 synthesis on plasma-activated, chitosan- 
modified silk eliminates the need for pre-synthesised nanoparticles and 
additional fixation steps, thereby simplifying processing. Plasma acti­
vation is compatible with pad–dry–cure systems, and the multifunc­
tional coatings preserve the intrinsic properties of silk, supporting their 
potential for high-value applications such as medical textiles and pro­
tective materials. Optimisation of the chitosan/TiO2/curcumin coating 
through ultrasound-assisted hydrothermal synthesis of TiO2 nano­
particles, the use of silane coupling agents, such as 3-glycidyloxypropyl­
triethoxysilane, and the incorporation of an additional bio-derived UV 
absorber and a compatible plasticiser enables enhanced UV-shielding 
capacity, mechanical flexibility, and laundering durability. These im­
provements are attributed to the systematic modulation of interfacial 
interactions and surface morphology within the polymer matrix.
Overall, this work demonstrates a process-oriented strategy for 
integrating biopolymer, inorganic, and organic components into high- 
performance coatings, highlighting the strategic importance of carbo­
hydrate polymers in developing of next-generation multifunctional and 
sustainable textile materials.

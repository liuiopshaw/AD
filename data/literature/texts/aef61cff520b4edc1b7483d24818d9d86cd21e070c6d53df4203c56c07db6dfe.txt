ABSTRACT: The replacement of dressings may cause secon-
dary damage to the wounds; thus, the real-time monitoring of
the state of wound dressings is crucial for evaluating wound
care processes. Herein, we report a smart dressing to self-
monitor residue nanomedicine on it during the application. We
load aminobenzeneboronic acid (ABA)-modiﬁed gold nano-
clusters (A-GNCs) on bacterial cellulose (BC) membranes as an
antibacterial wound dressing to display the amount of residual
nanomedicine (A-GNCs) by in situ colorimetry during the application in remedying multi-drug-resistant (MDR) bacteria-
infected wounds. A-GNCs emit bright orange ﬂuorescence under UV light, whereas the BC membrane is transparent at a
humidiﬁed state on the wounds. Thus, the BC-A-GNCs nanocomposite (BGN) shows decreasing intensity of orange
ﬂuorescence with the release of the A-GNCs, indicating the appropriate time points for the replacement of the dressing. The
BGN, which can realize accurate self-monitoring in a simple, low-cost, and eﬃcient way, thus holds great promise for broad
clinical applications.
KEYWORDS: gold nanoclusters, antibacterial activity, bacterial cellulose, wound dressing, smart self-monitoring
T
he problem of multi-drug-resistant (MDR) bacteria
has escalated over the decades due to the vast
application of antibiotics.1,2 In recent years, the
incidence of skin infection increased dramatically and poses
serious threats to public health because few available drugs can
ﬁght against MDR bacteria.3−6 Hence, the need to ﬁnd
eﬀective and new antimicrobials or materials with new
antibacterial properties is urgent.7 Many nanomaterials have
been developed as potential antimicrobial agents, such as
silver,8,9 zinc oxide,10 titanium dioxide, and copper oxide11
nanoparticles. Although these nanomaterials are eﬀective in
inhibiting bacteria, none of them is the best choice as
antimicrobial agent for in vivo medical applications due to their
varying degrees of toxicity.12,13 Studies demonstrated that gold
nanoparticles (GNPs)-based strategies can reduce the
incidence of skin, lung, and gastrointestinal tract infec-
tions.14−16 With the assistance of a variety of surface ligands,
gold nanomaterials have eﬀective antimicrobial activity with
excellent biocompatibility. Moreover, compared with GNPs,
gold nanoclusters (GNCs) with the same surface modiﬁcation
have stronger antibacterial activity.17 Furthermore, compared
with GNPs, GNCs can emit ﬂuorescence.18 In this study, we
develop aminobenzeneboronic acid (ABA)-modiﬁed GNCs
(A-GNCs), which can emit bright orange ﬂuorescence under
ultraviolet light and have eﬀective antibacterial activity.
Among various reported dressing scaﬀolds,19−21 bacterial
cellulose (BC) has unique properties such as high mechanical
strength, optical transparency, super softness, water uptake
ability, and excellent biocompatibility.22−24 The porous
structure of BC resembles the extracellular matrix of tissues,
which is suitable for serving as dressings to accelerate skin
regeneration.25 More importantly, the high transparency of wet
BC membranes provides the convenience to observe the
morphological change of the wound beneath the dressings.26
Herein, we incorporate A-GNCs into the BC scaﬀold to obtain
a wound dressing (BC-A-GNCs nanocomposites, BGN) that
bears the advantages of the ﬂuorescence of the A-GNCs and
the transparency of the BC. We evaluate the in vivo

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Chemical and Physical Characterization of A-GNCs.
We propose a one-step reaction method to synthesize the A-
GNCs in the presence of glutathione (GSH) and ABA as the
modiﬁcation agents. The GNCs fabricated without ABA are set
as a control. The average diameters of A-GNCs and GNCs are
approximately 2 nm with a clear lattice structure characterized
by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (Figure
2A,B). The narrow peaks in dynamic light scattering (DLS)
demonstrate their uniform size and good dispersity (Figure
2C). The A-GNCs are light yellow in the solution under
daytime light and emit intense orange ﬂuorescence (600 nm)
under an excitation at 365 nm (Figure 2D−F). X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis indicates the
simultaneous presence of Au (4f), C (1s), N (1s), O (1s),
and S (2p) electrons on A-GNCs, indicating the successful
functionalization of ABA on the GNCs (Supporting
Information (SI), Figure S1). After diﬀerent incubation times
(1, 3, and 7 d) in 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS)-containing
medium, the size of A-GNCs shows no signiﬁcant change
under DLS analysis (Figure S2), indicating that A-GNCs are
highly stable under these conditions for a long time. The high
stability of A-GNCs is important for their mass production,
preservation, and clinical applications.
Antibacterial Activity of A-GNCs. The minimum
inhibition concentration (MIC) of GNCs and A-GNCs against
diﬀerent bacteria and their corresponding clinical MDR strains
is characterized (Figure 3A). A-GNCs have excellent
antibacterial activity against many kinds of Gram-negative
bacteria (Escherichia coli, E. coli, MDR E. coli; Klebsiella
pneumonia, K. p, MDR K. p; Pseudomonas aeruginosa, P. a,
MDR P. a). Individual GNCs and ABA have no inhibition
eﬀects on any bacteria even at a concentration as high as 64
μg/mL (Table S1). By contrast, when ABA is modiﬁed on the
GNCs, the resulting A-GNCs can cause the collapse of the
bacterial cell wall, which merges together (Figure 3B),
indicating the potent antimicrobial eﬀect of the A-GNCs.
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) shows that the bacteria
treated by GNCs keep an intact cell wall structure, implying
that GNCs do not disrupt the bacterial cell wall.
Biological Safety of A-GNCs. We evaluate the blood
biocompatibility of the A-GNCs and GNCs by the hemolysis
test.27 We test the optical density at 567 nm, and the data
reveal that the A-GNCs have a wide range of safe
concentrations for blood-contacting applications (Figure S3).
When the concentrations of the agents rise to 64 μg/mL, ABA,
A-GNCs, and GNCs do not lead to signiﬁcant hemolysis of rat
erythrocytes (Figure S3, Figure S4). Furthermore, the cell
densities and morphologies of human umbilical vein
endothelial cells (HUVECs) cocultured with A-GNCs and
GNCs are similar to those of the control group, indicating the
good viability of the cells (Figure 4A). Considering that the A-
GNCs will be used to treat skin infections, we test the cell
viability of human aortic ﬁbroblasts (HAFs, ﬁbroblastic cell

Characterization of BC-A-GNCs Nanocomposites. BC
has a good mechanical property and a large speciﬁc surface
area, so it is suitable for loading the A-GNCs as a wound
dressing. A-GNCs solution is light yellow under daytime light
and emits orange ﬂuorescence under ultraviolet light (Figure
5A). We soak BC membranes in diﬀerent concentrations of A-
GNCs solutions and measure the concentration of unbound A-
GNCs by a ﬂuorospectrophotometer at selected time intervals.
By subtracting the free A-GNCs from the total A-GNCs in the
solution, we get the amount of A-GNCs bound on the BC
membranes. When the concentration of A-GNCs is 32 μg/mL,
almost all the A-GNCs absorb on the BC membrane (Figure
S5), indicating the high loading capacity of the BC. With the
increase of the concentration, more A-GNCs are loaded onto
the BC membrane and the loading become saturated due to
the limited space between the nanoﬁbers. 128 μg/mL A-GNCs
solution leads to a maximal loading, so we choose this
concentration to prepare BC-A-GNCs nanocomposites
(BGN). The ﬂuorescence of the A-GNCs becomes weak
with the decrease of the concentration from 128 to 0 μg/mL.
Under the concentration of 4 μg/mL, the ﬂuorescence is
invisible by the naked dye. We incubate BC membrane pieces
with diﬀerent concentrations of A-GNCs to obtain BGN,
which emit bright and evenly distributed orange ﬂuorescence
at concentrations from 128 to 32 μg/mL. With the decrease of
the concentration of A-GNCs, the ﬂuorescence of the BGN
becomes weak and uneven (Figure 5A). When the
concentration of A-GNCs in the BGN (8 μg/mL) is slightly
lower than the MIC (8.75 μg/mL), the A-GNCs are mainly
concentrated at the edge of the BC. This means that the BGN
will lose its therapeutic activity and should be replaced (Figure
5A). By taking advantage of the transparency of the BGN, we
can not only observe the healing process of the infected wound
without peeling the dressing but also monitor the residual dose
of the therapeutic agents on the dressing. We can avoid
secondary damage of the wounds caused by frequent and
blinded changing of the dressing (Figure 5A). Thus, the BGN
can realize real-time self-monitoring of the A-GNCs to guide
the replacement of the BGN.
We evaluate the morphological change of the BC
membranes after incorporation of A-GNCs. The incorporation
of A-GNCs does not signiﬁcantly inﬂuence the morphology of
the BC membrane indicated by SEM (Figure 5B). The
structural diﬀerences between the two sides of the BC
membrane lead to the diﬀerence in A-GNCs loading. The
more porous structure and the thinner ﬁbers of the bottom
side allow more A-GNCs to concentrate on the bottom side
than the top side (Figure 5B). We incubate bacteria with the
top and bottom sides of the BGN membrane for 24 h to
evaluate the antibacterial eﬀect of both sides. The inhibition
zones of the two sides are similar (∼12.6 mm) (Figure S6).
According to the release curve (Figure S12), more than 50% of
the A-GNCs are released within 24 h; this is the possible
reason for the similar antibacterial eﬃcacy of the two sides. In
addition, the dense layer on the top side can slow down water
evaporation, whereas the loose layer on the bottom side can
absorb tissue exudate. Thus, we place the bottom side of the
BGN toward the wound to optimize the function of the BC
membrane as a dressing.

The BGN membrane has a smaller speciﬁc surface area and

This means that the space of the pores in the BC membrane
has been occupied by the A-GNCs, indicating the successful

properties of the BC membrane by measuring the weight loss
of the dried membrane after degradation. The weight loss of
the BC membrane is 0.01% on day 1 and slowly increases to

quickly absorb water and approach a swelling balance within 6

indicating good swelling properties of the two materials. An
appropriate swelling property of dressings can absorb tissue
ﬂuid to prevent the accumulation of secreted ﬂuids and avoid
inﬂammation in the wounds.
We evaluate the hydrophilicity of the BGN, because ﬂuid
adsorption capability of a dressing is crucial for removing
wound exudate, which may hamper the speed of healing and
cause infection. The water droplets inﬁltrate into the BC and
BGN membranes within 1.4 s (Figure S10), demonstrating
that the membranes are hydrophilic and the incorporation of
A-GNCs has no signiﬁcant inﬂuence on the hydrophobicity/
hydrophilicity of the BC membrane. Considering that an ideal
wound dressing should have good strength, bending capability,
and ﬂexibility,30,31 we verify the mechanical properties of the
BGN membranes using a tensile tester at a ﬁxed speed of 20
mm/min at 25 °C. The elongation at break of BC membranes
increases with the incorporation of the A-GNCs. This may be
attributed to the improved compactness of the membranes
with the complexation of A-GNCs (Figure S11). The Young’s
modulus of BGN membranes and pure BC membranes is
around 4 MPa, which satisﬁes the ﬂexion and movement of the
wound site as dressings.
We further evaluate the nanoclusters’ release property of the
BGN membranes. Burst release occurs during the initial 3 d,
mainly due to the existence of parts of the A-GNCs near the
surface. After 5 d, the cumulative release of A-GNCs is
approximately 88%, which shows a slow and sustained release
proﬁle (Figure S12). Within 7 d, the release of the A-GNCs is
relatively stable, and the residual A-GNCs in the BGN
membrane is approximately 12 μg/mL (MIC, 8.75 μg/mL),
indicating that BGN will lose its therapeutic activity and
should be replaced around that time. Thus, the BGN
membrane can ensure the eﬀective antibacterial concentration
of A-GNCs in the infected wound during the treatment.
We evaluate the anti-inﬂammatory property of the BGN
membrane by testing their ability to eliminate NO triggered by
lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which is a biomarker of inﬂamma-
tion. Compared with the LPS group, the BGN membrane
reduces the concentration of NO signiﬁcantly, indicating the
potent anti-inﬂammatory eﬀect of the BGN membrane (Figure
S13). The BGN membrane has a strong ability to scavenge
DPPH free radicals, indicating the excellent total antioxidant
capacity (Table S5).

In Vivo Experiment. Both Gram-positive and Gram-
negative bacteria can occur on wound sites. Since Gram-
negative infection is the prevalent type in burn wounds, and
Gram-negative infection is harder to treat than Gram-positive
infection, especially for MDR bacteria, we perform the
experiments in Gram-negative bacteria-infected wounds. We
establish full-thickness infection skin wound models of P. a and
MDR P. a (divided into the BC and BGN groups) to study the
function of the smart wound dressing formed by the BGN
membranes (Figure 6). P. a is the most common bacteria
isolated from chronic infection wounds.32 We record the
wound areas and excise the wound tissues for histological
analysis at time points of the third, seventh, 10th, and 14th day.
Due to the transparency of the membranes, we can directly
observe the wound healing processes clearly under daytime
light and quantify the remaining amount of antibacterial A-
GNCs in the membranes through the color of the dressing
under ultraviolet light (Figure 6A,B). With the increase of
time, the A-GNCs loaded on the BC ﬁlm are gradually released
for antibacterial activity, and the dressing color gradually
becomes lighter. On the seventh day, the color of the BGN
becomes basically invisible, which means that the BGN
membrane will lose its therapeutic activity and should be
replaced (marked by red arrows). The above results are
consistent with the in vitro release test. The in situ colorimetry
can directly indicate the residual amount of A-GNCs and
suggest the adequate time for the replacement of the dressings.
The sizes of the BGN-treated wound areas are signiﬁcantly
smaller than those of the BC group (Figure 6A−C). After 14 d,
the ratios of the healed area of the P. a-infected wounds of the
BC and BGN groups are 75 ± 3.0% and 91 ± 3.1%,
respectively, whereas the ratios of the MDR P. a-infected
wounds of BC and BGN groups are 76 ± 3.8% and 96 ± 2.5%,
respectively (Figure S20). The results illustrate that the doping
of A-GNCs in the BC membrane could accelerate the healing
of the bacteria-infected wounds eﬀectively.
To correctly evaluate the wound, it is best to observe the
growing granulation tissues beneath the dressings. Dead or
inactivated tissue will not only hinder the wound healing
process but also increase the risk of infection and sepsis. We
can directly observe the wound healing processes clearly
through the transparent BGN membranes under daytime light
(Figure 6A); thus the frequent replacement of dressings and
secondary damage to the wound can be avoided. Debridement
is an ordinary operation to remove necrotic tissues in wounds
and prevent inﬂammatory reactions and infections of the
wounds. Thus, it is useful for the conventional dressings, which
cannot eﬀectively absorb the exudate and insulate bacteria
from invading. Timely debridement can remove necrotic
tissues, but it can damage healthy tissues and is accompanied
by sharp pain from tearing the dressing from underlying
tissues.33−35 After 3 d of infection, we performed a debride-
ment operation. The BGN membrane-treated wound shows no
obvious damage, while the traditional gauze causes the tearing
of the wound and severe bleeding (Figure 7A). The
transparent BGN membranes makes it easy to observe the
wound, and the BC membranes can be easily peeled oﬀ
without causing additional damage to the wound. After 14 d,
the ratios of the healed area of the P. a-infected wounds of the
gauze groups with and without debridement operation are 84
± 3.3% and 62 ± 3.6%, respectively, while the ratios of the
MDR P. a-infected wounds of the gauze groups with and
without debridement operation are 85 ± 3.0% and 68 ± 2.8%,
respectively (Figure 7B, Figure S21). The healing rates of the
gauze groups with debridement (∼85%) are signiﬁcantly
higher than those of the groups without debridement
operation (∼65%). By comparison, the BGN membrane-
treated wounds are almost completely healed (∼97%),
indicating the excellent wound healing condition provided by
the easy-to-replace and smart wound dressings. The in situ
colorimetry can directly indicate the residual amount of the
antibacterial A-GNCs in the membranes, which can save the
materials to the maximal extent and does not require
professional judgment by the patients.

are infected by diﬀerent bacteria day 3 postoperation. On the
10th day, the levels of all inﬂammatory factors of the BGN
group drop to the normal range, indicating the signiﬁcant anti-
inﬂammatory eﬀect of the BGN (Table S7). The expression
levels of interleukin (IL)-6 and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α
in the wound skin are evaluated by immunohistochemistry.
The levels of both inﬂammatory factors increase on day 3
postinfection and gradually decrease on day 7 and day 14
postinfection. The levels of IL-6 and TNF-α in the BGN group
are lower than those in the BC group (Figure S26), suggesting
that BGN treatment downregulates the expression of IL-6 and
TNF-α in the wounds. The above immunohistochemistry
analysis reveals the signiﬁcant anti-inﬂammatory eﬀect of the
BGN membrane.
CONCLUSIONS
To visually monitor the nanomedicine-loaded wound dress-
ings, we develop a strategy to display the residual nano-
medicine in the dressings by combining the ﬂuorescence of
nanomedicine and the transparency of the scaﬀolds. Amino-
benzeneboronic acid-modiﬁed gold nanoclusters, which show
potent antibacterial activity, are employed as the ﬂuorescent
nanomedicine, whereas the bacterial cellulose membrane is
selected as the transparent scaﬀold. During the healing process
of bacteria-infected wounds, the intensity of orange ﬂuo-
rescence of the A-GNCs in the transparent BC scaﬀold
decreases with the release of the nanomedicine into the
wound, which can suggest the replacement of the dressing
when the residual concentration of the nanomedicine (A-
GNCs) is lower than the MIC. Thus, the real-time monitoring
of the state of the dressing can be realized by simply using a
violet light lamp and the frequent replacement of the dressing,
which can lead to the damage of the wounds, could be avoided.
Moreover, this strategy can be extended to medical devices to
realize real-time monitoring of their service life with high
accuracy.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Materials. Aminobenzeneboronic acid (Mw = 136.94) and
tetrachloroauric acid (HAuCl4·3H2O) were from Sigma (USA).
Glutathione (Mw = 307.33) was from Alfa Aesar (USA). All other
chemicals and solvents are reagent grade.
Preparation and Characterization of A-GNCs. We used GSH
as a reductant to prepare A-GNCs. We mixed a fresh GSH aqueous
solution (100 mM, 0.6 mL) with an ABA solution (100 mM, 0.3 mL).
After that, we added a HAuCl4 aqueous solution (20 mM, 2 mL) into
the solution and kept the ﬁnal mixture volume at 20 mL under mild
stirring at 500 rpm at 70 °C for 24 h. We synthesized GNCs using the
same method mentioned above without the addition of the ABA as
the control group. To remove the unreacted chemicals, we
subsequently dialyzed the solution with a dialysis bag (3000 Da Mw
cutoﬀ, Solarbio) for 24 h in double-distilled water. We used a 0.22 μm
ﬁlter (Millipore) to sterilize the samples and stored them at 4 °C for
use.
We characterized the morphologies of A-GNCs by transmission
electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai F30, FEI Company, USA). We
used an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP, 7700x
ICP-MS, Agilent, USA) to measure the elemental gold to reﬂect the
concentrations of the A-GNCs. We measured the size and zeta
potential of the samples on a dynamic light scattering device
(Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Company, England). We characterized
the chemical element of the A-GNCs by X-ray photoelectron
spectroscopy (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc, USA).
Antibacterial Activity of A-GNCs. We measured the minimal
inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the A-GNCs after 24 h of
incubation at 37 °C with diﬀerent bacteria. We cultured bacteria in
the Luria−Bertani (LB) medium (10 g/L NaCl, 10 g/L casein
tryptone, and 5 g/L yeast extract, pH = 7) and diluted the original
concentration of bacteria to 1 × 104 colony-forming units (CFU/
mL). The bacteria were cultured in LB liquid media with the addition
of A-GNCs for 24 h in Corning 96-well plates (Costar 3599), setting
the control group without any addition. To explain the mechanism of
the antibacterial eﬀects, we treated bacteria with A-GNCs or GNCs at
37 °C for 4 h on a shaking bed at 260 rpm. We ﬁxed the nanocluster-

treated bacteria with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and dehydrated the samples
by gradient ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100% (v/v,
in water)) and observed the morphological change of the bacteria by
scanning electron microscope (Merlin, Zeiss, Germany).
Biological Safety of A-GNCs. We tested the biosafety of the A-
GNCs by hemolysis assay. We centrifuged fresh rat blood at 1500 rpm
for 15 min, added diﬀerent concentrations of A-GNCs, and incubated
the samples at 37 °C for 3 h, setting saline as a negative control and
ultrapure water as a positive control. We measured the optical density
of the samples at 567 nm (OD567 nm) by a microplate reader (Tecan
Inﬁnite M200) after centrifuging the samples at 1500 rpm for 5 min.
We observed the hemolysis of the A-GNCs or GNCs by taking
photographs.
We evaluated the cytotoxicity of A-GNCs by testing the cell
viability. We cultured the cells (HUVECs, MDCK, and HAFs, 105
cells/mL) in DMEM, 10% FBS (Invitrogen, USA), and 1%
penicillin−streptomycin (MP Biomedicals, USA) and incubated the
cells with diﬀerent concentrations of A-GNCs for 24 and 48 h at 37
°C with 5% CO2. We treated the cells with a cell counting kit (CCK-
8, Dojindo, Japan) and measured the absorbance at 450 nm by a
microplate reader. We stained the cells by Calcein/PI (Dojindo,
Japan) and observed the morphology of the cells by laser confocal
microscopy (Zeiss LSM 710, Germany).
Fabrication and Characterization of BC-A-GNCs Nano-
composites. We cultured the G. xylinus (ATCC53582) in diﬀerent
well plates to obtain diﬀerently sized BC membranes. The BC
membranes had consistent thicknesses. We sterilized the BC
membranes at 121 °C for 20 min and stored them at either room
temperature or 4 °C for use. We diluted A-GNCs in PBS (2, 4, 8, 16,
32, 64, 128 μg/mL) and soaked the BC membranes in the solutions
for 24 h, respectively. To determine the loading weight (LW) of BC,
the concentration of unbound A-GNCs in the supernatant was
measured by a ﬂuorospectrophotometer (RF-6000, Japan). The load
weight can be calculated according to the equation LW = W1 −W2,
where W1 represents the total amount of A-GNCs in the solution and
W2 represents the unbound A-GNCs in the supernatant. We dried the
resulting BC-A-GNCs nanocomposites (BGN) overnight under
vacuum at room temperature and sterilized the BGN by ultraviolet
irradiation for 30 min per side. We observed the morphological
structure of the BC and BGN by SEM. The BC and BGN membranes
were ground into powders and mixed with potassium bromide
powder to be pressed into tablets. An FTIR spectrometer (Thermo
Scientiﬁc Nicolet iS20) was used to scan the samples in the range of
4000−700 cm−1. We utilized AFM (MFP-3D Stand Alone, Asylum
Research) in the standard tapping mode to measure the surface
roughness of the BC and BGN membranes. We reported the average
value with standard deviation. We investigated the speciﬁc surface
area and pore volume of BC and BGN membranes by measuring
nitrogen sorption at 77.320 K in an Autosorb−1 gas sorption system
(ASAP2020Plus HD88). The samples were degassed at 60 °C under
reduced pressure prior to each measurement. We soaked the BC and
BGN membranes in PBS and DMEM for diﬀerent times. We took out
the swollen samples and wiped oﬀthe surface solution. The swelling
ratio (SR) of the membranes is calculated according to the following
formula: SR (%) = (Wt −W0)/W0 × 100%, where W0 represents the
original weight of the membrane and Wt represents the weight of the
membrane at a certain time point. We soaked the dried BC and BGN
membranes in PBS at 37 °C for diﬀerent times. We took out the
samples, washed them with distilled water, and dried them in an oven
at 37 °C for 72 h. We calculated the weight loss (WL) according to
the following formula: WL (%) = (W0 −W1)/W0 × 100%, where W0
represents the original weight of the membrane and W1 represents the
weight of the membrane after hydrolytic degradation. We used a
water contact angle analysis system (WCA, SA100, Germany) to
evaluate the surface wettability of the BGN. We tested the mechanical
properties of the BGN membranes by a tensile tester (Instron5567,
USA). We soaked the BGN membranes in PBS and measured the
concentration of A-GNCs by a UV−vis spectrophotometer (UV2450,
Japan) at selected time intervals to evaluate the release behavior. We
incubated RAW264.7 cells (1 × 104 cells per well) with LPS (0.2 μg/
mL) and BC or BGN membranes at 37 °C, setting medium-alone as a
control group. After 24 h, we measured the absorbance at 540 nm to
test the content of NO by the Griess assay. We mixed the BC or BGN
membrane with a DPPH solution (0.25 mmol/L) for 20 min at room
temperature and measured the absorbance at 515 nm. The DPPH
clearance rate (CR) was calculated according to the following
formula: CR (%) = [1 −(A1 −A2)/A0] × 100%, where A0 represents
the absorbance of the blank solvent and DPPH solution, A1 represents
the absorbance of BC or the BGN and DPPH solution, and A2
represents the absorbance of BGN and the blank solvent, respectively.
Antibacterial Activity and Biocompatibility of BGN Mem-
branes. We used the Kirby−Bauer disk agar diﬀusion method to
evaluate the antibacterial eﬀect of BGN membranes. We spread a
bacteria suspension (1 × 106 CFU/mL) on an agar plate and put
BGN membranes on the surface for 24 h at 37 °C. We administrated
the inhibition zone around the disk specimens by ChemiDoc XRS+
(Bio-Rad, USA). We analyzed the hemolysis eﬀect of the BGN
membranes by measuring the OD567 nm according to the method
mentioned above. We cultured HUVECs on the BGN membranes to
evaluate cytotoxicity according to the method mentioned above.
Animal Model and Histological Analysis. We purchased 24
Sprague−Dawley rats (SPF grade, female, ∼200 g, HFK Bioscience
Co., Ltd.) to evaluate the eﬀect of antibacterial wound dressings. We
administered the full-thickness infection skin wounds (2 cm in
diameter) and divided the rats into two groups: a P. a infection group
(n = 12) and an MDR P. a infection group (n = 12) randomly. We
performed all the animal experiments according to the Animal Care
and Use Committee of National Center for Nanoscience and
Technology (approval number: 2018-jianglab-01). We injected
pelltobarbitalum natricum (Sigma, 30 mg/kg) in the abdominal
cavity to anesthetize the animals. We infected the full-thickness
wounds at the back of each rat with pathogenic bacteria (108 CFU/
mL, 200 μL) for 30 min and covered the wounds with BC or BGN
membranes. We recorded the healing condition and the residues of A-
GNCs in the BGN membranes. After wiping the skin around the
wound with alcohol, we removed the covered dressing on the wound
and rinsed the wound three times with saline to remove the necrotic
tissues and recovered the wound with a new sterile dressing for the
following treatments. We euthanized the rats on the third, seventh,
and 14th day and excised the wound tissue. We ﬁxed the wound
tissues in 4% PFA for 24 h and embedded them in paraﬃn, cross-
sectioned them, and stained the tissues for histological analysis.
Meanwhile, we collected blood samples for the routine blood tests.
We centrifuged the blood at 4000 rpm for 10 min to obtain the serum
sample to test the levels of the biomarkers for liver function, kidney
function, and immunological reactions. We homogenated each
specimen in 1 mL of tissue lysis solutions (20 mM Tris-CI, pH =
7.4, 1 mM EDTA) on ice for 30 min and mixed it with 1 mL of SDS-
PAGE (sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis)
loading buﬀer. We centrifuged the mixture at 12 000 rpm for 15 min
and removed the insoluble precipitates. We used SDS gel electro-
phoresis to separate all the freshly prepared collagen and electro-
transferred it onto PVDF membranes in a buﬀer (3.0 g of Tris, 14.4 g
of Gly, 200 mL of M−OH, in 1 L of deionized water) at 400 mA for 4
h. We blocked the membranes with blocking buﬀer containing TBST
(20 mM Tris-HCI, pH 7.40, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Tween-20, 5% nonfat
dry milk) for 1 h at 4 °C. A 1:300 dilution of diﬀerent primary
antibody (Abcam) was added, and the sample was incubated
overnight at 4 °C, followed by treatment with a 1:1000 dilution of
HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody for 1 h at
37 °C. We dipped the sections into xylene for 15 min and ethanol for
5 min. We heated the sections in citric acid (pH = 6.0) for 15 min and
placed the sections in PBS (pH = 7.4) on a shaker for 15 min. We
placed the sections in 3% hydrogen peroxide and incubated them at
room temperature in the dark for 25 min. We added 3% BSA to cover
the sections and sealed them for 30 min at room temperature. We
added primary antibody overnight at 4 °C and covered the sections
with secondary antibody at room temperature for 50 min. We put the
sections in newly prepared DAB color developing solution and
counterstained them with hematoxylin stain solution for 3 min. We

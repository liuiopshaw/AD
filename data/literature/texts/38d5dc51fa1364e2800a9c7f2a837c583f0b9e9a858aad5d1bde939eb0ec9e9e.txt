Gold Nanoclusters Hot Paper
Cluster From Cluster: A Quantitative Approach to Magic Gold
Nanoclusters [Au25(SR)18]
Zhen Lei, Jiao-Jiao Li, Zi-Ang Nan, Zhan-Guo Jiang,* and Quan-Ming Wang*
Abstract: High-yield and large-scale synthesis are highly
demanded for the studies of gold nanoclusters. We developed
a “cluster from cluster” approach to assemble gold nano-
clusters with preformed atomically precise Au13 precursors.
This facile approach has proved to be very effective in the
synthesis of the well-known magic cluster [Au25(SR)18], which
could prepare the target cluster in high yield (overall yield up to
 100%) at large scale (gram-scale based on gold). This
method can be applied in the synthesis of 10 Au25 clusters with
different R groups. This work presents an important approach
that may be extended to high-yield and large-scale synthesis of
other metal nanoclusters.
Recent years have witnessed significant advances in con-
trolled synthesis of gold nanoparticles with atomic precision.
The ultrasmall gold nanoparticles in the quantum size regime,
also known as nanoclusters, exhibit molecular-like behaviour
and show intriguing structural diversity,[1–3] catalytic activi-
ties,[4–14] magnetism[15–18] and luminescence.[19–25] As more and
more gold nanoclusters are known, an obstacle presented in
front of synthetic chemists is that the yields of these clusters
are not high enough for practical applications. It is very
desirable to develop new synthetic methods to prepare gold
nanoclusters in high yield and at large scale.
Up to now, the most extensively studied gold nanocluster
is the magic cluster [Au25(SR)18] (Au25).[26] It was not only
due to its well-defined molecular structure and good stability,
but also its interesting properties and potential applications.
For example, Au25 was found to be a highly active catalyst in
hydrogenation of a,b-unsaturated ketones,[4] alkynes[8] and
aromatic aldehydes.[9] It also exhibits intense photolumines-
cence and electrochemiluminescence in the visible to near-
infrared region.[19,22,23,27] Interesting electrochemical, mag-
netic and chiral properties, and applications in sensing and
biotherapy were also reported.[16,27–30]
Although Au25 is thermodynamically stable, its synthetic
yield was reported  10% in 2004.[31] The yield was raised up
to 40% with a size-focusing procedure in 2008.[32] The size-
focusing synthesis of Au25 requires precise control of the
temperature and the stirring speed to achieve proper size
distribution of precursors. More recently, hydride doped
precursors were used to synthesize thiolate or alkynyl-
protected Au25 nanoclusters. A series of Au25 and Pt/Pd
doped Au25 can be obtained in high yields (up to  50% based
on gold) at large synthetic scale (  200 mg).[33,34] In addition,
efforts have been made to realize high yield preparation of
water-soluble Au25.[35] Nevertheless, in consideration of syn-
thetic ease and cost, a facile method to prepare Au25 in very
high yield (ideally quantitative) and at large scale is desired.
Herein, we report a “cluster from cluster” approach to
quantitatively synthesize [Au25(SR)18] nanoclusters with
a preformed atomically precise Au13 precursor (Scheme 1).
It should be noted that the synthetic yield of Au13 precursor is
 100%, which is the key for the very high overall yield of
Au25. Our facile approach involves the direct reduction of
a mixture of Au13 nanoclusters and polymeric (AuSR)x
complexes at room temperature in the air. This approach
affords very high yield (up to 100%) and large scale (gram-
scale) synthesis of [Au25(SR)18] clusters, and it is applicable
for 10 examples of [Au25(SR)18] with various R groups. The
development of such a facile method will facilitate the study
of gold cluster chemistry and nanoscience.

The synthetic concept is based on the anatomy of [Au25-
(SR)18]. The structure can be divided into a Au13 core and 6
[Au2(SR)3] staples. In order to rationally synthesize [Au25-
(SR)18], it is ideal to react a preformed Au13 precursor cluster
with [Au2(SR)3] complexes. The yield of Au25 will largely
depend on that of the Au13 precursor, therefore a high yield
synthetic method for Au13 is needed. Our strategy was
inspired by the finding by Konishi et al[36,37] that the formation
of [Au13(dppp)4Cl4]Cl (dppp = 1,3-bis(diphenylphosphine)-
propane) could be induced by hydrochloric acid. We use
a
carboxylate-functionalized
phosphine
ligand
P(CH2CH2COOH)3 (L) in the form of a hydrochloride salt
to realize the high yield synthesis of Au13, which was used in
due course for the synthesis of Au25.
Typically,
tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine
hydrochloride
L·HCl (1.0 mmol, 286.6 mg)
and Me2SAuCl
(1.0 mmol,
295.0 mg) were dissolved in a solution of CH2Cl2 (20 mL)
and MeOH (20 mL) under stirring. After the solution turned
clear, 10 mL freshly prepared ethanol solution of NaBH4
(1.1 mmol, 40.0 mg) was added. The resulting solution
turned dark red immediately. After the mixture was stirred
overnight in the dark, the solvents were rotary evaporated.
Then the residual solid was washed with a mixture of ether/
hexane (1:1, v:v; 5 mL  3) to afford a red solid with  100%
yield. MS data (Figure 1a) confirms that the product is
[Au13(L’)8Cl4]Cl (1·Cl), L’ = P(CH2CH2CO2CH3)3. It was
found that L was transformed to L’ as a result of methyl
esterization, because the reaction was run in methanol under
basic conditions.
Single-crystal X-ray structure determination[38] of 1 was
successfully conducted with its hexafluoroantimonate salt
[Au13(L’)8Cl4]SbF6 (1·SbF6). As shown in Figure 1b, 1 consists
a typical icosahedral Au13 core capped by eight phosphines
and four chlorides. Identical experimental isotope patterns in
ESI-MS spectra confirmed that 1·Cl and 1·SbF6 have the same
cationic part (Figures 1a and S1). The absorption peaks at
about 340 and 430 nm (Figure 1c) correspond with that of
[Au13(dppp)4Cl4]Cl.[37]
The second step is reacting 1·Cl with freshly prepared
polymeric thiolate-gold complexes. Specifically, Me2SAuCl
(0.1 mmol, 29.5 mg) was dissolved in CH2Cl2 (5 mL) and
MeOH (5 mL), which was followed by sequential addition of
thiolate (2,4-dichlorothiophenol, 0.1 mmol) and triethyla-
mine (0.1 mmol). The emergence of a white floc confirms
the formation of polymeric thiolate-gold complexes. Then the
suspension was stirred for about 5 min, filtered, and washed
with MeOH (5 mL). Mix the (AuSR)x with 1·Cl (7.7 
103 mmol) and thiolate (2,4-dichlorothiophenol, 0.05 mmol)
in CH2Cl2 (5 mL) and MeOH (5 mL), and a 1 mL ethanol
solution of NaBH4 (4.0  102 mmol, 1.5 mg) was dropwise
added. The pale red suspensions turned dark and clear at
once. After it was allowed to stir overnight with exclusion of
light, the solution was evaporated to dryness. The resulted
solid was washed with water and a mixture of ether/hexane
(1:1, v:v). After dried in vacuum overnight at room temper-
ature, products were obtained as a dark solid with 100%
yield.

As shown in Figure 3a, as-synthesized products of Na·3-
Na·10 show absorption bands at about 440 and 690 nm, which
are fingerprints of spherical Au25 nanoclusters. In the case of
Na·11, the peak at longer wavelength slightly shifted, and
a new peak emerged at about 470 nm. This is because
naphthalene group with larger p system is more capable of
stabilizing the electrons of sulfur atom through p-conjugation,
which leads to increase of the HOMO–LUMO gap of the gold
nanoclusters.[42] The structure of 11 is almost identical to the
one with 1-naphthalenethiolate as reported previously.[42] The
Au–Au (kernel), S–Au (kernel) and S–Au (staple) distances
in Na·11 in the range of 2.7553(19)–3.070(2), 2.387(11)–
2.446(10) and 2.152(13)–2.336(15) , are comparable to those
values 2.784, 2.400 and 2.306  as found in (TOA)[Au25(S-1-
NAP)18] (TOA = tetraoctylammonium), respectively.[42]
Moreover, we checked if this approach works for alka-
nethiols such as 2-phenylethanethiol and cyclohexanethiol.
The results indicated that the use of 2-phenylethanethiol led
to the formation of a mixture of Au20(SR)13 and Au33(SR)20,
and no spherical or biicosahedral Au25 was found (Fig-
ure S13). While in the case of cyclohexanethiol, spherical Au25
could be obtained but mixed with other species such as
Au34(SR)24 (Figure S15).
In this cluster-from-cluster approach, the high synthetic
yield is mainly attributed to the monodispersity of starting
materials Au13. Subsequently, mixtures of monodisperse Au13
and Au–thiolate oligomers leads to the formation of relatively
pure Au25 products. It is noted that Leong et al.[43] observed
the transformation from stibine-protected cluster [Au13-
(SbPh3)8(Cl)4]Cl to Au25(SG)18 via a ligand exchange with
excess GSH. Unfortunately, the synthetic yield of stabine-
stabilized Au13 precursor is not very high (30%). This
approach is different from the top-down approaches, includ-
ing the size focusing protocol.[44] It is also noted that Au25
protected by aromatic thiolates were synthesized by Li et al.
via polydisperse Aun(PPh3)m nanoparticles as precursors, but
the yield was relatively low (< 10%).[45]
In conclusion, we develop a “cluster from cluster”
synthetic approach to obtain monodisperse gold nanoclusters
based on the high yield prepared Au13 cluster precursor. This
facile approach has several advantages: it gives very high
synthetic yield, it can be used for large scale preparation, and
it can be applied in the synthesis of various Au25. We
anticipate that this approach may be extended to high yield
and large scale synthesis of other metal nanoclusters including
those protected by alkynyl ligands.[46] High yield and large
scale synthesis of homometallic and heterometallic nano-
clusters with protecting ligands other than thiolates is under
investigation.

A B S T R A C T
Skin wound infections caused by multidrug-resistant pathogens remain a significant clinical challenge due to the 
increasing prevalence of antibiotic resistance. Chitosan-modified manganese dioxide nanoparticles (CS-MnO₂ 
NPs) facilitate the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), acting as a nanozyme, while exhibiting inherent 
biocompatibility; however, uncontrolled release of ROS can lead to tissue toxicity, thereby limiting their ther­
apeutic utility. In this study, we present a biohybrid antibacterial platform (CS-MnO₂@LGG) developed by 
anchoring CS-MnO₂ NPs onto the probiotic Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG). In this “bacteria-against-bacteria” 
system, LGG secretes lactic acid and H₂O₂, creating a mildly acidic, oxidative microenvironment that precisely 
modulates the catalytic activity of the nanozyme, while simultaneously inhibiting pathogens through nutrient 
deprivation and ecological niche exclusion. In vitro studies demonstrated that CS-MnO₂@LGG achieved a syn­
ergistic bactericidal effect of 99.60% against Escherichia coli (E. coil) and 99.45% against Staphylococcus aureus 
(S. aureus), surpassing the efficacy of either component used alone. When incorporated into a hydrogel ointment, 
the biohybrid maintained long-term LGG viability, supporting short-term localized antibacterial activity without 
cytotoxicity. In vivo, CS-MnO₂@LGG effectively accelerated the healing of S. aureus-infected wounds through the 
synergistic action of catalytic and probiotic effects, although its application may be limited in immunocom­
promised individuals. Overall, this antibiotic-free strategy offers a safe and efficient approach for managing 
wound infections.
1. Introduction
Bacterial infections caused by multidrug-resistant pathogens are an 
escalating global health concern, often resulting in prolonged inflam­
mation, impaired tissue regeneration, and high treatment failure rates 
[1,2]. The declining efficacy of conventional antibiotics, primarily 
attributed to bacterial defense mechanisms such as biofilm formation, 
efflux pump activation, and adaptive resistance, has intensified the 
search for alternative antimicrobial strategies [3,4]. In recent years, 
various alternative approaches have been investigated, including the use 
of antimicrobial peptides, metal-based nanomaterials, photodynamic 
therapy, and bacteriophage treatment [5–7]. These modalities offer 
distinct mechanisms of action that circumvent traditional drug- 
resistance pathways, such as membrane disruption, catalytic reactive 
oxygen species (ROS) generation, or bacterial lysis, and are often more 
adaptable to the evolving complexity of infection microenvironments 
[8].
Nanozymes, nanomaterials with enzyme-like catalytic activity, have 
emerged as promising antibacterial agents due to their structural 
tunability, ROS-generating capacity, and biofilm penetration [9–13]. 
Chitosan-modified manganese dioxide nanozymes (CS-MnO₂ NPs) 
exhibit peroxidase- and oxidase-like activities that enable ROS-mediated 
bacterial destruction. However, excessive or uncontrolled ROS release 
can damage host tissues, and many nanozymes show limited adapt­
ability to the dynamic microenvironments of infected wounds, restrict­
ing clinical translation [14,15].
Analogous to the role of maggot therapy in promoting wound healing 
through necrotic tissue debridement and pathogen suppression, pro­
biotics exert antibacterial effects by modulating the local micro­
ecological balance and inhibiting the proliferation of pathogenic 
microorganisms [16]. Inspired by natural symbiotic defense systems, 
probiotics such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus (LGG) have emerged as potent

living antimicrobials with the capacity to act synergistically with con­
ventional therapies [17–19]. LGG stands out due to its strong epithelial 
adhesion, lactic acid production, and controlled hydrogen peroxide 
(H₂O₂) release [20], which collectively inhibit pathogens, disrupt 
quorum sensing, and support wound healing through immune modula­
tion [21]. Its antibacterial effects arise from lactic acid secretion, which 
acidifies the microenvironment; H₂O₂ generation, which induces 
oxidative stress; and competitive exclusion of pathogens through 
nutrient and adhesion site competition [22–24]. Importantly, the ac­
tivity of ROS-generating nanozymes can be modulated by the controlled 
metabolic output of LGG. Through gradual H₂O₂ release and microen­
vironment acidification, LGG enables on-demand activation of nano­
zyme catalysis while preventing uncontrolled ROS bursts that could 
cause tissue toxicity or nonspecific damage. This symbiotic interplay 
establishes a bacteria-guided, self-regulating antibacterial system that 
balances efficacy with safety, overcomes a key limitation of conven­
tional high-activity nanozymes, and advances the ‘bacteria-against- 
bacteria’ concept toward practical clinical application.
The synergistic strategy involving nanozymes and probiotics has 
been extensively explored in recent studies [21,25,26]. For example, Li 
et al. prepared AgAu@CeO₂, which, when combined with Lactobacillus 
reuteri, exhibits synergistic antibacterial effects through the photo­
thermal properties and acid production of the probiotics [27]. Wei et al. 
developed rGO@FeS₂ nanozymes that work synergistically with Lacto­
bacillus to combat Candida [28]. Additionally, Chang et al. developed 
multifunctional BL@DNA@Fe-Cur for the treatment of inflammatory 
bowel disease [29]. Herein, we employ CS-MnO₂ NPs with enzyme-like 
activity in combination with LGG, which produces acid and H₂O₂. Due to 
the acid-resistant nature of LGG, it progressively enhances the enzyme- 
like activity of CS-MnO₂ NPs while modulating the microenvironment, 
thereby acute antibacterial effects and minimizing damage to sur­
rounding healthy cells.
In this study, we designed a dual-functional antibacterial strategy by 
integrating catalytically active CS-MnO₂ NPs with LGG, forming a living 
probiotic-nanozyme hybrid (CS-MnO₂@LGG), as illustrated in Scheme 
1. In this construct, CS-MnO₂ NPs are stably anchored onto the nega­
tively charged LGG surface via multiple noncovalent interactions, 
including electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic 
forces, resulting in a robust nano-bio interface. This configuration en­
ables synergistic ROS generation through the catalytic activity of CS- 
MnO₂ NPs and the metabolic function of LGG, which continuously 
produces H₂O₂ and lactic acid to maintain a mildly oxidative and acidic 
microenvironment. Furthermore, CS-MnO₂ NPs not only catalyze ROS 
generation but also induce oxidative damage by depleting intracellular 
glutathione (GSH), a key bacterial antioxidant defense molecule, 
thereby sensitizing pathogens to oxidative stress [30]. This microenvi­
ronment enhances nanozyme catalytic efficiency and overcomes the 
inherent antibacterial limitations of LGG, resulting in direct growth in­
hibition of pathogens. In vitro, CS-MnO₂@LGG exhibited potent syner­
gistic antibacterial activity, achieving antibacterial efficiencies of 
99.24% against Escherichia coli (E. coli) and 99.77% against Staphylo­
coccus aureus (S. aureus). To facilitate translational application, the 
antibacterial material was incorporated into a hydrogel-based topical 
ointment, which enhanced wound adherence, moisture retention, and 
handling convenience while maintaining the structural and functional 
integrity of both components. The antibacterial performance of this 
composite ointment was evaluated in a S. aureus-infected murine skin 
wound model by examining bacterial morphological changes at the 
microstructural level. This approach harnesses the complementary ad­
vantages of living probiotics and inorganic nanozymes to improve acute 
antibacterial efficacy, biosafety, and wound-regenerative capacity, of­
fering a promising antibiotic-free strategy for infected wound manage­
ment. Although significant antibacterial activity and biocompatibility 
were demonstrated in vitro, further studies are warranted to address 
challenges in stability, scalability, and safety before clinical translation.

2. Experimental section
2.1. Preparation of CS-MnO₂ NPs and CS-MnO₂@LGG
Add 50 mg of bovine serum albumin and 0.1 g of water-soluble 
chitosan to a 100 mL solution of KMnO4 at a concentration of 2 mM. 
Then, slowly add a 100 mL solution of MnCl2⋅4H2O at a concentration of 
3 mM to the mixture at room temperature while continuously stirring 
with a magnetic stirrer. Afterward, the brown precipitate impurities are 
then separated from the solution by centrifugation. Finally, filter the 
upper layer of the brown, clear liquid through a 0.22 μm membrane 
filter. The CS-MnO₂ NPs solution was used at an approximate concen­
tration of 2.5 mM (in terms of Mn atoms), based on precursor input.
In the synthesis of CS-MnO₂@LGG, LGG was initially cultured in 
DeMan, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) medium, progressing from the late- 
log phase to the stationary phase. The culture was then centrifuged at 
3420 ×g for 5 min to collect the exponentially growing bacteria, which 
were subsequently re-suspended in PBS buffer. A 5% (w/v) chitosan 
solution was added to the bacterial suspension, and the mixture was 
incubated at 37 ◦C for 20 min to facilitate the adherence of chitosan to 
the bacterial surface, thereby forming a protective chitosan layer. A total 
of 20% (v/v) CS-MnO₂ NPs were incorporated into the mixture and 
stirred for 15 min to ensure uniform dispersion, specifically adding 2 mL 
of the CS-MnO₂ NPs solution to 10 mL of the LGG solution with an op­
tical density (OD) of 0.8. After centrifugation, the CS-MnO₂@LGG 
complex was re-suspended in PBS buffer (0.1 M PBS, 10 mL) for sub­
sequent applications. Meanwhile, the LGG unloaded with CS-MnO₂ NPs 
was retained for the subsequent comparative experiments.
2.2. Antibacterial effect of CS-MnO₂ NPs and CS-MnO₂@LGG composite
The antibacterial efficacy of the CS-MnO₂ NPs composite was eval­
uated using both a 96-well microplate assay and a colony counting 
method. E. coli (ATCC 25922) and S. aureus (ATCC 29213) were first 
inoculated into LB broth and cultured at 37 ◦C with shaking at 8 ×g for 
24 h. After incubation, the bacterial cells were harvested by centrifu­
gation, resuspended in sterile normal saline, and diluted to an initial 
concentration of approximately 1 × 107 CFU/mL. A 20 μL bacterial 
suspension was combined with 40 μL of CS-MnO₂ NPs solution at 
varying concentrations and incubated at 37 ◦C with shaking for one 
hour. Following incubation, 20 μL of the bacterial suspension was 
transferred to 180 μL of pre-mixed LB medium and incubated in a 96- 
well plate for 12 h to assess bacterial growth. Meanwhile, 20 μL of the 
bacterial culture was evenly spread onto LB agar plates and incubated at 
37 ◦C for 24 h, followed by further visual inspection to evaluate the 
antibacterial activity of the CS-MnO₂ NPs.
A 20 μL bacterial suspension containing E. coli or S. aureus was mixed 
with 10–50 μL of the CS-MnO₂@LGG solution (OD = 0.8). The resulting 
mixture was then diluted to a final volume of 200 μL using PBS and 
incubated at 37 ◦C with shaking for 2 h. Simultaneously, 20 μL of the 
incubated mixture was evenly spread onto MacConkey agar (MAC) or 
Baird-Parker agar plates and cultured at 37 ◦C for 24 h (Initial anti­
bacterial plate). In the meantime, 20 μL of the mixture was added to 180 
μL of pre-mixed LB1/MRS1 medium in a 96-well plate and incubated for 
an additional 24 h. The antibacterial activity was further quantified by 
counting the number of CFUs. The specific procedure is as follows: after 
culturing the mixture in a 96-well plate for 24 h, the contents of the wells 
were centrifuged and diluted to an OD value of 0.1. After incubation, 20 
μL aliquots were plated on selective agar plates to evaluate the final 
antibacterial effect: MAC for E. coli and Baird-Parker agar for S. aureus 
(Final antibacterial plate).
2.3. In vitro cytotoxicity assay
RS1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 
37 ◦C in a humidified incubator. The following day, treatment CS-MnO₂ 
NPs at concentrations of 125, 250, and 500 μM were added to the wells, 
and the cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. After treatment, the 
cells were gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and a 
Cell Counting Kit-8 was added to each well. The plates were then 
incubated at 37 ◦C for 2 h to assess cell viability. Absorbance was 
measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability (%) was 
calculated using the formula: Cell viability (%) = (Aₜ / A₀) × 100, where 
Aₜ is the absorbance of the treated group, and A₀ is the absorbance of the 
untreated control group. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.
2.4. Topical antibacterial ointment system
This antibacterial ointment is centered around the active strain LGG, 
which exhibits antibacterial properties in conjunction with CS-MnO₂ 
NPs to exert a synergistic effect on inhibition. Supplemented with 0.5% 
glucose and 0.1–0.2% yeast extract (10 mL, 0.1 M PBS), the formulation 
provides metabolic substrates for lactic acid bacteria, thereby enhancing 
their acid production and hydrogen peroxide capabilities. The base 
consists of a mixture of 1–2% gelatin, 0.3–0.5% carbomer, and 2–5% 
glycerin, creating a mild moisturizing colloidal environment charac­
terized by good adhesion and sustained-release effects. The pH is 
adjusted to 6.0–6.5 using sodium hydroxide and sodium lactate. To 
prevent contamination from external bacteria, a very low concentration 
(<0.05%) of potassium sorbate is added, and the sample is sterilized in 
an autoclave at 121 ◦C for 20 min. Meanwhile, CS-MnO₂@LGG (adding 
2 mL of the CS-MnO₂ NPs solution to 10 mL of LGG suspension with an 
OD value of 0.8 and then centrifuging) was performed by adding the 
aforementioned sterilized solution.
2.5. In vivo antibacterial assessment
The short-term antibacterial efficacy of probiotic-synergized nano­
zymes based on CS-MnO₂@LGG was evaluated using a S. aureus-infected 
wound model. Male mice (6–8 weeks old, 26–34 g) were used to 
establish full-thickness dorsal skin wounds (0.5 × 0.5 cm). A total of 40 
μL of an S. aureus suspension (1 × 106 CFU) was applied to each wound 
and incubated for 24 h to establish infection. The mice were then 
randomly assigned to four treatment groups: (I) PBS-treated control; (II) 
ointment containing CS-MnO₂ NPs; (III) ointment containing LGG; (IV) 
ointment formulated with CS-MnO₂@LGG (V); and (V) positive contrast 
Mupirocin (2%). Wound area and body weight were monitored and 
recorded daily. Hair around the wound area was trimmed as needed to 
ensure consistent image acquisition. After 13 days of treatment, the mice 
were euthanized, and wound tissues were harvested and fixed in 4% 
paraformaldehyde for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Each 
experimental group included 6 mice. This sample size was based on 
previously published murine infected wound models with comparable 
endpoints and variability and was considered sufficient to detect inter­
group differences while avoiding unnecessary animal use [31,32]. Prior 
to treatment, animals were randomly numbered and assigned to groups 
to minimize allocation bias. Wound healing progression was recorded 
for all animals; representative images are presented, while quantitative 
analyses were conducted using data from all mice. To further reduce 
potential observer-related bias, wound area measurements were per­
formed using predefined evaluation criteria under standardized image 
acquisition and analysis procedures applied consistently across all 
groups. Investigators involved in outcome assessment were blinded to 
group allocation during data quantification. All animal experiments 
were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care 
and use of laboratory animals at Shandong University and were 
approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the School of Pharmacy, 
Shandong University (Approval No. 20048).

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Characterization of CS-MnO₂ NPs nanozyme and CS-MnO₂@LGG
The synthesis of CS-MnO₂ NPs is schematically illustrated in Fig. 1a. 
Nanoparticles were prepared via a one-step, room-temperature stirring 
process, followed by filtration to yield a grey-brown liquid. X-ray 
diffraction (XRD, Fig. 1b) displayed broad peaks between 20◦and 30◦, 
indicative of an amorphous chitosan matrix, along with sharp reflections 
at 36.7◦and 65.6◦corresponding to the (100) and (110) planes of MnO₂, 
confirming the formation of a nanocrystalline phase [33]. Transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM, Fig. 1c) revealed irregularly agglomerated 
particles encapsulated by a low-contrast carbon shell derived from chi­
tosan, in contrast to unmodified MnO₂ NPs, which formed aggregated 
nanosheets with primary sizes of 40–50 nm (Fig. S1a, b). Elemental 
mapping confirmed the presence of Mn, C, N, and O in CS-MnO₂ NPs and 
demonstrated a uniform distribution of C and N surrounding Mn, indi­
cating successful and homogeneous chitosan functionalization, as 
illustrated in Fig. 1d. This is expected to improve dispersion stability and 
interfacial biocompatibility. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra 
further verified chitosan incorporation. Characteristic CS peaks were 
observed at 3467 cm−1, assigned to –NH₂ and –OH stretching vibrations, 
and at 2927 cm−1 and 2883 cm−1, attributed to –CH₃ and –CH₂/–CH 
stretching [34]. The amide I (C=O) and amide II (N–H) bands appeared 
at 1634 cm−1 and 1524 cm−1, respectively [35]. Additionally, while C- 
O-C asymmetric stretching bands at 1162 cm−1 and 1073 cm−1 were 
ascribed to pyranose sugar units [36]. Comparative analysis confirmed 
that both CS and CS-MnO₂ NPs retained the characteristic chitosan ab­
sorption peaks, indicating that functional groups remained intact during 
synthesis. Collectively, these results confirm the successful synthesis of 
CS-MnO₂ NPs with uniform chitosan coating, preserving bioactive 
functional groups that enhance material biocompatibility and provide a 
favorable interface for subsequent LGG immobilization.

3.2. Characterization and properties of CS-MnO₂@LGG
Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) encompass a diverse range of species, 
among which LGG is notable for its high production of H₂O₂ and lactic 
acid. This characteristic underlies its antibacterial efficacy and is 
contingent upon its proliferation [38]. Consequently, we conducted a 
detailed analysis of the metabolic pathways of LGG. Previous studies 
have demonstrated that LGG exhibits inhibitory activity against skin 
pathogens, particularly Staphylococcus aureus, and possesses adhesive 
properties that facilitate wound colonization [39]. Accordingly, LGG 
was selected as our model strain. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of 
native LGG reveals elongated, rod-shaped cells, some of which are ar­
ranged in short chains, with smooth surfaces that lack protrusions, as 
illustrated in Fig. 2a. In contrast, SEM images of CS-MnO₂@LGG show 
abundant irregular surface-bound particles or aggregates, while pre­
serving the characteristic rod morphology and showing no evidence of 
cell swelling (Fig. 2b), suggesting minimal morphological impact of 
nanozyme loading. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) map­
ping confirms Mn presence on the bacterial surface, with an average Mn 
content of 8.62% across three measurements, as depicted in Fig. 2c. The 
antibacterial activity of CS-MnO₂@LGG primarily arises from two as­
pects: first, the enzyme-mimicking properties of CS-MnO₂ NPs; second, 
the proliferation capacity of LGG and the synergistic effects of its 
metabolic products, particularly lactic acid and hydrogen peroxide, 
which can be likened to an armed enhancement of LGG. We systemati­
cally evaluated the impact of CS-MnO₂ NPs on LGG. Following nano­
particle loading, transmission electron microscopy confirmed that LGG 
retained normal binary fission (TEM, Fig. 2d). This approach signifi­
cantly enhances the antibacterial effect against harmful bacteria by 
combining a single LGG (probiotics) with nanozymes, thereby fully 
leveraging the advantages of each component.
To quantify tolerance, LGG was incubated in PBS containing CS- 
MnO₂ NPs at concentrations up to 416 μM (20% v/v). As the CS-MnO₂ 
NPs concentration increased to 416 μM, LGG viability declined to 
approximately 72.35%, demonstrating substantial resistance to the 
nanozymes (Fig. 2e). Notably, the chitosan coating mitigated the

3.3. Oxidase-like and peroxidase-like activity of CS-MnO₂ NPs
Oxidase-like catalytic activity of CS-MnO₂ NPs was evaluated using 
TMB as the chromogenic probe and monitored by UV–visible spectros­
copy. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, the CS-MnO2 NPs exhibit maximum ac­
tivity in oxidizing TMB in a lactic acid‑sodium lactate buffer at a pH of 
6.0. Furthermore, their oxidase-like performance is further enhanced in 
a lower pH (3.0–8.0) acidic environment (Fig. S4). In consideration of 
the subsequent antibacterial environment requirements, all buffers are 
selected to have a pH value of 6.0. Wound environments typically pre­
sent elevated pH due to bacterial colonization; however, LGG prolifer­
ation and lactic acid secretion can acidify the local microenvironment, 
simultaneously suppressing pathogens and augmenting nanozyme 
catalysis. At 250 μM, CS-MnO₂ NPs effectively oxidized colorless TMB to 
its blue oxidized form (oxTMB), as evidenced by the characteristic ab­
sorption at 652 nm (Fig. 3b). Addition of low-dose H₂O₂ (5 μM) further 
improved catalytic turnover. Comparable substrate oxidation was 
observed with ABTS and OPD, as demonstrated in Fig. S5.
To further elucidate the underlying catalytic mechanism, electron 
paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was employed using spe­
cific spin-trapping agents, 2,6-dimethylpyridine N-oxide (DMPO) and 
2,2,6,6-Tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP), to detect ROS, particularly su­
peroxide anions (O₂•-), singlet oxygen (1O2), and hydroxyl radicals 
(⋅OH). As illustrated in Fig. 3c, distinct signals for O₂•- and 1O2 are 
observable, alongside a characteristic quartet signal corresponding to 
⋅OH, exhibiting an intensity ratio of 1:2:2:1. This observation confirms 
the generation of these ROS during the catalytic process. Based on the 
experimental results, we hypothesize that during the catalytic process, 
the nanozyme CS-MnO₂ NPs can not only utilize their oxidase-like ac­
tivity to decompose O₂ into O₂⋅−and 1O2 but also decompose H₂O₂ into 
⋅OH through their peroxidase-like activity. When H₂O₂ is employed as a 
substrate, CS-MnO₂ NPs exhibit significant peroxidase-like activity, 
evidenced by a Km value of only 0.227 mM and a high Vmax value of 
0.72*10−7 M/s (Fig. S6). This suggests that, in addition to their oxidase- 
like activity (Km = 0.185 mM), the peroxidase-like enzymatic behavior 
contributes to the formation of a synergistic enzyme system, thereby 
enhancing the efficiency of catalytic reactions and further augmenting 
the oxidase-like activity. The CS-MnO₂@LGG was first immersed in MRS 
solution and allowed to react. Then, a buffer solution with pH 6.0 and 
TMB chromogenic agent was added (Fig. 3d). As the incubation time 
increased (10–40 min), the concentrations of by-products (lactic acid 
and H₂O₂) gradually increased, enhancing the TMB color development 
reaction, as expected. In a buffer solution with a higher concentration of 
lactic acid (25 mM, pH 6.0), the intensity of the EPR signal (⋅OH) was 
notably enhanced (compared to 5 mM, pH 7.0). Furthermore, the EPR 
signal intensity of CS-MnO₂@LGG exhibited a marked increase after 
incubation in MRS medium compared to its intensity before incubation 
(Fig. S7).

Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 3e, the DTNB assay facilitates the 
quantitative detection of GSH based on its characteristic absorbance at 
412 nm, and the CS-MnO₂ NPs themselves do not interfere with the 
chromogenic reaction [40]. Consequently, a standard calibration curve

interaction between CS-MnO₂ NPs and GSH was systematically evalu­

and be reduced to Mn2+ under physiological conditions, demonstrating 
significant capacity for GSH consumption. This reaction is accompanied 
by a notable decrease in absorbance at 412 nm (Fig. 3f). Quantitative 
analysis revealed that at an initial GSH concentration of 600 μM, 
treatment with 62.5/100 μM CS-MnO₂ NPs for 30 min reduced the GSH

dose dependency, significantly compromising the bacterial antioxidant 
defense system. Moreover, the depletion of GSH may promote intra­
cellular ROS accumulation, leading to membrane lipid peroxidation and 
damage to biomacromolecules, thereby enhancing short-term antibac­
terial efficacy [41].
3.4. Antimicrobial test in vitro
SEM imaging revealed treatment-induced structural alterations in 
bacterial cell membranes, as illustrated in Fig. 4a. In the untreated 
control group, E. coli displayed typical rod morphology with pilus- 
covered surfaces, while S. aureus appeared as smooth spheres, and 
LGG retained intact rods with complete membranes. Treatment with 
H₂O₂ induced mild structural changes: the surfaces of E. coli became 
rough, S. aureus exhibited minor shape irregularities, and LGG remained 
largely intact. In the CS-MnO₂ NP group, nanoparticles adhered to all 
three strains; E. coli showed slight distortion with localized collapse, 
S. aureus surfaces were rough with blurred contours, while LGG dis­
played no marked shrinkage or deformation, indicating partial mem­
brane disruption. The combination of CS-MnO₂ NPs and H₂O₂ resulted in 
the most severe damage: E. coli cells were deformed, collapsed, or 
ruptured; S. aureus exhibited signs of disintegration; and LGG showed 
mild wrinkling. Consistently, live/dead staining indicated the highest 
TRITC red fluorescence for E. coli and S. aureus in the combination 
group, confirming a synergistic antibacterial effect (Fig. 4b).

Antibacterial activity was quantified against E. coli and S. aureus using a 
96-well plate assay in sodium lactate buffer (pH 6.0) (Fig. 4c). At a 
concentration of 300 μM CS-MnO₂ NPs, bactericidal rates reached 
32.59% and 31.85%, respectively. The addition of 80 μM H₂O₂- 
mimicking LGG-derived peroxide significantly enhanced killing rates to 
58.79% and 55.56% (Fig. 4d). Selective plating on MAC agar for E. coli 
and Baird-Parker agar for S. aureus confirmed reduced colony numbers 
in the combination group, while LGG did not grow on either medium 
(Fig. S9). Additionally, similar trends were observed in the plate assay 
shown in Fig. 4e (growth of E. coli and S. aureus under different treat­
ment conditions), where the combination of CS-MnO₂ NPs and H₂O₂ 
showed reduced bacterial growth, supporting its short-term antibacte­
rial effect.
Notably, this antibacterial strategy not only exhibits an immediate 
short-term antibacterial effect but also allows LGG to continue prolif­
erating in the novel LB culture medium. Through competitive inhibition 
and the secretion of metabolites such as lactic acid and H₂O₂, LGG forms 
a synergistic mechanism with CS-MnO₂ NPs, thereby achieving a sus­
tained antibacterial effect within an acute treatment window. Initially, 
we inoculated LGG into the skin wounds of mice to observe any occur­
rences of infection, ulceration, or delayed wound healing. A standard­
ized full-thickness circular wound (5 mm diameter) was created on the 
dorsal skin of anesthetized mice. A comparative experiment was con­
ducted in which 50 μL of LGG and S. aureus suspension (106 CFU/mL) 
were locally applied to each wound, alongside sterile saline as a control, 
as illustrated in Fig. 4f. Wounds were photographed daily under 
consistent conditions to evaluate closure rate and inspected for clinical 
signs of infection, including erythema, exudate, scabbing, and ulcera­
tion. During the 7-day observation period, the results indicated that the 
S. aureus infection group exhibited significant inflammatory responses 
and tissue damage throughout the healing process, leading to delayed 
wound healing. This section shows typical features of acute purulent 
skin inflammation with folliculitis and perifollicular abscess formation 
(Fig. S10). The lesions are predominantly neutrophilic, consistent with a 
S. aureus-induced skin response. In contrast, the LGG treatment group 
demonstrated milder inflammatory responses, with no abnormal 
inflammation or ulcer formation, faster scab formation, and a signifi­
cantly accelerated healing process. These findings confirm that topical 
LGG is biocompatible and does not delay tissue repair, providing a basis 
for subsequent nanozyme-loading safety studies.
Acute antibacterial activity can be divided into two phases: anti­
bacterial action in PBS (Stage I) and incubation in culture medium 
(Stage II), as depicted in Fig. 5a. The synergistic antibacterial effect of 
CS-MnO₂@LGG was evaluated through antibacterial experiments 
against E. coli and S. aureus. As shown in Fig. 5b and c, during the Stage I 
and Stage II of the experiment, the antibacterial effect of CS-MnO₂@LGG 
on E. coli was difficult to observe clearly, which was attributed to the 
small morphological differences between the two bacteria. In the MAC 
medium, the number of colonies of E. coli can be counted (Fig. 5d). 
Combined with the untreated E. coli plate in B.C., the results of the plate 
coating experiment demonstrate that an increase in the volume of CS- 
MnO₂@LGG to 50 μL leads to a gradual reduction in the number of E. coli 
during both the initial and subsequent stages of antibacterial activity. 
The antibacterial effect of CS-MnO₂@LGG is significantly enhanced 
compared to the addition of only LGG. Under identical dosage condi­
tions, the LGG group exhibited a reduction of 0.80-log10 reduction (95% 
CI, 0.75–0.83; n = 3) during the subsequent antibacterial phase 
compared to the control group, achieving a bactericidal efficiency of 
84.32% (Fig. 5e). Following the addition of 50 μL of CS-MnO₂@LGG, a 
reduction of 2.40-log10 reduction (95% CI, 2.18–2.59; n = 3) units was 
observed during the Stage II compared to the control group, resulting in 
a bactericidal rate of 99.60%. Notably, in Stage II, the centrifuged pre­
cipitate from a single E. coli culture appeared light yellow (Fig. 5f). As 
the volume of CS-MnO₂@LGG increased, the precipitate gradually 
transitioned to white, indicating an upward trend that supports the 
previously mentioned synergistic antibacterial effect.
The antibacterial effect of CS-MnO₂@LGG against S. aureus was 
particularly pronounced during Stage II. As illustrated in Fig. 6a and b, 
increasing the usage volume of CS-MnO₂@LGG to 50 μL led to a gradual 
decrease in the number of S. aureus, which ultimately disappeared 
completely in Stage II. The Baird-Parker agar plate results show the 
colony counts of S. aureus during Stage I and Stage II. In comparison to 
the colony count of S. aureus in the B.C., following the addition of 50 μL 
CS-MnO₂@LGG, in Stage II, the reduction increased to 2.32-log10 
reduction (95% CI, 2.15–2.50; n = 3), resulting in a bactericidal rate of 
99.45% (Fig. 6c), which is stronger than the LGG group. Similar to the 
anti-E. coli group, the centrifugation precipitate from the single S. aureus 
group was yellow. As the volume of CS-MnO₂@LGG increased, the 
precipitate gradually turned white in Fig. 6d, indicating a clear trend 
that reflects CS-MnO₂@LGG antibacterial efficacy. We verified the 
growth of LGG in both MRS and the mixed medium LB1/MRS1, con­
firming normal proliferation in LB1/MRS1. Similarly, E. coli and S. aureus 
grew normally in both LB and LB1/MRS1. Absorbance measurements at 
600 nm revealed that the growth of these pathogenic bacteria in the 
mixed medium was 107.56–115.85% higher than in the single medium, 
indicating that the mixed medium does not hinder bacterial growth 
(Fig. S11a-c). Compared to other antibacterial materials (Table S1), 
these antibacterial test results provide a theoretical basis for subsequent 
experiments involving mouse wounds.
To evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of CS-MnO₂@LGG against 
antibiotic-resistant bacteria, we selected Methicillin-resistance Staphylo­
coccus aureus (MRSA) as the representative pathogenic bacterium for our 
study. As illustrated in Fig. S12a-b, MRSA was still abundant during 
Stage I; however, in the second phase, SEM observations indicated that 
MRSA had nearly completely disappeared. These findings were further 
corroborated by Baird-Parker agar plate experiments (Fig. S12c-d). A 
significant number of black MRSA colonies were observed in the control 
group, while the samples treated with CS-MnO₂@LGG showed almost 
complete disappearance in Stage II. The analysis of the plate colony 
counts indicated that the survival rate of MRSA was 1.48%, as illustrated 
in Fig. S12e, reflecting the antibacterial efficacy of CS-MnO₂@LGG.

3.5. Accelerated in vivo wound healing by CS-MnO₂@LGG unguent
Wound healing effects were compared between the experimental and 
control groups. The CS-MnO₂@LGG hydrogel ointment was applied 
uniformly to mouse dorsal wounds each morning and stored at −4 ◦C to 
preserve both probiotic viability and nanozyme activity. As mentioned 
above in Fig. 7a, the prepared CS-MnO₂@LGG ointment was uniformly 
applied to the surface of the mouse wounds. This method is similar to 
enhancing the antibacterial activity of LGG by arming it with nanozymes 
and further improving its antimicrobial efficacy by leveraging the nu­
merical advantage of LGG. Considering the morphological similarities 
between E. coli and LGG, subsequent acute antibacterial experiments in 
mice focused on S. aureus as the subject of investigation.
Biocompatibility is essential for the antibacterial application of the 
composite ointment. The in vitro hemocompatibility assay demon­
strated that Triton-treated red blood cell supernatants exhibited a bright 
red color, while those from CS-MnO₂@LGG (200 and 400 μL) remained 
colorless and transparent (Fig. 7b). The OD545 values for CS-MnO₂@LGG 
were significantly lower than those of the Triton group, with hemolysis 
rates below 5%, which is within the safety threshold for antibacterial 
materials. CCK-8 assays conducted on RS1 fibroblasts indicated no sig­
nificant cytotoxicity after 24 h of exposure to CS-MnO₂ NPs, with cell 
viability exceeding 93.42% even at 300 μM, comparable to PBS controls, 
as illustrated in Fig. 7c. Live/dead staining corroborated these findings, 
revealing predominant green fluorescence from viable cells (Fig. 7d). 
The DCFH-DA assay showed that RS1 cells treated with CS-MnO₂ NPs 
displayed a fluorescence profile similar to untreated cells, with no 
detectable green signal for ROS generation, indicating minimal cyto­
toxicity to normal cells (Fig. S13a, b). The prepared CS-MnO₂@LGG was 
immersed in buffer solutions with different pH values, and the concen­
tration of released Mn2+ in the supernatant was quantified by ICP-OES 
(Fig. S14). The results showed that at pH 5.0, the concentration of 
Mn2+ in the supernatant was 16.27 ppm, which is considered insuffi­
cient to induce cytotoxic effects in normal cells [42]. Flow cytometry 
analysis using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining showed low rates of apoptosis 
and necrosis, with 97.64% of cells residing in the viable (Q4) quadrant 
(Fig. 7e). In addition, the Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) assay showed 
no coagulation for the supernatant of liquid CS-MnO₂@LGG after 
overnight storage, suggesting a low endotoxin burden under the tested 
conditions (Fig. S15a). Using a quantitative kinetic turbidimetric LAL 
assay, the endotoxin level in the CS-MnO₂@LGG extract/supernatant 
was approximately 0.043 EU/mL, which is below the safety limit (0.5 
EU/mL) [43], indicating negligible endotoxin contamination under the 
tested conditions (Fig. S15b, c). The CS-MnO₂@LGG ointment was 
packaged in blank toothpaste tubes containing a stabilizing ointment 
and stored at −4 ◦C. SEM imaging revealed a uniform film-forming 
surface with a wrinkled morphology, likely resulting from gel drying 
shrinkage (Fig. 7f). The viability of LGG remained approximately 
83.65% after 15 days of storage (Fig. 7g; Fig. S16). In murine wounds 
infected with S. aureus, the topical application of CS-MnO₂@LGG 
reduced wound-surface pH from approximately 7.2 to 5.2 within 18 h 
(Fig. 7h), indicating LGG colonization and the establishment of an

3.6. Safety/contraindications explanation
Although probiotics have been explored for in vivo applications, 
additional validation is required before use in humans to address po­
tential clinical safety concerns [47]. In particular, it is important to 
confirm that the employed LGG can be effectively controlled by 
commonly used clinical antibiotics, thereby defining a clear controlla­
bility boundary in cases of unintended colonization, infection risk, or the 
need for antimicrobial therapy [48]. The in vitro susceptibility panel 
demonstrated that the LGG strain remained phenotypically susceptible 
to a range of clinically relevant antibiotics, including penicillin, eryth­
romycin, tetracycline, chloramphenicol, vancomycin, gentamicin, and 
ciprofloxacin, supporting its antibiotic controllability as part of the 
biosafety evaluation under the tested conditions (Table S3). In addition, 
the in vivo persistence of LGG delivered via the CS-MnO₂@LGG com­
posite ointment was evaluated in a murine wound model. Viable LGG 
became nearly undetectable after 2–3 days post-application, indicating 
transient local retention (Fig. S19). This limited persistence may reduce 
prolonged bacterial exposure and associated safety concerns in the 
wound microenvironment.
Whole-genome sequencing was conducted to assess potential genetic 
safety risks (Table S4). Although the LGG strain carried resistance genes 
related to lincomycin and streptogramin A, in vitro susceptibility testing 
confirmed its sensitivity to other commonly used clinical antibiotics. 
Moreover, no canonical virulence factors associated with wound infec­
tion, including hla, hysA, aur, sspA, icaA, and icaD [49,50], were 
detected (Table S5). Collectively, these results indicate that the strain 
poses a manageable risk of clinically relevant antimicrobial resistance 
and supports its safety for further evaluation. These findings inform the 
antibiotic controllability aspect of biosafety but should not replace a 
comprehensive safety evaluation, particularly for high-risk populations. 
Caution is recommended for individuals with severe immunocompro­
mise, impaired mucosal barriers, or those with indwelling medical de­
vices or critical illnesses. Importantly, this intervention is not intended 
as a routine or standard practice, but rather as an exploratory strategy to 
expand therapeutic options.
4. Conclusion
In summary, we developed a dual-functional biohybrid antibacterial 
platform by integrating chitosan-functionalized CS-MnO₂ NPs with LGG, 
forming a synergistic CS-MnO₂@LGG composite functional material. 
This hybrid system effectively combines the catalytic activity of nano­
zymes with the adaptive metabolic characteristics of probiotics. Spe­
cifically, LGG actively secretes lactic acid and H₂O₂, generating a mildly 
acidic and oxidative microenvironment, thus significantly enhancing 
ROS production by the CS-MnO₂ NPs. Moreover, the CS-MnO₂ NPs 
effectively deplete intracellular GSH, disrupting bacterial redox ho­
meostasis and further intensifying oxidative stress, which together 
contribute to short-term antibacterial activity. This integrated strategy 
illustrates a novel “bacteria against bacteria” concept, where beneficial 
microbes act as direct antagonists to pathogens and modulators of 
nanozyme performance. In vitro assays demonstrated that CS- 
MnO₂@LGG exhibited acute antibacterial activity, achieving inhibition 
rates of 99.60% against E. coli and 99.45% against S. aureus. Potential 
translational applications of this platform include hydrogel ointments 
for chronic wound care, where it could provide effective short-term 
antimicrobial activity. Notably, this work is positioned as a proof-of- 
concept and should be regarded as an exploratory strategy rather than 
a routine modality, without replacing standard wound care or conven­
tional anti-infective therapy. Future challenges include scalability, reg­
ulatory approval, and long-term safety and biocompatibility for clinical 
adoption. Overall, this study presents a promising probiotics-driven 
catalytic approach against multidrug-resistant infections and high­
lights the potential of coupling microbial metabolism with nanozymes.

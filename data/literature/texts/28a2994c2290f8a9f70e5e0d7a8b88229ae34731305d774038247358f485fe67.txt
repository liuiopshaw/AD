A B S T R A C T   
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a primary cause of dementia that affects millions of people worldwide and its 
prevalence is likely to increase largely in the coming decades. Multiple complex pathways, such as oxidative 
stress, tau and amyloid-beta (Aβ) pathology, and cholinergic dysfunction, are involved in the pathogenesis of 
Alzheimer’s disease. The conventional treatments provide only symptomatic relief and not a complete cure for 
the disease. On the other hand, recent studies have looked into the possibility of flavonoids as an effective 
therapeutic strategy for treating AD. Quercetin, a well-known flavonol, has been extensively studied for AD 
treatment. Therefore, this review mainly focuses on the pharmacokinetics properties of quercetin and its modes 
of action, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-amyloidogenic, and neuroprotective properties, which are 
beneficial in treating AD. It also highlights the nano delivery systems of quercetin, including liposomes, nano­
structures lipid carriers, solid lipid nanoparticles, nanoemulsions, microemulsions, self-emulsifying drug delivery 
systems, and nanoparticles reported for AD treatment. The remarkable potential of quercetin nanocarriers has 
been reflected in enhancing its bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, clinical studies must be 
conducted to explore it as a therapeutic strategy for Alzheimer’s disease.   
1. Introduction 
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia in 
people with age > 65 and accounts for more than 60–70 % of dementia 
cases worldwide (Barnes, 2022). On average, 50 million people are 
affected by Alzheimer’s worldwide and it is also predicted that this 
number may increase by 4 times, with 200 million cases by 2050 
(Barnes, 2022). With the growing global Alzheimer’s cases, it may 
continue to be a clinical and social problem worldwide (Fan et al., 
2020). The major clinical manifestations of Alzheimer’s disease are 
cognitive dysfunction, memory loss and abnormal personality changes 
due to pathologic changes within the brain. Several clinicians have been 
proposing related pathogenic mechanisms for AD. Amongst the pro­
posed theories, the most widely accepted theories are the amyloid-beta 
(Aβ) and tau receptor hypothesis (Fan et al., 2020; Madav et al., 2019). 
The pathologic causes may include the formation of senile plaque (SP) 
formed by Aβ and neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) comprising phosphor­
ylated tau proteins in the hippocampus (Scheuner et al., 1996). The 
brain atrophy in the hippocampus and a marked decrease in acetyl­
choline (Ach) levels are also observed (Andrade et al., 2019). 
The conventional treatments available are cholinesterase inhibitors, 
nitric oxide synthase inhibitors, glutamate regulators, antioxidants, and 
anti-inflammatory cholinergic drugs that provide symptomatic relief or 
temporary improvement in memory impairment but do not cure the

2. Pathophysiology of Alzheimer’s disease 
2.1. Cholinergic hypothesis 
As reported, many brain regions in AD have dysregulated cholinergic 
systems (Bekdash, 2021). The progression of AD symptoms is linked to 
structural modifications in cholinergic synapses, reduced cholinergic 
neurons and acetylcholine in the brain and neurodegeneration of nu­
cleus basalis of Meynert in the basal forebrain, the source of cortical 
cholinergic innervation (Stanciu et al., 2019). Acetylcholine is a 
neurotransmitter essential for learning, memory, and attention, and the 
cholinergic system is responsible for its synthesis, release and degrada­
tion (Z.-R. Chen et al., 2022). Choline acetyltransferase is a crucial 
enzyme required for the production of acetylcholine in the cytoplasm 
and requisite substrates for the synthesis are: choline, acetyl-coenzyme 
A and adenosine triphosphate. The acetylcholine levels were greatly 
decreased at synapses due to lower activity of choline acetyltransferase 
in Alzheimer’s brain in the regions: amygdala, hippocampus and cortex 
(BOWEN et al., 1976). The impairment of cholinergic innervation in the 
cerebral cortex of the brain is an early pathogenic event related to 
cognitive impairment in Alzheimer’s patients. This theory has led to the 
development and approval of cholinesterase inhibitors as symptomatic 
therapies (Hampel et al., 2018). 
2.2. Amyloid cascade hypothesis 
The “amyloid cascade hypothesis,” the alternative viewpoint, asserts 
that the degenerative alterations in Alzheimer’s brains are the outcome 
of a chain of actions started by dysfunctional processing of Amyloid 
precursor protein (APP) and led to the production, accumulation, ag­
gregation, and toxicities of Aβ (Kawahara & Kato-Negishi, 2011). 
Membrane-bound APP can undergo two distinct processes: the amyloi­
dogenic process releasing the Aβ-peptide and the non-amyloidogenic 
pathway that blocks the formation of Aβ and generates an APP 
secreted in its place (Hardy, 2017). The amyloid peptide Aβ1-42 for­
mation occurs more frequently and further makes up the plaque deposits 
(Karran et al., 2011)in various regions of the brain, including the cere­
bral cortex, hippocampus, amygdala, frontal lobe, cingulate gyrus, 
substantia nigra, and several brainstem nuclei (Bekdash, 2021). Alter­
ations in synaptic signalling, synaptic loss, and occasionally neuronal 
loss or degeneration and dementia are further neuropathological traits 
linked to Alzheimer’s disease (Ogbodo et al., 2022). 
2.3. Tau-phosphorylation hypothesis 
Alzheimer’s disease and other neurodegenerative diseases are pri­
marily reported to be caused by tau phosphorylation, a post- 
translational alteration (Noble et al., 2013). Tau is a microtubule- 
associated protein (MAP) that controls the stability and assembly of 
microtubules in neurons(Stoothoff & Johnson, 2005). Increased phos­
phorylation or hyperphosphorylation of tau can lead to microtubule 
dysfunction in tauopathies like Alzheimer’s disease and is linked to tau 
aggregation (Stoothoff & Johnson, 2005; Y. Xia et al., 2021). According 
to Alonso et al., hyperphosphorylated tau proteins tend to self-assemble 
into paired helical and straight filaments (Alonso et al., 2008). These 
filaments form a sequester with other typical tau proteins and MAP1 and

3. Flavonoids in Alzheimer’s disease 
Flavonoids are phenolic compounds and, one of the major classes of 
secondary metabolites, are commonly present within the plant kingdom, 
exhibiting a wide range of structures and biological functions (Shen 
et al., 2017; Agarawal et al., 2023). Flavonoids contribute to the nutri­
tional value of fruits and vegetables and are responsible for the colour 
and taste of plant foods and beverages. They play a pivotal role in 
safeguarding plants against biotic and abiotic stresses while contributing 
to plant growth and development (Joshi et al., 2021). Flavonoids are 
associated with multiple health-promoting functions because of their 
impact on critical cellular enzyme activities and their anti-carcinogenic, 
antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and anti-mutagenic actions (Meto­
diewa et al., 1997). 
Flavonoids share a fundamental C6-C3-C6 skeletal framework that 
distinguishes them from other organic compounds. The core structure of 
flavonoids involves a heterocyclic pyran ring (ring C), connecting ben­
zene rings A and B. Various substituents such as hydroxyl (–OH), 
methoxy (–OCH3), and glycosyl groups attach at different positions on 
the rings, leading to distinctive physicochemical properties and bio­
logical activities for each flavonoid compound (Sharma et al., 2022). 
Flavonoids can be grouped into subcategories: flavones, flavonols, fla­
vanones, flavan-3-ols, anthocyanidins, and isoflavones (Quideau et al., 
2011; Pant & Wairkar, 2022). The classification of flavonoids is depicted 
in Fig. 2. Their chemical alterations, like methylation, acetylation, and 
glycosylation, regulate biological activities, including solubility, stabil­
ity, and bioavailability (Koirala et al., 2016). The diverse structural 
composition of flavonoids positions them as a compelling focus for drug 
discovery, with ongoing research exploring their potential therapeutic 
applications across various disorders (Iwashina, 2000). 
Flavonoids may directly affect the brain due to their capacity to pass 
the blood–brain barrier. Multiple studies have been carried out to study 
the activity of flavonoids in neurodegeneration (de Andrade Teles et al., 
2018; Minocha et al., 2022). Flavonoids such as epicatechin-3-gallate, 
naringenin, quercetin, apigenin, genistein and myricetin have been 
found to have protective effects in neurodegenerative disorders (A. Khan 
et al., 2018; Weinreb et al., 2004). Here, we have taken an overview of 
quercetin as a therapeutic strategy for Alzheimer’s disease. 
4. Quercetin 
Quercetin, 
2-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-3,5,7-trihydroxychromen-4- 
one, is a widely occurring flavonol, with a molecular weight of 302.23 g/ 
mol (Almalki et al., 2017). The aqueous solubility of quercetin was 
0.00215 g/L at 25 ◦C (Srinivas et al., 2010). The logP value of quercetin 
is reported to be 1.82 and it exhibits pKa values (7.17, 8.26,10.13, 12.30 
and 13.11), which depicts its basic nature. It can be abundantly found in 
several plant sources like onions, apples, grapes, berries, broccoli, 
asparagus, tomatoes, capers, red leaf lettuce and tea (Ulusoy & Sanlier, 
2020). 
4.1. Pharmacokinetics parameters of quercetin 
Quercetin is mainly present as Quercetin-3-O-glucoside in its plant 
sources. It is absorbed by passive diffusion and a pH-dependent process 
facilitated by the organic anion transporting protein B (Chabane et al., 
2010). It was identified as a substrate of multidrug resistance-associated 
protein 2 efflux transporter in a study by Chabane et al. in Caco-2 cells 
(Chabane et al., 2010). The absorption of quercetin occurs in the upper 
segment of the small intestine and is absorbed better in glucoside form 
than quercetin aglycone (Crespy et al., 1999). A study demonstrated 
pharmacokinetic parameters after administering 500 mg of oral dose in 
humans and they observed the Cmax of 10.93 ng/mL in 4.83 h Tmax; 
AUC0-∞ of 4774.93 ng h/mL; and elimination half-life of 6.26 h (Riva 
et al., 2019). Quercetin exhibits limited oral bioavailability, less than 10 
% in humans, due to its inadequate solubility and absorption profile 
(Kandemir et al., 2022). 
Quercetin is widely distributed with large concentrations in the liver, 
kidney, lung and heart. A study by Boer et al. demonstrated that high 
buildup of quercetin and its metabolites are found in the lungs in rats, 
whereas in the liver and kidneys in the case of pigs (de Boer et al., 2005). 
Ren et al. performed a study in the culture of primary brain microvessel 
endothelial cells and primary astroglial cells to determine quercetin 
permeability across the blood–brain barrier. It was observed that 65.57 
% of quercetin showed permeation across the BBB (Ren et al., 2010). In 
another experiment using caco-2 cells, the apparent permeability of 
quercetin was found to be 5.8 x 10-6 cm/sec (Walgren et al., 1998). 
After absorption, quercetin undergoes phase II enzyme-mediated 
metabolism within the epithelial cells of the stomach and intestine 
and then further metabolized in the liver and kidney (Graf et al., 2006). 
This fact is supported by a study wherein upon oral administration, 93.3 
% of quercetin underwent metabolism in the gut and only 3.1 % was 
metabolized in the liver (X. Chen et al., 2005). The major metabolic 
processes involved in the production of quercetin conjugates are glu­
curonidation, sulfation and methylation (Hollman & Katan, 1997). The 
major metabolite of quercetin is quercetin-3-O-glucoronide and it is 
transported to the target tissue through plasma to show its biological 
action(Day et al., 2003). The half-lives of quercetin metabolites vary 
from 11 to 28 h (Boots et al., 2008). 
Manach et al. performed a study where rats were fed with a high 
quercetin diet of 2.5 g/kg for 14 days; it was observed that quercetin was 
found to be seven times higher in bile than plasma and other metabo­
lites, tamarixetin and isorhamnetin were also detected in bile (Manach 
et al., 1995). Tamarixetin and isorhamnetin are methylated derivatives 
of quercetin. Another study by Arts et al. supports that tamarixetin is not 
found in plasma, suggesting it is formed in the liver and immediately 
excreted (Arts et al., 2004). Thus, these studies indicate that methylation 
of quercetin favours biliary excretion. 
4.2. Pharmacodynamic action of quercetin in Alzheimer’s disease 
The formation of neurotoxic dimers, oligomers, and fibrils from Aβ 
peptides through aggregation is the most prominent feature of AD 
pathogenesis, leading to senile plaque formation (Omar et al., 2017). 
Senile plaque deposition and gene mutations PSEN1, PSEN2 and APP l 
lead to neuronal cell death and dementia (Reitz, 2012). Various in vitro 
studies showed that quercetin hinders Aβ aggregation by engaging in 
hydrophobic interactions and forming hydrogen bonds with the β sheet 
structure (Omar et al., 2017). Jimenez-Aliaga et al. performed a study in 
which Aβ 25–35, when incubated alone, exhibited robust fibrillation 
while the number of fibrils reduced notably after incubation with 10 µM 
quercetin (Jim´enez-Aliaga et al., 2011). The Catechol moiety of quer­
cetin plays a double function role; it serves as a metal chelator and in­
teracts with Aβ peptides to obstruct their aggregation. The metal 
chelation activity of quercetin enables competitive binding of it to the 
metal binding site on Aβ and its keto-enolate group helps in reducing 
oxidative stress caused by Ab-Cu+2 (Suganthy et al., 2016). 
Tau phosphorylation is majorly accelerated due to increased kinase 
activity, particularly cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5) and decreased 
activity of phosphatase activity and thus, inhibition of calcium-calpain- 
p25-CDK5 pathway has therapeutic importance in AD (Noble et al., 
2003). In a study, it was found that quercetin inhibits glycogen synthase 
kinase 3β and heat shock protein 70 by modulating PI3/Akt/GSK3 β 
pathway, which plays a significant role in phosphorylation and cogni­
tive dysfunction in AD (Jiang et al., 2016a). It was also reported that

Aβ is the primary component of senile plaques and is formed after 
two-step sequential proteolytic cleavage of APP by β- and γ-secretases 
(Jung et al., 2010). β-secretase upon action on APP leads to the forma­
tion of secreted APP (sAPP) and C-terminal fragment (CTF); these 
further undergo proteolytic processing by γ-secretase to produce Aβ 
peptide (Araki, 2016). Additionally, the deposition of misfolded Aβ 
peptide leads to the production of an insoluble plaque. BACE-1 regula­
tion has excellent potential to modulate Aβ and plaque deposition in AD 
(Huebbe et al., 2010). Quercetin had a direct inhibitory effect on BACE-1 
activity in a dose-dependent manner (Jim´enez-Aliaga et al., 2011). 
Another study suggested that along with inhibition of Aβ aggregation, it 
inhibits APP cleavage at site-β induced by BACE-1 to inhibit the for­
mation of Aβ (1–42) peptide (Suganthy et al., 2016). 
Oxidative stress results from a reactive oxygen species (ROS) buildup 
and vital events in neurodegenerative disorders. These free radicals 
damage cell components, including DNA, proteins, and lipids, and 
interfere with normal cellular signalling. This results in disruption of the 
normal cell reduction–oxidation equilibrium and also programmed cell 
death by apoptosis. Quercetin has been found to scavenge free radicals 
and protect from neuronal damage by oxidative stress through multiple 
pathways (Gilgun-Sherki et al., 2001). 
α-Nuclear factor (erythroid-derived 2)-like-pathway (Nrf2) regulates 
antioxidant enzymes and drug-metabolizing enzymes like glutathione S- 
transferase (GST) and NAD (P) H: quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1). 
The Nrf-2 pathway acts as the primary regulator of inducible antioxidant 
reactions, regulating energy metabolism, mitochondrial activities, and 
the maintenance of cellular redox balance (Vomhof-DeKrey & Picklo, 
2012). Yue Shi et al. studied elevated glucose-induced apoptosis in 
cultured dorsal root ganglion neurons of rats. Quercetin markedly 
increased Nrf-2 and hemeoxygenase-1, effectively scavenged ROS 
directly in a dose dependant manner, and also was observed to inhibit 
NF-κB signalling pathway that led to suppression of iNOS, COX-2, and 
proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α (Shi et al., 2013). 
Paraoxonase-2 (PON-2) is a mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme found 
in high concentrations in the striatum and is involved in scavenging of 
ROS (Giordano et al., 2011). In a study conducted in H2O2 and 2,3-dime­
thoxy-1,4-naphthoquinone induced oxidative stress, pre-treatment of 
quercetin in knockout mice increased PON-2 expressions. Therefore, 
quercetin significantly inhibited ROS production in striatal neurons 
(Costa et al., 2013). 
JNK pathway is one of the important pathways among the mitogen- 
activated protein kinase (MAPK) signalling pathway. Phosphorylation 
on threonine and tyrosine residue leads to the activation of JNKs, 
whereas a negative feedback loop initiated by MAPK phosphatases is 
responsible for its inactivation (A. Kumar et al., 2015). The activation of 
JNK and its target c-Jun are recognized as regulators in the induction of 
apoptotic cell death. Quercetin has been reported to inhibit JNK acti­
vation in 4-hydroxy-2-noneal-induced oxidative stress in RL34 cells 
(Uchida et al., 1999). In another study, quercetin inhibited H2O2- 
induced apoptosis in mesangial cells⋅H2O2 treatment led to activation of 
ERKs and JNK kinase by phosphorylation and quercetin pre-treatment, 
which resulted in attenuation of these pathways, thus suggesting the 
antioxidant potential of quercetin via inactivation of the JNK pathway 
(Verheij et al., 1996). 
Akt pathway controls multiple cell functions like cell apoptosis and 
survival, cell growth, metabolism and proliferation. It is a vital signal­
ling pathway focusing on protein kinase B. Its activation is linked with 
the formation of phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate. Xie et al. 
studied high-fat diet-fed mice to evaluate compromised hippocampus- 
dependent learning and memory linked with oxidative stress. Quer­
cetin at 0.005 % and 0.01 % w/w for 13 weeks was administered and 
post this period, hippocampal markers of oxidative stress, mRNA 
expression of Akt, PI3K and Morris water maze were evaluated. Escape

Inhibition of AChE enzyme is a commonly used therapy to treat mild 
to moderate AD. Various in vitro studies have indicated that quercetin 
acts as a competitive inhibitor of AChE and butyrylcholinesterase 
(BChE) in a concentration-dependent manner (Islam et al., 2013). 
Abdalla et al. conducted a study in cadmium-exposed rats and found that 
quercetin exhibits hydrophobic interactions and strong hydrogen 
bonding with AChE, therefore reducing acetylcholine hydrolysis and 
elevating its level in synaptic cleft (Abdalla et al., 2013). Various studies 
suggested that the absence and presence of –OH groups on the phenyl 
ring of compounds play a significant role in its ability to inhibit AChE 
and BChE because these –OH groups construct hydrogen bonds with the 
amino acid residues present on the active sites of enzymes (M. T. H. 
Khan et al., 2009). A study reported that quercetin at 50 mg/kg dose 
inhibited the activity of AChE in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus 
regions due to diabetes (Maciel et al., 2016). Another study observed 
that acute pre-treatment with a single dose of quercetin 1 h before 
administering scopolamine resulted in the avoidance of cognitive 
impairment induced by scopolamine (Richetti et al., 2011). Addition­
ally, a molecular docking investigation anticipated that quercetin out­
performs any traditional AChE inhibitor drugs (Islam et al., 2013). 
The major effects of quercetin include free radical scavenging, in­
hibition of enzymes like xanthine oxidase, superoxide dismutase, 
glutathione peroxidase, lipoxygenase and cyclooxygenase by modu­
lating signal transduction pathway, maintaining oxidative balance and 
modulating levels of oxidative stress markers like reduced glutathione, 
hydrogen peroxide, and lipid peroxidation (Xu et al., 2019). Quercetin 
also inhibits NF-κB activation, mitigates the formation of prostaglandin 
E2 and proinflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β, interleukin-6, and 
tumor necrosis factor-α and increases the production of anti- 
inflammatory cytokines interleukin-10 and transforming growth factor 
(Cai et al., 2021). All pathways of the mechanism of action of quercetin

5. Quercetin delivery systems for AD 
5.1. Liposomes 
Liposomes are bi-vesicular spherical drug delivery systems composed 
of phospholipids and cholesterol and can encompass hydrophilic as well 
as lipophilic molecules. Upon increasing the number of bilayers, the 
encapsulation efficiency of the system also increases for hydrophilic 
moieties. These liposomes offer several advantages, such as increased 
solubility, utilization of hydrophilic and hydrophobic moieties, 
increased bioavailability, etc (Nsairat et al., 2022). 
Tong-un et al. formulated quercetin liposomes (25 mcg/ml) for intra- 
nasal delivery by thin film hydration method and extrusion using 
α-phosphatidylcholine, and cholesterol. AF64A was administered at 2 
nmol through the intracerebroventricular route to induce memory 
impairment in adult male Wistar rats. Quercetin was administered (20 
μg) once daily continuously for 2 weeks before and one week after 
AF64A induction. In histopathological studies, it was found that AF64A 
decreased neuron density and quercetin reversed these changes. Addi­
tionally, the Morris water maze test showed increased escape latency 
time in disease-control rats with reduced retention time, whereas rats 
receiving 
quercetin 
liposomes 
showed 
significantly 
decreased 
acquisition and enhanced retention time. Therefore, the study indicates 
that via nasal route delivery, quercetin liposomes improved memory 
impairment caused by AF64A (Tong-un, 2010). 
5.2. Solid lipid nanoparticles 
SLNs are colloidal nanoparticles composed of lipids and surfactants 
wherein the drug is dispersed in the hydrophobic core of the system. 
SLNs are not used often due to the problems associated with poor drug 
loading and carrier explosion (Mukherjee et al., 2009). 
Quercetin Sold lipid nanocarriers (Q-SLN) were formulated by 
micro-emulsification method using compritol as lipid, and Tween 80 as a 
surfactant to study its activity in AlCl3-induced AD model. The particle 
size, zeta potential, entrapment and in vitro drug release were 0.152 µm, 
−20.7 mV, 87.65 % and 76.49 %, respectively. In biochemical studies, 
the % increase in MDA content and nitrite levels was 882.13 % and 
377.79 % and the % decrease in reduced GSH levels was 90.27 % in the 
disease control. In contrast, in the Q-SLN treated group, the % increase 
in MDA and nitrite was 17.18 % and 26.14 %, and the % decrease in GSH 
levels was 18.51 %. In the spatial navigation task behavioural test, it was 
observed that the time taken to reach the platform decreased in Q-SLN 
treated rats compared to the disease group. In the Elevated plus maze 
test, the Q-SLN treated group showed decreased mean retention la­
tencies on the 45th and 56th day than free quercetin, suggesting its 
increased potential as SLN formulation (Dhawan et al., 2011). 
Pinheiro et al. formulated RVG29 peptide-functionalized SLNs for 
brain delivery of quercetin by homogenization method followed by 
ultrasonication using cetyl palmitate as solid lipid and polysorbate 80 as 
surfactant. The particle size, zeta potential, PDI and entrapment effi­
ciency were 201 nm, 0.13, −20 and 84 %, respectively. LDH assay was 
done in hCMEC/D3 cell line, which showed no cytotoxicity. In in vitro 
analysis, the permeability of RVG29 Q-SLN was observed to be 1.5 times 
greater than non-RVG29 functionalized Q-SLN across the blood–brain 
barrier model. This delivery system was also confirmed to inhibit 
amyloid-beta formation in thioflavin T binding assay, proving its po­
tential for neurological disorders (Pinheiro et al., 2020b). 
5.3. Nanostructured lipid carriers 
Nanostructured lipid carriers are new-generation nanoparticles 
comprised of lipids, surfactants, and co-surfactants, offering control and 
targeted drug release and the advantage of loading lipophilic and hy­
drophilic drugs in solid or liquid form (Raju et al., 2021). NLCs provide 
better storage stability and have also shown improved drug permeability 
from the matrix (Chauhan et al., 2020; Fang et al., 2013). 
Quercetin-loaded NLCs (Q-NLCs) for intranasal delivery were pre­
pared by hot pressure homogenization. The particle size was 118.2 nm 
with PDI of 0.220, zeta potential of −20.1 mV and entrapment efficiency 
of 88.74 %. SEM evaluation reported a needle-like shape of the formu­
lation, which showed sustained release for 6 h. Similarly, 76.71 ± 1.97 
% and 26.73 ± 3.6 % quercetin were diffused from NLC and plain free 
quercetin after 6 h in an ex vivo nasal permeability study. In brain dis­
tribution studies, the Cmax of Q-NLC was observed to be 93.637 ± 19.88 
µg/ml at tmax of 60 min, whereas the Cmax of free quercetin was 42.26 ±
9.90 µg/ml at tmax of 90 min. Therefore, Q-NLC enhanced brain targeting 
with improved bioavailability and could be a promising delivery system 
for neurodegenerative disorders (Patil & Mahajan, 2018). 
Kumar et al. formulated biocompatible quercetin NLCs (Q-NLCs) by 
emulsification technique with tocopherol acetate, solid lipid (Compri­
tol) and phospholipid (Phospholipon 90G) in 1:6:3 ratio and quercetin to 
lipid ratio was 1:5. The optimized batch was characterized for particle 
size, PDI, zeta potential and encapsulation efficiency, results were 74.61 
± 4.23 nm, <0.3, −8.9 mV, 89.91 ± 6.41 %, respectively. The formu­
lation was stable for six months at a storage temperature of 5 ◦C ± 3 ◦C; 
however, significant changes were observed at higher temperatures. The 
antioxidant activity of Q-NLC was 94.38 ± 3.81 %, whereas that of pure

exhibited a 5.4-fold higher bioavailability of Q-NLC than quercetin. 
Similarly, pharmacokinetic properties also improved, Cmax of Q-NLC 
was observed to be 20.63 ± 1.09 µg/mL, whereas that of quercetin was 
6.40 ± 0.29 µg/mL and Tmax of Q-NLC was found to be 5.4 h as

properties of NLCs enhanced the bioavailability and, thus, can offer 
better brain delivery and efficacy of quercetin in neurological disorders 
(P. Kumar et al., 2016). 
Quercetin SLNs and NLCs functionalized with transferrin were 
designed by hot homogenization to penetrate the blood–brain barrier 
through overexpressed transferrin receptors in brain endothelial cells. 
NLCs comprised cetyl palmitate as solid lipid, miglyol 812 as liquid lipid 
and polysorbate 80 as surfactant, whereas SLNs comprised cetyl 
palmitate (500 mg) as solid lipid and polysorbate 80 as surfactant. The 
particle size of NLCs was 219 ± 13 nm and the zeta potential value was 
−28 ± 2 mV. The entrapment efficiency of Q-NLC was observed to be 81 
± 1 % and that of SLNs was 234 ± 18 nm, –32 ± 8 mV, and 77 ± 11 %, 
respectively. Additionally, they researched hCMEC/D3 cell monolayers 
that revealed NLCs more permeated the blood–brain barrier. The Aβ 
investigations were done by thioflavin T binding assay; then, fluores­
cence intensity was measured. It showed that 30 μM Q-NLC-transferrin 
and Q-SLN-transferrin could prevent the development of fibrils depicted 
in the decreased fluorescent intensity of thioflavin T after 24 h incuba­
tion than amyloid-β alone (Pinheiro et al., 2020a). 
5.4. Nanoemulsion 
Nanoemulsions are nanoparticulate colloidal systems that are 
kinetically stable and composed of oil, water, and emulsifiers. These 
droplet-like structures provide improved efficacy and bioavailability 
(Wilson et al., 2022). 
Quercetin nanoemulsion (Q-NE) was formulated by homogenization 
technique to study its effect in the AlCl3-induced AD model. Intraperi­
toneal Q-NE injection (15 mg/kg/day) was administered for 30 days to 
diseased rats. At the end of the study, the brain was isolated and esti­
mated for oxidative stress and inflammatory markers by ELISA test, and 
the HPLC analysis method was used to study the level of neurotrans­
mitters. The diseased group showed an increased level of oxidative stress 
and inflammatory markers, whereas the level of oxidative stress 
markers, i.e., SOD, GSH, MDA, and AOPP, in the Q-NE treated group was 
decreased and found to be 313.36 U/g tissue, 42.87 m mol/g tissue, 
22.82 nmol/g tissue and 7.23 ng/g tissue, respectively. The level of in­
flammatory markers such as IL-1β, TNF-α and adiponectin in Q-NE 
treatment group were estimated to be 31.01 Pg/mL, 28.02 Pg/mL, and 
3.12 µg/g tissue, respectively. The level of brain neurotransmitters was 
elevated in the disease group and significantly reduced in Q-NE treated 
group and were observed to be dopamine: 4.37 µg/g tissue, norepi­
nephrine: 4.012 µg/g tissue, and serotonin: 3.31 µg/g tissue. Thus, 
treatment with Q-NE has excellent potential to be used in neuronal 
disorders (Alaqeel et al., 2022). 
5.5. PLGA (poly-lactide-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles 
The most popular polymer for drug administration is poly-lactic-co- 
glycolic acid (PLGA), which is a biodegradable copolymer composed of 
poly-lactic acid (PLA) and poly-glycolic acid (PGA) (Bala et al., 2004). 
In a study conducted by Sun et al., quercetin PLGA NPs (PLGA-Q- 
NPs) were prepared by double emulsion solvent evaporation technique 
using PLGA, quercetin, ethyl acetate and PVA to study its effect on Aβ 
fibrils in SH-SY5Y cells and APP/PS1 mice. In vitro drug release showed 
an initial 11.23 % burst release for the first 2 h and then steady release 
was observed between 2 and 48 h. Thioflavin T assay was utilized to 
estimate Aβ fibril formation and reduced fluorescence intensity by 
PLGA-Q-NPs was observed; thus, fibrillation by 53 %. In addition, PLGA- 
Q-NPs showed enhanced cell viability of neuronal cells at 10 µg/mL, 20 
µg/mL and 30 µg/mL. It was found that PLGA-Q-NPs exhibit 
concentration-dependent inhibition of neurotoxicity of Zn2+ Aβ systems. 
In the Morris water maze test, 10 mg/kg, 20 mg/kg and 30 mg/kg of 
PLGA-Q-NPs were injected in APP/PS1 mice. It was observed that 10 
mg/kg concentration could slightly decrease escape latency time. In 
contrast, at 20 mg/kg and 30 mg/kg, there was a significant reduction in 
escape latency, indicating improved spatial memory of mice. Formula­
tion ameliorated cognition and memory impairments, increased thera­
peutic index, and reduced side effects. Hence, PLGA Q NPs can be used 
therapeutically in Alzheimer’s disease (Sun et al., 2016). 
5.6. Other nanoparticles 
Halevas et al. prepared quercetin magnetic mesoporous silica 
nanoparticles with tetraethyl orthosilicate. TEM evaluation indicated 
the presence of agglomerated core–shell nanospheres with 200–250 nm 
particle size. The zeta potential was found to be −40 ± 1.19 mV. BET 
analysis performed for other physicochemical parameters like average 
pore size, surface area and pore volume were observed to be 273.12 Ả, 
108.33 m2/g and 0.74 cm3/g, respectively. In vitro analysis showed 
50.28 % release after 48 h; the release was high for an initial 60 min and 
then became steady after 6 h. The impact of formulation on Aβ aggre­
gation was studied by circular dichroism spectropolarimetry at two ra­
tios of quercetin: Aβ; 0.5:1 and 1:1. There was a significant reduction of 
Aβ fibril formation at 0.5:1 ratio and complete inhibition of fibrillation 
at 1:1 ratio. Antioxidant activity was analyzed in 1 μM Aβ exposed 
neuronal hippocampal cells, leading to ROS production that caused 10 
times greater fluorescence, whereas incubation with Q-mesoporous 
nanoparticle showed 28.8 % decrease in fluorescence. Thus, the findings 
suggested the anti-amyloid and antioxidant activity of quercetin mag­
netic nanoparticles (Halevas et al., 2020). 
Quercetin-modified polysorbate 80 (P-80)-coated AuPd core–shell 
nanostructure (Qu@P80AuPd NPs) was fabricated by hydrothermal 
method. In 30 mg of concave cubic AuPd core–shell, 25 mg of quercetin 
in 5 mg/mL of ethanol solution was added and then centrifuged to 
obtain Qu@P80AuPd NPs. The particle size was 62 nm and X-ray 
diffraction identified the presence of two metals i.e., Au and Pd with 
different diffraction peaks. Aβ aggregate formation was estimated in 
SHSY5Y cells by Thioflavin T assay and it was observed that fluores­
cence intensity decreased significantly with 10 μg/ml of Qu@P80AuPd 
NPs. The cell survival rate decreased to 37 % in the control group, 
whereas it showed a significant rise to 74 % with 10 μg/ml of 
Qu@P80AuPd NPs (90 % restoration of cell survival). Additionally, 
caspase-3 activity was found to be 100 % in the control group and 218 % 
in the Aβ-incubated group, whereas it was inhibited significantly, 
resulting in reduced apoptosis. To study the brain permeation, Transwell 
assay was utilized in which it was observed that bEnd3 cells incubated 
with ruthenium complex showed no fluorescence. In contrast, the green 
fluorescence signal increased significantly in cells treated with 
Qu@P80AuPd NPs, indicating improved permeability of quercetin in 
BBB. In vitro findings suggest that Qu@P80AuPd NPs (10 μg/ml) can 
stimulate SHSY5Y cells autophagy, enhance fusion of autophagosomes 
and lysosomes, increase blood–brain barrier permeability, promote the 
elimination of Aβ fibrils and protects SHSY5Y cells from Aβ induced 
toxicity (90 % restoration of cell survival) therefore, can be used as 
potential autophagy inducer therapeutic strategy for AD (Liu et al., 
2019). 
Quercetin-loaded selenium nanocomposites (P-80Que@SeNCs) were 
prepared by combining quercetin and Na2SeO3 in different molar ratios, 
10:1, 8:1, 6:1, 4:1, and 2:1. The composites were further modified with 
polysorbate 80 and acacia and the optimized batch was selected with 6:1 
ratio and 2 % of acacia along with 1 % w/v P80. The physicochemical 
parameters were 89.90 ± 4.17 nm particle size, a zeta potential of −9.89 
± 0.51 mV. SEM evaluation indicated that acacia covered the P- 
80Que@SeNCs, thus preventing aggregation of the formulation. DPPH 
method was employed to compare the antioxidant activity of

Quercetin-Conjugated Superparamagnetic Iron Oxide Nanoparticles 
(Q-SPIONS). At low concentrations, negatively charged SPION has 
demonstrated protective properties against neurodegeneration. Q-

et al. Quercetin was added to dextran-coated SPIONs. SEM analysis 
confirmed a spherical shape with 30–50 nm particle size. In vivo studies 
were performed in AlCl3-induced male Wistar rats wherein behavioural 
tests like the Morris Water Maze and Passive avoidance tests were car­
ried out. Results showed increased latency to escape in the disease 
group, whereas 25 mg/kg/day for 42 days QT-SPION treated group 
opposed the increased escape latency on 21st and 42nd day in the Morris 
Water Maze test. In the Passive avoidance test, the intensity of cognitive 
impairment decreased (retention latency decreased) at the 21st and 
42nd day in the treatment group. Additionally, qPCR results suggested 
increased APP expression and decreased mir101 in the disease group, 
while these results were opposed in the Q-SPIONS treatment group. A 
histopathological study was performed, and it was revealed that iNOS 
expression decreased in the Q-SPION-treated group, and a notable in­
crease was observed in GPx and CAT antioxidant enzymes. The findings 
of this study reveal that Q-SPIONS can enhance learning and improve 
cognitive impairment (Amanzadeh Jajin et al., 2021). 
Quercetin Nanoparticles (Q-NP) were formulated by pulse laser 
ablation method using quercetin and PVP (2 %). The particle size was 
50 nm and the zeta potential was −19.7 mV. In vitro drug release of free 
quercetin powder showed 4 % release and reached saturation at 2 h, 
whereas Q-NP showed 5 % release in 1st hour and 25 % total release 
before 8 h, which was 6 times more than quercetin. The inhibitory effect 
of Q-NPs (20 µg/ml) on Aβ42 fibrillation was estimated by thioflavin T 
fluorescent assay. Compared to free quercetin, Q-NPs showed a decrease 
in fluorescent intensity i.e., exhibited a greater inhibitory effect on Aβ42 
fibrillation. Additionally, H2O2 (200 µM) and Aβ42 (40 µM) were used to 
induce the production of ROS and DCFH-DA fluorescent indicated that 
ROS levels were neutralized with Q-NPs (20 µg/ml). SHSY5Y cell 
viability raised from 40 % to 70 % with 10 µg/ml of Q-NPs. These 
findings indicate that Q-NPs have huge potential as Aβ regulators and 
ROS scavengers in AD (Han et al., 2018). 
5.7. Exosomes 
Plasma exosomes of quercetin were formulated to study its action in 
okadaic acid-induced cognitive dysfunction in AD mice. Exosome pellets 
were prepared from blood plasma of Sprague Dawley rats and 1 mg 
quercetin was dissolved in 200 µl DMSO with 2 % Tween 80 and further 
incorporated into exosome. Upon intravenous administration (12 mg/ 
kg), the pharmacokinetic profile of free quercetin and quercetin exo­
somes were analyzed. It was detected that t1/2 of quercetin exosomes 
prolonged to 89.14 min than free quercetin (36.47 min). The AUC of 
quercetin exosome was 7.5 times higher than free quercetin. The iso­
lated brain of rats revealed that quercetin concentration increased by 2.5 
folds in the cerebrum and 1.5 folds in the cerebellum than free quercetin. 
Morris water maze test showed increased memory and learning perfor­
mance and crossing numbers, high target quadrant occupation, and 
decreased escape latency in the quercetin exosome-treated group, 
among all others. Western blot test and quantitative analysis for CDK5 
modulated tau phosphorylation showed that the number of NFTs was 
reduced, caspase 9 and caspase 3 expression was downregulated, and

6. Conclusion 
With the increasing trend in using herbal medicines, flavonoids have 
observed a boom due to their versatile action in several diseases, Alz­
heimer’s being one of them. Alzheimer’s disease is becoming a more 
worldwide severe health concern, and current therapies are not always 
effective in significantly improving symptoms. Quercetin, a flavanol, has 
been the drug of interest as it offers therapeutic action through diverse 
modes like suppression of Aß aggregation, modulation of tau phos­
phorylation, and its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. 
Further, the implication of nanotechnology would enhance the thera­
peutic potential of quercetin. Currently, quercetin is being explored, but 
commercialization of quercetin-incorporated products with thorough 
preclinical and clinical trials would open new gates for the treatment of 
Alzheimer’s disease.

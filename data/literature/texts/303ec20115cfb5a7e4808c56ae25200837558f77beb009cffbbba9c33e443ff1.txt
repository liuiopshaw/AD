A B S T R A C T   
In this work, a well-known Au25 NCs with high purity was prepared by simple one-pot reducing method. The as- 
synthesized Au25 NCs exhibited excellent antibacterial efficiency toward Gram-negative bacteria in a dose- and 
time-dependent manner, which could be used as nanoantibiotics to replace harmful antibiotics. The antibacterial 
assays showed that almost 100% bacteria were killed at lower concentration (100–150 μM) within a short time 
(30–60 min), providing a rapid and effective killing outcome for Gram-negative bacteria. After that, antibacterial 
mechanism was mainly investigated at cellular level via destruction of membrane integrity, disruption of anti­
oxidant defense system, metabolic inactivation, DNA damage, as well as at molecular level via transcriptome 
analysis (RNA sequencing) for the first time. RNA sequencing results showed that differentially expressed genes 
(DEGs) related to biosynthesis of cell wall and membrane, glycolysis and TCA cycle, oxidative phosphorylation 
and DNA replication and repair were significantly affected. It was concluded that synergetic effect of membrane 
damage, oxidative stress, DNA damage and energy metabolism eventually led to the Gram-negative bacteria 
growth inhibition and death.   
1. Introduction 
Over the last decades, the abuse of antibiotics has significantly 
increased the spread of antibiotic-resistant pathogens, causing the 
serious global health problem as well as environmental pollution (Crofts 
et al., 2017), especially in water, wastewater and food (including 
aquatic and terrestrial animal feed). Therefore, there is an urgent de­
mand for new alternatives towards traditional antibacterial agents. The 
recent developments of nanotechnology have opened up new opportu­
nities of different nanomaterials in antimicrobial therapy as they can fill 
the gaps where antibiotics frequently fail (Makabenta et al., 2020). 
Many different families of antibacterial nanoparticles (also called 
“nanoantibiotics”) have been reported to control and combat bacterial 
infection both in vitro and in vivo, including carbon-based nanomaterials 
(Xin et al., 2019), metal nanoparticles (Slavin et al., 2017), semi­
conductor nanoparticles (Karunakaran et al., 2018) and polymeric 
nanostructures (Rahman et al., 2020). Among these nanoantibiotics, 
metal nanoparticles (such as gold, silver, copper and titanium) exhibit 
favorable physicochemical characteristics such as direct interaction 
with phospholipid bilayer, binding to cytosolic proteins and formation 
of reactive oxygen species, resulting in significant levels of antibacterial 
activity (Gold et al., 2018).

Many researchers have tried to redesign metal nanoparticles by 
controlling the size (Dai et al., 2020; Fu et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2019), 
shape (Alshareef et al., 2017) and surface chemistry (Zheng et al., 
2018a) to decrease the ecological toxicity. Silver-based nanoparticles 
are the most extensively used antibacterial agents in both Gram-positive 
and Gram-negative bacteria for water disinfection (Deshmukh et al., 
2019), wound healing (Ye et al., 2019) and food packaging industries 
(Sarwar et al., 2018). However, the great challenge of Ag nanoparticles 
was accompanied by worse biocompatibility and safety concerns for its 
clinical application. In this regard, gold as a noblest metal is superior to 
silver due to its bioinertia and high stability (Hammer and Norskov, 
1995), leading to the excellent biocompatibility and low toxicity to the 
environment. Just because of this property, it had been assumed that Au 
nanoparticles (Au NPs) themselves exhibited unsatisfactory antibacte­
rial effects, except grafting antimicrobial compounds on its surface 
(Zheng et al., 2017). However, Zheng et al. have firstly reported that the 
ultra-small Au nanoclusters (Au NCs) can also show similar antibacterial 
activity to Ag nanoparticles without functionalization by precise con­
trolling the size of bulk Au NPs (~10 nm) to nanocluster range (<2 nm). 
Afterwards, more recent works were focused on the investigations on 
dealing with antibacterial activity of Au NCs modified by various ligands 
such as antimicrobial peptide (Zheng et al., 2019), enzymes (Kalita 
et al., 2018), peptides (Prada et al., 2019) and so forth (Xie et al., 2018; 
Yougbare et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2018b). 
In present work, a stable 6-mercaptohexanoic acid (MHA)-protected 
Au NCs containing 25 Au atoms and 18 thiolate ligands (Au25 NCs) was 
prepared by one-pot reducing Au (III) to Au (0). And the antibacterial 
activity against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria were 
evaluated by using growth curves and viable bacteria counting. Ac­
cording to the results of antibacterial effects, we focused on the effect of 
Au25 NCs on the membrane structure and integrity of bacterial cells by 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM), live/dead cell staining assay, 
fluorescence microscopy; the disruption of antioxidant defense systems 
by the increase of intracellular ROS level and decrease of glutathione 
(GSH); the metabolic inactivation by Alamar Blue assay and measure­
ment of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH); and the DNA damage. Finally, 
transcriptome analysis of differential gene expression were utilized to 
shed light on the potential molecular mechanisms that involved in 
antibacterial activity of Au25 NCs against Gram-negative bacteria from a 
global perspective. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials 
Unless other noted, all reagents were obtained from commercial 
suppliers and used without further purification. Ultrapure water with a 
specific resistance of 18.2 MΩ was used throughout the experiment. The 
microorganisms were obtained from the Guangdong Microbial Culture 
Collection Center, Guangzhou, China. 
2.2. Preparation of Au25 NCs 
A typical synthesis followed published procedures with some modi­
fications outlined below (Chen et al., 2018). Briefly, a freshly prepared 
aqueous solution of HAuCl4⋅3H2O (20 mM, 5 mL) and MHA solution (5 
mM, 40 mL) were mixed with 47 mL of ultrapure water under vigorous 
stirring. After stirring for 60 min, the color of opaque solution was 
changed from dark yellow to light yellow, indicating the formation of Au 
(I)-MHA complexes. Then, 6 mL of NaOH (1 M) was added to form a 
colorless and transparent solution. Afterwards, 2 mL of freshly prepared 
NaBH4 solution (110 mM in 0.2 M NaOH) was introduced into above 
solution to proceed under gentle stirring (500 rpm) at room temperature 
for 42 h. The UV-Vis spectroscopy of reaction solution at different time 
intervals were measured to determine the extent of the reaction. The 
final product were purified and concentrated by triple ultrafiltration 
with a molecular weight cuf-off of 8000 Da. According to the reported 
molar absorption coefficient (ε) of Au25 NCs at 670 nm (ε = 8.8 × 103 
M−1⋅cm−1) (Yu et al., 2012), the concentration and yield of raw product 
could be calculated on the basis of Au. 
2.3. Instruments for Au25 NCs characterization 
UV-Vis absorption spectra of solution were recorded on Lambda 25 
UV/Vis spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer, USA) in the range of 
300–1100 nm. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were obtained 
over the wavelength region from 4000 to 500 cm−1 with a resolution of 
2 cm−1 using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS500 apparatus. The particle 
size distribution was determined in water solution by a particle analyzer 
(Delsa Nano C, Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). Transmission elec­
tron microscopy (TEM) were taken on Talos L120C (FEI, Japan) mi­
croscope operating at 120 kV. High resolution transmission electron 
microscopy (HR-TEM) was performed on a JEM-2100 (JEOL, Japan) at 
an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The molecular formula of Au25 NCs 
was determined by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) 
on a Bruker micro TOF-Q system operating in the negative ion mode. 
The optical density at 600 nm (OD600) and fluorescence intensity were 
measured on a SpectraMax M3 (Molecular Devices, USA) microplate 
reader. The fluorescent images were taken with a fluorescence micro­
scope (Olympus IX71, Olympus, Japan). Polyacrylamide gel electro­
phoresis (PAGE) was performed on EPS 300 with a Mini-Protean Tetra 
Cell system (VE 180, Tanon, Shanghai) at 150 V for 4 h in the fridge. 
2.4. Antibacterial assays 
2.4.1. Instant antibacterial effect 
The typical Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli and A. baumannii) and 
Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus and B. subtilis) were used as model 
organisms in the following study. The instant antibacterial effect of each 
kind of bacteria were assayed by colony counting method according to 
the literature with some modifications (Zhao et al., 2017). Briefly, 100 
μL of diluted bacterial suspensions with a concentration of 2 × 108 
CUF/mL were resuspended in 900 μL PBS containing 25, 50, 100, 150, 
and 200 μM Au25 NCs (on the basis of Au atoms), respectively. After 
incubation at 37 ◦C for 30, 60, 90 and 120 min, the bacterial cells were 
taken out and serially diluted with sterile water, spread onto the LB agar 
plates and incubated at 37 ◦C overnight. The survival of the bacteria was 
determined by plate counting. All treatments were performed three 
times in parallel. 
2.4.2. Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and 
minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) 
MIC and MBC of as-prepared Au NCs were determined by a standard 
96-well microbroth dilution method reported previously (Karunakaran 
et al., 2018). PBS was used to prepare two-fold serial dilution of the 
working solution concentrations (Au25 NCs). In brief, 200 μL of Au25 NCs 
with the highest concentration were added to the first hole. After adding 
100 μL PBS into the other holes, 100 μL of liquid were taken out from the 
first hole to mix into the second hole, leading to the twice dilution of 
Au25 NCs. The same way was used to double dilution for the rest holes 
until the last two holes. 100 μL of the solutions in the last second holes 
were directly removed after mixing, instead of mixing to the next holes, 
and the last ones were the control group with 100 μL PBS. Finally, 100 μL 
of bacteria diluted by culture medium were added to each hole with a 
final concentration of 106 CUF/mL, and incubated at 37 ◦C with a 
shaking for 12 h. The growth curves of bacteria were monitored by 
measuring OD600 in a microplate reader. The MIC value was defined as 
the lowest concentration of which there was no rise in the growth 
curves. For MBC determination, after the measurement of growth 
curves, 10 μL of the treated bacteria suspensions of each group were 
taken out and streaked on the agar plates to allow the bacteria growing 
at 37 ◦C. The MBC value was defined as the lowest concentration of

2.5. Antibacterial mechanism assays 
Membrane integrity of treated and untreated bacteria were investi­
gated by morphology observation, LIVE/DEAD bacterial staining, and 
protein leakage assay. The morphology changes of bacteria were 
observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at an electron accel­
eration voltage of 5.0 kV on S-4800 SEM (Hitachi, Japan). SYTO 9 and 
propidium iodide (PI) fluorescence imaging assay was utilized to reflect 
the membrane integrity of bacterial cells on a fluorescence microscope. 
The concentration of leaked proteins were determined by BCA Protein 
Quantification Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, China). The oxidative 
stress was evaluated by the measurement of intracellular ROS genera­
tion and activity of intracellular GSH, which could be measured by in­
cubation of bacteria with 2,7-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) 
and 5,5-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) respectively. The meta­
bolic activity of bacteria was measured by Alamar Blue assay and the 
activity of respiratory dehydrogenase enzyme. The genomic and plasmid 
DNA were extracted from bacteria by Takara MiniBEST Bacterial 
Genomic DNA Extraction Kit and Plasmid Purification Kit (Takara Bio 
Inc., Japan) according to the protocol of the manufacturer, respectively. 
The details of above experimental procedures were given in the Sup­
plementary Information. 
2.6. Transcriptome analysis 
To evaluate the transcriptional changes of bacteria in response to 
Au25 NCs, E. coli were treated with Au25 NCs for 0, 20, 40, 60 min, 
respectively. Total RNA was then extracted by TRIzol-based method 
(Life Technologies, CA, USA). RNA quality control was performed with 
agarose gel electrophoresis by NanoPhotometer® spectrophotometer 
(IMPLEN, CA, USA) and Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Santa 
Clara, CA). cDNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina sequencing 
platform by Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Synthesis of Au nanoclusters 
Au25(SR)18 is the most widely used Au NCs because of its good sta­
bility in aqueous solution, which is composed of an icosahedral Au13 
core and six [SR-Au(I)-SR-Au(I)-SR] protecting motifs (Omoda et al., 
2018). In this study, we chose 6-mercaptohexanoic acid (MHA) as the 
model thiol ligand for the synthesis of Au25(SR)18 through the reduction 
of basic NaBH4. As shown in Fig. 1A, the UV-Vis spectrum can be used to 
identify the formation of ultra-small Au25 NCs. Before the addition of 
NaBH4, the solution did not show any absorption peak, which was 
ascribe to the formation of Au (I)-MHA complexes. The color of the 
solution changed from yellow (the color of Au (III)) to colorless (the 
color of Au (I)). On the contrary, four different absorption peaks could 
be observed at 400, 450, 670, 770 nm, respectively, in the presence of 
reducing agent, which are the characteristic absorption of Au25 NCs (Yu 
et al., 2012). Moreover, with the extension of reaction time, the peak at 
450 and 670 nm became increasingly evident (Fig. S1A and S1B), further 
indicating the high purity of as-prepared Au25 NCs in the raw product. 
The estimated yield (~93%) of Au25 NCs was determined by absorbance 
value of raw product at 670 nm according to the reported molar ab­
sorption coefficient of Au25 NCs (Yu et al., 2012). The color of the so­
lution also changed from colorless to light yellow, brown and 
red-brown, which is another simple way to identify the formation of 
Au25 NCs. Unlike Au25 NCs, Au NPs with burgundy color exhibited only

3.2. Antibacterial effect of ultral-small Au25 NCs 
The antimicrobial activity of Au25 NCs was firstly evaluated by 
instant antimcirobial effect after the fixed concentraion of bacteria were 
treated with different time and concentration. As shown in Fig. 2, the 
antibacterial effects of Au25 NCs on different bacteria (Gram-negative 
and Gram-positive) were quite different. The overall viability of Gram- 
negative bacteria demonstrated time- and concentration-dependent 
antibacterial activity of Au25 NCs (Fig. 2A and B). It is clearly noticed 
that killing efficiency enhanced with increase in both time and con­
centration of Au25 NCs. In particular, E. coli were significantly more 
vulnerable to Au25 NCs than A. baumannii. Almost 40% of E. coli was 
killed after treated with 100 μM Au25 NCs for 30 min. However, only 
10% of viable bacteria were remained after 60 min. No bacteria were 
grown in agar plate after treated for 60 min (100 μM), as well as the 
concentration of Au25 NCs that reached to 150 μM (Fig. 2E). In order to 
verify the antibacterial effect originated from ultra-small structure, both 
Au (I)-MHA complexes and Au NPs were introduced to interact with 
E. coli. A minimum killing effect was observed at the concentraion of Au 
atom extremely higher than that of Au25 NCs, as can be seen in Fig. S4, 
illustrating that the enhanced antibacterial effect was dependent on the 
overall ultra-small Au NCs rather than the ligand or other structures. 
Nevertheless, the antibacterial effect of Au25 NCs against Gram-positive 
bacteria were not observed obviously even in a long time and high 
concentration (Fig. 2C and D), and a large number of viable colonies 
grew well on the agar plates both in the control group and treatment 
group (Fig. 2E), suggesting the worse binding efficiency between Au25 
NCs and Gram-positive bacteria. The different antibacterial effect of 
Au25 NCs was in good agreement with previously reported work, which 
is ascribe to the different membrane structure between Gram-negative 
and Gram-positive bacterial cell walls (Zhu et al., 2019). 
Then, the antibacterial activity was evaluated by measuring the 
growth curve with the increasing concentration of Au25 NCs. As shown 
in Fig. S5, the growth curves clearly exhibited a dose-dependent anti­
bacterial activity of synthesized Au25 NCs against Gram-negative bac­
teria, which was in line with the number of colonies grown on LB plates 
of instant antibacterial effect. The minimum inhibitory concentration 
(MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) were obtained 
through the growth curves and colony-forming ability. As we can see 
from Table 1, the MIC and MBC values of Au25 NCs against E. coli were 
found to be 50 and 100 μM, respectively, at which no bacteria grew and 
the viability was reduced to zero. Similarly, we also obtained the MIC 
and MBC values of A. baumannii. In addition, the MBC/MIC ratio was 
calculated to be much less than 4, which was widely used as a mean­
ingful parameter to evaluate the bactericidal potential of any antibac­
terial agent (Roy et al., 2019). However, for Gram-positive bacteria, 
Au25 NCs did’t show any inhibitory action in our concentration range. 
The same results were also obtained in other reported Au NCs with 
different ligands (Table S1). Yang et al. had prepared a DNA scaffolded 
super-nanoclusters (super-Ag NCs) with enhanced antibacterial effect 
against E. coli, but they showed slight inhibitory effects on the growth of 
S. aureus due to the different composition of cell walls (Yang et al., 
2018). Besides, it had been reported that bovine serum albumin and 
papain directed Au NCs also didn’t show any antibacterial effect toward 
S. aureus (Wang et al., 2019). However, Zheng et al. (2017) had revealed 
the size dependent antibacterial properties of Au NCs against S. aureus, 
as well as wide-spectrum antimicrobial activity. The different antibac­
terial results may ascribe to the aggregation of Au NCs in a extended 
reaction time (42 h), resulting the size of more than 2 nm. Nevertheless, 
compared with other reported Au NCs (Table S2), the MIC in this work is 
lower than that bovine serum albumin or quaternary ammonium salts 
protected Au NCs (Xie et al., 2018), illustrating the excellent antibac­
terial effect of Au25 NCs against Gram-negative bacteria. 
3.3. Antibacterial mechanism of Au25 NCs 
Research on antibacterial mechanism is essential for the develop­
ment of clinically available nanoantibiotics as well as some antibacterial 
products (e.g. coating, dressing, membranes and hydrogels). Given this 
situation, the antibacterial mechanism was systematically investigated 
at the cellular and molecular levels, respectively. Previous studies on the 
antibacterial behavior of metal-based nanoparticles have shown that 
multiple mechanisms play important roles in their antibacterial activity 
(Gold et al., 2018). These metal nanoparticles always interact with 
bacterial cell via three major pathways: (1) interactions with phospho­
lipid bilayer; (2) binding to cytosolic proteins; (3) formation of reactive 
oxygen species (ROS). There is no exception in the antibacterial mech­
anism of ultra-small Au25 NCs. Base on it, membrane integrity, meta­
bolic activity, oxidative stress and DNA damage were performed to 
illustrate the interaction between Au25 NCs and bacterial cells. 
3.3.1. Membrane integrity 
As an essential component in all bacterial cells, the cell membrane or 
cell wall is vital for the protection of bacteria. Once the bacterial outer 
membrane or cell wall is damaged by physical or chemical action, the 
components in cytoplasm will leakage outside the cell, leading to the 
bacteriostatic and bactericidal effects. It has been reported that Au25 
NCs possess the intrinsic enzyme mimicking activity and can damage 
bacterial outer membranes (Zheng et al., 2018b). Therefore, we exam­
ined the membrane integrity through Live/Dead cell fluorescence 
staining assays after incubation the E. coli and S. aureus with Au25 NCs. 
Here, the bacteria were stained with both propidium iodide (PI) and 
SYTO 9, displaying red fluorescence and green fluorescence, respec­
tively. PI can be used to label the dead cells because of the strong 
DNA-binding ability after cell membrane is damaged, whereas SYTO 9 
can label all bacteria whether they are alive or dead (Parandhaman 
et al., 2015). As shown in Fig. 3A–H, it could be seen that two bacterial

3.3.2. Oxidative stress 
Generation of oxidative stress is a highly recognized antibacterial 
mechanism of metal nanoparticles through oxidative damage to essen­
tial cellular components, such as proteins and nucleic acids (Gold et al., 
2018; Parandhaman et al., 2015). To examine the oxidative stress 
induced by Au25 NCs, ROS generation was measured using a highly 
specific fluorescent probe (DCFH-DA), which can penetrate into cells 
without fluorescence and is hydrolyzed by enzymes to produce DCFH. In 
the presence of ROS, DCFH is easily oxidized to yield DCF with green 
fluorescence. As shown in Fig. 4A, ROS level of E. coli treated with Au25 
NCs still displayed an obvious dose-dependent oxidation behavior. 
When the concentration of Au in nanoclusters reached 100 μM, ROS 
level increased approximately 1.5-fold compared with the control 
group. A nearly 5-fold increase in ROS production was observed after 
treated at 4×MIC (200 μM). For S. aureus, only a 1.5-fold increase was 
noticed even at higher concentration. Thus the growth of Gram-negative 
bacteria was efficiently inhibited by augmenting the ROS level. 
Reduced GSH is an important intracellular molecule in bacterial 
antioxidant defense system, which can protect cells from toxic com­
pounds, and it has become an indicator of intracellular oxidative stress 
(Choudhary et al., 2017). It has been reported that excessive ROS inside 
the cells could induce the disequilibrium of prooxidant-antioxidant 
system, resulting the oxidation of GSH (-SH) to glutathione disulfide 
(-S-S-) (Choudhary et al., 2017). The produced glutathione disulfide is 
incapable of protecting cells from oxidative damage, ultimately leading 
to cell death. In this study, the concentration of thiol groups in GSH can 
be quantitatively detected using Ellman’s assay, and the formation of 
glutathione disulfide can be measured by the loss of GSH (Cao et al., 
2019). As shown in Fig. 4B, the results demonstrated that E. coli treated 
with increasing concentration of Au25 NCs exhibited a progressive loss of 
GSH. For E. coli treated at MIC, the loss of GSH was ~10% and increased 
significantly to 25%, 40% and 45% on treatment at 2×MIC, 3×MIC and 
4×MIC, respectively. However, little effect on the loss of GSH in 
S. aureus was observed even at 200 μM, which is consistent with the 
results of ROS generation. The intracellular ROS production and 
depletion of GSH verifies that ROS-mediated oxidative stress play a 
significant role in the antimicrobial action of Au25 NCs against 
Gram-negative bacteria. 
3.3.3. Metabolic activity 
The bacteria undergoing treatment of different nanomaterials can 
result in intracellular abnormal energy metabolic. Therefore, it is 
necessary to investigate the metabolic activity of bacteria interacting 
with Au25 NCs. The Alamar Blue (Resazurin) assay was firstly used to 
evaluate the overall bacterial metabolic activity. The assay is based on 
the fact that purple resazurin can be converted to its pink form resofurin 
only by metabolically active cells, which is achieved by functionally 
active enzymes in viable cells (Roy et al., 2019). The results depicted in 
Fig. 4C showed that the metabolic activity of E. coli decreased with the 
increase of Au25 NCs concentration. Only 50% metabolic activity 
remained at MIC, and it decreased to 20% at 2×MIC, which ultimately 
rendered them metabolically inactive. In addition, we further measured 
the activity of respiratory chain dehydrogenase, which is critical 
important to form integral bacterial respiratory chain and participate in 
the proper functioning of respiratory metabolism of bacteria (Roy et al., 
2019). Among them, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is known to be a 
peripheral membrane respiratory enzyme that helps cells maintain 
redox balance, and is located on the cytoplasmic side of the inner 
membrane. The enzymatic activity of dehydrogenase was determined by 
the conversion of colorless iodonitrotetrazolium chloride (INT) to dark 
red iodonitrotetrazolium formazan (INF) under the catalysis of LDH. As 
shown in Fig. 4D, the remained LDH activity in living bacterial cells is 
almost 100% after incubated for 4 h without Au25 NCs, while the treated 
cells exhibited viable enzymatic activity. For E. coli, a 20% decrease in 
LDH activity was observed after treated with 2×MIC of Au25 NCs. With 
the increase of concentration, the activity of LDH reduced gradually and 
reached to 50% at 4×MIC. Further prolonging the exposure time be­
tween bacteria and Au25 NCs could decrease LDH activity due to the 
damage or leakage of enzymes. For S. aureus, the bacterial cell after 
treated with high concentration of Au25 NCs did not exhibit any meta­
bolic arrest. The results therefore indicated that Au25 NCs could hinder 
the enzymatic function of respiratory chain dehydrogenase, and in turn, 
cause fatal damage to Gram-negative bacteria. 
3.3.4. DNA damage 
It is widely accepted that metal nanomaterials could easily enter 
inside the bacterial cells once the membrane integrity has been 
destroyed, and then interfere with DNA replication either directly or 
indirectly, so as to achieve the antibacterial effects (Parandhaman et al., 
2015). For one thing, the metal ions were able to directly bind to 
intracellular DNA to intercalate into the DNA. For another, the genera­
tion of ROS could also lead to the DNA damage (Gold et al., 2018). In 
order to further understand the antibacterial mechanism of Au25 NCs 
against Gram-negative bacteria, the possibility of DNA damage was 
analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Both plasmid and genomic DNA 
were extracted from treated and untreated cells. Fig. 4E represented the 
DNA fragmentation in bacterial cells after treated with Au25 NCs at 
different concentrations. The electrophoresis pattern clearly showed a 
bright and compact DNA band (Lane 1) for control group, indicating the 
existence of macromolecule DNA, whereas both plasmid and genomic 
DNA extracted from E. coli treated with Au25 NCs showed variable band 
intensities depending on the concentration of Au25 NCs (Lane 2 to Lane 
5). An obvious DNA damage was occurred when the concentration of 
Au25 NCs was 100 μM (Lane 3), and the extensive damage was noticed at 
150 μM (Lane 4) or higher concentration (Lane 5) of Au25 NCs with 
abundant DNA fragments. However, no changes of DNA band was 
observed for S. aureus, indicating the negligible damage of Au25 NCs 
against Gram-positive bacteria. These results demonstrated that Au25 
NCs, in addition to damage the cell membrane of Gram-negative bac­
teria, could also affect nucleic acids of bacterial cells to destruct 
macromolecule DNA into small fragments, subsequently interfering the 
biological function (Li et al., 2019). 
3.4. Transcriptome sequence analysis 
According to the above results, we concluded that the main anti­
bacterial mechanism of Au25 NCs at cellular level was induced by 
destruction of membrane integrity, disruption of antioxidant defense 
system, metabolic inactivation, as well as DNA damage. Therefore, to 
gain further insight into the antibacterial mechanism of Au25 NCs at the 
molecular level, the whole transcriptome RNA sequencing was utilized 
to assess the specific response at mRNA level. Before the sequencing, the 
quality of RNA was firstly evaluated by the rRNA ratio (23S/16S) and 
RNA Integrity Number (RIN) (Fig. S7). The obtained Aligen electro­
pherograms showed that only degraded RNA was observed for E. coli 
after long-term exposure (>20 min) to Au25 NCs, proving that antibac­
terial activity could be achieved through RNA damage, just like DNA 
damage induced antibacterial mechanism. Based on this result, tran­
scriptome analysis was subsequently performed on Au25 NCs-treated 
samples with treatment time of 20 min, as well as the control samples. 
As shown in Table S3, after quality filtering of raw reads with low 
quality, over 1.4 × 109 clean reads of all samples were obtained with 
average quality values ≥30 (Q30) for more than 95% of the sequence. In 
addition, more than 98% of clean reads were mapped to the reference 
genome sequences, indicating the successful library construction and 
RNA sequencing.

membrane of E. coli were pretty affected by Au25 NCs (shown in Fig. 6).

wall structures were significantly down-regulated in response to Au25

3.4.2. Genes related to energy metabolism 
Energy metabolism plays a vital role in molecular functions, bio­
logical process, physiological behavior, growth and even survival of 
organisms. However, the balance of energy in the organism will be 
seriously affected by environmental stress, which requires additional 
energy to maintain or restore the homeostasis (Wang et al., 2018). In 
most organisms, the glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA 
cycle) are the vital pathways for energy production. And they also plays 
a significant role in anabolism, and are necessary to the synthesis of 
compounds such as fatty acids, carbohydrates and amino acids (Kor­
otetskiy et al., 2020). As shown in Fig. 7, it was found that glycolysis 
(ko00010) and TCA cycle (ko00020) in E. coli were significantly affected 
by Au25 NCs treatment. Among them, 20 of 29 glycolysis genes were 
down-regulated, as well as 21 of 26 TCA related genes. In particular, 
sucB encoding 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase complex for the conver­
sion of 2-oxoglutarate to succinyl-CoA, sdhA and sdhB encoding the 
succinate dehydrogenase for the catalysis of fumarate to succinate were 
significantly down-regulated in TCA cycle pathway. The results pre­
sented a general inhibition of both pathways, indicating a decrease in 
amino acid biosynthesis activity and oxidative phosphorylation (Kor­
otetskiy et al., 2020). Besides, the expression of genes related to pyru­
vate metabolism (ko00620) were significantly down-regulated 
including the NADP-dependent malic enzyme (maeA), aldehyde dehy­
drogenase (aldB) and malate dehydrogenase (mdh), which provided the 
precursor for de novo fatty acid biosynthesis (He et al., 2019). Taken 
together, we concluded that Au25 NCs could disrupt energy metabolism 
to inhibit the growth of bacteria. 
3.4.3. Genes related to oxidative stress 
Oxidative stress response of pathogens toward external stimulus is 
generally used to understand their acclimatization mechanisms, and to 
elucidate stress signaling and regulatory molecular processes (Maurya 
et al., 2020). In the present study, based on the GO enrichment analysis, 
all five up-expressed DEGs related to the “response to oxidative stress” 
were significantly enriched in the category of BP (shown in Fig. 5). 
Besides, 10 of 11 significantly down-regulated genes involved in 
oxidative phosphorylation (ko00190) were observed in the Au25 
NCs-treated group (Fig. 8A). As we all know, oxidative phosphorylation 
is an intracellular biochemical process that can form proton gradient and 
eventually lead to the conversion of ADP to ATP when substance is 
oxidized (Wang et al., 2020). However, bacteria had to decrease 
oxidative phosphorylation under the stimulus of Au25 NCs, so that they 
could adapt the stressful environment they were in. Simultaneously, 
oxidative phosphorylation further led to the accumulation of

3.4.4. Genes related to DNA replication and repair 
Previous study has shown that the production of ROS can cause se­

the death of bacteria. Therefore, some of genes related to DNA synthesis, 
replication and repair have been found to be differentially expressed. It 
is well known that folate, as a methyl donors, plays an important role in 
the synthesis of DNA nucleotides (e.g. thymine) (Wang et al., 2020). In 
this study, the expression of pabC gene participated in the folate 
synthesis was down-regulated in the Au25 NCs-treated group, indicating 
that Au25 NCs treatment could bring folate deficiency to E. coli, and then 
lead to the nucleic acid damage (Wang et al., 2020). Besides, the DNA 
replication and repair systems is also crucial for microbial survival.

positive bacteria. The as-prepared Au25 NCs were found to have excel­
lent potential to cause instant bactericidal behavior and long-term

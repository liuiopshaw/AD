a b s t r a c t
Antibiotic resistance is considered as one of the greatest health threats worldwide, and we are in a staring
competition with microbes as antibiotic resistance mounts faster than our current rate of developing new
and effective antibiotics. Therefore, newer metal-based antimicrobial agents with easily tuned physico-
chemical properties have been developed to ﬁght against these antibiotic resistant bacteria. In this
review, we begin with describing the mode of action of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) in damaging the bac-
terial extracellular membrane and their intracellular components that allows them to exhibit wide spec-
trum antimicrobial effect. The review also contains our insights on understanding not only the correlation
between the NPs’ physicochemical properties and their bactericidal mode of action but also the possible
strategies to tune these physicochemical properties to optimize their bactericidal properties. The second
focus of this review is on the emerging and highly efﬁcient antimicrobial agents, ultrasmall Ag nanoclus-
ters (Ag NCs). Ag NCs are ultrasmall NPs with core sizes less than 2 nm, and they contain ‘‘countable” Ag
atoms as the core, which is protected by a certain number of organic ligands. The atomically precise prop-
erty of Ag NCs provides a good platform to design and manipulate Ag NCs at atomic level to achieve opti-
mized antimicrobial efﬁcacy, which also favor the antimicrobial mechanism study.

1. Introduction
Antibiotic resistance is considered as one of the greatest health
threats by the World Health Organization (WHO) [1,2]. The ﬁrst
case of bacterial resistance could be traced back to 1967 in which
penicillin resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumonia) was
reported in Australia. In 1995, penicillin resistance was recorded
for 23.6% of total S. pneumoniae isolates found in the US, and by
2000, this number has reached 85% of total S. pneumoniae isolates
[3]. To date, the resistance is neither restricted to a certain bacteria
strain nor against a speciﬁc antibiotic. Centers for Disease Control
and Prevention (CDC) recorded that more than 70% of all bacterial
causing infections could resist at least one of the main antimicrobial
agents currently used in the clinic [4,5]. Moreover, antibiotic resis-
tance is not an isolated problem of any particular country. Global
surveillance data collected by WHO identiﬁed approximately 79%
of bacteria have developed resistance to one or more antibiotics
[6]. Each year, in the US alone, more than 2 million cases of illness
and 23,000 deaths are attributed to these antibiotic resistant bacte-
ria and the numbers are expected to be increasing over the years
[7]. Similarly, it has been estimated that each year close to 25,000
people in Europe and 700,000 people worldwide die due to drug
resistant bacterial infections [8,9]. The other real impact due to
antibiotic resistance is not only limited to the increase in the com-
plication risks and mortality rates, but also includes the increase in
the healthcare cost. This antibiotic resistance has been estimated to
rake over $20 billion in healthcare cost per year (and close to $35
billion cost for productivity) in the US [4]. Undoubtedly, the ideal
solution to this antibiotic resistance problem would be the develop-
ment of new classes of antibiotics in this arms race with bacterial
evolution. Nevertheless, new antibiotics usually take decades to
develop, making it impossible to curtail this pressing antibacterial
resistance problem any time soon. In addition, it is a costly process
and the new antibiotics will only be effective for a limited time
before the inevitable resistance sets in again. This has economically
inhibited pharmaceutical companies from developing new classes
of antibiotics [10,11]. As such, there is an urgent need to develop
an alternative antimicrobial agent that is cost-effective and power-
ful enough to be ahead of the bacterial evolution.
Metals like silver (Ag), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), and magnesium
(Mg) have been used to treat diseases long before the pharmaceu-
tical antibiotic revolution. In addition, there is no single bacterial
adaptation strategy that could provide them with universal resis-
tance to all these metals, suggesting even if one antimicrobial
metal failed, other metallic-based antibiotics could step in and ﬁll
in the gap [12,13]. More importantly, in the wake of nanotechnol-
ogy advancement, material scientists could control the physico-
chemical properties of these metal nanomaterials to produce
effective antimicrobial agents without exerting toxicity to the
human patient [14,15]. Taking these into consideration, antimicro-
bial metal nanoparticles (NPs) could be the solution to the pressing
antimicrobial resistance that we are now facing. To date, a new
class of ‘nanometallo-antibiotics’ consisting of numerous metal
NPs have been produced and investigated for their antimicrobial
properties [16–18]. This review discusses one of the most promis-
ing nanometallo-antibiotics, Ag nanomaterials. These Ag nanoma-
terials have been well-developed in the ﬁeld for the past two
decades, and their effectiveness as antimicrobial agents has been
well-documented in the literature. In addition, a number of efﬁ-
cient synthetic strategies have been recently developed toward
achieving Ag nanomaterials with well-deﬁned (and controlled)
attributes and physicochemical features [19–27]. Despite the
wealth of information on the Ag NPs’ physicochemical features
and the NPs antimicrobial activities, the link between these issues
is not clearly elucidated. Hence, the discussions present in this
review will focus on understanding the role of Ag NPs’ physico-
chemical properties in determining their antimicrobial efﬁcacy.
In this review, we begin with describing the Ag NPs’ mode of
action in damaging the bacterial extracellular membrane and their
intracellular components, allowing these NPs to exhibit wide spec-
trum antimicrobial effect. The review also contains our insights on
understanding not only the correlation of these physicochemical
properties to the Ag NPs’ bactericidal mode of action but also the
possible strategies to tune the Ag NPs’ physicochemical properties
to optimize their bactericidal properties. With this understanding,
we hope to provide a mechanistic framework to further tune the
Ag NPs’ antimicrobial efﬁcacy, giving the next generation Ag NPs
a ﬁghting chance in overcoming the continually evolving resis-
tance of microbial pathogens. In-depth and detailed mechanistic
discussions of the anti-bactericidal mode of action can be found
elsewhere [12]. Although human toxicity is an integral part in
assessing the application of Ag NPs as antimicrobial agents, we
would like to refer the readers to other recent reviews on the
related topic [28–30].
As the physicochemical properties of any NPs would dictate the
initial interactions with cells, the ability to tune the NPs’ physico-
chemical properties with high precision is therefore a big advan-
tage
in
deciphering
the
nanometallo-antibiotics
effects
on
bacteria. Emerging over the horizon of the synthesis control of
Ag NPs are atomically precise Ag nanoclusters (or NCs at atomic
precision). Ag NCs are ultrasmall NPs with core sizes less than 2
nm, and they contain ‘‘countable” Ag atoms as the core, which is
protected by a certain number of organic ligands. Ag NCs hold dis-
crete electronic states and molecular-like properties, such as well-
deﬁned molecular structure, quantized charging, HOMO–LUMO
transitions, molecular magnetism, molecular chirality, and strong
luminescence [31–41]. In particular, Ag NCs show distinctly differ-
ent physicochemical properties from their larger counterparts, the
Ag NPs (particle size above 3 nm). Those unique physicochemical
properties would increase the acceptance of Ag NCs in many
biomedical applications, such as bioimaging, biosensing, and
antimicrobial agents [42–50]. For example, Ag NCs feature with
highly tunable antimicrobial efﬁciency that could be attributed to
their unique and tailorable physicochemical properties, including
ultrasmall size (high surface to volume ratio) and tunable Ag atoms
and surface ligands per Ag NC. The discussion on the design and
working mechanisms of Ag (and also gold (Au)) NCs-based antimi-
crobial agents forms the second focus of this review article.
2. Damages to bacterial membrane
Ag NPs could interact with the bacterial membrane, leading to
bacterial membrane damage, which would subsequently kill the
bacteria. Ag NPs would ﬁrst accumulate on the surface of bacterial
membrane, penetrate into the bacteria, and ﬁnally change the per-
meability of bacterial membrane, causing a substantial damage of
the membrane [51]. Therefore, Ag NPs’ physiochemical properties
that could facilitate the interaction between Ag NPs and the bacte-
rial membrane would become important to inﬂuence these NPs
capability to invoke bacterial damage. These parameters of Ag
NPs include size, shape, and surface. In particular, it was found that
Ag NPs featuring with small sizes, (1 1 1) facets, and certain pro-
tecting ligand layers could better interact with the bacteria, result-
ing in high efﬁcacy in damaging the bacterial membrane.
2.1. Membrane damage mechanism
In terms of working mechanisms of Ag NPs, as illustrated in
Fig. 1a, the Ag NPs begin with a tight attachment and accumulation

on the membrane surface, followed by their penetration into the
bacteria, which would ﬁnally kill the bacteria [51–53]. For
instance, Ag NPs with diameter up to 80 nm would be transported
through both the inner and outer membrane of Pseudomonas
aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa). As a result, most of the Ag NPs were
accumulated in the cytoplasmic space of the bacteria, while only
a small portion of Ag NPs could be seen on the bacterial membrane.
It was found that the accumulation rate of Ag NPs was depended
on the kinetics of the extrusion pump of the bacteria [54]. Ag
NPs would also accumulate around the bacterial membrane and
cause a certain damage of Escherichia coli (E. coli) through forma-
tion of pits on the cell wall, which could increase the permeability
of the wall [55]. In addition, Ag NPs would also affect the mem-
brane vesicles of E. coli. This was evident by the dissolution and
dispersion of the E. coli membrane vesicles upon their exposure
to 10 mg/mL of Ag NPs, where the disorganization and scattering
of cell membrane components were clearly observed [56].
In addition to the pits formation, the capability of Ag NPs in
damaging the cell membrane was also reﬂected from the leakage
of bacterial contents, such as reducing sugars and proteins, as
shown in Fig. 1b and c [56,57]. This has been veriﬁed by a recent
study, where green ﬂuorescent protein (GFP) expressing E. coli
were exposed to Ag NPs (10 nm). In this study, perforation on
the bacterial wall and a substantial increase of GFP content in
the medium were observed [58].
A direct evidence of the Ag NPs’ damaging effect on the bacterial
cell is the collapse of bacterial wall, which has been often observed
in various bacterial cell models (e.g., E. coli, Scrub typhus (S. typhus),
P. aeruginosa and Vibrio cholerae (V. cholerae)) exposed to the Ag
NPs [51]. The collapse of the bacterial cell wall is attributed to
the capability of Ag NPs in interacting with one of the bacterial cell
wall components. Indeed, the data from attenuated total reﬂec-
tance–Fourier transform infrared (ATR–FTIR) spectroscopy indicate
that Ag NPs could interact with the membrane biomolecules of
E. coli, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and L-a-phosphatidyl-etha
nolamine (PE) [59]. In particular, facilitated by the hydrogen bond-
ing formation, Ag NPs would interact with the outer leaﬂet
lipopolysaccharide (LPS). In addition, Ag NPs could also break the
phosphodiester bond within the PE, resulting in the phosphate
monoesters formation. This change would make the alkyl chain
highly disordered, causing the loss of inherent amphiphilic proper-
ties of PE, which could destroy the bacterial cell membrane and
ﬁnally lead to the leakage of the cell contents. A recent study also
suggests that Ag NPs could induce surface charge neutralization of
the bacterial membrane, thus altering the permeability of the bac-
terial membrane [60]. In particular, the atomic force microscopy
(AFM) data suggest that the membrane structure of the bacteria
drastically changed following the bacteria interaction with Ag
NPs, whereas the FTIR data indicate that an order-to-disorder tran-
sition of the bacterial membrane was occurred during the killing

Another recent mechanistic study also suggests that the capa-
bility of Ag NPs in damaging the bacterial cell wall could stem from
their interaction with the peptidoglycan layer of the cell wall

actions have led to the changes in the secondary structure (a-helix)

peptidoglycan building blocks, such as amino sugars and muramic

interaction of the peptidoglycan with Ag NPs or with the released
Ag+ ions. The involvement of Ag+ ions in the cell membrane dam-

by Feng et al. [62]. In their attempt to decouple the effect of the Ag
NPs and Ag+ ions, the authors exposed the bacterial cells to AgNO3,
and they observed the detachment of the cytoplasmic membrane
from the cell wall (Fig. 1d). As the thicker cell membrane would
act as a better barrier, the collapse of the S. aureus cell wall was
observed in lesser degree when compared to those in the E. coli.
Ag+ ions could induce a similar bacterial membrane collapse and
detachment as the Ag NPs, which indicates that the release of
Ag+ ions from Ag NPs could be a key factor that causes the bacterial
damage [63].
In addition to Ag NPs interaction with the cell wall components,
their membrane damaging capability could also be attributed to
their capability in producing free radical species, such as reactive
oxygen species (ROS), inside or outside the bacteria [64,65]. For
example, Kim et al. used the electron spin resonance spectroscopy
and found that the Ag NPs generated free radicals to inhibit the
bacterial growth by attacking the bacterial membrane [64].
Another convincing evidence was provided by Hwang et al., who
exposed the oxidative stress responsive bioluminescent E. coli to
Ag NPs (10 nm in diameter). The authors observed the increasing
promoter induction of the protein/membrane damage clpB gene,
which suggests that the Ag NPs have induced the membrane dam-
age using ROS. The involvement of ROS in inducing the bacterial
damage was demonstrated through the up-regulation of the gene
promoter responsible to suppress the superoxide species within
the cells, sodA. Moreover, the authors also observed the reduction
in the bioluminescent readout, after the addition of the superoxide
dismutase and catalase enzymes, which further supported the
involvement of ROS in the Ag NPs induced membrane damage [65].
Moreover, it was found that the Ag+ ions released from Ag NPs
could damage the function of bacterial membrane. In particular,
low concentration of Ag+ ions could induce the difference in pH
and electric potential (DpH and Dw) of the inside-out membrane
vesicles in V. cholerae, leading to the failure of transmembrane res-
piration and H+ leakage. Interestingly, the Na+ NADH: ubiquinone
oxidoreductase (NQR), a known speciﬁc target for Ag+, was not
involved in this process [66].
2.2. Physicochemical properties of Ag NPs governing membrane
damage
Recent studies have suggested that the Ag NPs damaging effect
on the bacterial membrane could be strongly correlated to their
physicochemical properties, such as size, shape and surface chem-
istry [67–69]. Moreover, it is clear from the mode of action (dis-
cussed above) that the main prerequisite for Ag NPs to induce
the bacterial membrane damage is the ability to interact with
the cell membrane. As such, efforts to optimize interactions of
the Ag NPs with the bacterial cell wall should start with the under-
standing of the inﬂuence of the physicochemical properties of Ag
NPs on their interactions with the bacteria surface.
2.2.1. Size
Size of the NPs is one of the most important aspects in deter-
mining their interaction behavior with cells [70]. The further the
Ag NPs size gets reduced, the higher surface to volume ratio these
NPs would possess. This in turn would allow the NPs to better
interact with the bacteria as compared to the same dose of their
larger
counterparts.
Indeed,
the
size-dependent
interaction
between the Ag NPs (size range 1–100 nm) and the bacteria was
observed in a recent study using high angel annular dark ﬁeld
(HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). Ag
NPs with diameter of 1–10 nm were found to be the most effective
in killing the bacteria due to their preferential direct interaction
with the bacteria membrane [51]. In a more recent study, the
size-dependent bactericidal effects of Ag NPs were found in NPs
with the same coating, but with different sizes from 5 to 100 nm.
The authors noted that the smallest Ag NPs (5 nm in diameter)
showed the highest antimicrobial efﬁciency as well as the fastest
bacterial killing behavior after their interaction with the bacteria
(Fig. 2a) [67]. These studies exempliﬁed the contributions of the
Ag NPs’ size to their interaction with the bacterial cell membrane
and the resultant bactericidal properties. As such, reducing size
of Ag NPs could be an efﬁcient way to achieve a high antimicrobial
efﬁcacy.
2.2.2. Shape
Shape of the Ag NPs could directly inﬂuence the available con-
tact area required to facilitate the interactions of Ag NPs with the
bacterial membrane. A comparative study conducted by using
polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP)-coated Ag NPs with different shapes
suggests a correlation between the shape of Ag NPs and their bac-
tericidal properties. For example, Ag nanoplates (two-dimensional
(2D) structure) showed the highest antimicrobial activity towards
S. aureus and E. coli, when compared to Ag nanorods (one-
dimensional
(1D)
structure)
and
spherical
Ag
NPs
(zero-
dimensional (0D) structure), as shown in Fig. 2b and c. It was pos-
tulated that Ag nanoplates hold the largest surface area, which
would provide the largest contact area to interact with the bacte-
rial cell wall, damage it, and ﬁnally kill the cells [71]. In addition
to inﬂuencing the necessary contact area with the bacterial cells,
the shapes of Ag NPs also inﬂuence the Ag atom distribution on
the NPs. It was found that Ag NPs with (1 1 1) facets possess a high
density of Ag atoms that are highly reactive against the bacterial
wall [51]. In a more recent work, truncated triangular Ag NPs with
(1 1 1) lattice plane exhibited the highest antimicrobial activity
against E. coli, when compared to the spherical and rod-shaped
Ag NPs. In particular, triangular Ag NPs contained mostly high-
atom-density (1 1 1) facets, showing high antimicrobial activity.
The above experimental evidences highlight the key role of Ag
NPs’ active facets in facilitating their interactions with the cell wall
[68].
2.2.3. Surface
Surface coating would affect the physicochemical properties of
Ag NPs. These properties include surface charge, binding tendency
towards bacteria, and aggregation and dissolution potential. There-
fore, surface coating on Ag NPs becomes one of the major aspects
to be optimized in designing the antimicrobial Ag NPs [72]. The

importance of surface capping was evidenced by the Ag NPs’
inability in damaging the bacterial membrane when they were
uncoated. Due to their naked (and active) surface, uncoated Ag
NPs would ﬁrst interact with the serum albumin (via a non-
speciﬁc protein binding) in the in vivo setting, resulting in the
absence of their antimicrobial efﬁciency [73]. The coating layers
outside the Ag NPs would create unique surface properties, and
the one with high afﬁnity towards the bacteria would lead to good
performance of Ag NPs as antimicrobial agents. For example, Kujda
et al. synthesized charge-stabilized and surfactant-free Ag NPs
with similar size, shape and surface charge. These Ag NPs, however,
had a different surface property, which was obtained through the
introduction of different reducing agents without other com-
pounds (i.e., polymeric stabilizing agents or surfactants) [74]. In
particular, Ag NPs reduced with the organic cell permeabilizers,
such as gallic acid and tannin, showed better antimicrobial efﬁ-
ciency as compared to the Ag NPs prepared with the inorganic cell
permeabilizers, such as sodium hypophosphite and sodium hex-
ametaphosphate. The authors suggested that the reduction treat-
ment with the gallic acid and tannin would facilitate a better
attachment of the Ag NPs to the bacterial surface. In addition,
owing to their intrinsic permeabilization properties, more Ag NPs
were able to penetrate into the bacteria. This suggests that the sur-
face capping identity is one crucial factor in determining the Ag
NPs’ antimicrobial properties.

example, a cationic surfactant, such as hyper-branched poly(ami-
doamine)
with
terminal
dimethylamine
groups
(PAMAM–N
(CH3)2), was designed and used as capping agent for Ag NPs. It
was found that the capping agent not only protected Ag NPs in

3. Damages to subcellular structures
It has been shown that Ag NPs would lose their antimicrobial
ability under strictly anaerobic conditions, where Ag(0) could not
be oxidized into Ag+. This is also valid in the treatment with high
concentration of Ag NPs (3-order of magnitude higher than the
minimum lethal concentration (MLC) of Ag NPs under the aerobic
condition), as shown in Fig. 3a [81]. Therefore, the generation and
release of Ag+ ions is one of the deterministic factors that could
inﬂuence the antimicrobial ability of Ag NPs. The Ag+ generation
and release process have been attributed to the generation of
ROS. The elevated ROS level is known to damage the DNA, proteins,
and enzymes in cells, which could in turn interfere with the normal
metabolism of the bacteria [82–84]. In addition, the released Ag+
ions and Ag NPs could also bind with these subcellular organelles,
leading to the perturbation of their functions in bacteria. In this
section, we will discuss the effects of the physicochemical proper-
ties of Ag NPs, including size, oxidation state, aggregation and dis-
solution process, and surface properties, on their interactions with
the subcellular structures of bacteria.
3.1. Mechanisms of subcellular function perturbation
The generation and release of Ag+ ions is often initiated with the
oxidation of Ag(0), which could in turn produce ROS and ﬁnally kill
the bacteria [85,86]. ROS is a class of radical species including
hydroxyl radicals (OH), superoxide anion (O2

by causing damages to DNA, proteins, and enzymes inside the cells
[82–84].

ions could be considered as soft acid. As such, Ag+ is expected to
show high afﬁnity to the soft base like thiolate ligands, which are

3.1.1. Generation of ROS
The surface of Ag NPs could be oxidized to release Ag+, and gen-
eration of superoxide radicals and other ROS were observed during
the treatment. Using bacterial strains that are sensitive to the
superoxide radicals, it was found that the Ag+ could induce the
ROS generation and successfully kill E. coli and S. aureus, as shown
in Fig. 3b and c. In particular, the key component of the as-
generated ROS was detected to be the superoxide radical, and
H2O2 was not present in the system [85]. Similarly, it was also
shown that Ag NPs would not generate hydroxyl radicals, as veri-
ﬁed by the oxidative-stress-damage sensitive bacteria strains [65].
In another example, ROS generated by the Ag NPs was reported
to inhibit the nitriﬁcation process in the nitrifying bacteria, per-
turbing the cells’ metabolism and killing the cells as a result [94].
The generated ROS was also reported to disturb the function of
DNA and enzymes in order to disrupt the normal metabolism
and kill the bacteria [95]. In addition, the Ag NPs could oxidize
the DNA. The resulting DNA damage would be detrimental to the
bacterial cells as it introduces mutation to the bacterial genome
that could be carried forwards to next generation of bacteria
[96]. In addition, the superoxide could cause the iron leaching from
protein or enzymatic [4Fe–4S] clusters. The released Fe in turn
would adsorb on the DNA surface and oxidize it through a well-
known Fenton catalytic process, in which the Fe would reduce
the intracellular hydrogen peroxides into reactive radicals. These
reactive radicals would then attack and break the DNA strands
[97–99], propagating further damage on the subcellular organelles.
3.1.2. Afﬁnity towards subcellular structures
In addition to the radicals generated by the Ag NPs, the subcel-
lular organelle damage could also be contributed to the high afﬁn-
ity of both the released Ag+ ions and Ag NPs towards these
organelles. For example, Xu et al. reported that the Ag could attach
to the [4Fe–4S] clusters of fumarase A, which is a member of dehy-
dratase family [100]. Owing to the high afﬁnity to the subcellular
organelles, Ag+ was reported to bind to the bacterial enzymes, such
as tryptophanase (TNase) from E. coli, as shown in Fig. 3d. This
binding position was reported to be determined by the surface
coating of Ag NPs [93]. Ag NPs could also inﬂuence the respiratory
enzymes inside the bacteria, disturbing the normal metabolism of
the cells. For instance, Ag NPs would inhibit the activity of respira-
tory chain dehydrogenases on E. coli and S. aureus in dose depen-
dent manner, as shown in Fig. 3e [56,57]. The kind of inhibition
would induce the disruption in the bacterial respiratory chain,
leading to cessation of respiration [101,102]. Moreover, recent
report also showed that polysaccharide-capped Ag NPs could dam-
age the bacterial cytoskeleton, and alter the expression and posi-
tion of the bacterial cytoskeletal proteins (FtsZ and FtsA) [34].
Ag NPs could also inhibit the bacteria growth by exerting their
inﬂuence on the generation of Adenosine triphosphate (ATP), an
important energy supplier for bacteria. In particular, Ag+ ions
released from Ag NPs could penetrate inside E. coli and interact
with the ribosome. As a result, these ions would inhibit the expres-
sion of enzymes and proteins, which are necessary for ATP produc-
tion [103]. Speciﬁcally affected by these ions was the expression of
succinyl-CoA synthase, which reduces the catalytic formation of
succinate. In addition, these ions would also inﬂuence the respira-
tory chain by reducing the electron transfer and ATP generation in
the cells. Proteomic analysis (two-dimensional electrophoresis and
proteins identiﬁcation by mass spectroscopy) revealed that Ag NPs
could up-regulate the expression of the envelope protein precur-
sors, indicating the dissipation of the proton motive force. It also
suggests that Ag NPs could destabilize the bacterial membrane,
induce the collapse of the plasma membrane potential, and deplete
the levels of intracellular ATP. This further attests the capability of
Ag NPs in inﬂuencing the bacteria metabolism [104].
In addition, Ag NPs might also induce DNA condensation in
E. coli and S. aureus. The DNA was observed to be in a condensed
state at the center of the cell. This condensed DNA crippled the
ability of bacteria to replicate their DNA, which in turn led to the
killing of bacteria [57,62]. In addition, Ag+ ions released from Ag
NPs could bind to the various pairs of DNA [105]. Nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) spectroscopy analysis of self-complementary
DNA oligonucleotide with three consecutive imidazole nucleotides
in the center showed that the duplex comprising of three imida-
zole–Ag+–imidazole base pairs was formed after the addition of
Ag+ into the system. This data indicates that the Ag+ could translo-
cate into the DNA base pairing without any major change observed
on the inherent double helix structure [106].
3.2. Physicochemical properties of Ag NPs governing subcellular
function disruption
As the key factors of inducing the damage in subcellular struc-
tures are determined to be oxidation of Ag(0) to Ag(I) to generate
and release Ag+, the following Ag NPs’ physicochemical properties,
such as size, oxidation state, aggregation or dissolution rate, and
surface coating, would inﬂuence their antimicrobial efﬁciency. To
increase the antimicrobial ability, Ag NPs are typically designed
to possess small size, high oxidation state, and proper dissolution
behavior instead of aggregation. In addition, the protecting surface
of Ag NPs is also very important to determine the Ag+ releasing
behavior. Moreover, Ag NPs combination with other functional
bactericides could also be used to improve their antimicrobial
activity.
3.2.1. Size
Size is one of the most important factors to determine the
antimicrobial efﬁciency of Ag NPs, in which the smaller sized Ag

were

face to volume ratio, allowing more Ag+ to be released from the Ag
NPs, as shown in Fig. 4a [77,108–110]. Researchers also found that
by reducing the diameter of Ag NPs to be less than 10 nm, the high
concentration of Ag+ ions released from the Ag NPs became the
deterministic step of the observed antimicrobial effect. The
increase in the size of Ag NPs led to the reduction of the Ag+ con-
centration, indicating the importance of NP size in determining
the antimicrobial efﬁcacy [111]. The enhanced antimicrobial abil-
ity might also be attributed to the better solubility of smaller sized
Ag NPs in solution, which could be oxidized easier to release Ag+.
More speciﬁcally, there are two different Ag NPs’ size measure-
ments, hydrodynamic size and physical size (or core size, which
refers to the size of Ag core). It was found that the hydrodynamic
size, instead of the physical size, would determine the antimicro-
bial efﬁciency of Ag NPs [112]. For example, when Ag NPs of the
same physical size were incubated with the bacteria, the one with
a smaller hydrodynamic diameter showed better antimicrobial
efﬁciency. This is because the smaller hydrodynamic sized Ag
NPs have thinner coating layer, which would favor the release of
Ag+ ions into the system and kill the bacteria as the result.

3.2.2. Oxidation states
Since the release of Ag+ ions is also an important factor in
achieving the antimicrobial effects of Ag NPs, it is pivotal to taking
the Ag NPs’ oxidation states into consideration in the design of
antimicrobial Ag NPs. In anaerobic condition, the Ag NPs were less
toxic towards E. coli as compared to the Ag+ ions. The absence of Ag
oxidation was contributed to their failure to exerting the bacterici-
dal effect, indicating the importance of Ag+ in the antimicrobial
behavior of Ag NPs [81,113]. It was also found that partially oxi-
dized Ag NPs would possess some degree of antimicrobial ability,
while zero-valent Ag NPs did not, indicating that the amount of
chemisorbed Ag+ on the surface of Ag NPs would determine the
exerted antimicrobial effects [107].
In another example, a reversible antimicrobial ability of Ag NPs
was achieved by doping Ag NPs with semiconductor materials,
such as titanium dioxide (TiO2). This doping led to the formation
of a photo-switching Ag NP composite with adjustable oxidation
states and tunable antimicrobial ability (Fig. 4b and c) [114]. For
instance, under irradiation of UV-A at a wavelength of 300–400
nm, the Ag+ would be reduced to Ag(0) and the antimicrobial abil-
ity of Ag NPs was curbed. Comparatively, under visible light irradi-
ation, the Ag(0) within the composite would be oxidized back to
Ag+, which resulted in the recovery of their antimicrobial ability.
This data highlights the importance of the oxidation states of Ag
NPs, and further exempliﬁes that a high oxidation state of Ag
NPs is crucial to achieve good antimicrobial efﬁcacy.

documented that the aggregation of Ag NPs would show decreased

being released from the aggregates. In contrast, the increase in
the dissolution of Ag NPs is known to enhance the bacterial killing
as it would provide more Ag+ ions to kill the bacteria. This aggrega-
tion and dissolution process is highly inﬂuenced by the environ-
ment conditions (e.g., high concentration of biomolecules, ions,
and molecular oxygen), in which the Ag NPs’ size and surface

It has been shown that the Ag NPs would aggregate into bigger
sizes in solution with a high electrolyte content, in which the Ag

The ions that are readily available in any biological system have
been identiﬁed as one of the most common compounds that could
cause the aggregation of Ag NPs in solution. In particular, divalent
cations, such as Ca2+ and Mg2+ were reported to cause the aggrega-

proteins, which could bind to Ag NPs and prevent them from
aggregating with each other [120].
Efforts to enhance the antimicrobial killing could be achieved
by promoting the Ag NPs’ dissolution that allows the release of
Ag+ ions to poison the bacteria. The capping and the size of Ag

evidenced from a comparative study (Fig. 4d), where citrate, poly-

most unstable as they were weakly stabilized by charge repulsion.
In contrast, PEG- and PVP-coated Ag NPs were sterically stabilized,
and they showed much better stability in the complex environ-
ment. As mentioned previously, the dissolution and aggregation
states of Ag NPs in the complex media are also inﬂuenced by the
size of Ag NPs. Smaller Ag NPs showed higher dissolution rate,
which was found to be irrelevant to the surface coating of Ag
NPs [122]. It was found that the surface of Ag NPs could be easily
oxidized, and Ag2O, the oxide surface layer around the uncoated
Ag NPs could also affect the dissolution behavior of Ag NPs [123].
It is expected that the smaller sized Ag NPs contained more oxide
surface layer, and therefore their dissolution process could be fas-
ter. Moreover, the removal of the oxide surface layer around the Ag
NPs either through washing or by H2 reduction would lead to the
non-dissolvable (or difﬁcult to be dissolved) Ag NPs, resulting in
the loss of their antimicrobial ability.
In addition, other small molecules inside the biological complex
medium (e.g., cysteine, chloride, and oxygen) could also induce the
decomposition of Ag NPs. For example, cysteine could induce the
dissolution of zero-valent Ag NPs through its absorption on the
Ag NP surface, leading to the formation of Ag-sulfhydryl bonds
[124]. Chloride could also inﬂuence the dissolution rate of Ag
NPs. At low Cl concentration, the AgCl layer formed around the
Ag NPs would inhibit their dissolution, while soluble AgClx
(x1)
would be generated at high Cl concentration, as shown in
Fig. 4e. The overall trends showed diminish of the antimicrobial

of Ag+ ions from the Ag NPs was inhibited. In contrast, the Ag
NPs would recover their antimicrobial efﬁciency at high Cl/Ag
environment, in which the dissolution was greatly favored [125].
Besides Cl, sulﬁde could also consume Ag+ to inhibit their antimi-

order to Ag0, O2 and Tris, but in the inverse ﬁrst-order to Ag+ [127].
Surface coating of the Ag NPs could also determine their aggre-
gation and dissolution in the complex in vivo environment. Though
capping the Ag NPs with non-ionic and bulky ligands could help
keep the integrity of Ag NPs, these ligands could potentially hinder
the release of Ag+ ions from the Ag NPs, reducing their overall
antimicrobial efﬁciency. As such, the balance between the relative
stability and the proper dissolution proﬁle should be taken into
account in the design of antimicrobial Ag NPs. For example, the
aggregation and dissolution process of Ag NPs were studied by
coating Ag NPs with citrate, sodium dodecylsulfate (SDS), and
Tween 80. Tween-coated Ag NPs showed the lowest aggregation
trend, while the negatively-charged citrate-coated Ag NPs showed
the highest dissolution [128]. Undoubtedly, the nonionic steric sta-
bilizer Tween-coated Ag NPs showed the lowest dissolution behav-
ior. In another case, Ag NPs protected by bulky ligand SDS, Tween
80, and PVP 360 showed enhanced antimicrobial efﬁcacy due to
their superior performance in preventing the aggregation [129].
In addition, several other factors, such as pH and storage time,
could also inﬂuence the dissolution of Ag NPs. For example,
thiol-functionalized methoxyl polyethylene glycol (PEG-SH) pro-
tected Ag NPs showed a faster dissolution kinetics in acetic acid
(pH = 3) solution than in ultrapure water. Some uncoated or elec-
trostatically stabilized Ag NPs would aggregate at low pH [130].
The dissolution rate was also observed to be faster when decreas-
ing the size of Ag NPs [131]. In addition, the storage time of Ag NPs
could also inﬂuence their antimicrobial ability, as evidenced by the
increase of antimicrobial effectiveness exerted by the aged Ag NPs
when compared to the fresh Ag NPs. This was attributed to the
increase of Ag+ ions released from the aged Ag NPs, allowing them
to be more effective in killing bacteria than those freshly prepared
Ag NPs [132].
3.2.4. Surface coating
The protective layer outside the Ag NPs also needs to be well-
considered in the design of antimicrobial Ag NPs. Multi-element
and multi-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) detected
metallic Ag on the surface of PVP-protected Ag NPs. In contrast,
the surface shell of Ag2S was also detected when a strong thiolate
ligand, cysteine, was used to protect the Ag NPs. This Ag2S surface
protected Ag NPs showed enhanced antimicrobial ability towards
both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria [133]. The authors
postulated that the stronger thiolate ligand protected Ag NPs might
be more active than those Ag NPs coordinated with weaker ligands.
This data highlights the inﬂuence of surface ligand as well as sur-
face structure on the antimicrobial ability of Ag NPs.
Moreover, the surface of Ag NPs is also important in controlling
the open facets that could affect their antimicrobial ability [134]. In
addition to controlling the interaction of Ag NPs with the bacterial
surface, the surface of Ag NPs also controls their aggregation and
dissolution condition. The ideal surface ligands on the Ag NPs need
to be strong enough to hinder the aggregation of Ag NPs, ensuring
effective surface area to facilitate the interaction between the Ag
NPs and the subcellular structures of bacteria. On the other hand,
the ideal surface ligands are also able to facilitate the release of
Ag+ ions at a desirable rate to kill bacteria as the stability of Ag
NPs would also inﬂuence their antimicrobial activity [135,136].

bacterial killing process could be recovered after the surface ligand
of Ag NPs, i.e., CTAB, damaged the bacterial membrane [68]. In
addition, sulﬁdation of Ag NPs could form insoluble Ag2S layer
around the NPs and inhibit the antimicrobial ability due to the lack
of released Ag+ [140,141].
Controlled release of Ag NPs could be tuned by incorporating
other materials to produce Ag NP composites. For instance, Ag
NPs with diameter of 2–10 nm could be dispersed in mesoporous
silica NPs, which showed high antimicrobial activity towards
Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria over one month treat-
ment [142]. In addition, Ag NPs could be encapsulated in meso-
porous silica NPs to treat Gram-positive and Gram-negative
bacteria through oxidative dissolution of the Ag NPs [143]. Ag
NPs could also be in situ grown on silicon nanowires to realize
long-term antimicrobial behavior [144]. Another efﬁcient strategy
is to load Ag NPs into poly(ethylene imine) network to achieve a
certain level of antimicrobial effect through the released Ag+ ions
[145]. In addition, there is one recent example using Ag-zeolites
on nanoﬁber mats as efﬁcient and tunable releasing antimicrobial
vehicles [146]. Moreover, the Ag NPs could be deposited on the
surface of NPs containing phosphate-based biodegradable ceramic
materials to achieve triggered release of Ag by the bacteria. As the
growing bacteria require mineral for their growth, the uptake of
mineral from the ceramic carrier would lead to the dissolution of
the carrier, and the release of Ag could kill the bacteria [147]. Ag
NPs could also be formed and dispersed in oil using in paints, help-
ing to curb the growth of Gram-positive S. aureus and Gram-
negative E. coli [148]. Another recent study reported that Ag+ could
be infused into cysteine modiﬁed molybdenum disulﬁde (MoS2)
coated with a cationic polyelectrolyte layer, which would work
as an antimicrobial depot. The antimicrobial depot showed
enhanced broad-spectrum antimicrobial ability due to increased
release of Ag+ that could directly act onto the cell walls of microor-
ganisms [149].

ergistic effect, as demonstrated by the Ag NPs–AMP complex. In
particular, AMP could insert in the bacterial membrane to form
transient pores through destroying the phospholipids bilayers,
which allow the partner Ag NPs to penetrate through the mem-
brane and exert their antimicrobial action in the bacterial cells
[153]. Similarly, lysozyme could also be used to catalyze the forma-
tion of Ag NPs to produce a highly efﬁcient antimicrobial com-
pound. The high antimicrobial performance was attributed to the
combinatorial mode of action from the two types of functional
antimicrobial agents. Lysozyme could destroy bacterial membrane,
allowing the Ag NPs to access to the cells and interact with the sub-
cellular structures of bacteria to cause damage [154]. Ag NPs com-
bination with other two antimicrobial agents, chitosan and iodine,
showed that they could work synergistically to attach on bacteria,
damage bacterial wall, and generate the ROS [155]. In addition to
the active bactericides Ag NPs, iodine could generate iodine atoms
that could penetrate bacterial wall and produce ROS. Polycationic
chitosan contribution to the whole construct was to facilitate the
structure to attach onto the negatively-charged bacterial wall that
leads to the intracellular contents leakage. In another case, b-
cyclodextrin (b-CD) protected Ag NPs showed enhanced antimicro-
bial ability that was depicted to follow Trojan horse mechanism. b-
CD helped Ag NPs to better interact with the bacteria, allowing the
complex to be internalized by the bacteria. Once internalized, the
b-CD was digested by bacteria, as shown in Fig. 5b [156], allowing
the Ag+ to be released from the Ag NPs.
In addition to the antibiotics, Ag NPs could also be combined
with some oxide NPs to achieve enhanced antimicrobial effects.
For instance, Ag NPs combination with TiO2 could inhibit bacterial
growth under visible-light irradiation [157–159]. TiO2 embedded
in Ag NPs could achieve antimicrobial ability through electron-
transfer-induced ROS generation [160]. In a recent study, the anti-

the mammalian cells [161]. Combination of Ag NPs and iron oxide
(Fe3O4) NPs conjugated on the graphene oxide surface has been
reported to achieve multifunctional antimicrobial effects, as shown
in Fig. 5c. Photothermal treatment using this composite was facil-
itated by their strong near-infrared (NIR) absorbance and was pro-
ven to inhibit the S. aureus growth. The repeat use of this

4. Silver nanoclusters: new generation of silver-based
antimicrobial agents
According to previous discussion, we have identiﬁed some key
physicochemical properties, including size, surface coating, and
oxidation states, which enable material scientists to adjust the
antimicrobial ability of Ag NPs. Considering the importance of
these physicochemical properties, recent studies have progressed
to tuning these properties to create the new generation of Ag-
based antimicrobial agents. One of the examples of these new gen-
eration Ag antimicrobials is the newly generated Ag NCs that pos-
sess ultrasmall particle size with tunable atom number and
surface. Reducing the size of Ag NPs further to the Ag NCs form,
typically in the size range of 1–2 nm, have been demonstrated to
be efﬁcient to improve their bactericidal performance. For exam-
ple, it was reported that Ag NCs outperformed Ag NPs with the
same Ag concentration in killing E. coli [174].
Ultrasmall Ag NCs have even higher surface to volume ratio
when compared to the Ag NPs, allowing these Ag NCs to have
higher amount of Ag+ being released at faster rate, which could
potentially enhance their performance in killing the bacteria. It
was found that Ag16(SG)9 (SG refers to glutathione, GS–H) could
be used to kill P. aeruginosa. Comprising of 16 Ag atoms and 9 pro-
tecting ligands (GSH) on the outer layer, these Ag NCs hold tremen-
dous amount of surface Ag atoms, which could be easily released
and oxidized. Indeed, the oxidation of the released Ag atoms and
the induction of intracellular ROS production have contributed to
the enhanced bactericidal properties of Ag NCs, which outper-
formed chloramphenicol of the same concentration (Fig. 6a)
[175]. Similarly, DNA-protected Ag NCs also showed good antimi-
crobial ability, where the antimicrobial efﬁciency of Ag NCs was
determined by the sequence of DNA that protects the Ag NCs
[176]. In a recent study, antimicrobial Ag NCs protected by poly-
ethyleneimine (PEI) with molecular weight from 0.6 to 25 kDa
were synthesized [177]. It was found that Ag NCs protected by
PEI with lower molecular weight led to higher antimicrobial efﬁ-
ciency, which might be caused by the easier releasing of the Ag.
In addition, ultrasmall Ag NCs hold comparable sizes with sub-
cellular organelles, allowing them to have higher chance to interact
and damage these organelles of the bacteria. For example, it was
found that bare Ag NCs containing 2 or 3 atoms (Ag2 and Ag3) gen-
erated through the electrochemical technique could interact and
distort the DNA structure of the bacteria [178]. It was noted that
the lifetime of this distortion was two to three orders of magnitude
longer than the common organic intercalators, such as ethidium
bromide and proﬂavine, indicating their strong ability to disturb
DNA. Moreover, through their binding, Ag3 curtailed the binding
of DNA-binding proteins, such as topoisomerase IV, E. coli DNA gyr-
ase, and the restriction enzyme HindIII [179].
Design of the Ag NCs to possess higher oxidation states could
also enhance their bactericidal effect. This was exempliﬁed
through the formation of Ag NCs with the same atom number
and surface coating but different oxidation state. In a comparative
study, Ag NCs with higher oxidation states (the dominant Ag
remained in Ag NCs are Ag+ species, GSH–Ag+–R NCs) were shown
to have better antimicrobial performance (Fig. 6b) to both Gram-
negative and Gram-positive bacteria when compared to the Ag
NCs with lower oxidation states (all Ag atoms were at zero valence,
GSH–Ag0–R NCs) [180]. This was attributed to the higher afﬁnity of
Ag+-rich Ag NCs towards negatively-charged bacterial surface,
where the released Ag+ could generate the ROS inside the bacteria.
In addition to their intrinsically antimicrobial properties, size
and surface ligand of the Ag NCs could be easily tuned to further
enhance their antimicrobial ability. Thanks to the recent develop-
ment in cluster synthetic chemistry, several efﬁcient protocols
have been reported to produce different sized Ag NCs, including
Ag62 [183], Ag44 [184–186], Ag32 [187], Ag25 [188,189], Ag21
[190], Ag9 [191], Ag8 [192], Ag7 [192–194], Ag3 and Ag2 [178].
The capability to ﬁne tune the Ag core content brings the capability
of material scientists to modulate the antimicrobial properties of
these structures. Moreover, a number of Ag NCs protected by dif-
ferent ligands have been developed, including DNA [195–197],
lysozyme [198], GSH [199], dihydrolipoic acid (DHLA) [200,201],
carboxymethyl-b-cyclodextrin (CM-b-CD) [174], and tiopronin
[202] protected Ag NCs, and some of them show high efﬁciency
in killing bacteria. The ability to adjust the Ag NCs’ surface ligands
provides feasibility in tuning their interaction with the bacteria as
well as controlling the releasing rate of Ag+.
In addition, the surface ligand of Ag NCs could also be changed
without inﬂuencing the core Ag atom number. For example, the
outside ligand of Ag44(SR)30 could be exchanged among 5-
mercapto-2-nitrobenzoic acid (MMBA), 4-ﬂuorothiophenol (4-
FTP), 4-nitrothiophenol (4-NTP) or 2-naphthalenethiol (2-NT)
[203]. In another case, ligand-exchange process could also reversi-
bly change the size of Ag NCs. This reversible change occurred
among Ag35(SG)18, Ag44(4-FTP)30, and Ag37(SG)21 [204], giving fur-
ther testament to the tunability of Ag NCs’ size and surface proper-
ties. In a very recent study, the inﬂuence of surface ligand towards

To improve the antimicrobial efﬁciency of Ag NCs, an efﬁcient
antimicrobial hybrid could be developed through conjugation of
Ag NCs with commercialized antibiotics, such as daptomycin
(Fig. 6c) [181]. The as-designed antimicrobial hybrid (D-AgNCs,
here D refers to daptomycin) showed efﬁcient antimicrobial ability
as the hybrid could localize Ag NCs as well as daptomycin within
the network. Localized daptomycin inside the hybrid structure
could effectively damage the bacterial membrane, while the local-
ized Ag NCs could generate ROS to oxidize the bacteria lipid bilayer
to further intensify the membrane damage. This in turn allows
more hybrids to enter the bacteria and generate localized high
ROS concentration within the cell, which could subsequently
induce a severe DNA damage and kill the bacteria.
In clinical setting, knowing the release amount of Ag+ will allow
clinicians to introduce the right amount of Ag-based antimicrobial
agents to minimize the potential systemic cytotoxicity that could
arise from any commercialized Ag-based antimicrobial agents.
The ability to accurately determine the amount of released Ag+,
unfortunately, is not existent (or very difﬁcult to achieve) for the
case of Ag NPs based antimicrobial agents. As the atom number
of Ag inside the Ag NPs could not be directly determined, the
amount of released Ag+ could only be measured through induc-
tively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP–MS). Moreover,
the inaccuracy was also compounded by the fact that the Ag NPs
are required to undergo complicated separation process like cen-
trifugation, ultraﬁltration, and dialysis, where not all Ag NPs could
be completely removed. In addition, Ag+ could either be lost during
these steps or even remain as AgCl in the biological solutions. In
another way, as free Ag+ or sedimentary AgCl would not contribute
to the UV–vis absorption at surface plasmon resonance (SPR)
region of Ag NPs at 400 nm, we could only roughly estimate
the amount of released Ag+ (resulting in a decrease in the SPR
intensity at 400 nm) [213]. However, this method is also impos-
sible to exactly determine the amount of Ag+ ions that have been
released.
In contrast, by adopting Ag NCs as the bactericidal source, for
the ﬁrst-time material scientists are able to accurately determine
the amount of Ag+ being released to the system. Once the atom
number within the Ag NCs is changed, the molecular absorption
(UV–vis absorption spectra) of Ag NCs would show obvious trans-
formation. For instance, the unique UV–vis absorption peaks of
Ag44 are at 415, 485, 538, 645, and 835 nm, while the peaks of
Ag25 are at 334, 390, 490, and 678 nm [185,188]. Armed with
this characteristic absorption spectrum, material scientists could
accurately determine how many Ag atoms have been released from
a particular Ag NC conformation. In addition to this characteristic
UV–vis absorption spectrum, the atom number inside Ag NCs could
also be determined by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry
(ESI-MS). Overall, Ag NCs provide a good platform for antimicrobial
process study, as the changes in Ag atom number during the inter-
action of Ag NCs with the bacteria could be monitored through
UV–vis absorption and ESI-MS (or other characterization tech-
niques), which could shed some light on the aggregation or disso-
lution process of Ag NPs during the bacterial killing process [214].
Therefore, the atomically precise Ag NCs could also be feasible in
standard development, as the antimicrobial ability of different Ag
NCs could now be compared (and benchmarked) at atomic and
molecular level.
In addition, one unique property of Ag NCs is that they show
strong luminescence [196,197,215–221]. This could facilitate the
development of traceable antimicrobial agents, in which the lumi-
nescent signal of Ag NCs could be used to monitor the Ag NCs
movement and their interaction with bacterial cells. In addition
to monitoring their interaction with the bacterial cell wall, the
luminescent signal could also be used to detect their cellular inter-
nalization and accumulation. Lastly, it is also possible to study the
antimicrobial mechanism with the help from the luminescence of
Ag NCs. In particular, changes in the luminescence color or even
luminescence quenching could be expected when the Ag+ was
released from the Ag NCs, whereas the aggregation of Ag NCs might
further enhance the intensity of their luminescence.
Very recently, the general applicability of the strategy in tuning
the size of NPs to sub-nanometer range was proved by conferring
antimicrobial ability to inert gold nanoparticles (Au NPs) through
decreasing their size to ultrasmall gold nanoclusters (Au NCs)
about 1 nm. We found that ultrasmall Au NCs could kill both the
Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, showing their poten-
tial as a wide-spectrum bactericide (Fig. 6d) [182]. This wide-
spectrum antimicrobial activity is attributed to the Au NCs’ ultra-
small size, allowing them to possess high surface to volume ratio.
Consequently, when the Au NCs were internalized, their active sur-
faces could induce a metabolic imbalance in the cells which leads
to an increase of intracellular ROS production, conferring bacteria
killing effect as the end result.
5. Conclusion
In this review, we have discussed the mode of action of Ag NPs
towards the bacterial extracellular membrane and intracellular
components.
We
have
also
identiﬁed the
direct
correlation
between the physicochemical properties of Ag NPs and their
antimicrobial efﬁciency. In general, smaller sized Ag NPs hold
higher surface-to-atom ratio, which favors their interaction with
the bacterial membrane. In addition, the smaller Ag NPs could also
release higher amount (with a faster kinetics) of Ag+, which could
generate more ROS in the process. Higher amount of released Ag+
could also enhance their interaction efﬁciency with the subcellular
organelles of the bacteria. Similarly, surface coating of Ag NPs also
plays a key role in their interaction with the bacterial membrane,
the release kinetics of Ag+, and their following afﬁnity to the sub-
cellular organelles of bacteria. Other physicochemical properties
of Ag NPs, such as shape, oxidation states, and the aggregation
and dissolution state, are also known to inﬂuence their antimicro-
bial ability.
From the aforementioned physicochemical properties, we could
ﬁnd some general rules that could be adopted to further enhance
the Ag NPs’ antimicrobial efﬁcacy. These guidelines and principles
have driven the community to start to search better Ag-based
antimicrobial agents in the ﬁeld of ultrasmall Ag NCs. Ultrasmall
Ag NCs showed improved antimicrobial efﬁciency compared with
the Ag NPs of the same Ag dose, indicating their intrinsic high abil-
ity to kill bacteria. This general rule was also conﬁrmed by our
recent study, that ultrasmall Au NCs acquired antimicrobial ability
which is absent in their larger counterpart Au NPs.
In addition, the Ag atom number in the core of Ag NCs as well as
the outside protecting ligands could be readily adjusted at atomic
(and molecular) level, which could greatly favor the ﬁne control of
their antimicrobial ability. The Ag NCs’ intrinsic high antimicrobial
efﬁciency as well as the ability to accurately tune their properties
could possibly help to overcome the current evolving antimicrobial
resistance in many microbial pathogens. Lastly, the atomic preci-
sion of Ag NCs allows these clusters to be used as standard for
any commercialized Ag-based antimicrobial agents. However, fur-
ther development of Ag or Au NCs-based antimicrobial agents
would require more concerted efforts from heterogeneous commu-

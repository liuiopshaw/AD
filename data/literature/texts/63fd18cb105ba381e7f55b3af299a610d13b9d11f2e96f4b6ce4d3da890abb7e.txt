The interactions of materials with light determine their applications in 
various fields. In the past decade, ultrasmall metal nanoclusters (NCs) 
have emerged as a promising class of optical materials due to their unique 
molecular-like properties. Herein, the basic principles of optical absorp-
tion and photoluminescence of metal NCs, their interactions with polarized 
light, and light-induced chemical reactions, are discussed, highlighting the 
roles of the core and protecting ligands/motifs of metal NCs in their interac-
tions with light. The metal core and protecting ligands/motifs determine the 
electronic structures of metal NCs, which are closely related to their optical 
properties. In addition, the protecting ligands/motifs of metal NCs contribute 
to their photo­luminescence and chiral origin, further promoting the interac-
tions of metal NCs with light through various pathways. The fundamentals of 
light–NC interactions provide guidance for the design of metal NCs in optical 
applications, which are discussed in the second part. In the last section, 
some strategies are proposed to further understand light–NC interactions, 
highlighting the challenges and opportunities. It is hoped that this work will 
stimulate more research on the optical properties of metal NCs and their 
applications in various fields.
1. Introduction
Light interacts with matter in various ways. Some examples 
of light interactions with matter are reflection, scattering, and 
absorption. Reflection is the process by which light returns to 
the original medium after interacting with matter. In a scat-
tering process, light deviates from the straight light path due 
to the interactions with matter. Alternatively, light may also 
be absorbed by matter, which is the absorption process in 
light interactions. Based on these interactions, various optical 
materials have been developed. To give a few examples, the flat 
silver layer specular can reflect light, making it a good mate-
rial for mirrors. The transparent and refractive properties of

2. Optical Absorption
Optical absorption is the process by which matter absorbs the 
energy of photons. This process is usually accompanied by the 
transition of electrons to higher energy levels. Through this pro-
cess, the photon energy is also transformed into the internal 
energy of the absorber. The optical absorption of metal NCs is 
similar to that of molecules, and they show different responses to 
light of different wavelengths. Such responses vary greatly among 
metal NC species, which is the origin of their various colors. This 
is in sharp contrast to the larger counterparts of metal NCs (i.e., 
metal nanocrystals, >3  nm), which typically show collective sur-
face plasmon resonance (SPR) at a specific wavelength.[23,24] For 
example, spherical gold (Au) nanocrystals exhibit strong optical 
absorption at ≈2.4 eV (equivalent to ≈550 nm wavelength), causing 
these Au nanocrystals to appear red. It was found that the tran-
sition of optical absorption properties between Au NCs and 
nanocrystals occurs between the sizes of Au246 and Au279.[25,26]
2.1. Core Electronic Structure
The electronic structure of the metal core is central to the optical 
absorption of metal NCs. From the structure point of view, 
there is an electron-rich metal core in the structure of metal 
NCs.[14,27–29] Depending on the type of metal NCs, the metal core 
can be protected by a shell of ligands or binding motifs con-
structed by metal–ligand bonding. However, regardless of the 
structure of the ligands that protect the core of metal NCs, the 
metal core plays a major role in forming the molecular orbitals 
(MOs) that determine the electronic structures, especially the 
frontier MOs, including the highest occupied molecular orbital 
(HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO).
The determination of metal NC structures and advances in 
theoretical calculations provides insights into the core elec-
tronic structures. Thiolate-protected Au25 NCs are one of the 
best-studied metal NCs.[30,31] Au25 NC has an icosahedral Au13 
core in the middle of the molecule, which is surrounded by 
six V-shaped –S–Au–S–Au–S motifs in a nearly octahedral 
arrangement.[32–34] Therefore, it has a molecular formula of 
Au25(SR)18, where SR denotes a thiolate ligand (e.g., phenyl­
ethylthiolate (PET)). Its anionic variant (i.e., [Au25(SR)18]−) has 
a Au135+ core from an electronic point of view, resulting in an 
electron count of 8, which is a closed-shell structure according 
to the superatom view of metal NCs.[35] Its optical absorption 
spectrum shows characteristic absorption bands at 1.8, 2.75, 
and 3.1 eV photon energies (equivalent to 690, 450, and 400 nm 
wavelength, respectively), as shown in Figure  1A.[32] In con-
trast, its neutrally charged variant (i.e., [Au25(SR)18]0) and cati-
onic variant (i.e., [Au25(SR)18]+) show slightly different optical 
absorption. Compared with the energy gap of [Au25(SR)18]−, the 
energy gap of the HOMO → LUMO transition of [Au25(SR)18]0 
and [Au25(SR)18]+ decreases or increases, respectively.[36,37] This 
is associated with the spontaneous symmetry breaking in the 
Jahn–Teller effect when the number of valence electrons (with 
d symmetry) reduces from 8 to 7 and 6.[37]
Time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) calcula-
tions have been applied to correlate the optical absorption spec-
trum with the structure of [Au25(SH)18]−, as shown in Figure 1B. 
The theoretical spectrum matches well with the experimental 
spectrum, by showing that the three optical absorption bands 
can be matched with the corresponding electronic transi-
tions between the discrete electronic states. The first absorp-
tion band (peak a in Figure 1A) corresponds to the intraband 
HOMO → LUMO transition and the last absorption band 
(peak c in Figure  1A) corresponds to the interband transition 
(d → sp). The second absorption band (peak b in Figure  1A) 
is slightly more complicated because it contains features from 
both interband (d → sp) and intraband transitions (sp → sp), 
and their energy gaps are similar. In addition to these three 
peaks, [Au25(SR)18]− also exhibits another shoulder peak at 
≈780  nm (equivalent to 1.59  eV). This peak becomes distinct 
at low temperature (78 K).[38] The formation of this peak can 
be explained by spin-orbit coupling, which is well-predicted by 
simulation studies.[39] The spin–orbit coupling will cause the 
triply degenerate HOMO to split into an energy difference of 
≈0.2 eV, which is equivalent to the energy difference between 
this peak and peak a (1.8 eV).
The importance of the metal core in the optical absorption of 
metal NCs is reflected in the contribution of metal atoms to the 
electronic structures. Further research on [Au25(SH)18]− shows 
that the LUMO, HOMO, HOMO−1 and LUMO+1 orbitals are 
mainly composed of the atomic orbitals (AOs) of the 13 Au 
atoms in the icosahedral core.[40] Therefore, small changes in 
the metal core may cause drastically different electronic struc-
ture of metal NCs and the way the metal NCs absorb light.

2.2. Ligand Anchoring Point
The ligand anchoring point refers to the part of the ligand 
that directly interacts with the metal core. It can be sulfur (S), 
selenium (Se), phosphorus (P), carbon (C), or other atoms, 
depending on the type of ligands used to protect metal NCs.[45] 
These atoms form strong bonds with the metal core, which 
stabilizes and protects the metal core. Therefore, the interac-
tion between the ligands and the metal core should also play 
an important role in the electronic structure of metal NCs, and 
therefore is a crucial factor in determining the optical absorp-
tion of metal NCs.
The importance of ligands can be demonstrated using a 
similar icosahedral M135+ (M = Au, Ag) as an example. Figure 3 
shows the simulated optical absorption spectra of [M25(SH)18]− 
and its structural components (i.e., M135+ and [M2(SH)3]−).[40] It 
can be seen that the optical absorption spectrum of [M25(SH)18]− 
is not simply a linear addition of those of M135+ and [M2(SH)3]−. 
The HOMO–LUMO transitions of Au135+ and Ag135+ occur at 
2.78 and 3.72  eV, respectively, while the optical absorption of 
[Au2(SH)3]− and [Ag2(SH)3]− is in the UV region above 3  eV. 
Instead, the HOMO–LUMO transitions of [Au25(SH)18]− and 
[Ag25(SH)18]− are 1.63 and 1.64  eV, respectively. The lower 
HOMO–LUMO transition energy suggests that the bonding

2.3. Ligand Body
The ligand body is the outer part of the ligand that does not 
bond to the metal atoms, such as aliphatic chains and aro-
matic rings with functional groups. Since no direct bonding 
to the metal core is observed from the ligand body, the optical 
absorption of metal NCs will likely be similar if they differ 
only in the ligand body. However, some studies have shown 
that even for the same metal NCs, the type of ligands may 
cause a red or blue shift of the absorption wavelength.[50] As 
shown in Figure 5A, when the aliphatic ligand is replaced by 
the aromatic ligand, the optical absorption band of the same 
Au NCs is redshifted.[51] However, the energy gap of the corre-
sponding transition is not responsive to the type of ligands.[52] 
Instead, the effect of the ligand body on optical absorption 
may be a result of vibrational and rotational transitions. The 
ligand body, especially the part connected to the anchoring 
point, plays an important role in the vibration and rotation 
of the NC molecules.[53,54] Several factors, such as conjuga-
tion and steric hindrance may restrict the free vibration or 
rotation of NCs.[55,56] Therefore, for metal NCs with different 
types of ligand body, the vibration and rotation states may be 
different. According to the Franck–Condon principle, when 
two vibrational wavefunctions overlap most significantly, a 
favorable electronic transition occurs. Therefore, when the 
vibration energy between the metal NCs is different, a redshift 
or blueshift of the absorption peaks can be expected. These 
vibronic structures are clearly visible at low temperatures.[38,57]
In addition to the effects of vibration and rotation energy, 
the charge anisotropy of the ligand body may also affect the 
optical absorption properties of metal NCs. The optical absorp-
tion spectra in Figure 5B shows significant differences among 
the bi-ligand-protected Au25 NCs.[58] When NCs are protected 
by negatively charged thiolate ligands collocating with positively 
charged or neutral ones, optical absorption at 780 and 980 nm 
can be observed. This is because the charge anisotropy occurs 
when a neutral or positively charged ligand protects the metal 
core together with a negatively charged ligand. As a result, the 
Au25 NC undergoes a structural distortion process in solution, 
generating new energy states that contribute to absorption at 
780 and 980 nm.
3. Photoluminescence
After metal NCs absorb light, they enter an excited state with 
energy higher than the ground state. The lifetime of the excited 
states is usually short (ranging from nanoseconds to micro­
seconds),[59,60] so they tend to return to the ground state through 
various relaxation pathways. Under special circumstances, 
photo­ns may be radiated when electrons return from the 
excited state to the ground state, known as photo­luminescence.

of photons may result in a singlet excitation state and subse-

from a singlet state to a triplet state. After that, optical radiation 
occurs when the electrons return from the triplet state to the 
ground state. Nevertheless, regardless of fluorescence or phos-
phorescence, the emission wavelength is redshifted from the 
excitation wavelength, resulting in a Stokes shift in the photo­
luminescence process. The photoluminescence properties of 
metal NCs have attracted a lot of research interest, not only in 
the excitation and emission wavelengths, but also in the emis-
sion intensity, which is crucial for various applications.[61–63]
3.1. Core Electronic Structure
Similar to optical absorption, the photoluminescence proper-
ties of metal NCs are also highly related to their size, compo-
sition, and structure, which determine the electronic structure 
of metal NCs. For example, Negishi and Tsukuda reported the 
synthesis and characterization of nine different Au NCs (size 
from Au10 to Au39).[64] The photoluminescence properties of 
these NCs, including photoexcitation and photoemission, are 
very different. In another example, although Au25 and Ag25 NCs 
have a similar structure, they exhibit distinctly different photo-
luminescence properties due to the difference in composition 
(Figure 6A).[65,66] In particular, Ag25 NCs emit at 850 nm, which 
is a different wavelength from that of Au25 NCs.
Theoretical calculations provide some insights for the photo­
luminescence phenomenon of metal NCs. For example, DFT 
and TD–DFT calculations on the thiolate-protected Au25(SR)18 
NCs show that the Au13 core slightly expands upon excitation.[67] 
Therefore, the energy states split even further due to the loss of 
symmetry, which causes the large Stokes shift of the NCs. In 
contrast, the Stokes shift of Au38(SR)24 is much smaller due to 
its structural rigidity.[68] In addition, it was found that multiple 
excited states may contribute to their photoluminescence. Nev-
ertheless, all these involved excited states are derived from the 
core of metal NCs and are highly correlated with the structure 
distortion and electron redistribution,[69] which indicates the 
origin of photoluminescence and its dependence on the core 
electronic structure.
3.2. Surface Motifs: Aggregation-Induced Emission (AIE)
In addition to the excitation of electrons in the metal core, the 
aggregation-induced emission (AIE) of surface motifs also con-
tributes to photoluminescence of metal NCs. AIE involves the 
restriction of intermolecular rotation and intramolecular vibra-
tion of the surface motifs of metal NCs, thereby minimizing 
nonradiative decay.[70,71] When interacting with light, the elec-
trons on the surface motifs, instead of the core electrons, enter 
the excited states. The surface excited states then go through 
photoemission accompanied by ligand-to-metal charge transfer 
(LMCT) or ligand-to-metal–metal charge transfer (LMMCT). As 
a result, photoluminescence derived from AIE generally fea-
tures long lifetime (usually in microseconds), large Stokes shift, 
and long emission wavelength.[7,72]
The study on the AIE properties of metal NCs originates 
from the photoluminescence properties of Au(I)–GSH com-
plexes (GSH denotes glutathione).[73] As shown in Figure  6B, 
the water-soluble Au(I)–GSH complexes do not exhibit photo­
luminescence in solution. However, the aggregation of these 
complexes can induce strong photoluminescence (e.g., using 
ethanol as a poor solvent to induce aggregation). Upon aggre-
gation, the Au(I)–GSH complexes become highly lumines-
cent, emitting at 565 and 610  nm (Stokes shift > 200  nm).

3.3. Ligand Type
Similar to the effects of ligands on the optical absorption of 
metal NCs, ligands also play important roles in photolumines-
cence by fine-tuning the electronic structures of metal NCs. 
Both the ligand anchoring point and ligand body can affect 
the emission wavelength and quantum yield of metal NCs. 
For example, the photoluminescence of Au25 NCs protected by 
different thiolate ligands has been studied, and ligands with 
strong electron donating capability or electron-rich atoms (and 
functional groups) can enhance the photoluminescence of 
metal NCs.[77]
More importantly, the photoluminescence pathway (emis-
sion from the core or surface motifs of metal NCs) may depend 
on the choice of ligands. An interesting comparison lies among 
metal NCs with 22 Au atoms protected by 18 ligands of different 
types, namely alkynyls and thiolates. The crystal structure of 
alkynyl-protected Au22 NC has been resolved. It features a Au7 
bitetrahedral core protected by a Au3(CCR)4 trimer motif and 
Au6(CCR)6 ring (Figure 7A).[78,79] On the other hand, although 
the molecular structure of thiolate-protected Au22(SR)18 has not 
yet been resolved, theoretical studies suggest that it has a sim-
ilar structure as the alkynyl-protected Au22 NC (Figure  7B).[80] 
In addition, both alkynyl- and thiolate-protected Au22 NCs 
exhibit photoluminescence at ≈665  nm (Figure  7C,D).[74,78,79] 
The quantum yield of alkynyl-protected Au22 NCs is in the 
range of 0.65% to 4.6%, depending on the end groups of the 
alkynyl ligands, while the quantum yield of thiolate-protected 
Au22 NCs is ≈8% (using GSH as the protecting ligand). How-
ever, the origin of photoluminescence can be attributed to dif-
ferent sources. On the one hand, it has been proposed that the 
photoluminescence of alkynyl-protected Au22 NCs originates

is restricted by the rigidification of the core by the phenyl

and non-radiation-relaxation is inhibited by the restriction of 
rotation and vibration of these motifs. The enhanced rigidity

4. Interactions with Polarized Light
The interaction between metal NCs and polarized light is 
another noteworthy interaction due to their special intrinsic 
chirality. As light is an electromagnetic wave, it is composed 
of oscillating electric and magnetic fields. Polarized light refers 
to the light whose electric field oscillates in a specific direc-
tion. For polarized light, similar interactions to typical light 
(e.g., optical absorption and photoluminescence) can also be 
observed. However, when polarized light interacts with chiral 
materials that lack microscopic mirror symmetry, optical activi-
ties are observed. When linear polarized light passes through 
a chiral material, the orientation of the optical axis plane may 
be rotated, which is known as optical rotation. When circu-
larly polarized light interacts with chiral materials, different 
responses may occur for left-handed and right-handed circularly 
polarized light, a process called circular dichroism (CD). Metal 
NCs may have chiral properties from their molecular-like struc-
tures, just like biomolecules. The development of X-ray crystal-
lography in the field of metal NCs reveals the total structure 
of a few metal NC species, many of which are chiral in struc-
ture.[83–86] However, the origin of chirality may differ among NC 
species. Here, we will present several examples of how chiral 
metal NCs interact with polarized light. For detailed research, 
readers can refer to several good review articles, which system-
atically discuss the synthesis, characterization, optical activity, 
and chiral origin of chiral metal NCs.[87–89]
4.1. Chiral Metal Core
As discussed above, optical absorption and photoluminescence 
largely depend on the metal core of NCs, and it is expected that 
the chiral core of NCs will have a considerable influence on 
their interaction with polarized light. For example, phosphine-
protected Au20 NCs exhibit intrinsic chirality in the core.[90,91] 
As a result, the two enantiomers (R-Au20 and L-Au20) show 
strong CD absorption signals in the range of 300 to 800  nm 
(Figure  8A).[92] These optical absorption peaks correspond to 
the electronic transitions in MOs, mainly from the metal core.
4.2. Chiral Arrangement of the Ligand Anchoring Point
The helical arrangement of ligands is a popular origin of chi-
rality in coordination chemistry. Given that metal NCs can be 
regarded as a metal core protected by bidentate motifs, many 
metal NCs exhibit chirality in this way. Au38(PET)24 is one 
example.[93,94] It features a face-fused biicosahedral Au23 core, 
three short Au(SR)2, and six long Au2(SR)3 staple motifs. The 
staple motifs are staggered on the core in a clockwise or coun-
terclockwise manner, forming two enantiomers of this NC. 
These two enantiomers also exhibit mirror-like CD absorption 
properties (Figure 8B), and their anisotropy factor is similar to 
(or even higher than) that of the intrinsic chiral metal core.[95] 
This result suggests that the chiral arrangement of the ligand 
anchoring point may induce differences in the interactions of 
metal NCs with left-handed and right-handed circularly polar-
ized light.
4.3. Ligand Body
The ligand body of metal NCs can also induce molecular chi-
rality. The simplest example is that the ligand has a chiral 
center, such as a chiral carbon atom. Metal NCs protected by 
chiral ligands, such as cysteine,[96,97] GSH,[64] and BINAP,[98] 
have been previously reported. Regardless of whether the metal 
NCs are structurally symmetric, these metal NCs exhibit optical 
activity similar to the chiral center of the ligand. In addition 
to the chiral center, the chirality on the ligands may be trans-
ferred to the superatomic state of metal NCs,[99] and it can also 
affect self-assembly of the metal NCs by introducing chiral 
helical structures. In a recent report, Ag30 NCs co-protected 
by carboranetrithiolate and phosphine ligands were synthe-
sized and crystallized.[100,101] Interestingly, its recrystallization 
in polar solvents undergoes a conglomerate crystallization pro-
cess. The functional groups on the ligand body induce various

5. Light-Induced Chemical Reactions
When metal NCs interact with light, instead of physical relaxa-
tion, the absorption of photon energy may induce chemical 
reactions. One type of light-induced chemical reactions is the 
decomposition of metal NCs, which destroys the unique prop-
erties of metal NCs. In particular, the sensitivity of Ag to light 
makes Ag nanomaterials (including Ag NCs) easy to decom-
pose when interacting with light. This instability issue can be 
addressed by introducing light-insensitive metal atoms (e.g., 
Au) into Ag NCs.[103] Nevertheless, instead of decomposition, 
the uptake of photon’s energy may trigger a well-controlled 
chemical reaction that can convert the metal NCs into other 
species with the desired properties. It was reported that in 
the presence of light and oxygen, [Au23(S-c-C6)16]− (S-c-C6  = 
cyclohexyl mercaptan) can grow into Au28(S-c-C6)20, which 
exhibits optical absorption at 435 and 550 nm.[104] The energy 
of the photons that can induce the reactions should be higher 
than the HOMO–LUMO gap of [Au23(S-c-C6)16]− (1.55  eV), 
so that the NCs can enter the excited state. The excited state 
is further transformed into Au28(S-c-C6)20 through a pathway 
involving oxidation, decomposition, and re-assembly. In 
another example, light-induced conversion from GSH-protected 
Au NCs to Au nanoparticles can enhance visible light photo-
catalysis activity.[105] Compared with Au NCs, the light-induced 
reactions of Ag NCs have been rarely reported, which may be 
due to their photostability issues, although there are reports on 
the light-induced morphology[106] and composition transforma-
tion[107] of Ag nanoparticles.
6. Highlighted Applications of Metal NCs Based 
on Their Interactions with Light
The interesting optical properties of metal NCs lay the founda-
tion for the design of optical materials based on metal NCs in 
various applications. According to our discussions above, the 
optical properties of metal NCs are highly related to their size, 
composition, ligand surface, and structure, and their interac-
tions with light of different wavelengths determine the design 
strategies of these optical materials in various applications. In 
this section, we will highlight several such designs and discuss 
how to use the interactions of metal NCs with light in the design 
of optical materials. Since there are several recent reviews on the 
design of metal NCs for optical sensors,[108–110] here we will focus 
on three other optical applications, including photosensitizers, 
light emitters, and bioimaging agents. Readers interested in the 
design of metal NCs-based optical probes for sensing applica-
tions can refer to several recent review articles.[111–113]
6.1. Photosensitizers
Photosensitizers absorb the energy of the photon and generate 
excited states when interacting with light. The excited state 
energy then transfers the energy to nearby molecules. The 
prolonged lifetime of the excited states can improve the uti-
lization of light. Therefore, this type of optical materials (i.e., 
photosensitizers) are widely used in photocatalysis and photo-
dynamic therapy. For example, in the process of photocatalytic 
water splitting to produce hydrogen, large bandgap semicon-
ductors such as TiO2 show high efficiency, but their response 
to visible light is insufficient.[114] Photosensitizers can be used 
to extend the photoactivity of the semiconductors, especially in 
the visible light range. As shown in Figure 9A, the TiO2 thin 
film mainly absorbs light in the UV range. By depositing GSH-
protected Au NCs on the TiO2 film, the light absorption can be 
effectively extended to the visible light range.[115] Further studies 
have shown that the light harvested by metal NCs is related to

6.2. Light Emitters
Due to the unique light-emitting properties, metal NCs can be 
used as light emitters in light-emitting devices (LEDs), which 
are energy-saving alternatives to conventional incandescent 
lamps. A popular LED configuration is downconversion, in 
which one or more light emitters are used to generate control-
lable colors under blue or UV radiation. It has been demon-
strated that the photoluminescence properties of metal NCs 
can be utilized when they are incorporated in a thin film struc-
ture.[121] Luminous efficiency is one of the key indicators for 
evaluating the performance of light emitters, and it is closely 
related to the quantum efficiency of metal NCs. For example, 
Ag NCs with quantum efficiency higher than 95% are a good 
candidate for yellow-emitting LEDs.[122] The tunable emitting 
properties and large Stokes shift (possibly covering the entire 
visible-light spectrum) are one of the advantages of using metal 
NCs as a light emitter. For example, incorporating orange- and 
blue-emitting Cu NCs into a commercial GaN LED chip can 
produce a white-light down conversion LED.[123,124] Other advan-
tages may include low toxicity and high stability.[125,126] A recent 
report suggested that Au NCs protected by proteins can be used 
as biophosphors, avoiding the use of toxic elements such as Cd, 
while maintaining good efficiency, brightness, and stability.[127]
6.3. Bioimaging Agents
Bioimaging represents a real-time noninvasive technique for 
visualizing living cells, tissues, and organisms. Metal NCs are 
good candidates of bioimaging agents, not only because of their 
small size and low toxicity (which facilitates rapid renal clear-
ance),[18] but also because of their attractive photo­luminescence 
properties, especially in the second NIR window (1100–
1700  nm). Within this window, the optical scattering of bio-
molecules and tissues is minimized, thereby facilitating the 
bioimaging of deep biological tissues and improving the spatial 
resolution of the images.[128] For example, Au25(GSH)18 exhibits 
an emission center at 1120 nm under excitation at various wave-
lengths.[129] Such a long emission wavelength can penetrate 
the skull and achieve cerebral blood vessel imaging.[129,130] 
Figure 9C shows the principal component analysis (PCA) over-
laid images with the arterial (red) and venous (blue) vessels of 
the brain-injured mice taken in the second NIR window using 
Au25(GSH)18 as a bioimaging agent. It was found that there are 
significant injured signals in the left brain of the stroke mice. 
Besides imaging in the second NIR window, conventional 
NIR imaging can also be achieved by the metal NCs showing 
emission in this region. For instance, in vivo blood vessel and 
kidney imaging using Au NCs showed good resolution.[131,132] 
Although a number of Ag and Cu NCs also exhibit photolumi-
nescence, their applications as imaging agents are less studied 
most likely due to the biocompatibility and stability issues.
7. Conclusion and Outlook
We have discussed the interactions of metal NCs with light, 
especially optical absorption and photoluminescence, inter-
actions with polarized light, and light-induced chemical reac-
tions of metal NCs. We have discussed the contributions of 
the light interactions of the core and the ligands (including the 
ligand anchoring point and ligand body) of metal NCs. Such 
molecular- and atomic-level understandings of the interactions 
between metal NCs and light have led to various optical proper-
ties and paved the way for various applications. In recent years, 
we have witnessed the application capability of metal NCs in 
various fields, and we have highlighted a few designs of optical 
materials based on the interactions of metal NCs with light.
Although progress has been made in understanding the 
interactions of metal NCs with light, the fundamental question 
in designing metal NCs with the desired optical properties still 
exists. On the one hand, the optical absorption of metal NCs is 
highly related to their electronic structures. Existing synthetic 
strategies have been proven to be effective in finely controlling 
the NC structures,[133–135] but there is still a long way to achieve 
the desired electronic structures of the metal core and the pro-
tecting ligands/motifs by design. In addition, as the optical 
absorption properties are not a linear addition of the core and 
the protecting ligands/motifs, it brings more complexity in 
the design. On the other hand, the photoluminescence prop-
erties of metal NCs are even more complicated because they 
may involve multiple relaxation pathways upon excitation. An 
in-depth understanding of the origin of photoluminescence 
may be of great importance to the design of metal NCs with 
desired photoluminescence properties. We propose that both 
the interband transitions from the core and the AIE from the 
surface motifs may contribute to the photoluminescence of 
metal NCs. Both the core and the surface motifs in the struc-
ture of metal NCs can feature photoluminescence properties. 
Therefore, when these two structural units are combined in a 
metal NC, they may both be the origin of photoluminescence.

We hope that this Perspective will stimulate more research 
on the interactions of metal NCs with light. The resolving of 
the crystal structure of metal NCs has greatly benefited the 
studies on their structure–property relationship. In addition, 
theoretical calculations can help reveal the electronic structure 
of metal NCs, and even predict their optical absorption and 
photo­luminescence spectra. The development in this aspect will 
surely provide more in-depth insights into the interactions of 
metal NCs with light. For instance, by revealing the molecular 
structure of Au22(SR)18 protected by thiolate ligands, it is pos-
sible to better understand the origin of its photoluminescence. 
The development of the structure–property relationship also 
helps to understand the differences in the interactions of Au, 
Ag, and Cu atoms with light. A recent study found that Ag17 and 
Cu17 NCs with a similar structure exhibit different photophysical 
properties, which may be due to the differences in the atomic 
size and the metal–metal interactions.[139] Similarly, although 
the optical absorption of [Ag25(SR)18]− has some of the features 
of [Au25(SR)18]− (a broad peak at ≈675 nm), other features, such 
as an intense peak at ≈490 nm, seem to be unique to Ag NCs.[65]
Moreover, the application of machine learning is emerging 
in the field of materials discovery. It can be a powerful tool for 
predicting or revealing hidden clues related to the optical prop-
erties of metal NCs.[140–142] The optical absorption spectra or 
photoluminescence spectra from both experiments and simula-
tions are valuable in the process of machine learning. Last but 
not the least, it can be expected that the aforementioned studies 
on the interactions of metal NCs with light will promote the 
growth of their applications in different fields.
Overall, metal NCs have shown unique interactions with 
light, and have attracted interest in both basic and applied 
research. We hope that with the continuous efforts of scientists 
in interdisciplinary fields including cluster chemistry, physical 
chemistry, nanochemistry, materials chemistry, and materials 
science and engineering, metal NCs will become a promising 
class of optical materials.

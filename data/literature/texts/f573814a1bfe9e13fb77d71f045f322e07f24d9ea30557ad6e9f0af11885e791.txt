Antibacterial Materials
Activating the Antibacterial Effect of 4,6-Diamino-2-pyrimidinethiol-
Modified Gold Nanoparticles by Reducing their Sizes
Yangzhouyun Xie, Junchuan Yang, Jiangjiang Zhang, Wenfu Zheng,* and Xingyu Jiang*
Abstract: Adequately decorated gold nanoparticles (GNPs)
have excellent antibiotic activities against multidrug-resistant
(MDR) bacteria. Nanoparticles exhibiting Gram selective
antibacterial actions are beneficial to precise therapy. Here,
we present a strategy to tune the antibacterial spectrum of
a small molecule (4,6-diamino-2-pyrimidinethiol, DAPT)-
modified GNPs (DAPT-GNPs, DGNPs) by adjusting their
sizes. Compared to large (ca. 14 nm diameter) DGNPs
(lDGNPs) and medium-sized (3–4 nm diameter) DGNPs
(mDGNPs), which have no antibacterial effect or only target
Gram-negative (G) bacteria, ultrasmall DGNPs (uDGNPs,
< 2 nm) have a broad antibacterial spectrum, especially
showing an over 60-fold increase in antibacterial efficacy
against
Gram-positive
(G +)
bacteria.
Moreover,
the
uDGNPs-functionalized scaffolds (agarose gel) can serve as
general wound dressings for healing burnt infections. Our
strategy is insightful for exploring properties of the nano-
materials and their applications.
Antibiotic nanomaterials are promising as fighters in the
battle against superbugs, where traditional antibiotics have
failed.[1,2] Among the antibiotic nanomaterials, functionalized
gold nanoparticles (GNPs) stand out because of their
excellent capability in combatting bacteria, particularly multi-
drug-resistant (MDR) bacteria.[3] Moreover, many function-
alized GNPs show negligible toxicity to mammalian cells
which is a prerequisite for a clinical antibiotic.[4] Thus,
functionalized GNPs have great potential in the antibacterial
field, such as the prevention of bacterial infection of wounds
and the sterilization of medical devices.[5] Researchers are
trying to find methods to adjust and predict the antimicrobial
spectra of GNPs against Gram-positive (G +) versus Gram-
negative (G) bacteria.
Decreasing the size of nanoparticles is beneficial for
acquiring novel properties. For instance, reduce the size of
silver (Ag) NPs could enhance their antimicrobial efficacy
and tune their antibacterial spectra.[6] However, AgNPs suffer
from high toxicity to mammalian cells, which impede their
applications.[7] Reducing the size of GNPs can change the
bioactivity of GNPs and their metabolism in vivo. GNPs with
size less than 2 nm could be metabolized rapidly by renal
clearance, and thus their toxicity could be significantly
decreased in vivo.[8,9] Our previous work shows that 4,6-
diamino-2-pyrimidinethiol
(DAPT)-modified
GNPs
(DGNPs) with a size of 3–4 nm can specifically kill G-
bacteria with excellent biocompatibility.[10] DAPT is an analog
of 2-pyrimidinethiol that exists in Escherichia coli (E. coli)
transfer RNA (tRNA),[11] thus, it has the potential to inhibit
the bacterial synthesis of tRNA.[12] Also, the positively
charged DAPT is easy to interact with negatively charged
bacteria cell walls (Supporting Information, Figure S1). Con-
sidering that many nanomaterials have different properties
when their sizes change, we wonder if decreasing the size of
DGNPs but keeping the same surface ligand modification can
adjust their antibacterial spectra while retaining their excel-
lent biocompatibility. To test our hypothesis, in this work, we
explore the antibiotic activity of DGNPs with different sizes
against various bacteria (Supporting Information, Table S1).
As expected, DGNPs with the size of about 14 nm show no
antibacterial effect, DGNPs with the size of about 3.5 nm
show antibacterial activity against G bacteria, whereas
DGNPs with size less than 2 nm exhibit broad-spectrum (G
and G +) antimicrobial activity against both wild type and
MDR strains, showing an over 60-fold increase in antibacte-
rial efficacy to kill G + bacteria compared with the circa
3.5 nm DGNPs. To designate the DGNPs with different sizes,
we call DGNPs with the size of about 14 nm, 3.5 nm, or
1.9 nm
as
large
DGNPs
(lDGNPs),
medium
DGNPs
(mDGNPs), and ultra-small DGNPs (uDGNPs) respectively
in this study. Like mDGNPs and lDGNPs, uDGNPs display
excellent biocompatibility. We explore the potential applica-
tion of uDGNPs in healing bacteria-infected wounds by
doping them in wound dressing scaffolds, since the injured
wounds are susceptible to the invasion of bacteria, whether
they are G or G + . By embedding uDGNPs in agarose gel,
we develop wound dressing that can significantly accelerate
the healing of G and G + MDR bacteria-infected burnt
wounds. Thus, the DGNPs, possessing spectrum-adjustable
antimicrobial activities, are promising candidates for biomed-
ical applications.

long-term storage, with negligible aggregation after preser-

To test our hypothesis that the size of the DGNPs could
tune their antibacterial spectra, we evaluate the antibacterial
activity of the differently sized DGNPs by measuring their
minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) via a traditional
broth dilution method. We employ a broad range of bacteria,
including G, G + , and their MDR counterparts, to screen
the antibacterial spectra of lDGNPs, mDGNPs, and uDGNPs.
It should be noted that HAuCl4 or DAPT alone has no
antibacterial activity (Table 1). As our previously reported
work, mDGNPs have good antimicrobial activity against G-
bacteria as well as the corresponding clinical strains with MIC
values between 4 and 16 mgmL1. But they are inactive
against G + bacteria (MIC > 128 mgmL1), possibly due to the
thick peptidoglycan layer of G + bacteria (Table 1).[14] By
comparison, lDGNPs with larger size have no antibiotic
effects on G + bacteria (MIC > 128 mgmL1) or G- bacteria
(MIC > 64 mgmL1; Table 1). Strikingly, the decrease of the
particle size expands the antibacterial spectrum of the
DGNPs. Compared with the larger DGNPs, uDGNPs not
only have good antimicrobial activity against G bacteria but
also exhibit over 60-fold increase in antibacterial efficacy in
killing G + bacteria (MIC  2.5 mgmL1) (Table 1). More-
over, uDGNPs can kill clinical MDR isolates, such as MDR

We investigate the antibiotic mechanisms of DGNPs using
E. coli and S. aureus as the model bacteria. We employ
propidium iodide (PI) staining to explore if the permeability
of bacterial membranes will change in the presence of
DGNPs. PI can specifically bind to DNA or RNA to induce
enhanced fluorescence, but it cannot enter through the intact
membrane of viable cells. lDGNPs show a negligible change
in the permeability of bacterial membranes (Figure 2a), while
mDGNPs can only increase the permeability of membranes
of E. coli (Figure 2b). By comparison, uDGNPs cause
a significant increase in the membrane permeability, both
for E. coli and S. aureus, in a concentration-dependent
manner (Figure 2c). We study the morphological changes of
bacteria treated with uDGNPs using scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) and TEM. uDGNPs (10 mgmL1) induce
the blurring of the cytoplasm membrane boundaries, the loss
of intracellular contents, and the collapse of the cells
(Supporting Information, Figure S6a,b). The live/dead stain-
ing imaging proves the uDGNPs-induced membrane injury,
increased membrane permeability, and the death of bacteria
(Supporting Information, Figure S6c,d). We apply a fluoro-
phore dye diSC3(5) (a cyanine dye that can show increased
fluorescence upon the dissipated membrane potential) to
study the effects of DGNPs on the cytoplasmic membrane
potential. As expected, we observe unchanged membrane
potential in lDGNPs-treated bacteria (Figure 2d). By con-
trast, a gradual dissipation of membrane potential in the
mDGNPs-treated E. coli and uDGNPs-treated bacteria (both
E. coli and S. aureus) are shown in Figure 2e and f,
respectively, which is presented by the fluorescence changes
of diSC3(5). In short, the smaller size can broaden the
antibacterial spectra of DGNPs by significantly affecting the
bacterial cell membrane, including membrane integrity, and
transmembrane potential.
We further investigate the biochemical processes trig-
gered by DGNPs when they encounter the bacteria. We
measure the intracellular ATP levels to evaluate the influence
of DGNPs on bacterial metabolism. The addition of lDGNPs
does not affect the bacterial ATP level (Figure 2g). By
contrast, the presence of mDGNPs or uDGNPs leads to the
decrease of ATP levels in the mDGNPs-treated E. coli
(Figure 2h) and uDGNPs-treated bacteria (both E. coli and S.
aureus) (Figure 2i) respectively in a concentration-dependent
manner. The biosynthesis of ATP depends on the membrane
potential.[16] Thus, the potential dissipation caused by the
membrane damage can result in the reduction of ATP
synthesis and influence the subsequent ATP-associated
metabolisms, which will inhibit the survival of the bacteria.
Many of the NP-based antimicrobial compounds induce cell
death via modulating the reactive oxygen species (ROS)
production.[17] To test whether the DGNPs cause oxidative
damages to the bacteria, we measure the intracellular ROS
levels in the DGNPs-treated bacteria. We determine the
cellular ROS levels using a commercial total ROS assay kit.
lDGNPs and mDGNPs do not induce a noticeable increase in
cellular ROS (Figure 2j–k). By contrast, a sharp rise of ROS
in both E. coli and S. aureus appears in the presence of
uDGNPs (Figure 2l), which may kill bacteria through an
oxidative damage-related pathway. It is worth mentioning
that mDGNPs do not cause the rise of ROS in E. coli,
although they can kill E. coli and decrease the ATP level and
increase the permeability of the membrane of E. coli. By
contrast, uDGNPs trigger the generation of ROS in E. coli,
implying the different antibacterial mechanisms between
uDGNPs and mDGNPs. The catalytic activity of uDGNPs is
supposed to be one of the reasons for the ROS generation.[18]
Collectively, the dominating antimicrobial actions of DGNPs
include the disruption of the membrane integrity, the
dissipation of the membrane potential, and the generation
of intracellular ROS (only induced by uDGNPs). lDGNPs
show no antimicrobial activity at all. With the decrease of size,

(only for G), whereas uDGNPs have wide-spectrum anti-
microbial activity. The interaction between the particle and
the bacteria can lead to instability of the bacterial cell wall,
the imbalance of the cellular metabolism, and over-produc-
tion of intracellular ROS, causing the death of the bacteria.
This might be one possible reason for the broad-spectrum
antimicrobial effects of uDGNPs. Next, we explore the
biocompatibility and application of uDGNPs for their excel-
lent broad-spectrum antibacterial activities.
For a preliminary assessment of the toxicity of uDGNPs,
we test their effect on the viability of human cell lines,
including human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs)
and human smooth muscle cells (HSMCs) with cell counting
kit-8 (CCK-8). uDGNPs are not toxic (cell viability > 95%)
to mammalian cells at a concentration up to 160 mgmL1 (32-
fold of the MIC) after incubating for 24 h (Supporting
Information, Figure S7a). We investigate the hemolytic
activities of uDGNPs using rat erythrocytes. We observe no
hemolytic activities (hemolysis < 5%) of uDGNPs with con-
centrations below 320 mgmL1 (Supporting Information, Fig-
ure S7b), demonstrating that uDGNPs are well suited to
blood-contacting applications (such as wound healing dress-
ings).[5] Hence, uDGNPs, exhibiting negligible toxicity to
mammalian cells, are promising for further animal experi-
ments.
The broad antibacterial spectrum of uDGNPs, along with
their excellent biocompatibility, make them promising agents
for antimicrobic wound dressings. Hydrogels can create an
ideal water environment for wound healing and offer
a promising alternative to serve as the scaffolds for the
preparation of dressings for burn wounds.[19] We select
agarose gel (A) as the crosslinking substrate to encapsulate
uDGNPs for wound dressing. We prepare uDGNPs-embed-
ded agarose gel (Au@A) via heating the mixture solution of
agarose and uDGNPs. Au@A gradually turns from liquid to
gel as the concentration of agarose increases from 0.1% to
0.4% (Supporting Information, Figure S8), along with the
declined accumulated release amount of the loaded uDGNPs
(Supporting Information, Figure S9). The rise of agarose
concentration results in a thicker gel layer that prevents the
release of uDGNPs.[20] To be noted, Au@A with agarose
concentration of 0.2% can be applied directly to the skin just
like hand cream and realize controllable drug release
(Supporting Information, Figure S10). We observe the mor-
phology of Au@A by SEM and TEM (Figure 3a). The layered
structure of agarose gel remains unchanged after complexed
with uDGNPs, indicating that the addition of uDGNPs does
not affect the structure of the gel. We observe the uDGNPs in
the Au@A by TEM (Figure 3a), which is also confirmed by
the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Supporting
Information, Figure S11).
To demonstrate the practical applicability of the Au@A,
we test their antibacterial activity in healing thermal burn
injury of rats exposed to clinical isolates, including MDR E.
coli and MDR S. aureus. We prepare three-round burnt
wounds (diameter, 2.0 cm) on the dorsal sides of female
Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats by a homemade electric iron at
4008C (45 s, 60 W).[21] We treat each rat with MDR E. coli or
MDR S. aureus to build a bacterial wound infection model.
Then we apply Au@A, agarose gel (A), and gauze (traditional
wound dressing) as dressings on the wounds (Supporting
Information, Figure S12). After 24 days, the areas of all the
wounds significantly decrease (Supporting Information, Fig-
ure S13a, S14a). Compared with the A and gauze group,
wounds are healed in Au@A group (Figure 3b,d; Supporting
Information, Figures S13b and S14b), accompanying with
dramatic dropped bacterial counts (Figure 3c,e; Supporting
Information, Figures S15, and S16), indicating that Au@A is
efficacious for bacteria elimination and wound healing.

In this study, we demonstrate that it is possible to tune the
antimicrobial activity of DGNPs by controlling their sizes.
Ultrasmall
DAPT-modified
GNPs
(uDGNPs)
(< 2 nm),
unlike their larger counterparts with narrow spectral anti-
bacterial activity (mDGNPs) or without antibacterial activity
(lDGNPs),
exhibit
broad
spectral
antimicrobial
effect.
Decreasing the size of DGNPs improves antibacterial efficacy
against G + bacteria by an over 60-fold. The effective
inhibition on bacteria as well as their drug-resistant counter-
parts indicates that uDGNPs could be a potential alternative
antibacterial agent to fight against these pathogens which are
listed in the Global Priority released by the World Health
Organization (WHO).[22] By decorating the commonly used
wound dressings (hydrogels) with uDGNPs, we find excellent
antibacterial effects of the dressings in healing the burnt
wounds infected by broad spectral MDR bacteria. Further
studies on biosafety and pharmacokinetics of uDGNPs will be
beneficial for broadening their application scope in the future.
Moreover, the size-dependent properties of nanomaterials
provide a unique insight into the design of novel nano-
materials for a broad range of applications in biomedicine.

Review
Engineering Ultrasmall Metal Nanoclusters as
Promising Theranostic Agents
Kaiyuan Zheng1 and Jianping Xie1,*
Metal nanoclusters (NCs) are ultrasmall nanoparticles with intriguing molecule-
like physicochemical properties. In recent years, metal NCs have been used as
theranostic agents in biomedicine. In this short review, we correlate the physico-
chemical properties of metal NCs to their biomedical applications, shedding some
light on the design of metal NC-based theranostic agents in cancer therapy,
imaging-guided therapy, antimicrobial agents, and bioimaging. Such design prin-
ciples could be readily realized in atomically precise metal NCs, leveraging recent
advances in synthetic chemistry and the structural resolution of metal NCs.
Correlation between Theranostic Ability and Metal Nanocluster (NC) Properties
In recent years, a large number of metal NC species have been successfully produced due to
major advances in synthetic chemistry [1–4]. Metal NCs are ultrasmall nanoparticles (NPs) with
a core size below 2 nm. They feature discrete electronic states, unique geometric structures,
and intriguing physicochemical properties [1,2]. The synthetic chemistry for metal NCs has
been well-developed over the past two decades, leading to a number of efﬁcient synthetic
methodologies capable of producing metal NCs with well-deﬁned molecular formulae and
structures at the atomic level [3,4]. These metal NCs often feature unique molecule-like physico-
chemical properties, such as well-deﬁned molecular structures, quantized charging, highest
occupied molecular orbital (HOMO)–lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) transitions,
molecular magnetism, molecular chirality, and strong luminescence [1,2]. In recent years, metal
NCs have emerged as a promising class of theranostic agents (see Glossary), ﬁnding increas-
ing acceptance in the ﬁeld of cancer therapy, bioimaging, biosensing, and biomedicine [5–11].
In this review, we focus our discussion to water-soluble metal NCs protected by thiolate ligands
(or thiolate-protected metal NCs, an emerging class of metal NCs over the past decade) by
highlighting some successful attempts in the design of metal NCs as theranostic agents in
biomedical applications. Some of the design concepts demonstrated in thiolate-protected
metal NCs can also be extended to NCs protected by other types of ligands, such as DNA and
proteins [5,9,12,13].
The physicochemical properties of metal NCs largely determine their theranostic capability. Both the
metal core and surface shell of the metal NCs affect their physicochemical properties (Figure 1).
For example, the metal core of NCs can absorb radiation energy and emit secondary electrons;
therefore, metal NCs can be used to construct radiotherapy (RT) agents [14]. In addition, the
metal core of NCs can also absorb energy from light, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS),
which is the basis for a good photodynamic therapy (PDT) agent [15]. A common surface ligand
is glutathione (GSH) (a naturally occurring tripeptide), which provides good biocompatibility for metal
NCs [14,16]. The ultrasmall size of metal NCs can further beneﬁt their tumor targeting and renal
clearance [16,17]. Moreover, metal NCs often exhibit strong luminescence due to their unique
electronic structures [18–20], which can be used to construct bioimaging probes for various
applications such as imaging-guided therapy [21,22]. In addition, metal NCs can also induce
ROS generation inside bacteria, leading to a high efﬁcacy in killing the bacteria [23,24].

The correlations between theranostic capability and metal NC physicochemical properties are
inﬂuenced by both their metal core and surface-layer attributes. Thus, in this short review article,

engineering metal NCs as theranostic agents for biomedical applications. Here, we center our
discussion on gold (Au) NC-based theranostic agents and extend some of the design concepts
to NCs of other metals, such as silver (Ag) and copper (Cu).

PDT and Imaging-Guided Therapy
Similar to RT treatment, Au NCs with the aforementioned properties can also serve as PDT agents.
In particular, Au NCs can generate singlet oxygen (1O2) through photoexcitation, which can be
used to kill cancer cells (Figure 3A). The efﬁcient generation of singlet oxygen by Au NCs is attrib-
uted to their unique O2 adsorption sites and electronic structures [15]. In particular, the bandgap of
Au25(SR)18 is ∼1.3 eV, larger than that of 1O2 (0.97 eV), providing a driving force for energy transfer.
In addition, the Au NCs also feature long lifetimes of the electronic excited states (~μs) and suitable
O2 adsorption sites, which all favor the energy transfer from Au NCs to molecular oxygen to gen-
erate 1O2. It has been shown that Au25(SR)18
– can work as an effective photosensitizer by produc-
ing 1O2 under visible/near-infrared (NIR) irradiation at 532, 650, and 808 nm. The as-generated 1O2
can lead to a high killing efﬁciency in the photodynamic cancer therapy (Figure 3B) [15].
In addition, Au NCs can also conjugate with other photosensitizers, constructing a multifunctional
agent with improved PDT efﬁciency. The improved efﬁcacy can be attributed to: (i) integration of
two different photosensitizers (organic molecules and Au NCs) into one system; and (ii) a localized
high concentration of the photosensitizers in the as-designed theranostic agent that can effec-
tively produce ROS (and localize high concentrations of ROS at the intended location), leading
to an improved efﬁciency in PDT. For example, Au NCs have been conjugated with tumor-
targeting folic acid (FA) via a covalent bond, together with another photosensitizer (chlorin e6,

Traceable Drug Delivery and Controlled Release
Au NCs with the aforementioned properties (e.g., EPR effect, renal clearance, biocompatibility,
and luminescence) can also be used to construct carriers to deliver various molecules
(e.g., drug, gene, and siRNA) for cancer therapy (Figure 4A). In a typical drug-delivery system con-
structed with Au NCs, drug loading on the surface of the Au NCs can also affect the treatment per-
formance of the metal core. For example, ultrasmall Au NCs protected by transactivating
transcriptor (TAT) peptide were used as nucleus-targeting probes to deliver genes for high transfec-
tion efﬁciency and to perform ﬂuorescence imaging and PDT under NIR-light photoexcitation
(Figure 4B) [36]. In another study, nanocomposites consisting of Au NCs and reduced graphene
oxide (GO) have been constructed to carry doxorubicin (DOX), leading to synergistic damage on
HepG2 cells [37]. Bovine serum albumin (BSA)-protected Au NCs can also be conjugated with
Herceptin and the nanocomposites showed speciﬁc nuclear targeting and good performance in kill-
ing breast cancer cells and tumor tissue [38]. Red-emitting insulin-protected Au NCs have been
used to reduce the blood-glucose level (as diabetes medicine); these NCs also feature
ﬂuorescence imaging and X-ray CT functionality [39]. Moreover, luminescent Au NCs can also be
used to deliver both antigens and immune adjuvants to antigen-presenting cells as self-vaccines
with higher immunostimulatory activity, realizing imaging-guided cancer treatment [40]. In a sepa-
rate study, Au NCs were used to deliver CRISPR/Cas9 to knockout Pcsk9 to decrease the level
of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, preventing and treating cardiovascular disease [41]. This
nanocomposite can also be used to deliver siRNA of nerve growth factor (NGF) for gene silencing
in Panc-1 cells and pancreatic tumors, aiming at pancreatic cancer treatment (Figure 4C,D) [42].
In addition, luminescent Au NCs can also be a useful component of traceable controlled-release
drug-delivery systems to achieve imaging-guided therapy. For example, luminescent Au NCs
have been used to label FA-modiﬁed pH-responsive amphiphilic polymers, featuring a core-
satellite structure that encapsulates hydrophobic anticancer drug paclitaxel, capable of releasing
the drugs in mildly acidic endosomal/lysosomal compartments (Figure 4E) [43]. This multifunc-
tional theranostic system can achieve visible tumor targeting and controlled release. Another
example is the formation of monodispersed and stable nanocomposites by crosslinking lumines-
cent Au NCs with cationic polymers. The nanocomposites showed improved luminescence
through the aggregation-induced emission pathway [44]. In addition, the self-assembled
Au NCs inside the nanocomposites are not only traceable through multiple imaging techniques,

Antimicrobial Agents
Ag NC-Based Antimicrobial Agents

ability of Ag NPs is attributed to their chemical properties (Figure 5A). For example, by generating

deliver them into bacteria. For example, Au NCs protected by quaternary ammonium (QA) can
effectively target and combat multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial infections in vivo. This has
been achieved by synergistic physicochemical mechanisms, including cationic QA-induced cell
membrane disruption and ultrasmall Au NC-induced ROS generation to disturb the bacterial
metabolism [54]. More recently, the QA-protected Au NCs have been used to coat the orthodon-
tic devices, which can inhibit the bacterial adhesion and bioﬁlm formation [55]. Moreover, the
luminescent Au NCs were also used in imaging-guided bacterial counting, proving to be an
effective antimicrobial platform. In addition, Au NCs with antimicrobial peptides self-assembled
on the surface can effectively kill MDR bacteria and they worked well in wound healing [56].
Au NCs can also be protected by other antimicrobial agents, such as lysozyme [57] or vancomy-
cin [58], to achieve bacterial killing ability. In a recent report, Au NCs have been developed as
endotoxin blockers to prevent sepsis caused by Gram-negative bacterial infection [59].
Other Emerging Theranostic Applications
Luminescent Metal NCs in Bioimaging and Biosensing
One unique feature of metal NCs is their intrinsic luminescence due to their unique electronic
states and structure, making them promising candidates for bioimaging, biosensing, and
imaging-guided therapy. Luminescence includes ﬂuorescence and phosphorescence, where
the former has short lifetimes (~ns) and small Stokes shift (b100nm), while the latter holds long
lifetimes (~μs) and large Stokes shift (N100nm). NC ﬂuorescence is usually caused by the quan-
tum conﬁnement of free electrons from sp–sp intraband transitions, and the size of NCs can
strongly inﬂuence their excitation and emission wavelengths. NC phosphorescence is caused
by the electron intersystem crossing and kinetically unfavored ‘triplet-to-singlet’ transitions and
the surface effect is the major inﬂuencing factor on the excitation and emission wavelengths [5].
It should be noted that the ﬂuorescence and phosphorescence could coexist in one NC species.
As for the thiolate-protected metal NCs, their strong ligand–metal interaction results in the ligand–
metal charge transfer and the lifetime is normally in the range of 0.1–10μs, which is orders of
magnitude longer than the autoﬂuorescence of biological systems (usually b 10ns), leading to
their excellent behaviors in bioimaging [5].
Some thiolate-protected metal NCs showed strong luminescence and promising bioimaging
results. For example, the lipoic acid-protected Au NCs performed as long-term (N20 days) trace-
able probes for in vivo living cell imaging [60] and dihydrolipoic acid-protected Au NCs have a QY
of ~1−3% with reduced photobleaching [61]. Besides small thiolate ligands, Xie and colleagues
also reported the protein-directed synthesis method to generate highly ﬂuorescent Au25 NCs in
BSA [62]. The ﬂuorescence emissions were also tunable from green to NIR by assembling two

Au NCs at the ferroxidase active sites of ferritin heavy chain, which were ideal bioimaging probes
for ferritin receptor-mediated targeting [63]. Moreover, the stability of luminescent NCs is an
important consideration. A recently developed simple and versatile strategy can produce metal
NCs (Au, Ag, and Cu) protected by designed proteins with high photoluminescence and good
photo-stability and they were also very stable under physiological conditions [64].
As for in vivo bioimaging, the NIR imaging probes, especially those NIR-II imaging probes that
reach the region of 1100–1700 nm, are usually favorable. This is due to decreased optical scat-
tering at this wavelength range, which can beneﬁt deep penetration of photons, further increasing
the resolution in biological tissue. The HOMO–LUMO electronic transition of some NCs is at
relatively lower energy (1.4–1.8eV), leading to strong absorption peak in the NIR range for
imaging. For example, zwitterionic bidentate thiol ligand-protected Au NCs feature an emission
wavelength up to 1300nm. They have been applied as in vivo imaging probes and showed
improved contrast and resolution [65]. Recently, Xie and colleagues reported doping hetero-
atom into Au25(SG)18, and the alloyed NCs have an emission wavelength in the range of
1100–1350 nm. The as-designed alloyed NCs worked well for in vivo brain vessel imaging and
tumor metastasis and exhibited high renal clearance efﬁciency to avoid long-term toxicity [21].
Beyond bioimaging, the luminescent Au NCs can also serve as effective optical probes to
construct optical sensors. For example, it was found that QA and oligopeptide-functionalized
Au NCs could target highly reactive oxygen species (hROS, such as •OH, ClO−, and ONOO−)
in living cells and zebraﬁsh [66]. Moreover, Au NCs can be assembled onto the silica particles
or carbon dots for dual-emission hROS detection in vitro and in vivo, where hROS quenched
the ﬂuorescence of Au NCs, and the emission from the silica particles or carbon dots acted as
an internal reference [67, 68]. Stimuli-responsive emission of Au NCs can also be used to detect
the amine molecules [69]. Besides, there are reports about using human serum albumin-
protected Au NCs for drug screening [70], streptavidin-labeled Au NCs for early and sensitive
detection of HIV infection [71], and NIR GSH-Au NCs for intracellular temperature sensing [72].
In addition, some advanced and complicated detection systems can be constructed with metal
NCs. For example, Au NCs with 26 Au atoms protected by nine peptides have been used to
precisely diagnose cancer invasion/metastasis [73].
Renal Clearance, Toxicity, and Pharmacokinetic Behavior
Zheng and colleagues conducted a systematic investigation on the renal clearance, kidney
imaging, toxicity, and pharmacokinetic behavior of Au NCs. The ultrasmall Au NCs have high
renal clearance efﬁciency [16]. NIR-emitting Au NCs protected by GSH worked well in noninva-
sive imaging of kidney clearance kinetics and hold enhanced kidney contrast, with imaging time
windows over the conventional NIR dyes [74]. Au NCs were also used to noninvasively differen-
tiate kidney dysfunction stages in a mouse unilateral ureteral obstruction model [75]. Moreover,
the Au NCs were successfully used as X-ray imaging probes to obtain renal clearance information
in both normal and injured kidneys [76]. In addition, NIR-emitting Au25(SG)18 was used in nonin-
vasively high temporal and spatial resolution PA imaging, to observe the transport through the
aorta to the renal parenchyma and then ﬁltration into the renal pelvis, which allows accurate quan-
tiﬁcation of the glomerular ﬁltration rate of individual kidneys in normal and pathological conditions
[77]. Besides, renal clearable NIR-emitting [198Au]Au NCs with molecule-like pharmacokinetics
and radioactivity worked well as a single-photon emission computed tomography imaging
agent [26].
The authors also evaluated the toxicity of the Au NCs in mice and monkeys and the results
showed that the toxicity is dose-dependent, with an injection dose above 15 mg kg–1. The Au

accelerate the clearance of off-target free DOX via renal elimination, resulting in effective cancer
treatment and low toxicity at the same time [79]. In addition, they reported that the GSH-mediated
biotransformation in liver inﬂuenced the in vivo transport of Au25 NCs, where the GSH efﬂux from
hepatocytes can cause high local concentration of GSH and cysteine in liver sinusoids to trans-
form the surface chemistry of NCs, reducing the afﬁnity of NCs to serum proteins and changing
their blood retention, targeting, and clearance [80].
Inﬂuencing Factors in Theranostic Applications
It should be noted that the size and surface of Au NCs plays a key role in determining their
theranostic behaviors. It was found that only a few-atom difference would lead to signiﬁcantly
different theranostic application efﬁcacy. For instance, a four- to nine-fold reduction of the renal
clearance ability was observed for Au18, Au15, and Au10-11 when compared with Au25, which
are only different by a few atoms [81]. The smaller Au NCs were more easily trapped by the
glomerular glycocalyx, resulting in slower renal clearance. In another example, Au5Peptide3
(peptide: H2N-YHWYGYTPQNVI-KKKKYCC-COOH) induced CNE1 cell apoptosis while
Au22Peptide10 did not. This can be due to the poor internalization ability of the larger Au NCs
into mitochondria [82]. Moreover, the surface of Au NCs is also crucial in determining their cellular
interaction and it was found that lower surface coverage caused faster cellular interaction and
stronger membrane binding but lower cellular uptake, while higher surface coverage would
induce slower cellular interaction and lower membrane binding but higher cellular uptake [83].
The surface of Au NCs can also inﬂuence their antimicrobial efﬁcacy [51].
The stability of metal NCs is also an important inﬂuencing factor in biomedical applications. In
general, metal NCs with greater stability were chosen for biomedical applications. For example,
metal NCs with greater photostability showed improved bioimaging and biosensing performance
[64,65]. The stability of metal NCs is determined by both their metal core and surface ligand. For
the metal core of NCs, one can adjust the NC structure or dope the heteroatoms inside the NCs
to improve their stability [1]. As for the surface ligand, the selection of surface ligand, such as the
length of the ligand, is crucial. For example, it has been shown that a very short surface ligand
failed to provide good protection for metal NCs. However, too long surface ligand might also
increase the hydrodynamic diameter of metal NCs, which will inﬂuence their pharmacokinetics
behavior inside the biological system [16,17].
Besides Au and Ag (mono-metallic) NCs, alloy (or bi-metallic) NCs can further diversify and enrich
the physicochemical properties of metal NCs. Some alloy NCs showed enhanced luminescence
intensity after heteroatoms doping and they can be used in constructing luminescent probes for
bioimaging and biosensing applications [18]. For instance, doping a small amount of Ag (~2 wt%)
in Au NCs achieved a three- to ﬁve-fold improvement on luminescence intensity [84]. More
recently, Xie and colleagues successfully designed alloy NCs for in vivo brain vessel imaging

[21]. In addition, isotopes can also be doped into NCs and the isotope-doped metal NCs can be
used for positron-emission tomography (PET) imaging. For example, 64Cu-doped Au NCs were

through Cerenkov resonance energy transfer [85,86]. Moreover, alloying can also be used to

density effects on the renal clearance and tumor targeting of metal NCs [87]. When the particle
density decreased, the renal clearance efﬁcacy increased in the early elimination phase, while
the passive tumor targeting decreased. However, more systematic investigations are required
to understand the inﬂuencing factors in the theranostic applications, such as size, structure,
composition (alloying), surface, and stability of metal NCs. This will provide useful information to
probe the correlation of physicochemical properties and theranostic applications of metal NCs
at atomic level.
Other Applications
Over the past decade, other interesting biomedical applications of Au NCs have also been devel-
oped. One useful feature of Au NCs is their intrinsic catalytic property [88]. For instance, Au NCs
with peroxidase-mimicking activity and renal clearance ability were used to establish a multifunc-
tional protease nanosensor that could detect disease by direct colorimetric urinary readout in less
than 1 hour [89]. In addition, peroxidase-mimicking Au NCs can also be used to detect breast
cancer cells [90]. Another case was through conjugation of GO with lysozyme-protected
Au NCs to achieve high peroxidase-like activity over a broad pH range and also selective,
quantitative, and rapid colorimetric detection of cancer cells [91]. Moreover, Au22(SG)18 can
work as light absorber to transfer electrons towards photosynthetic biohybrid systems for biosyn-
thesis of acetic acid from CO2 in nonphotosynthetic bacteria [92]. There are other interesting
applications of metal NCs. For example, Au22(SG)18 assemblies can mimic the invasion of eukary-
otic cells by pathogens [93]. Ultrasmall Au NCs can also pass through the BBB and have worked
as theranostic agents against Alzheimer’s disease and other central nervous system disorders
[94,95]. Au NCs showed anti-inﬂammatory effects on the recovery of the destructive cartilage/
bone for rheumatoid arthritis treatment [96]. In another study, Ag NCs served as energy
acceptors for upconversion phosphors to construct a luminescence resonance energy transfer
probe for biothiol detection [97].
Concluding Remarks and Future Perspectives
In summary, metal NCs are effective and promising theranostic agents in biomedical applications,
including cancer therapy (e.g., RT, PDT, imaging-guided therapy, and drug delivery), antimicrobial
agents (both Ag and Au NCs), and bioimaging and biosensing. The theranostic ability of metal
NCs is dictated by their intrinsic physicochemical properties, which are attributed to both the
metal core and the surface of the NCs. In-depth discussions on the correlation between the
theranostic ability and physicochemical properties presented in this review article would shed
some light on the design of desirable NC-based theranostic agents for biomedical applications.
The recent cluster chemistry development [1–4] has made possible the atomic level management
of the size, structure, composition, and surface of metal NCs, further paving their way toward
promising theranostic agents in biomedical applications.
However, some challenging issues in the design of NC-based theranostic agents (see Outstanding
Question) still require concerted efforts from researchers from multidisciplinary backgrounds,
including materials chemistry, inorganic and organic chemistry, physical chemistry, nanochemistry,
nanomedicine, biomedicine, and medical doctors. For example, the community needs to provide
more systematic and in-depth investigations on the correlation of size, structure, composition,
and surface of metal NCs to their theranostic performance in biomedicine. Another interesting

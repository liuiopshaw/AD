A B S T R A C T   
Nanoalloys have been well attracted due to their unique physiochemical and optical properties. Although some 
nanoalloys are known to remain stable in complex biological environment because of the electron compensation 
effect (ECE), the effective modulation of ECE has yet to be explored. Here, we have demonstrated that the ECE 
can be modulated after adsorption of carboxylate on gold silver nanoboxes (GSNBs), accompanied with the 
release of alloy nanoclusters, as supported by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy and the electron density dif­
ference calculation. Moreover, the modulation efficiency is dependent on the pH of solution and the number of 
carboxylate groups. Using citrate as a representative, about 80 % silver components of GSNBs can be oxidized 
and released within 30 min in the presence of H2O2, producing highly-efficient antibacterial alloy clusters for 
both in vitro and in vivo applications. These results may offer a deep insight into modulation of intermetallic 
electron transfer of nanoalloys to develop new and effective antibacterial strategies.   
1. Introduction 
Alloy nanostructures have received general interest owing to unique 
physiochemical and optical properties, which have been widely applied 
in the field of catalysis,[1–9] biosensing,[10,11] bioimaging,[12–14] 
nanomedicine[15–21] and cancer theranostics.[22–26] For instance, 
porous AgPd0.38 nanocages can catalyze surface-bound reactive oxygen 
species (ROS) to selectively kill bacteria over mammalian cells.[27] Ag- 
Au core-sheath nanowires show enhanced conductive, stretchable, and 
biocompatible properties, making it convenient for the fabrication of 
wearable and implantable soft bioelectronic devices.[28] Gold-silver 
nanocages, in virtue of the hollow structure for drug-loading, NIR 
absorbance for photothermal activation, and controllable surface for 
flexible decoration, have been universally applied in the field of nano­
medicine.[24,29–32]. 
Note that these nanoalloys are acknowledged to be stable and can be 
used in vivo. As well known, for the composite of two kinds of metals, it is 
conductive to form a galvanic cell structure in the presence of 
electrolyte, which results in the oxidation of less inert metal.[33–35] In 
reality, however, this spontaneous reaction does not occur for some 
noble metals because of electron compensation effect (ECE).[36,37] ECE 
occurs when two noble metals (Ag or Cu vs Au, Pd or Pt) are arranged in 
a consecutively sputtered structure.[38–40] Taking the Au/Ag paradigm 
as an example, due to the difference in electronegativity, Ag atoms will 
firstly lose electrons in sp orbits, which will be accepted by sp orbits in 
Au atoms. Next, the saturated electrons of Au force an electron back- 
donation to Ag atoms through d orbits. Overall, this electron- 
redistribution results in enriched electrons for Ag atoms, which pre­
vents them from oxidation and improves the stability of nanoalloys.[38] 
Due to this reason, ECE can even avoid Ag oxidation in vivo.[37]. 
Considering above facts, the effective modulation of ECE triggered 
by endogenous biomolecules may be of great significances in biomedical 
theranostics. Here, we have found that the ECE of gold silver nanoboxes 
(GSNBs) can be modulated by carboxylates such as citrate. Silver at the 
interface of Au/Ag may lose electrons and become active to oxidization 
after attachment with citrate (Scheme 1). Under this circumstance,

2. Material and methods 
2.1. Materials and instruments 
Silver nitrate (AgNO3), sodium bisulfide (NaHS), polyvinyl pyrroli­
done (PVP, Mr = 40000), ethylene glycol and acetone were purchased 
from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Sodium citrate, potassium citrate, citrate 
acid (H3Cit), peroxide hydrogen (H2O2), tartrate, succinate, glutamate, 
oxalate, acetate, chloroauric acid (HAuCl4) and sodium nitrate (NaNO3) 
were obtained from Sinopharm ltd. (China). Notably, AgNO3 and NaHS 
solution were prepared in dark before use. 
The UV–vis spectra were recorded by a Biomate 3S spectrophotom­
eter (Thermo Fisher, USA). The transmission electron microscopic 
(TEM) images were performed by a JEM-2100 microscope operating at 
200 kV (JEOL, Japan). The silver release analysis was performed using 
an Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) (Agilent, 
USA). The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were obtained on a 
Nicolet iS5 (Thermo Fisher, USA). A MALDI-TOF MS (AB SCIEX 4800, 
AB SCIEX, USA) was used to identify the released ultrasmall clusters. 
The X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) was measured on a PHI 5000 
VersaProbe X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (ULVAC-PHI, Japan). The 
confocal microscopic images were acquired with a Nikon Ti-E confocal 
microscope (Japan). 
2.2. Synthesis of GSNBs 
GSNBs were synthesized by improving the previous methods.[41] 
Briefly, Ag nanocubes (~40 nm in length) were obtained through taking 
PVP as capping agents and ethylene glycol as reducer. The ratio of gold 
to silver was regulated by adding certain amount of 1 mM HAuCl4 to Ag 
nanocubes solution. After purification, GSNBs with surface plasmonic 
resonance (SPR) absorbance peak at ~ 500 nm was stored for further 
use. 
2.3. Characterization of GSNBs after different treatments 
GSNBs (20 μg mL−1 100 μL), citrate (50 mM, 50 μL) and H2O2 (1 mM, 
50 μL) were mixed in 37 ◦C. Other mixtures, i.e., (GSNBs only), (GSNBs, 
H2O2), (GSNBs, citrate), (GSNBs, H3Cit) and (GSNBs, H3Cit, H2O2) were 
set as control groups. After incubation for 30 min, the UV/Vis spectra 
and TEM for each sample were checked for comparison. 
To prepare a sample for IR spectra measurement, GSNBs (20 μg 
mL−1, 200 μL) were incubated with citrate (50 mM, 100 μL) for 30 min 
and then subjected to centrifugation (8500 rpm, 10 min) twice to 
remove excess PVP and citrate. After incubation with citrate and H2O2 
for 30 min, GSNBs was ultrafiltered (10 kDa, 14,000 rpm) for 10 min 
before freeze-drying. GSNBs without any treatment was used as control 
group. All samples were subject to cryodesiccation for characterization. 
2.4. Detection of released silver from GSNBs after different treatment 
The above-treated GSNBs mixtures were subjected to ultrafiltration 
(molecular weight cut-off of 10 kDa, with an average diameter of 
membrane pores around 1.5 nm) at 14,000 rpm for 5 min. The filtrate 
(molecular weight less than 10 kDa) was collected for ICP-MS analysis to 
inspect the release of ultrasmall metal species. These species are also 
investigated by the MALDI-TOF MS to probe the accurate components. 
2.5. Evaluation of electron compensation effect (ECE) of GSNBs in the 
presence of citrate 
The first-principles were employed to perform all spin-polarization 
density functional theory (DFT) calculations within the generalized 
gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) 
formulation. The projected augmented wave (PAW) potentials were 
chosen to describe the ionic cores and take valence electrons into ac­
count using a plane wave basis set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV.

Partial occupancies of the Kohn–Sham orbitals were allowed using the 
Gaussian smearing method and a width of 0.05 eV. The electronic en­
ergy was considered self-consistent when the energy change was smaller 
than 10−6 eV. A geometry optimization was considered convergent 
when the energy change was smaller than 0.05 eV Å−1. The vacuum 
spacing in a direction perpendicular to the plane of the structure is 15 Å. 
The Brillouin zone integration is performed using 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst- 
Pack k-point sampling for a structure. Finally, the adsorption energies 
(Eads) were calculated as Eads = Ead/sub -Ead -Esub, where Ead/sub, Ead, and 
Esub are the total energies of the optimized adsorbate/substrate system, 
the adsorbate in the structure, and the clean substrate, respectively. 
2.6. pH-dependent etching behaviours of GSNBs by carboxylates and 
H2O2 
The pH-dependent etching behaviors of GSNBs by carboxylates and 
H2O2 were monitored by UV/vis spectra. Note that the maximum 
absorbance peak wavelengths of etched GSNBs are different for different 
carboxylates. For example, for acetate, the peak wavelength was 940 nm 
at pH 10.2. After normalization, the ratio of OD940 nm/OD500 nm vs pH 
was used to depict the pH dependent etching behaviors by acetate. The 
same method was applied for all other carboxylates. 
2.7. anti-MRSA activity by GSNBs-citrate-H2O2 mixture 
MRSA was diluted for a final concentration with OD600 of 0.4 (~1.0 
× 107 CFU/mL). Then 50 μL bacterial solution was incubated with 
GSNBs (20 μg mL−1, 100 μL), citrate (50 mM, 50 μL) and H2O2 (1 mM, 
50 μL) and their pairwise combinations for 5 h. After that, 10 μL of the 
above mixture was added with 90 μL liquid nutrient media for further 
growth in 96-well plates. The OD600 was acquired after incubation of the 
mixture for 12 h at 35 ◦C. A group without any treatment was set as the 
control. 
2.8. In vivo anti-MRSA infection by GSNB-citrate-H2O2 mixture 
All in vivo experiments were performed according to the guidelines 
for Biological Experimentation with the approval of the National Insti­
tute of Biological Science and Animal Care Research Advisory Com­
mittee, Southeast University. The mice infected by MRSA were divided 
into 3 groups (n = 3). In the experimental group, a mixture containing 
GSNBs (20 μg mL−1, 100 μL), citrate (50 mM, 50 μL) and H2O2 (1 mM, 
50 μL) was gently smeared on wound. A same amount of PBS and van­
comycin were used as negative and positive control, respectively. The 
area of wound and body weight was recorded every-two days. Seven 
days later, all mice were sacrificed. The blood, skin of wound and main 
organs was collected for blood and H&E assays. To determine the count 
of living bacteria after treatment, the bacteria at wound sites were 
collected for LB counting before sacrifice of mice. 
2.9. Statistical analysis 
All data were manifested as mean ± SD. Student t-test was used for 
statistical significance. The Image J software was applied for quantiza­
tion of microscopic images.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Citrate triggered release of alloy clusters from GSNBs 
Firstly, GSNBs, with a surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak at 
~500 nm (black curve, Fig. 1A), were prepared by a traditional method. 
[29] Since the SPR peak wavelength exhibits etch-dependent red-shifted 
effect when GSNBs are etched, the noble metal release from GSNBs can 
be primarily estimated through the shift in this typical SPR absorption. 
No shift in SPR peak wavelength is observed for GSNBs when treated 
with H2O2, H3Cit, or citrate only (Fig. 1A), indicating that GSNBs are 
intact. While in the presence of H2O2 and citrate, the SPR peak wave­
length shifts to larger than 1100 nm within 30 min (red curve, Fig. 1A 
and Fig. S1), corresponding to a quick etching process. Moreover, this 
process is pH-dependent since the etching speed was largely decreased 
when citrate was replaced with H3Cit (blue curve, Fig. 1A). 
The etched products were separated through ultra-centrifugation 
and quantified by ICP-MS. The total amount of released silver species 
from GSNBs treated by citrate/H2O2 was almost 8 times compared with 
that treated by only H2O2 in 30 min (Fig. 1B), which demonstrates 
GSNBs are rapidly etched by citrate/H2O2. GSNBs show high stability 
and are almost intact after treatment with H2O2 up to 250 μM (Fig. 1C). 
When GSNBs are co-incubated with citrate and H2O2, metal species were 
released from GSNBs and the coating became warping. The MALDI-TOF 
MS analysis (Fig. 1D, Table S1) showed that the as-generated species 
mainly contain citrate capped alloy clusters such as [Au2Ag20Cit10]- (m/ 
z 4439.52), and [Au1Ag19Cit12]- (m/z 4516.39), and silver clusters such 
as [Ag19Cit15]- (m/z 4884.49) and [Ag25Cit15]- (m/z 5529.41). 
Since the etching process occurs in mixtures with multiple factors 
such as the pH and saline effect, their possible influence is also inves­
tigated. No etching process occured after treament with H2O2 solution in 
NaOH of pH 9.0 or in 50 mM NaNO3 in the presense of H2O2, suggesting 
only alkaline environment or saline cannot induce the release of alloy 
species (Fig. S2). In addition, the effect by metal cations is neglectable 
because the etch still works well when sodium citrate is replaced by 
potassium citrate (Fig. S3). In contrast, H2O2 alone can easily etch Ag 
nanocubes, the precursors of GSNBs (Fig. S4). When increasing thick­
ness of gold layers, the red-shift in absorbance peak wavelength of 
GSNBs is gradually decreasing after treatement with H2O2 and citrate 
(Fig. S5). For example, the absorption peak wavelength of GSNBs at 749 
nm hardly changes after treatement (Fig. S5D), suggesting the etching 
processes become ineffective. This confirms that gold coating can pre­
tect Ag core of GSNBs from oxidation. It also proves that citrate, but not 
H3Cit, can largely augment etching process of GSNBs. 
3.2. Theoretical analysis of ECE modulation by citrate 
The rationale behind the etching process was probed. First, citrate, as 
well as many di- and tricarboxylate compounds, can attach on the sur­
face of Ag.[42] This phenomenon can also be applied to GSNBs, as re­
flected by the FT-IR spectra (Fig. S6). The characteristic FT-IR peaks at 
1648 cm−1 is attributed to C––O stretching vibration in pyrrolidone ring 
PVP onto GSNBs (black line), while the evident peaks at 1587 cm−1 and 
1401 cm−1 are attributed to the stretching vibration of C––O and surface 
bending of C–H in GSNBs-citrate (red line), respectively, which are 
consistent with the attachment of citrate. These data prove that citrate 
can replace PVP and stabilize the released silver species. Furthermore, it 
can be speculated that ECE in GSNBs may be modulated in the presence 
of citrate. This possibility was evaluated using X-ray photoelectron 
spectroscopy (XPS) spectra (Fig. 2A). The binding energy of Ag 3d for 
GSNBs treated with citrate is higher than that for GSNBs treated with 
tartrate and NaNO3, respectively, indicating Ag loses electrons in the

presence of citrate. Interestingly, it seems that the escaped electrons are 
not transferred back Au orbit because the binding energy of Au 4f5/2 and 
4f7/2 also shifts to a higher binding energy (Fig. 2B). 
To provide more theoretical evidences, the first-principle based 
density functional theory (DFT) was further performed to reveal the 
mechanism. As shown in the electron density distribution map in 
Fig. 2C, a decrease of electron population closely near to center of Ag 
atoms and an increase of electron at the far-end simultaneously occurs, 
corresponding to the loss of electron in sp orbit and the addition of 
electron in d orbit, respectively. This electron redistribution is comple­
mentary to that of Au atoms, which agrees with ECE.[36] Meanwhile, 
compared with silver atoms (−0.431 eV), gold atoms (−1.256 eV) have 
a strong adsorption energy with citrate (Fig. S7). As a result, two of 
three carboxyl groups from citrate could be anchored onto Au atoms, 
while only one of them adsorbed onto Ag atom. After adsorption of 
citrate, the metal atoms lose more electrons (Fig. 2D). Moreover, the 
electron density also decreases for almost all adjacent atoms without 
direct adsorption of carboxyl groups, implying the general loss of elec­
trons from Ag atoms (Fig. S8). 
3.3. pH dependent ECE modulation by carboxylate 
To verify the effect of carboxylate on the etching process, a series of 
citrate was prepared at different alkaline pH. As shown in Fig. S9, the 
etching process worked efficiently at pH from 8.8 to 9.6, but became 
partially inhibited at pH 10.3 and totally inert at pH 11.4. Therefore, it 
was indicated that an appropriate pH range was indispensable for the 
rapid release of alloy species. Moreover, it seems that both acidic and 
strong alkaline environment may prohibit the etching process (Fig. 1), 
suggesting that the modulation of ECE may be simultaneously affected 
by the deprotonation of carboxylate, as well as the pH dependent redox 
potential of metal species. 
To verify this view, besides citrate, a series of carboxylates were also 
investigated. Similar pH-dependent effect can be observed (Fig. 3), 
indicating that all carboxylates can modulate ECE only in a certain 
working pH range. The corresponding maximum wavelengths are listed 
in Table S2. As can be seen, the etching efficiency varies greatly for 
different carboxylate. Generally, the working pH range was relevant 
with the number of carboxyl groups. For examples, tricarboxylate (cit­
rate) and dicarboxylates (succinate and oxalate) had a larger working 
pH range than monocarboxylate (acetate). Succinate shows a wider 
working pH range than oxalate, implying the alkyl C2-spacer between 
carboxylate groups may offer better ECE modulation. This outcome was 
in concordance with the fact that carboxylates with two-alkyl chain 
distance between terminal –COOH entailed the most efficient adsorption 
onto Ag surface found by Yin’s group [42]. Moreover, the addition of 
–OH showed a negative modulation effect, by comparison of tartrate 
(with two adjacent hydroxyl groups) and succinate (hydroxyl group- 
free). The working pH range for citrate was largest (7.7–11.0) among 
all carboxylates, which also showed a maximum SPR wavelength shift 
(up to 1100 nm). 
The pH-dependent etching efficiency can be relevant with the stan­
dard redox potentials of the following etching reactions: 
2Ag(s) + H2O2 + 2H+ ↔ 2Ag++2H2O Eθ = 0.17 V (1). 
2Ag(s) + H2O2 ↔ 2Ag++2OH– Eθ = -0.72 V (2). 
Although silver oxidation by H2O2 is conductive to occur under 
acidic conditions (eq. 1), the protonated carboxyl groups do not favor 
ECE. On the contrary, the oxidation reaction turns unfavorable because 
of a large negative redox potential under neutral/alkaline environment 
(eq. 2). Therefore, the etching behaviors only occur in an appropriate pH 
range. Since citrate has more negatively charged carboxylic groups than 
other carboxylates (Fig. S10), it shows the most significant etching ef­
fects. Furthermore, the choice of pH is important because the etching 
process can also be reversed at alkaline pH (Fig. S11). In a short sum­
mary, the paradoxical role of pH in the process of ECE modulation de­
termines the etching efficiency.

4. Conclusion 
In summary, we have demonstrated a facile method to realize the 
quick release of ultrasmall silver species from stable GSNBs. The 
adsorption of deprotonated carboxylate could modulate ECE at Au/Ag 
interface, under which circumstance silver species can be easily oxidized 
and etched at limited pH range. This paradoxical effect was available for 
many carboxylates, among which citrate showed the most excellent 
etching capability. The acquired etching products of ultrasmall alloy 
clusters showed excellent in vitro and in vivo anti-infection efficiency. 
This foundation may offer a deep understanding of modulation of 
electron transfer of nanoalloys and bring about new and effective anti­
bacterial strategies.

Metal Nanoclusters for Cancer Imaging and Treatment
Haiguang Zhu, Ziping Wang, Qiaofeng Yao, Ping Zhang,* Jianping Xie,* and Xun Yuan*
Cancer poses a signiﬁcant global health challenge, necessitating precise
diagnosis and eﬀective therapeutic strategies to improve survival outcomes.
Metal nanoclusters (NCs) have emerged as promising candidates for cancer
imaging and treatment, attributed to their remarkable cancer-targeting
capabilities, high imaging signal-to-noise ratio, potent anti-cancer activity,
eﬃcient in vivo clearance, and superior biocompatibility. In this review, a
comprehensive overview of the design and applications of metal NCs for
cancer imaging and treatment, focusing on their core–shell designability at
the atomic and molecular levels is provided. The unique advantages of metal
NCs for cancer imaging and treatment are emphasized, encompassing
aspects such as clinical translation, pharmacokinetics, targeted delivery, and
biocompatibility. The applications of these metal NCs in cancer imaging,
treatment, and imaging-guided treatment is summarized, focusing on how
their core–shell designs and physicochemical properties inﬂuence therapeutic
eﬃcacy. Finally, a perspective on the impending challenges and promising
avenues for the advancement of metal NCs in the realm of cancer imaging
and treatment are oﬀered.

1. Introduction
Cancer, a major threat to global health,
claims ≈10 million lives annually, empha-
sizing the importance of cancer theranos-
tics for improving patient outcomes.[1–4]
Currently, traditional cancer theranostics
primarily rely on methods such as imag-
ing examinations, endoscopy, chemother-
apy, radiotherapy, and surgery. While these
theranostics strategies have seen advance-
ments in the battle against cancer, the inﬁ-
nite proliferation of malignant tumor cells,
genetic heterogeneity, and complex metas-
tasis pathways continue to present seri-
ous challenges for accurate theranostics.
Moreover, some invasive procedures, in-
cluding endoscopy and surgery, not only
inﬂict pain on patients but also con-
tribute to rising medical costs. Conse-
quently, there is an urgent need to ex-
plore innovative medications that can over-
come the inherent limitations of tradi-
tional therapies, enabling non-invasive and
precise cancer imaging and treatment.[5,6]
Metal NCs, a new type of atomically precise ultrasmall nano-
material (<3 nm) characterized by a “metallic core–ligand
shell”,[7–15] have been considered auspicious candidates in non-
invasive and precise cancer imaging and treatment due to
their unique advantages.[16–19] The metal core, composed pri-
marily of atoms such as gold (Au), silver (Ag), copper (Cu),
platinum (Pt), or their alloys, is encapsulated by an outer lig-
and shell of organic molecules. Small molecule ligands include
thiols,[20] selenides,[21,22] alkynes,[13] and phosphines,[23] while
large molecule ligands encompass proteins,[24] polymers,[25]
polypeptides,[26] etc. (Figure 1). These ligands bind ﬁrmly to the
metal core via coordination bonds, forming a stable coordination
structure that protects against aggregation and oxidation. Collec-
tively, both the metal core and ligand shell determine the physic-
ochemical properties of metal NCs. Speciﬁcally, atomically pre-
cise metal NCs can integrate advantages associated with both in-
organic nanoparticles (NPs) (size/composition-tunable physico-
chemical attributes) and organic nanomaterials (surface chem-
istry (surface ligands and surface grafting)-depend physicochem-
ical attributes), oﬀering precise tunability of size and composi-
tion at the atomic level, as well as tailored surface chemistry at
the molecular level.[27–29] Such engineered physicochemical prop-
erties of metal NCs based on the core–shell designability at the
atomic and molecular level provide more opportunities and pos-
sibilities in manipulating the functions of NCs for non-invasive
and precise cancer imaging and treatment.[30–32]

By manipulating the size and composition of the metal core
and altering the physicochemical characteristics of surface lig-
ands (chain length, rigidity, and electron-donating abilities), the
photoluminescence (PL) properties of metal NCs can be tuned
from the visible light region (400–700 nm) to the ﬁrst near-
infrared region (NIR-I: 700–1000 nm) and further to the sec-
ond near-infrared range (NIR-II: 1000–1700 nm).[33] This tun-
ability provides an opportunity for high-resolution PL imaging.
Moreover, the size, composition, and surface chemistry of metal
NCs signiﬁcantly impact their light absorption,[34–38] photother-
mal conversion eﬃciency,[39–43] and the generation eﬃciency
of photoinduced reactive oxygen species (ROS).[44–49] These at-
tributes facilitate the development of agents for photoacoustic
(PA) imaging, photothermal therapy (PTT), and photodynamic
therapy (PDT) in cancer treatment.
Metal NCs demonstrate exceptional X-ray absorption capa-
bilities due to the high atomic number and density of their
metal cores—for instance, Au possesses a high atomic num-
ber (Z = 79) and a density (19.32 g cm−3).[50] This makes metal
NCs ideal agents for computed tomography (CT) imaging of
tumors,[51–53] as well as radiosensitizers for cancer radiotherapy
(RT).[54–57] Furthermore, the radiotherapeutic eﬀect can be signif-
icantly enhanced by doping the metal core with other radionu-
clides, such as technetium-99 (99Tc) and lutetium-177 (177Lu)[58]
and tailoring the surface chemistry.[59] The core–shell designabil-
ity of metal NCs allows for integration with radionuclides like
68gallium (Ga), 64Cu, 89zirconium (Zr), and 89strontium (Sr) for
positron emission computed tomography (PET) imaging,[60–63] as
well as gadolinium (Gd) chelates and iron oxide for magnetic res-
onance (MR) imaging.[64–66] The design ﬂexibility of metal NCs,
concerning both the metal core and surface ligands, not only en-
hances the eﬃciency of individual cancer imaging and treatment
modalities but also enables the development of the integration of
imaging and treatment for cancer.
Moreover, the core–shell structure of metal NCs oﬀers sub-
stantial advantages in cancer imaging and treatment due to
its precise control, which satisﬁes several fundamental require-
ments in this ﬁeld. First, metal NCs exhibit uniformity and de-
terminacy in size, composition, and surface chemistry. This char-
acteristic eﬀectively mitigates clinical translation barriers that of-
ten arise from heterogeneity in size, composition, and surface
chemical properties.[67] Second, the pharmacokinetics and in vivo

In recent years, metal NCs have demonstrated signiﬁcant po-
tential for cancer imaging and therapy, attracting rapidly grow-
ing research interest as evidenced by the exponential increase in
publications. While existing reviews have systematically explored
the design and diverse biomedical applications (e.g., antibacte-

and integrated imaging. The impact of core–shell designability of
metal NCs on imaging performance for targeted cancer is un-

cer therapy is discussed, encompassing chemotherapy, RT, PTT,

2. The Advantages of Metal NCs for Cancer
Imaging and Treatment
2.1. Potential for Clinical Translation
The atomically precise metal NCs provide opportunities for fu-
ture clinical applications by addressing the long-standing ques-
tion concerning clinical translation barriers caused by hetero-
geneity in size, composition, and surface chemical properties.
Currently, extensive research on various nanomaterials for can-
cer imaging and treatment, while most of them have not yet re-
ceived approval from the United States Food and Drug Admin-
istration (FDA) for clinical application. The size, composition,
and surface chemistry of traditional nanomaterials are often in-
consistent and ambiguous. These slight diﬀerences in parame-
ters can result in signiﬁcant variations in the body’s clearance
rates, biodistribution, and cancer targeting, posing major obsta-
cles to clinical translation. As a response, the FDA has outlined
a recommendation titled “Industrial Pharmaceutical Products”,
which emphasizes the importance of identifying critical quality
attributes (CQAs) determining the functionality and impact of
nanomaterials on product performance.[67] These attributes in-
clude, but are certainly not limited to, chemical composition,
average particle size, particle size distribution, general shape,
and morphology, as well as physical and chemical stability. Metal
NCs possess a precisely deﬁned molecular structure, allowing for
their core–shell structure regulation at the atomic and molecu-
lar levels, thereby demonstrating substantial potential for clinical
translation.
Currently, metal NCs have not yet entered clinical use,
with only limited preclinical studies reported in primates.[85]
For instance, SG-Au NCs exhibited favorable safety proﬁles in
cynomolgus monkeys following intravenous administration at
250 mg kg−1 over 90 days.[85] This study represents a milestone
as it advances metal NCs into preclinical trials. The absence of
clinical trials for metal NCs primarily reﬂects their status as an
emerging class of nanomedicines, for which standardized regu-
latory frameworks remain underdeveloped. Collaborative eﬀorts
with agencies such as the FDA and European Medicines Agency
(EMA) will be critical for establishing these guidelines. Notably,
structurally deﬁned metal complexes, including cisplatin, carbo-
platin, oxaliplatin, nedaplatin, and auranoﬁn, have achieved suc-
cessful clinical translation. This strongly supports the transla-
tional potential of metal NCs, as they share precise structural fea-
tures with these established agents.
2.2. Size/Composition/Surface Chemistry Dependent
Pharmacokinetics
Metal NCs exhibit favorable pharmacokinetic properties, which
are a crucial aspect in determining cancer therapeutic eﬀects. The
pharmacokinetics involves the processes occurring post-drug
administration, including absorption, distribution, metabolism,
and excretion. Essential parameters in pharmacokinetics such as
the distribution half-life (t1/2a), elimination half-life (t1/2b), distri-
bution volume (Vd), clearance rate (CL), and the area under the
curve (AUC, the total amount of drug that the body is exposed to
in a certain period) inﬂuence the amount of drug exposure within
the body.[86,87] Due to the distinct physicochemical characteristics
of metal NCs, they show excellent blood pharmacokinetics char-
acterized by short t1/2a (<6 min), long t1/2b (1–12 h), and substan-
tial AUCs.[88,89] These properties enable metal NCs to disperse
rapidly throughout the body’s tissues and prolong their retention.
The pharmacokinetics of metal NCs are dependent on their size.
For instance, metal NCs with smaller sizes exhibited longer re-
tention periods due to their high aﬃnity with the glycocalyx in
normal blood vessels (Figure 2a,b).[72]
In addition to size, the metal core composition and surface
ligands (e.g., surface charge, chain length, and type) also play
a crucial role in determining the pharmacokinetics of metal
NCs.[71,77,89–94] For instance, compared with Ag NCs, the high-
density Au NCs migrated to the blood wall edge more rapidly.
As a result, in laminar blood ﬂow, Au NCs circulate more slowly
through the blood vessels than Ag NCs, resulting in slower kid-
ney clearance and longer blood retention (Figure 2c).[71] Addi-
tionally, positively and negatively charged metal NCs are excreted
more slowly than neutral NCs, likely because their high surface
activity is readily recognized by macrophages and captured by
blood-rich organs.[95] This size/composition/surface chemistry-
dependent behaviour makes metal NCs suitable for exploring
the interplay between these factors and their pharmacokinetics.
By precisely adjusting the size, composition, and surface chem-
istry of metal NCs, researchers can eﬀectively control their phar-
macokinetics, oﬀering a promising avenue for enhancing cancer
imaging and treatment strategies.
2.3. Sized/Composition/Surface Chemistry Dependent Cancer
Targeting Attribute
The cancer-targeting attribute of theranostic agents is crucial for
cancer imaging and treatment. Metal NCs oﬀer signiﬁcant po-
tential in cancer imaging and treatment through both passive
and active targeting mechanisms. Passive targeting is facilitated
by the EPR eﬀect, which allows metal NCs to accumulate in tu-
mors more eﬀectively than organic dye molecules such as IRDye
800CW, due to their prolonged retention in tumors and rapid
clearance from normal tissues (Figure 3a).[78] The eﬀectiveness
of passive targeting of metal NCs in tumors increases linearly
with decreasing sizes. This is attributed to the EPR eﬀect of
the tumor, which is characterized by leaky tumor microvascula-
ture and impaired lymphatic drainage, resulting in longer metal
NCs retention.[91] Moreover, the coordination between the metal
and ligand could improve cancer cellular internalization. A no-
table example of enhanced cellular internalization is observed
in consensus tetratricopeptide repeat (CTPR) proteins-protected
metal NCs (CTPR-metal NCs) (Figure 3b).[96] While the origi-
nal CTPR proteins lacked the ability to be internalized by cells
(Figure 3c), the CTPR-metal NCs exhibit a remarkable capability
to enter MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells without the need for

In addition to passive targeting, metal NCs can also achieve
active targeting through ligand engineering. For instance,
molecules like folic acid (FA),[79,98–101] hyaluronic acid,[102–104] and
proteins[105,106] can bind to the overexpressed receptors on the
surface of cancer cells. Furthermore, the targeting speciﬁcity
and selectivity of metal NCs can be signiﬁcantly enhanced by
conjugating metal NCs with ligands that are speciﬁc to can-
cer cells. These ligands include peptides,[107,108] aptamers,[109–111]
and antibodies,[112–114] which can speciﬁcally recognize and bind
to certain antigens present on the surface of cancer cells. For
example, conjugating anti-CD326 antibodies with cyclodextrin-
protected Au NCs (Ab@Au NCs) has shown eﬀective breast tu-
mor targeting activity through the speciﬁc recognition of CD326
on cancer epithelial cell membranes (Figure 3e,f).[97] Conse-
quently, the core–shell ﬂexibility and customizability of metal
NCs make them eﬀective materials for both passive and active
cancer-targeting strategies. By regulating size, composition, and
surface chemistry, the eﬃciency of cancer targeting using metal
NCs can be further enhanced.

crucial for ensuring their biocompatibility. The unique architec-

environment of living organisms, minimizing their chemical re-
activity and, consequently, their toxicity. For example, Au44 NCs-
based cancer treatment drugs did not damage the major organs
(e.g., heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues) of the mice
(Figure 4a).[115,116] Furthermore, the blood function (white blood

The metabolic and excretory pathways of metal NCs are critical
determinants of their biosafety. Metal NCs are primarily elim-
inated via renal renal,[56,72] hepatic,[61,117] muscular,[76] and sali-
vary routes.[74] Research demonstrates that their size, composi-
tion, and surface chemistry govern these processes.[68,71,75] For
instance, smaller NCs (e.g., Au25 NCs <3 nm) undergo more ef-
ﬁcient renal clearance than larger counterparts (e.g., 6-nm GS-
Au NPs) due to enhanced glomerular ﬁltration.[72] Furthermore,
surface chemistry modulation—such as hydrophilic ligand in-
corporation or charge adjustment—reduces in vivo accumula-
tion and nonspeciﬁc interactions, thereby enhancing excretion
eﬃciency.[75]
Ligands form strong chemical bonds with the metal core,
such as metal-sulfur bonds, which enhance the stability of
NCs and reduce the likelihood of aggregation. The functional
groups, charge, polarity, chain length, and steric hindrance
of surface ligands further aﬀect the physiological stability of
metal NCs.[116,118–120] For instance, SG, 3-mercaptopropionic
acid (MPA), or 3-mercapto-2-methylpropanoic acid (MMPA)
molecules, which possess numerous hydrogen bonds, eﬀec-
tively encapsulate Au NCs and protect them from solvent
eﬀects.[121,122] Selecting antioxidative ligands, such as dihy-
drolipoic acid (DHLA) and cysteine (Cys), is particularly advan-
tageous for enhancing biocompatibility, as these ligands can mit-
igate oxidative stress and prevent cellular damage.[123–127] Engi-
neering of the metal core and ligand shells is also essential for
ensuring in vivo biocompatibility of metal NCs under high-dose
and long-term exposure conditions. For example, SG-Au NCs ex-
hibited no adverse eﬀects on organ function parameters in mice
even at 1,059 mg kg−1 over 14 days. Moreover, these NCs main-
tained excellent biocompatibility in cynomolgus monkeys follow-
ing intravenous administration of 250 mg kg−1 for 90 days.[85]
Overall, the atomic-level precision and core–shell-dependent
pharmacokinetics, targeting capabilities, and biocompatibility of
metal NCs make them promising candidates for cancer imag-
ing and therapy. As summarized in Table 1, metal NCs exhibit
distinct advantages compared to other nanomaterials, such as
carbon dots (CDs),[128,129] liposomes,[130–132] polymer NPs,[133,134]
and semiconductor quantum dots (QDs),[135,136] while also pre-
senting speciﬁc challenges. Key strengths include their atomic-
level precision, facile functionalization enabled by core–shell de-
sign, unique suitability for theranostics, and high biocompatibil-
ity. However, challenges related to stability (particularly for cer-
tain metals) and production scalability require further research
for practical application.
3. Cancer Imaging
The accurate and non-invasive imaging of tumors is crucial for
enhancing diagnostic precision, enabling early detection, alleviat-
ing patient discomfort, and improving treatment outcomes. Cur-
rently, metal NCs with desired functions are employed as imag-
ing probes in several imaging modalities, including PL imaging,
PA imaging, PET imaging, CT imaging, MR imaging, and LIBS
imaging. With advancements in technology, tumor imaging has
evolved from traditional single-mode imaging to more sophisti-
cated multi-mode imaging techniques. These include dual-mode
imaging such as PL/PA, PL/CT, and PET/CT, as well as triple-
mode imaging like PL/CT/MR. Such multi-modal approaches
signiﬁcantly enhance the accuracy of tumor diagnosis and mit-
igate issues like false positive signals that are often associated
with single-mode imaging. In this chapter, we will explore the
various single-modal and multimodal imaging techniques based
on metal NCs for cancer imaging.

3.1. PL Imaging
Metal NCs exhibit distinctive PL properties, including adjustable
PL wavelength, strong PL, large Stokes shift, and low light bleach-
ing, making them suitable probes for cancer PL imaging.[137–139]
A fundamental understanding of the PL mechanism of metal
NCs advances their development in cancer imaging. The PL of
metal NCs is primarily attributed to the quantum conﬁnement
eﬀect (QCE) and charge transfer transitions. QCE arises from
the discretization of electron energy levels due to the ultra-small
size of the metal core, leading to electronic transitions that in-
clude both intra-band transitions (sp←sp) and inter-band transi-
tions (sp←d).[140,141] Consequently, the regulation of the size and
composition of the metal core of metal NCs can signiﬁcantly in-
ﬂuence their band structure and state density, thereby aﬀecting
electron transitions and PL properties.[138,142–145]
Charge transfer transitions encompass ligand-metal charge
transfer (LMCT) or ligand-metal-metal charge transfer (LMM-
CT).[146,147] Speciﬁcally, 𝜋electrons in the ligand transition to the
d orbital of the metal, resulting in PL of the metal NCs. Therefore,
manipulating the electron-donating ability of the surface ligands
can eﬀectively change the PL properties of metal NCs.[148–151]
In addition, ligand type and structure (chain length, steric re-
sistance, density, hydrophobicity, and charge) can aﬀect the sur-
face rigidity of metal NCs, and thus aﬀect the PL properties of
metal NCs.[152–155] These core–shell controllable PL properties al-
low metal NCs to emit light across various wavelengths, ranging
from the visible regions (400–700 nm) to the ﬁrst and second
near-infrared regions (NIR-I: 700–1000 nm, NIR-II: 1000–1700
nm) (Figure 5a).[88,156–158] With remarkable tumor-targeting ca-
pabilities, metal NCs represent a new generation of PL probes
with vast potential for cancer imaging applications. The evolu-
tion of metal NCs in cancer PL imaging, particularly in terms of
PL bands, can be categorized into three stages - from early visible
light to NIR-I and further to NIR-II - representing a signiﬁcant
advancement in the ﬁeld.
In early research, researchers focused on the PL of metal NCs
in the visible region. The initial studies involved synthesizing lu-
minescent metal NCs, investigating their PL performance and
mechanism, and validating their utility in cancer imaging appli-
cations. Lin et al. employed a ligand exchange method to syn-
thesize Au NCs@DHLA, which exhibited intense red PL upon
UV excitation (Figure 5b,c).[164] Subsequent conjugation with
streptavidin further enhanced their visible PL imaging perfor-
mance, making it comparable to that of streptavidin-conjugated
ﬂuorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), a commonly used ﬂuores-
cent probe in bioimaging, in human hepatoma cells (HepG2)
(Figure 5d). Building upon this, various metal NCs with cancer-
targeting capabilities were developed based on their core–shell
designability for visible PL imaging of cancer.[99,166–169] However,
the inﬂuence of biological tissue autoﬂuorescence on PL sig-
nals led to low sensitivity in visible PL imaging, exacerbated
by the scattering and absorption of visible light in tissue, re-
stricting penetration depth and spatial resolution. Consequently,
metal NCs with visible light emission were primarily used for
superﬁcial tumor imaging or as PL markers in cellular stud-
ies. Nevertheless, advancements in regulating the PL mecha-
nism during this phase inspire the development of metal NCs
with NIR emission for better cancer imaging in-depth tissue in
vivo.
NIR, characterized by deeper tissue penetration capabilities
compared to visible light, has emerged as a promising modal-
ity for imaging deep-seated tumors (Figure 5e). Metal NCs with
NIR-I emission (700–1000 nm) were fabricated by manipulat-
ing the metal core and engineering ligands for in vivo tumor
imaging.[170] Wu et al. demonstrated that the bovine serum al-
bumin (BSA)-protected Au NCs exhibited a maximum emission
wavelength of ≈710 nm. This property enables in vivo NIR-I
imaging of the MDA-MB-45 tumor by leveraging the EPR eﬀect
(Figure 5f).[165] Moreover, the abundant surface functional groups
of BSA allowed for the modiﬁcation of Au NCs with cancer-
targeting molecules, enhancing active tumor-targeting eﬃcacy
and NIR imaging performance.[100,101]
With recent advancements in NIR-II imaging technology, re-
searchers have increasingly focused on utilizing metal NCs with
NIR-II emission (1000–1700 nm) for cancer imaging. NIR-II
imaging oﬀers distinct advantages over visible light and NIR-
I, such as a high signal-to-noise ratio (up to 22.5), deep pene-
tration depth (>1 cm), and high spatial and temporal imaging
resolution (10 μm and 4 ms), making it well-suited for deep

3.2. PA Imaging
Metal NCs with strong optical absorption in the visible and NIR
regions could convert light energy into heat energy via the non-
radiative transition.[177] This conversion process signiﬁcantly en-
hances the generation of robust PA signals, enabling the use
of metal NCs for tumor detection through PA imaging. More-
over, the PA signals of metal NCs could be optimized by ma-
nipulating their metal core and ligand shell. Zhang et al. engi-
neered alloy Ag@Pd NCs with remarkable photothermal conver-
sion eﬃciency (59.32%) under a 1270 nm laser, enabling them
to exhibit outstanding PA imaging capabilities, thereby facilitat-
ing the precise visualization of human gastric cancer cells (MKN-
45) following intravenous administration (Figure 7a).[178] Higbee-
Dempsey et al. reported the use of ICG-coated Au NCs as a con-
trast PA imaging agent (Figure 7b) with signiﬁcant PA signal in-
tensity in tumors. This intensity eﬀectively diﬀerentiated tumor
signals from the background PA signals of oxygenated and de-
oxygenated hemoglobin, thereby allowing in vivo tumor visual-
ization (Figure 7c,d).[179]
The PA imaging performance of metal NCs could be enhanced
by grafting with photothermal molecules. Yang et al reported that
the ligand engineering of Au44MBA26 NCs with Cy7 enabled the
photothermal conversion eﬃciency to improve from 34.96% to
65.12%, thereby providing high-time-resolution PA imaging for
precise visualization of 4T1 tumor-bearing mice (Figure 7e,f).[115]
Furthermore, enhancing the aggregation degree of ligands of
metal NCs contributes to improving the PA intensity. For in-
stance, encapsulating precise Au25 NCs protected by linear thi-
olated ligands (HSCnH2n+1, n = 4–16) into discoidal phospho-
lipid bicelles increased PA intensity more than threefold, achiev-
ing excellent PA imaging for cancer cells. These aggregation-
enhanced PA signals could be attributed to the restriction of the
intramolecular motion of the surface ligands.[180] Recently, Zhou
et al. provided new insights into the mechanism underlying the
aggregation-enhanced PA phenomenon. They proposed that the
inhibition of intermolecular energy transfer from excited Au NCs
to surrounding medium molecules, such as solvent molecules
and dissolved O2, plays a crucial role in this enhancement.[181]
This might facilitate PA imaging for tumors in vivo by reducing
the interference of tissues.

3.3. PET Imaging
PET imaging is an advanced nuclear medicine technology that
provides important information about the function of organs
and tissues by detecting the distribution of radioactive tracers
within the body. Metal NCs can serve as probes for tumor PET
imaging by utilizing a radioactive metal core or by doping the
metal core with radioactive elements. Among the various ra-
dionuclides, 64Cu stands out due to its high imaging proper-
ties and suitable half-life (12.7 h), making it an ideal radionu-
clide for PET imaging.[182] Moreover, ligand engineering of metal
NCs with targeting molecules could achieve the speciﬁc target-
ing of cancer cells and further improve sensitivity and precision
for PET cancer imaging.[60,61,183–185] The plerixafor functionalized
64CuAu NCs could target and identify tumor sites by detecting
C–X–C chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4), an up-regulated receptor
in tumor and lung metastasis of 4T1M orthotopic breast cancer,
thereby achieving PET imaging performance with high sensitiv-
ity and accuracy in the detection of CXCR4 expression in early
stage tumors and lung metastasis (Figure 8a).[61] The surface
conjugation of BSA-protected 64CuCu NCs (64CuCu NCs@BSA)
with tumor target peptide luteinizing hormone-releasing hor-
mone (LHRH) achieved 4 times higher tumor uptake than that of
original 64CuCu NCs@BSA, thereby providing a sensitive, accu-
rate, and deep penetration PET imaging in orthotopic lung can-
cer in vivo (Figure 8b,c).[60] The inclusion of other radionuclides
(e.g., 68Ga, 89Zr, and 89Sr) in metal NCs could also enhance the
performance of PET imaging in cancer imaging.[62,63]

concentration-dependent CT signal in tissues[56] with a CT value
reaching 326 Hounsﬁeld Units (HU) 6h post-injection, surpass-

(3:2) in BSA achieves enhanced CT performance, with an HU

3.5. MR Imaging
MR imaging is a medical imaging technology that utilizes strong
magnetic ﬁelds and radiofrequency pulses to generate high-
resolution images. It is widely used for the early detection and
assessment of tumors. The core–shell regulation of metal NCs
at the atomic and molecular levels positions them as a novel
class of probes for tumor MR imaging by integrating with ex-
isting MR agents (Gd and its chelates,[187–190] Gd2O3,[65,191] and
Fe3O4
[66,192,193]). For instance, Sun et al. developed MR imaging

the hydrophilic BSA molecules, which increase the coordination
of outer sphere water molecules.[187] Zhao et al. synthesized anal-

commercially available Gd-DTPA probes (Figure 10b,c).[188]

3.6. LIBS Imaging
Recently, metal NCs have received extensive attention in LIBS
imaging for tumors. This imaging technology involves direct-
ing a high-energy laser pulse at the sample surface to create a
high-temperature and high-pressure environment, resulting in
the ionization and excitation of the surface substances to form
plasma (Figure 11a).[194,195] This plasma emits speciﬁc wave-
lengths that contain information regarding the elemental compo-
sition and content of the sample material. By analyzing the wave-
length and intensity of these spectra, researchers can identify all
elemental species and their concentrations (Figure 11b–e).
For instance, bidentate zwitterionic molecules protected Au
NCs (AuZwMe2) have been shown to exhibit improved tumor up-
take and prolonged blood retention. Upon intravenous adminis-
tration of AuZwMe2, LIBS measurements revealed the presence
of Au element in the vicinity of the tumor at 30 min (Figure 11f),
with the signal intensifying over time indicating enhanced tissue
penetration and accumulation after 24 h (Figure 11g), thereby
providing the distinct LIBS image of subcutaneous U87MG
tumor-bearing mice.[196]
3.7. Integrated Imaging Technology
Individual imaging technologies like CT, MR, and PET have
made signiﬁcant advancements in cancer imaging; however, they
may not always provide accurate diagnoses for certain tumor
types. Consequently, using multiple imaging techniques is often
necessary for precise tumor diagnosis. This sequential approach
can be costly, time-consuming, and labor-intensive, potentially
aﬀecting patient health and introducing additional challenges.
To overcome these issues, integrated imaging technologies that
combine two or more modalities have been developed, showing
promise in improving diagnostic accuracy and eﬃciency. These
integrated methods leverage the strengths of various imaging
techniques to deliver more comprehensive tumor information,
reducing the chances of misdiagnosis. Additionally, they facil-
itate the detection of small tumor lesions, enabling early can-
cer diagnosis, which can decrease unnecessary medical interven-
tions and drug usage, ultimately reducing treatment costs for
patients.
Notably, the integration of the various individual imaging tech-
niques utilized by metal NCs enables multimodal imaging, sig-
niﬁcantly enhancing the accuracy and eﬃciency of cancer diag-
nosis. Metal NCs can not only convert absorbed light energy into
PL through radiative transitions but also convert light energy
into heat energy through non-radiative transitions, making them
a promising dual-mode PL/PA imaging probe for tumors.[197]
Moreover, ﬁne-tuning the size of the metal core and the ligand
coverage can optimize the balance between PL and PA signals.[94]
Additionally, the metal cores of metal NCs possess a high X-
ray attenuation coeﬃcient, making them promising candidates
for CT/PL dual-mode imaging of tumors.[198–201] Meanwhile,
ligand engineering of NIR-emitted Au NCs with the radio-dense
element of iodine could enhance the CT imaging performance,
allowing for visualization of malignant thyroid cancer through
PL/CT dual-mode imaging (Figure 12a).[202]

4. Cancer Treatment
The unique physicochemical properties of metal NCs are not
only valuable for cancer imaging but also hold promise for can-
cer treatment. They can absorb light to generate ROS and heat,
enabling their use as agents for PDT and PTT, respectively. Ad-

including chemotherapy, RT, PDT, PTT, GT, and combination

4.2. RT

4.3. PDT

uses harmless light to activate a photosensitizer, producing
high levels of ROS to destroy tumor cells. Metal NCs, new-
generation photosensitizers, provide more opportunities for the

the Fermi wavelength, results in discrete energy level struc-

cupied molecular orbital-lowest unoccupied molecular orbital
(HOMO-LUMO) transitions and tunable optical absorption span-
ning from visible to NIR light. Metal NCs could generate ROS
under light irradiation through two distinct pathways: electron
transfer (type I) and energy transfer (type II) (Figure 15a). Im-
portantly, the tunable optical absorption of metal NCs, spanning
from visible to NIR light, allows for ROS generation un-
der NIR irradiation, eﬀectively treating cancer and overcom-

4.4. PTT

non-radiative pathways, establishing them as eﬀective photother-

into large sizes in the tumor microenvironment (Figure 16a),

4.6. Combination Therapy
Combination therapy, which integrates various metal NCs
monotherapies, aims to achieve synergistic eﬀects in cancer treat-
ment. Notably, both PDT and PTT require light irradiation, mak-
ing the combination of PTT and PDT of metal NCs frequently
used in cancer treatment.[264–266] For instance, Au25(Capt)18 NCs,
characterized by high photothermal conversion eﬃciency and ro-
bust 1O2 generation (Figure 18a–d), can eﬀectively induce tumor
cell death through PTT and PDT under 808 nm irradiation, with
contributions estimated at 28.86% and 71.14%, respectively. This
dual approach has demonstrated eﬀective treatment of skin squa-
mous carcinoma.[265] However, a competitive relationship exists
between ROS generation and photothermal conversion. When
electrons in the ground state absorb external photon energy and
transition to an excited state, they can return to the ground state
through radiative transitions (PL) or non-radiative transitions
(photothermal conversion). Alternatively, these electrons may be
captured to generate ROS during their return to the ground state.
To achieve simultaneous enhancement of ROS generation and
photothermal conversion, strategies such as inhibiting PL or in-
creasing the excitation of electrons in the ground state can be em-
ployed. The metal core and ligand shell determine the electronic
structures and energy bands of metal NCs.[35] By altering the size
of the metal core, introducing diﬀerent metal atoms, or selecting
various ligands, the bandwidth can be reduced, facilitating the ex-
citation of electrons in the HOMO. This approach may improve
both ROS generation and photothermal conversion eﬃciencies.
Furthermore, ligands with strong electron-donating capabilities
can enhance charge transfer from the ligands to the metal core,
thereby providing more charge carriers for both processes. Ad-
ditionally, surface grafting of other photothermal molecules or
photosensitizers can further enhance the eﬃciencies of ROS
generation and photothermal conversion in metal NCs. There-
fore, manipulating the metal core, composition, and surface
chemistry of metal NCs could enhance photoinduced ROS gen-
eration and photothermal conversion eﬃciencies,[41,267,268] ulti-
mately contributing to the eﬀectiveness of combination therapies
that involve both PTT and PDT.
Metal NCs could eﬀectively absorb high-energy ionizing radi-
ation for the generation of ROS, facilitating combination ther-
apies such as RT with PTT or PDT for cancer treatment. Hua
et al. reported the development of pH-based size-tunable Au
NCs by combining ICG with CS-modiﬁed Au NCs. These CS-
Au-ICG NPs are delivered to the tumor site via the EPR ef-
fect, where they form pH-triggered aggregates (≈1000 nm) in
the acidic tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing tumor
aggregation and retention (Figure 18e). Upon exposure to NIR
laser irradiation, these large aggregates decompose into Au NCs,
ultimately achieving synergistic PTT and RT treatment in 4T1
tumor-bearing mice through dual irradiation with NIR and X-ray
(Figure 18f).[269]
Metal NCs are also capable of absorbing sound energy to gen-
erate ROS, a therapy known as sonodynamic therapy (SDT). The
synergistic combination of non-invasive light and sound energy
could oﬀer innovative approaches for improving cancer treat-
ment outcomes. Recently, Kayani et al. found that BSA-protected
Au NCs (Au NCs-BSA) exhibited remarkable photothermal con-
version and ROS generation when exposed to both laser irradia-
tion and ultrasound. Moreover, loading with Dox achieved eﬀec-
tive triple therapy (chemotherapy/PTT/SDT), successfully killing
the human triple-negative MDA-MB-231 and estrogen receptor-
positive MCF7 breast cancer cell lines.[270]
The versatile surface chemistry of metal NCs also enables their
combination with chemotherapeutic agents, facilitating combi-
nation therapies such as chemotherapy with PDT, PTT, and
RT.[104,271–276] Gao et al. demonstrated this by preparing cytara-
bine (Cyt)-loaded Au NCs through graphene QDs-assisted pho-
tocatalytic reduction and charge-dipole interactions. The result-
ing composite exhibits excellent photothermal conversion ca-
pabilities under NIR irradiation while delivering Cyt in a pH-
sensitive manner, releasing the drug in the acidic environment of
tumors. Consequently, this composite shows remarkable chemo-
photothermal combined eﬃcacy in killing cancer cells with an in-
activation rate of 97%, signiﬁcantly outperforming single-mode
PTT (27%) or chemotherapy (79.2%).[277]
Chen et al. designed an X-ray-triggered prodrug system (Au-
Pt) by conjugating Pt prodrug compounds with Au NCs. Under
X-ray irradiation, photoelectrons from the Au NCs transfer to the
Pt(IV) center, releasing cytotoxic Pt(II) drugs while simultane-
ously generating ROS, thereby exhibiting strong antitumor ef-
fects (Figure 18g). This unique radiation-driven electron transfer
mechanism allows the prodrug system (Au-Pt) to achieve spa-
tiotemporal controlled RT-assisted chemotherapy, highlighting
its potential for the combined treatment of advanced cancer.[271]
The controllable core–shell structure of metal NCs endows
them with signiﬁcant potential for immunotherapy and com-
bined regimens. As an emerging modality, immunotherapy ac-
tivates or enhances the patient’s immune system against tumor
cells, oﬀering high speciﬁcity and reduced side eﬀects. For in-
stance, Zhang et al. designed bivalent Au NCs (biGC@PNA) acti-
vated by radiofrequency.[278] Precise regulation of Au(I)/Au(0) ra-
tios induced tumor cell pyroptosis, releasing damage-associated

applications.
5. Integration of Imaging and Treatment
Metal NCs demonstrate both remarkable imaging capabilities
and therapeutic eﬃcacy in cancer therapy, facilitating imaging-
guided cancer treatment.[178,213,276,279–290] By integrating imaging
technology with therapeutic approaches of metal NCs, signiﬁ-
cant advantages have emerged for cancer treatment, including
enhanced targeting, multi-modal imaging capabilities, improved
treatment eﬃcacy, and reduced side eﬀects. This integration fa-
cilitates accurate imaging and treatment of cancer. Moreover, the
core–shell designability of metal NCs allows for the design of
intelligent imaging and treatment platforms that can respond
to speciﬁc stimuli, such as the tumor microenvironment. This
capability promotes intelligent drug release and therapeutic ef-
fects while enabling real-time monitoring of the cancer treatment
process.

spatial and temporal imaging resolution. For instance, Bagh-
dasaryan et al. introduced phosphocholine (PC)-coated molec-
ular Au25 NCs (AuPC) as an intratumoral NIR-II PL probe
(Figure 19a).[291] The PC coating on these Au NCs minimizes
non-speciﬁc binding to serum proteins, cells, and tissues, which

properties, making them suitable for CT imaging-guided can-

with CT imaging can further improve therapeutic eﬃcacy. For
example, Zhang et al. conjugated Dsg-3 antibodies targeting
lung squamous carcinoma cells with Au NCs for dual-mode
PL/CT imaging, accurately identifying carcinoma sites and en-

MR, and CT) and combined PDT and PTT, positioning them as

highly toxic ROS, thereby enhancing therapeutic eﬃcacy through
chemodynamic therapy.[294]
6. Summary and Outlook
Metal NCs have demonstrated considerable potential in cancer
imaging and treatment because they oﬀer unique advantages
for cancer imaging and treatment, including promising clinical
translation, favorable pharmacokinetics, targeted delivery, and
good biocompatibility. Moreover, the core–shell designability of
metal NCs at both atomic and molecular scales allows for the cus-
tomization of their functions, creating an ideal platform for de-
veloping imaging agents for various modalities, such as PL imag-
ing, PA imaging, PET imaging, CT imaging, MR imaging, LIBS
imaging, and integrated imaging. Additionally, metal NCs can be
utilized for diverse treatment modalities, including chemother-
apy, RT, PTT, PDT, GT, and combination therapies. Furthermore,
the integration of the strengths of diﬀerent imaging techniques
and therapeutic approaches makes metal NCs particularly well-
suited for imaging-guided cancer treatment and the development
of intelligent cancer imaging and treatment platforms, oﬀering
more opportunities for cancer therapy.
Currently, signiﬁcant eﬀorts have been made by the global sci-
entiﬁc community to advance the application of metal NCs in
cancer imaging and treatment. However, many urgent issues and
challenges still need to be addressed. In this context, we highlight
several critical challenges and opportunities that require collabo-
rative eﬀorts from the research community.
1) The continuous advancement of synthesis technologies now
enables the precise design and preparation of metal NCs with
tailored structures and compositions. Concurrently, enhance-
ments in single-crystal analysis techniques facilitate detailed
observation of the internal structures of metal NCs, including
atomic arrangements and coordination conﬁgurations. These
developments collectively provide unprecedented opportuni-
ties to elucidate the intricate structure-property relationships
within these materials. A profound understanding of such
structure-activity relationships is crucial for unlocking the po-
tential of metal NCs in cancer imaging and therapy. Precise
structural control allows for the optimization of their optical

2) Metal NCs with strong PL can signiﬁcantly enhance imag-
ing quality and accuracy while reducing the required dosage.
However, the PL quantum eﬃciency of water-soluble metal
NCs remains insuﬃcient (below 20%). Therefore, improving
the PL quantum eﬃciency of water-soluble metal NCs is an
important research direction that requires ongoing eﬀorts.
The mechanisms behind the enhanced PL intensity of water-
soluble metal NCs are multifaceted, involving various factors
such as intermolecular interaction, quantum size eﬀects, lig-
and eﬀects, aggregation states, and solvent eﬀects. A deeper
understanding of these mechanisms can provide signiﬁcant
support for the design of high-performance PL metal NCs.
3) Nanoenzymes have emerged as a promising tool in tumor
treatment,[295] with metal NCs gaining particular attention as
clusterzymes in this ﬁeld. However, these clusterzymes pri-
marily operate through two therapeutic mechanisms in can-
cer cells characterized by elevated H2O2 levels.[178,228] For in-
stance, metal NCs can function as peroxidases (POD), catalyz-
ing the decomposition of elevated H2O2 levels in cancer cells
into toxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH), which eﬀectively kill tu-
mor cells. They can also act as catalases (CAT), facilitating the
conversion of H2O2 into O2 to alleviate hypoxia-related symp-
toms. Despite these advancements, there is potential for fur-
ther innovation. Future research should focus on engineer-
ing clusterzymes with additional enzymatic activities, such
as SG peroxidase, thioredoxin peroxidase, adenosine deam-
inase, and tyrosinase. These enzymes are crucial in modulat-
ing oxidative stress, regulating immunosuppression, mediat-
ing metabolic processes, and inﬂuencing signaling pathways,
all of which signiﬁcantly aﬀect the proliferation, survival, and
migration of cancer cells. However, research on the enzymatic
catalytic activity of metal NCs remains in its infancy; there-
fore, regulating the metal core or organic ligand shell of the
NCs at the atomic and molecular levels to uncover their in-
ﬂuence on activity is an important challenge that needs to be
addressed in future studies.
4) Recently, chiral metal NCs have been extensively studied and
have demonstrated remarkable performance of chiral metal
NCs across various ﬁelds.[296–299] Despite this, their applica-
tion in cancer imaging and treatment remains relatively lim-
ited. These chiral metal NCs possess distinct left- and right-
handed structures, which signiﬁcantly inﬂuence their interac-
tions with biomolecules. This unique characteristic enhances
drug targeting and biocompatibility, thereby endowing chiral
metal NCs with considerable potential in cancer imaging and
treatment. Consequently, future exploration and investigation
into their use in cancer treatment could lead to revolutionary
breakthroughs, oﬀering safer, more eﬀective, and more pre-
cise therapeutic options for cancer patients.
5) In vitro diagnostic testing for cancer, particularly through the
detection of tumor markers in blood or urine, holds signiﬁ-
cant clinical value. Metal NCs, due to their unique physico-
chemical properties, are gradually demonstrating advantages
in in vitro cancer diagnostics.[300,301] However, for certain
early-stage cancers or small tumors, the concentrations of
tumor markers in blood or urine are extremely low, which
presents a challenge to the detection sensitivity of metal NCs.
Furthermore, the presence of various interfering substances
in blood and urine, such as other proteins or metabolites, may
aﬀect the ability of metal NCs to recognize tumor markers.
Therefore, reducing background interference and improving
the sensitivity and speciﬁcity of metal NCs for detecting tu-
mor markers in blood or urine remains an urgent challenge
that needs to be addressed.
6) Brain tumors are serious conditions that raise signiﬁcant con-
cerns within the ﬁelds of neurology and oncology. The pres-
ence of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) presents unique chal-
lenges for the imaging and treatment of brain tumors, as it
prevents most compounds from entering the brain from the
bloodstream. Although the use of metal NCs for intracranial
disease imaging and treatment shows great promise, research
on breaching the BBB remains in its early stages, with many
questions still unanswered. Key inquiries include how the
size, composition, and surface chemistry of metal NCs inﬂu-
ence their interactions with the BBB, their delivery eﬃciency
to the brain, and their elimination pathways once inside.[302]
Furthermore, there is an urgent need to explore strategies to
optimize the pharmacokinetics of metal NCs in the brain, in-
cluding factors such as the time required for them to pene-
trate the BBB, their retention time within the brain, and their
half-lives for elimination.
7) Metal NCs have the potential to regulate immune responses
through various mechanisms, such as activating dendritic
cells, promoting macrophage maturation, and stimulating T
cell activity. These responses enhance the body’s ability to rec-
ognize and eliminate cancer cells, which has led to increasing
interest in the use of metal NCs for tumor immunotherapy.
However, the immune system’s responses are inﬂuenced by
several factors, including individual variability, tumor types,
and the tumor microenvironment. Therefore, understanding
the interactions between metal NCs and immune regulation
mechanisms—which encompass both the activation and in-
hibition of immune cells—alongside the design of metal NCs
that can eﬀectively modulate immune responses, remains a
signiﬁcant challenge.
8) Current research on metal NCs has yielded valuable preclini-
cal insights into their potential for cancer diagnosis and ther-
apy, primarily through studies in small animal models. How-
ever, translating these ﬁndings to clinical applications neces-
sitates extending imaging studies to larger mammals and
primates. These models more accurately recapitulate human
physiology and pathophysiology, enabling a more reliable as-
sessment of the eﬃcacy and safety of metal NC-based imaging
agents. Furthermore, investigating the pharmacokinetics and
biodistribution of metal NCs in larger animals provides criti-
cal translational data for designing eﬀective human imaging
protocols. Regarding therapy, diverse metal NC-based thera-
peutic approaches exhibit diﬀerential eﬃcacy against cancer.
Systematically evaluating the impact of these varied modali-
ties on treatment outcomes is essential. Comparative analysis
of their respective advantages and limitations will enable the
identiﬁcation of optimal therapeutic strategies tailored to spe-
ciﬁc cancer types. Such comparative analyses are essential for

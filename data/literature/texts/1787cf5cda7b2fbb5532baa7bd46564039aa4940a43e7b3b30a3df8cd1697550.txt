ABSTRACT: Nondestructive detection and discrimination of fungal
pathogens is essential for rapid and precise treatment, which further
eﬀectively prevents antifungal resistance from overused drugs. In this work,
ﬂuorescent gold nanoclusters served as the basis for discriminating
Candida species. Varied on surface ligands, these gold nanoclusters
demonstrated diﬀerent optical properties as a result of the perturbation
eﬀects of ligands. The biointerface interaction between the surface ligands
of gold nanoclusters and the cell walls of Candida species can be
constructed, and their restriction on ligands perturbation eﬀect produced
enhanced ﬂuorescence signals. Owing to the variation of the cell wall
composition, cells of diﬀerent Candida species demonstrated diﬀerent
degrees of association with the gold nanoclusters, leading to discriminable
amounts of ﬂuorescence enhancements. The reverse signal response from these gold nanoclusters gives rise to a synergistic and
eﬀective assay that allows identiﬁcation of Candida species.
KEYWORDS: ﬂuorescence, gold nanocluster, biointerface, interface interaction, fungi discrimination
■INTRODUCTION
Invasive fungal infections with high mortalities has been a
major threat to public health.1,2 In nosocomial settings,
Candida albicans (C. albicans) is a serious human fungal
pathogen that can adhere to host cells and infect urinogenital
regions, respiratory tracts, and skin.3,4 Meanwhile, the
infections accompanied by non-albicans Candida (NAC)
species parasitization, such as Candida parapsilosis and Candida
glabrata, have raised concern as well.5,6 C. albicans is
susceptible to azole antifungal agents in actual clinical
treatments, whereas antifungal resistance to azoles has been
observed in some NAC species.7−9 Diﬀerent Candida species
possess diﬀerent antifungal susceptibilities. The overuse of
antifungal drugs is responsible for antifungal treatment
failure.10,11 Thus, it is essential to conduct the species
identiﬁcation in advance of antifungal treatment.
The routine strategy to discriminate Candida species is
based on microbe culture and morphology observation. The
resemblance of colonial morphologies poses diﬃculty in
species identiﬁcation.12 Cell composition analysis including
rDNA analysis, phospholipid pattern diﬀerence, or cell wall
proteins identiﬁcation were developed for accurate diagno-
sis.13−16 Although these analysis methods on a molecular scale
can improve accuracy, these procedures require professionals
and speciﬁc equipment, which are complicated and time-
consuming. The cell wall is the ﬁrst part of Candida cells to
come into contact with extraneous components.17−19 The
diﬀerent surface N-glycans that N-linked branched glycans on
asparagine residues of proteins and protein content in the cell
walls of diﬀerent Candida species largely involve the surface
hydrophobicity status.20,21 The extraneous components, which
act as a signal source, interact with cell walls at biointerfaces.22
On the basis of diﬀerent surface physical statuses of Candida
species, the diﬀerent association with Candida species can be
converted to detectable signals with satisfying ﬁdelity, which
will open a way to simply and rapidly discriminate Candida
species.
Fluorescent signal tracking has been a powerful and eﬃcient
method to gain temporal and spatial information on dynamic
events in complex biosystems.23−28 Fluorescent materials, such
as organic dyes, quantum dots, metal nanoclusters, and
ﬂuorescent composites, have been developed for biodetec-
tion.29−32 Among them, ﬂuorescent gold nanoclusters have
emerged from ordered aggregates of gold atoms and gold(I)−
thiolate motifs.33−35 The special Au0@AuI−ligand core−shell
structure endows the gold nanoclusters structural stability and
reconﬁgurable surfaces.36−38 Through facile modiﬁcation of
the surface ligands, functional groups are induced to the gold
nanoclusters.39 Then the biointerface interaction between gold

In this work, we have designed and prepared two ﬂuorescent
gold nanoclusters with 11-mercaptoundecanoic acid (MUA)
and oligo(ethylene glycol) (OEG) ligands, which are denoted
as AuNC−MUA and AuNC−MUA/OEG, respectively. We
aim to introduce interfacial interactions between ligands and
cell walls for Candida species discrimination. As shown in
Scheme 1, both gold nanoclusters can interact with cells to
restrict organic ligand perturbation and produce enhanced
ﬂuorescent signals. Owing to the cell wall diﬀerences of
Candida species, the diﬀerent interactions between gold
nanoclusters and cell walls enable gold nanoclusters to produce
discriminative signal responses. Either gold nanocluster
displays diﬀerent and speciﬁc responses to the same Candida
cells. The multiple ﬂuorescence responses may collectively
reﬂect the diﬀerence in structure of the Candida species, which
enables us to discriminate Candida species precisely and
eﬃciently.
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
Materials. 1-Aminooctaethylene glycol (NH2−PEG8−OH) was
purchased from Zhengzhou Alfa Chemical Co., Ltd. All other
chemicals in AuNC preparation were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich
without further puriﬁcation. C. albicans and yeast-extract tryptone
dextrose (YTD) medium were purchased from Beijing Bio-Med
Technology Development Co., Ltd. Candida parapsilosis (C. para-
psilosis) and malt extract medium were purchased from Ningbo
Mingzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd.
Preparation of AuNC−MUA and AuNC−MUA/OEG. AuNC−
MUA was prepared according to the published procedure43 with
minor modiﬁcations. In brief, 137 μL of gold(III) chloride trihydrate
(50 mM) and 180 μL of tetrakis(hydroxymethyl)phosphonium
chloride (1%) were injected into 8 mL of sodium hydroxide solution
(pH 9.0) under stirring. Fifteen minutes later, the reactants were aged
at 4 °C in darkness for 24 h. Then 200 μL of sodium tetraborate
solution (pH 9.0) and 75 μL of 11-mercaptoundecanoic acid solution
(0.1 M in ethanol) were added to the above reactants (1 mL) under
stirring for 1 day to synthesize AuNC−MUA. The product solution
was ultraﬁltrated by centrifugation to remove the unreacted reactants,
and then the volume was ﬁxed to 1 mL for further treatment.
AuNC−MUA/OEG was synthesized by amide formation between
the carboxyl groups of AuNC−MUA and the amino groups of NH2−
PEG8−OH. First, 1-(3-(dimethylamino)propyl)-3-ethyl-carbodiimide
hydrochloride (EDC) and n-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were added
to the 1 mL of AuNC−MUA and then activated for 15 min in
darkness (240 rpm, 25 °C). The ﬁnal molar ratio of [MUA]/[EDC]/
[NHS] was 1:0.2:0.05. After ultraﬁltration, NH2−PEG8−OH (88 μL,
10 mg/mL) was added, and the resultant solution was allowed to
react for 4 h under the same conditions to get AuNC−MUA/OEG.
Finally, AuNC−MUA/OEG solution was ultraﬁltrated by centrifuga-
tion to remove the unreacted reagents. The molar ratios of [MUA]/
[EDC]/[NHS] were adjusted to 1:0.05:0.0125 and 1:0.1:0.025 to
obtain the other two gold nanoclusters, named AuNC−MUA/OEG-1
and AuNC−MUA/OEG-2.
Calculation Methods. The geometries of the ground state of
Au−MUA and Au−MUA/OEG were optimized using the B3LYP
functional with the basis of Def2-SVP. At the same time, the atom-
pairwise dispersion correction with the Becke-Johnson damping
scheme (D3BJ) was used to improve the calculation accuracy.44 And
there is no imaginary frequency for the optimized structures. Then the
single-point energy was calculated based on B3LYP/def2-TZVP
calculation level. On the basis of these results, the distribution and
energy levels of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and
lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), and electrostatic
potential (ESP) analysis, were carried out by Multiwfn 3.8.45 All the
calculations were performed with the ORCA 4.2.0 quantum chemistry
package.46

solutions without AuNC were used as control groups.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Preparation and Characterization of AuNC−MUA and
AuNC−MUA/OEG. The AuNC−MUA nanoclusters were ﬁrst
prepared with an average diameter of 2.2 nm (Figure 1a). The
2.35 Å lattice spacing was clearly observed in the AuNC−
MUA, which is consistent with the (111) interplanar spacing of
a face-centered cubic gold crystal.43 Through the amide
formation of the carboxyl groups of MUA and the amino
groups of NH2−PEG8−OH, hydrophilic OEG was modiﬁed
on the surface of AuNC−MUA to obtain AuNC−MUA/OEG.
Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra are shown in Figure
S1 (Supporting Information). Compared to AuNC−MUA, the
weakened carboxyl vibration peak at 1710 cm−1 in AuNC−
MUA/OEG indicated the consumption of carboxyl groups
from MUA.40 The peaks appearing at 1638 and 1566 cm−1
were respectively from a CO stretching vibration and C−N
stretching vibration coupled with a N−H bending vibration,48
which indicates the formation of amide groups. Moreover, the
AuNC−MUA/OEG showed a C−O stretching peak of NH2−
PEG8−OH at 1106 cm−1.49 The FTIR results proved the
successful grafting of OEG ligands. A lower contrast in Figure
1b might be attributed to the content OEG ligands. However,
a similar size and lattice spacing of AuNC−MUA/OEG to
those of AuNC−MUA are displayed in the TEM image, which
could conﬁrm that the crystal structures were well preserved
after the ligand modiﬁcation.
The surface composition of gold nanoclusters was further
investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As
shown in Figure 2a, the binding energies of Au 4f in AuNC−
MUA located at 87.81 and 84.15 eV. The peaks were assigned
to Au 4f5/2 (88.11 eV) and Au 4f7/2 (84.46 eV) of AuI with Au
4f5/2 (87.70 eV) and Au 4f7/2 (84.05 eV) of Au0. The
coexistence of AuI and Au0 chemical states demonstrated the
preparation of gold nanoclusters.36 For AuNC−MUA/OEG,
the binding energies of Au 4f are 87.79 and 84.12 eV (Figure
2b), which was similar to those of AuNC−MUA. The peaks
could be assigned to Au 4f5/2 (88.11 eV) and Au 4f7/2 (84.46
eV) of AuI, with Au 4f5/2 (87.65 eV) and Au 4f7/2 (84.00 eV)
of Au0. The calculated AuI/(Au0 + AuI) ratio of AuNC−
MUA/OEG in Table S1 was 42.09%, which was close to that
of AuNC−MUA (42.11%). The results clearly conﬁrmed that
OEG modiﬁcation did not disturb the structure of Au0@AuI−
ligand, which are in agreement with the TEM data vide supra.

Optical Properties of AuNC−MUA and AuNC−MUA/
OEG. As the ligands largely inﬂuence the ﬂuorescence of gold
nanoclusters,50 we investigated the optical properties of the
two gold nanoclusters. As shown in Figure 3a, AuNC−MUA
showed a maximum absorption at 386 nm. In the enlarged
ﬁgure, a slight blue shift was observed in the AuNC−MUA/
OEG, whose maximum absorption occurred at 380 nm. As
shown in Figure 3b, AuNC−MUA exhibited an emission peak
at 504 nm with a quantum yield (QY) of 2.01% upon
excitation. For the AuNC−MUA/OEG, the emission peak
deﬁnitely blue-shifted to 490 nm along with a considerable
decrease. The QY was 0.43%. To explore the change of optical
properties after OEG modiﬁcation, we further measured the
ﬂuorescence lifetimes of both gold nanoclusters (Figure 3c).
The emission intensity of AuNC−MUA/OEG decayed faster
than that of AuNC−MUA. We then ﬁtted the curves and
calculated that the average lifetimes of AuNC−MUA and
AuNC−MUA/OEG were 117.3 and 103.1 ns, respectively.
The long lifetimes may suggest that the ligand-to-metal charge-
transfer process dominated the emission of gold nano-
clusters.51
Because of the strong AuI−S bond in the shell structure, the
hybrid orbitals of ligands and surface AuI construct charge-
transfer states (Figure 4a). However, as conﬁrmed by XPS
results, the AuI contents in both AuNC−MUA and AuNC−
MUA/OEG were the same. The HOMO and LUMO electron
densities of Au−MUA and Au−MUA/OEG structures were
studied, and the corresponding ESP maps are shown in Figure
4b. The HOMO and LUMO for Au−MUA and Au−MUA/
OEG localized at the same moieties (Figure S2). The HOMO
and LUMO energy levels of Au−MUA were the same as those
of Au−MUA/OEG. The maximum and minimum ESP values
of Au−S in Au−MUA, represented by blue and red spheres,
were 175 and −102 kJ/mol. These electrostatic potentials were
similar to that of Au−MUA/OEG, which indicates the
bonding nature was consistent between Au−MUA and Au−
MUA/OEG. Considering the ﬂexibility and hydrophilicity of
OEG, the conformations of the ligands increased. Thus, the
rigidity of the Au−MUA/OEG shell weakened, which may
increase the nonradiative rate knr.34 According to the formulas,

The radiative rate kr is constant for the gold nanoclusters. The
increased knr of Au−MUA/OEG resulted in the shortened
lifetime τ and the decreased QY. Meanwhile, the conforma-
tional arrangement of OEG may generate large dipole
moments (Figure 4c), which can eﬀectively perturb the
electronic states of the proximal gold core and cause the
blue shift of the absorption and emission peaks of Au−MUA/
OEG.52 Moreover, we simply adjusted the OEG content via
changing the [MUA]/[EDC]/[NHS] molar ratio. As shown in
Figure S3, when the OEG content increased, the emission
intensity of gold nanoclusters decreased. Therefore, the
diﬀerence in the photoluminescences of both gold nanoclusters
were basically involved with the conformation variation of the
ligand, as the ﬂexible OEG interacted with the Au core in a
nonbonding manner, thus biasing the photoluminiscence
behavior.
To conﬁrm the perturbation eﬀects of OEG, we measured
the ﬂuorescence spectra of both gold nanoclusters at 77 K.
When the temperature dropped from 298 to 77 K, the
molecular motion and collision reduced, which would
signiﬁcantly suppress the nonradiative decay of the excited
state species, thus consequently elevating the proportion of the
radiative downward transition. The emission intensity of
AuNC−MUA increased by 7 times, whereas that of AuNC−
MUA/OEG increased by 70 times (Figure 5a,b). The higher
enhancement in AuNC−MUA/OEG compared to that in
AuNC−MUA may be attributed to the reduction of conforma-
tional change of OEG and the nonradiative decay. Notably, the
emission peak of AuNC−MUA/OEG red-shifted to 504 nm at
77 K, which was the same as that of AuNC−MUA. The results
further proved the perturbation eﬀects of OEG on the
photoluminescence process of gold nanoclusters. The
restricted ligands motion and reduced nonradiative decay
could enhance the ﬂuorescence signals of gold nanoclusters.
Candida Species Identiﬁcation with Gold Nano-
clusters. On the basis of the enhanced emission of gold
nanoclusters with a restricted ligand, the biointerface
interactions between surface ligands and Candida cells can
be constructed to restrict ligand motion. The corresponding
ﬂuorescent signals were ﬁrst collected for Candida species
identiﬁcation. As shown in Figure 6a, after the addition of C.
albicans and C. parapsilosis solution to AuNC−MUA, the
emission intensities of both samples were enhanced. The
intensity increase for C. parapsilosis was higher than that for C.
albicans. Whereafter, when AuNC−MUA/OEG was added to
C. albicans and C. parapsilosis, ﬂuorescence enhancement was
also observed. However, the intensity increase for C. albicans is
much higher than that for C. parapsilosis (Figure 6b), which
was contrary to the results of AuNC−MUA. The QY of
AuNC−MUA in C. albicans and C. parapsilosis solutions were
increased to 2.22% and 2.57% (Figure 6c). The lifetimes of
AuNC−MUA were measured (Figure S4a) and calculated to
be 122.0 and 125.1 ns for C. albicans and C. parapsilosis. The
higher QY and longer lifetime of AuNC−MUA were observed
in the C. parapsilosis sample. For AuNC−MUA/OEG (Figure
S4b), the QY and lifetime in C. parapsilosis solution were
0.51% and 109.2 ns. The higher QY of 0.69% and longer
lifetime of 111.8 ns were observed in the C. albicans sample.
We quantiﬁed the emission changes of the gold nanoclusters in
the presence of diﬀerent Candida species by the ratio of
variable to initial intensity ((I −I0)/I0). As shown in Figure
6d, the ratio of AuNC−MUA was C. parapsilosis > C. albicans,
whereas that of AuNC−MUA/OEG was C. albicans > C.
parapsilosis. The obvious ﬂuorescence increase indicated the
presence of biointerface interactions between surface ligands
and Candida cells. The reverse ﬂuorescence responses of these

The ITC measurements were conducted to gain deeper
insight into the biointerface interactions. First, the ζ-potentials
of C. albicans and C. parapsilosis were measured to be −24.1
and −36.6 mV, respectively. Because of the surface carboxylic
acid groups or ether bonds, AuNC−MUA and AuNC−MUA/
OEG exhibited negative charges of −33.6 and −33.8 mV. As
the Candida cells and gold nanoclusters carried the same
charge, the electrostatic attraction could be excluded in the
biointerface interactions. In Figure 7a, the negative enthalpy
change (ΔH) indicated the hydrogen bond in the binding of
AuNC−MUA on C. albicans. The positive entropy change
(TΔS) came from the hydrophobic interaction. |TΔS| > |ΔH|
indicates the main driving force is hydrophobic forces.53
However, the ΔH and TΔS were both positive for C.
parapsilosis, which indicated the hydrophobic eﬀect in the
AuNC−MUA binding process (Figure 7b). Considering the
cell wall structures of the two species, C. albicans contains long
and dense oligosaccharides in mannan, which masks the
hydrophobic proteins, whereas C. parapsilosis has short
oligosaccharides, which expose hydrophobic proteins (Scheme
2a). They diﬀer considerably in the outer layer of
mannoproteins, which results in the higher cell-surface
hydrophobicity of C. parapsilosis compared to that of C.
albicans.54 The formed hydrogen bond between carboxyl
groups of MUA and mannans of C. albicans (Scheme S1a) may
contribute to the negative ΔH, while the positive TΔS may be
ascribed to the hydrophobic interaction between alkyl chains
of MUA and hydrophobic proteins. For C. parapsilosis, the
direct hydrophobic interaction was present between MUA and
exposed hydrophobic proteins, which beneﬁts the immobiliza-

For AuNC−MUA/OEG with hydrophilic OEG chains, the
negative ΔH and positive TΔS indicated that the hydrogen
bonds and hydrophobic interactions coexisted in the interface
interactions (Figure 7c,d). However, |ΔH| for C. albicans was
much higher than that for C. parapsilosis, which indicated the
existence of more ordered structures. The ether moieties of
OEG chains can form hydrogen bonds with hydroxyl groups in
polysaccharides of cell walls,55 rendering the multivalent
complexation between the gold nanoclusters and the fungi
cells, which prevailed in the original hydration of both
components. Therefore, between AuNC−MUA/OEG and C.
albicans, the newly formed hydrogen bonds were greater in
number (Scheme S1b), which contributed to the large
amplitude of enthalpy, |ΔH|. Meanwhile, hydration layers of
OEG provide steric hindrance of proteins56 to weaken the
interaction between AuNC−MUA/OEG and exposed hydro-
phobic proteins of C. parapsilosis. As a result, the biointerface
interaction between AuNC−MUA/OEG and C. albicans
(Scheme 2b) was stronger, which restricted ligand perturba-
tion and led to the higher emission of AuNC−MUA/OEG in
C. albicans solution shown in Figure 6b.
On the basis of this mechanism, we measured the responsive
ﬂuorescent signals in the presence of diﬀerent concentrations
of Candida cells (Figure S5). As the colony forming units
(CFU) increased from 5 × 103 to 5 × 105, (I −I0)/I0 values of
all the samples increased, resulting from the enhanced interface
interactions of gold nanoclusters and Candida cells. Because of
the better interaction between AuNC−MUA and C. para-
psilosis, the ratio for AuNC−MUA at each concentration of C.
parapsilosis increased from 5.66% to 44.25%, which was higher
than that of C. albicans (from −2.30% to 31.64%). However,
the ratio was almost unchanged with 5 × 105 CFU of C.
parapsilosis, which may be attributed to the complete
association of AuNC−MUA with C. parapsilosis. For
AuNC−MUA/OEG, because of the good association of
AuNC−MUA/OEG with C. albicans, the ratio at each
concentration of C. albicans increased from 17.83% to
201.79%, which was higher than that of C. parapsilosis (from
4.24% to 64.12%). Thus, both of the two gold nanoclusters
allowed quantitative detection of diﬀerent Candida species.
The discrepant association of ﬂuorescent gold nanoclusters
with two Candida species can be directly visualized by
ﬂuorescence images. As displayed in Figure 8a, the C. albicans
and C. parapsilosis exhibit similar morphologies without
ﬂuorescence. After incubation with AuNC−MUA, the
ﬂuorescence of C. parapsilosis cells is much brighter than
that of C. albicans cells (Figure 8b), which results from the
hydrophobic interactions of AuNC−MUA with the relatively
higher hydrophobic surface of C. parapsilosis. The adsorbed
AuNC−MUA then diﬀuses through the cell walls and cell
membrane,57 and ﬁnally enters the C. parapsilosis cells. For the
case of AuNC−MUA/OEG, the stronger interaction between
AuNC−MUA/OEG with a relatively higher hydrophilic
surface of C. albicans enabled better assistance for the entry
of AuNC−MUA/OEG into C. albicans cells. Thus, C. albicans
cells displayed stronger ﬂuorescence than C. parapsilosis cells
(Figure 8c). Owing to the diﬀerent biointerface interactions,
the two similar Candida species can be well and facilely
discriminated by the speciﬁcally enhanced ﬂuorescence signal
from ligand-functionalized gold nanoclusters.
■CONCLUSION
In conclusion, we have prepared AuNC−MUA and AuNC−
MUA/OEG and investigated well the perturbation eﬀect of
ﬂexible ligands on the photoluminescence features. Through
construction of biointerface interactions between surface
ligands of gold nanoclusters and Candida species, the ligand
perturbation can be eﬀectively controlled through the
produced enhanced emission ﬂuorescence. Depending on the
cell wall diﬀerences of Candida species, surface hydrophobicity
status diﬀers greatly between diﬀerent Candida species.
Discriminative ﬂuorescence enhancements were observed
from the two gold nanoclusters as a result of the diﬀerent
hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions at the biointer-
face. Moreover, each gold nanocluster can further mark speciﬁc
Candida cells, which enable reliable identiﬁcation of Candida
species. Therefore, utilizing the surface-functionalized gold
nanoclusters, we can simply construct biointerface interfaces.

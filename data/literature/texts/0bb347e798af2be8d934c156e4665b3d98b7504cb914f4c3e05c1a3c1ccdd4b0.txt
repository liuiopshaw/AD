A B S T R A C T
Development of fruit packaging with antibacterial properties is of great interest. Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO 
NPs) are widely used to enhance the antibacterial properties of chitosan-based packaging materials. ZnO NPs, 
with sizes ranging from 5 to 100 nm, exhibit strong antibacterial activity, though they also pose potential risks to 
human health. To address this concern, this study develops a novel approach that leverages the photothermal 
properties of larger ZnO NPs (>100 nm) to develop a light-responsive antibacterial coating. ZnO NPs (5 wt%) 
with a spindle morphology and photothermal activity were synthesized and incorporated into a chitosan/gelatin 
(1:1) blend, acting as light-to-heat converters. When exposed to light, these ZnO NPs induced a localized tem­
perature increase of 10.3–14.2◦C. This rise in temperature facilitated the movement of polymer chains, triggering 
a reversible thermal transition in the coating. Consequently, the encapsulated antibacterial agent polyhexa­
methylene guanidine (PHMG) was released. The release of PHMG was accelerated by up to 386% under light. 
The antibacterial rate of the coating, when exposed to light, achieved 97.3% against E. coli and 99.4% against 
S. aureus, respectively. Furthermore, blueberries packaged with the coating showed inhibited microbial growth 
by 29.1% and reduced weight loss under light. This approach presents a novel alternative to conventional 
strategies that rely on the reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated antibacterial activity of small ZnO NPs 
(<100 nm), thereby mitigating potential nanotoxicity concerns while enabling on-demand antimicrobial action, 
making it particularly suitable for food packaging applications.
1. Introduction
Antibacterial packaging offers an effective solution to inhibit mi­
crobial contamination in fruits (Firouz et al., 2021; Prasad & Kochhar, 
2014; Sung et al., 2013; Yildirim et al., 2018). Biobased and/or biode­
gradable polymers are increasingly used in the development of anti­
bacterial packaging due to their renewability, biodegradability and 
biocompatibility (Fl´orez et al., 2022; Moeini et al., 2021). Among these 
materials, chitosan stands out as the second-most abundant 
polysaccharides in the world after cellulose (Kumar et al., 2020; Priya­
darshi & Rhim, 2020). It is antimicrobial, film-forming and soluble in 
dilute organic acids (Bakshi et al., 2020). These attributes make chitosan 
particularly appealing for the development of antibacterial packaging 
materials (Fl´orez et al., 2022) (Alqarni et al., 2023; Fan et al., 2023; 
Haghighi et al., 2019; Jridi et al., 2014; Kumar et al., 2018; Lin et al., 
2020; Xu et al., 2021; Yadav et al., 2020). Despite its advantages, chi­
tosan still faces challenges in packaging applications. First, chitosan is 
rigid and brittle (Chen et al., 2018). Chitosan’s Its packaging

tensile elongation (~5%), and high elastic modulus (~7600 MPa) (Miles 
et al., 2016; Rodríguez-Nú˜nez et al., 2014). The properties of chitosan 
can be improved by blending it with flexible polymers like gelatin. 
Gelatin is a renewable biodegradable polypeptide consisting of α-, β-, 
and γ-chains (Ramos et al., 2016; Roy & Rhim, 2020). At pH above the 
isoelectric point of gelatin (pl = 4.5–5.2) or below 6.2–6.5, chitosan and

interact and form a polyelectrolyte complex (Wang et al., 2021). Chi­

parency and enhanced mechanical, thermal and barrier properties 
compared with their individual films (Chen et al., 2021; Haghighi et al., 
2019; Wang et al., 2021).
Another challenge is that the antibacterial performance of chitosan 
needs to be enhanced for active packaging applications. Chitosan con­
tains positively charged amino groups (Farasati Far et al., 2024), which 
can interact with the anionic lipopolysaccharide on Gram-negative 
bacteria or the teichoic acid on Gram-positive bacteria to depolarize 
cell membrane and cause cell death (Farasati Far et al., 2024) (Li & 
Zhuang, 2020). However, the amino groups only provide a relatively 
weak positive charge center(Li & Zhuang, 2020; Yilmaz Atay, 2019). 
Additionally, chitosan has poor solubility above its pKa (pH = 6.5)(Qu 
et al., 2023; Vallapa et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2020), limiting its anti­
bacterial function in natural environments(Qu et al., 2023; Vallapa 
et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2020). Haldorai et al. found that chitosan films 
inactivated only 7% of E. coli after 24 h at 37◦C(Haldorai & Shim, 2013). 
Other studies reported similar findings(Al-Naamani et al., 2017; Jaya­
kumar et al., 2023). Under certain conditions, chitosan can even pro­
mote bacterial growth(Sun et al., 2022). To enhance its antimicrobial 
activity, researchers have incorporated metal or metal oxide nano­
particles into chitosan (Sun et al., 2022; Vallapa et al., 2011).
Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) are among the most used metal 
oxides for enhancing chitosan’s antibacterial properties. Under UV light 
irradiation above the bandgap energy of ZnO NPs, they generate reac­
tive oxygen species (ROS) via photocatalytic water splitting and exhibit 
antibacterial activity(Joe et al., 2017). Generally, the antibacterial ac­
tivity of ZnO NPs increases with their particle size(da Silva et al., 2019). 
Silva et al. reported that small ZnO NPs (≤6.8 nm) exhibited bacteri­
cidal activity, while larger ZnO NPs (≥21.2 nm) showed bacteriostatic 
effects(da Silva et al., 2019). Similarly, Raghupathi et al. observed that 
the antibacterial activity of ZnO NPs decreased by approximately 35% 
and 55% as particle size increased from 12 to 25 and 88 nm, respectively 
(Raghupathi et al., 2011). They also found that ZnO NPs larger than 
100 nm had no significant effect on inhibiting S. aureus growth at a 
6 mM dose (Raghupathi et al., 2011). Although smaller ZnO NPs 
(5–100 nm) exhibit stronger antibacterial activity, they can pose health 
risks by crossing the air-blood barrier and translocating to systemic 
tissues(Cary & Stapleton, 2023; De Matteis, 2017; Konduru et al., 2014; 
Liu et al., 2016).
Interestingly, ZnO NPs with appropriate morphology and size exhibit 
localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) (Cui et al., 2023; Gong 
et al., 2020). This property enables them to trap light and convert it into 
heat (Cui et al., 2023; Gong et al., 2020). This unique property has led to 
their use, along with other semiconductors such as gold nanoparticles 
(Au NPs), as photothermal agents in cancer treatment and chemo­
photothermal therapy(Basuki et al., 2017; Cui et al., 2023; Gong et al., 
2020; Kim et al., 2018; Pallavicini et al., 2021; Vimala et al., 2017). 
Vilama et al. developed a chemo-photothermal therapy system using 
zinc oxide nanosheets (ZnO NS) for breast cancer treatment (Vimala 
et al., 2017). ZnO NS was coated with folic acid-functionalized poly­
ethylene glycol (FA-PEG-ZnO NS) and loaded with drug doxorubicin. 
Upon near-infrared (NIR) irradiation, the temperature of the 
FA-PEG-ZnO NS solution exceeded 50◦C within 2 min. This heating ef­
fect depended on the concentration of ZnO NS and laser power intensity 
and was more effective than that of carbon nanotubes. Similarly, Basuki 
et al. developed a light-triggered drug delivery system based on the 
photothermal properties of Au NPs(Basuki et al., 2017). When exposed 
to visible light, Au NPs incorporated in an agarose hydrogel caused a 
localized temperature increase, inducing reversible softening of the 
hydrogel and triggering the release of the pre-loaded therapeutic bev­
acizumab. The release profile was adjustable by varying the concen­
tration of Au NP, agarose, and the light exposure parameters. The 
biological activity of the released bevacizumab was highly retained. 
These studies suggest that nanoparticles such as ZnO NPs and Au NPs 
can generate a localized heat under light exposure to soften polymer 
matrices and trigger the release of encapsulated active substances. This 
strategy, which utilizes the photothermal properties of large-sized 
(>100 nm) ZnO NPs, offers a potential solution to mitigate the 
adverse health effects associated with smaller-sized ZnO NPs.
Therefore, in this study, we hypothesize that ZnO NPs with appro­
priate morphology can act as light-to-heat converters in a chitosan/ 
gelatin matrix, inducing the release of encapsulated antibacterial agents. 
Specifically, ZnO NPs in the chitosan/gelatin matrix can generate a 
localized temperature increase under light. Given that both chitosan and 
gelatin have a relatively low glass transition temperature (~ 40◦C), they 
are sensitive to heat and can undergo reversible softening. This softening 
triggers the release of the incapsulated antibacterial agent. Polyhexa­
methylene guanidine (PHMG), a broad-spectrum, approved food sani­
tizer (Wang et al., 2020), is used as the antibacterial agent in this study 
due to its good biocompatibility, low cost, and nontoxicity(Zhang et al., 
2021). The amino groups on chitosan and gelatin can interact with the 
guanidine groups on PHMG to form hydrogen bonds(Chen et al., 2021), 
aiding in the retention of PHMG in the coating matrix.
This study is novel in two aspects. First, while the photothermal 
functions of ZnO NPs have been widely studied in biomedical applica­
tions(Basuki et al., 2017; Cui et al., 2023; Gong et al., 2020; Kim et al., 
2018; Pallavicini et al., 2021; Vimala et al., 2017), their use in food 
packaging has received limited attention. Second, most previous 
research focused on the ROS-based antibacterial function of ZnO NPs, 
where their antibacterial efficacy is highly dependent on particle size. 
Smaller ZnO NPs provide better microbial inhibition but pose greater 
health risks. In contrast, this study offers a new strategy by utilizing the 
photothermal properties of large ZnO NPs (> 100 nm) to develop food 
packaging with light-triggered antibacterial activity.
The objectives of this study is: (i) To synthesize and characterize ZnO 
NPs (>100 nm) with photothermal activity; (ii) To fabricate a light- 
responsive coating by integrating the ZnO NPs and the antibacterial 
agent polyhexamethylene guanidine (PHMG) into a chitosan/gelatin 
matrix; (iii) To elucidate the light-triggered mechanism by investigating 
the photothermal-induced temperature increase, the consequent 
reversible softening of the polymer matrix, and the accelerated release 
profile of PHMG; (iv) To quantitatively evaluate the light-controlled 
antibacterial efficacy of the coating against Escherichia coli and Staphy­
lococcus aureus; (v) To demonstrate the practical application potential of 
the coating by evaluating its ability to extend the shelf life of blueberries 
under light exposure.
Overall, this work seeks to provide a new strategy for developing safe 
and effective light-responsive active packaging while mitigating the 
health risks associated with small-sized ZnO NPs. It engineers a light- 
responsive release system by pairing the photothermal converters with 
a low-Tg chitosan/gelatin matrix, achieving controlled antimicrobial 
activity triggered by light, a novel, safe, and efficient strategy for active 
food preservation.

2.2. Synthesis of ZnO NPs
ZnO NPs were prepared via microwave synthesis. ZnCl2 (0.15 g) and 
Na2CO3 (10 g) were dispersed in deionized water (50 g) in a three- 
necked flask and stirred at 35◦C for 2 h to ensure homogenization. 
The mixture, with an estimated pH of ~11, was then transferred to a 
microwave synthesizer (XH-MC-1, Beijing Xianghu Technology Devel­
opment Co., LTD, China). The synthesis was performed under contin­
uous microwave irradiation (800 W) with a ramp rate of 16 ◦C/min and 
constant magnetic stirring at 500 rpm, using an open-vessel configura­
tion at atmospheric pressure. The reaction was carried out at setpoint 
temperatures of 60, 70, 80, and 90◦C for 10 min, respectively. After 
microwave treatment, the flask was left undisturbed at room tempera­
ture for 12 h to allow complete sedimentation of the ZnO NPs. The su­
pernatant was then filtered using a microporous membrane filter 
(Φ50 mm, pore size of 0.22 μm, Tianjin Jinteng experimental equipment 
Co. Ltd., China), and the collected powder was dried in an air oven at 
60◦C for 6 h. ZnO NPs synthesized at 60, 70, 80, and 90◦C were labeled 
as ZnO-60, ZnO-70, ZnO-80, and ZnO-90, respectively. The yield of the 
ZnO NPs was 83 ± 5% across different batches. Reactions involved are as

2.3. Preparation of chitosan/gelatin (CS/G) coatings
Chitosan powder (1% w/v) was dissolved in an acetic acid aqueous 
solution (1%) and stirred (magnetic stirrer, HJ-6N, Henan Yuhua In­
strument Co., LTD, China) for 1 h at 50◦C. Gelatin powder was dissolved 
in distilled water and stirred for 30 min at 50◦C to prepare gelatin so­
lution (1% w/v). ZnO NPs and PHMG were added to gelatin solution 
followed by mixing (magnetic stirrer, HJ-6N, Henan Yuhua Instrument 
Co., LTD, China) with chitosan solution, to obtain base solutions for CS/ 
G coating (Table 2). Film samples were prepared by casting the base 
solution in a petri dish (14 g, diameter of 90 mm, Beijing Kemei 
Biotechnology Co., LTD, China) followed by drying at room temperature 
for 12 h. The film samples were conditioned at room temperature for 
24 h prior to analysis.
2.4. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization
The morphology of ZnO NPs was characterized using SEM (Zeiss 
Sigma 300, Germany). The sample was coated with a 5 nm gold layer 
and observed at a low vacuum mode and an operating voltage of 50 kV.
2.5. Fourier transform infrared spectrometry (FTIR) characterization
The coating sample (film) was analyzed using a FTIR spectropho­
tometer (Nicolet iZ10, Thermo Scientific, USA) with a KBr module at a 
resolution of 8 cm−1, scans of 32 and a wavenumber range of 
400–4000 cm−1.
2.6. X-ray diffraction (XRD) characterization
The crystal structure of ZnO NPs was determined using an X-ray 
diffractometer (Bruker D2 PHASER diffractometer, Germany), with a Cu 
Kα radiation as the X-ray source, a scanning speed of 10◦/min, and a 2θ 
range of 20–80◦.
2.7. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) characterization
Thermal properties of the coating sample (film) were determined 
using a differential scanning calorimeter (Q100, TA Instruments, USA). 
The sample (5–10 mg) was sealed in a standard aluminum pan and 
heated from −50◦C to 200◦C at a rate of 10 ◦C/min under nitrogen flow. 
The glass transition temperature (Tg) was determined at both the onset 
and the midpoint of the step change in heat capacity (ΔCp) using the 
Universal Analysis 2000 v 4.5 A software (TA Instruments, USA).
2.8. Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA)
DMA was carried out on a dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA 
Q800, TA Instrument, USA) at a tensile-film mode. Specimens 
(30 ×5 ×0.8 mm), non-irradiated or irradiated by a sunlight simulator 
lamp (25w, Dongguan Chonghan products Co., LTD, China) for 20 min, 
were measured from 0 to 80◦C at a frequency of 1 Hz, with constant 
amplitude of 3 μm, and at a heating rate of 5 ◦C/min. The storage 
modulus was recorded as a function of temperature.
2.9. Characterization of photothermal properties of ZnO NPs and coating
The sample, ZnO NPs (0.3 g) prepared at different temperature or 
coating solution with ZnO NPs (0, 5, 10, 15 wt%), was placed in a plastic 
centrifuge tube (1.5 mL), irradiated by an Infrared point laser (808 nm, 
1 W/cm2, Zhuhai Tengxing photoelectric technology Co., LTD, China) 
for 3 or 5 min. Temperature change of the sample was recorded using a 
multichannel temperature tester (Shenhuaxuan Technology Co. LTD., 
China). The light source used in this study was selected based on pre­
vious research investigating the photothermal effect of ZnO NPs and 
other metal nanoparticles (Macharia et al., 2016; Ou et al., 2016; 
Oudjedi & Kirk, 2024; Shi et al., 2018; Wang & Yu, 2024). The mono­
chromatic 808 nm laser was employed here to precisely characterize and 
confirm the fundamental photothermal conversion mechanism of the 
synthesized ZnO NPs. For all subsequent application-oriented tests, a 
broadband sunlight simulator lamp was used to mimic realistic storage 
conditions and validate the system's functionality under practical light 
irradiation.

2.11. Characterization of antibacterial performance
To evaluate the antibacterial activity of the coating (with or without 
light irradiation), coating sample (CS/G, CS/G/ZnO or CS/G/ZnO/ 
PHMG, film, diameter of 1 cm) was added to a bacterial suspension 
(S. Aureus or E. Coli, OD600 =0.1, 10 µL) together with sterilized 
deionized water (200 µL). The sample was subjected to light exposure 
using a sunlight simulator lamp (25w, Dongguan Chonghan products 
Co., LTD, China) for 0–30 min. The treated solution (100 μL) was then 
coated on an agar plate, incubated (incubator, Shanghai Lichen Bangxi 
Instrument Technology Co., LTD, China) at 37◦C for 24 h，and sub­
jected to colony counting using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). The anti­
microbial efficiency (AE, %) was calculated as: AE = (N0-N)/N0 
× 100%, where N0 and N represent the bacteria number of control (CS/ 
G coating) and experimental groups (CS/G/ZnO or CS/G/ZnO/PHMG), 
respectively.
2.12. Food packaging application of coating
The coating (CS/G/ZnO/PHMG, 13 mL) was applied to a paper 
substrate (13 ×13 cm, wrapping tissue paper) and conditioned at 30◦C 
for 12 h. Blueberries (20–40 g) wrapped by the coated paper were put in 
a desiccator with a relative humidity of 80% and light irradiation by a 
sunlight simulator lamp (25w, Dongguan Chonghan products Co., LTD, 
China) for up to 5 days. The temperature inside the desiccator was 
approximately 35◦C during the testing. Total viable count (TVC) of 
bacteria, total yeast and mold count (TYMC), total flavonoid content, 
total polyphenol content, weight loss, and enzyme activities of the 
blueberry samples during the storage were measured (Supporting In­
formation, Text S2). Blueberries wrapped with paper coated with 
coating of chitosan/gelation (1:1, wt%) served as the control group. 
Blueberries wrapped with paper coated with chitosan/gelation/ZnO/ 
PHMG (45:45:5:5, wt%) served as the experimental group. Triplicate 
storage units were used for blueberry application, and each storage unit 
had a 20 g of uniform-sized blueberries.
2.13. Data analysis
All the experiments used a completely randomized design with a 
minimum of three independent replicates (n ≥3). Statistical signifi­
cance (P < 0.05) was determined using one-way analysis of variance 
(ANOVA) for comparing groups defined by a single independent vari­
able. Where ANOVA indicated significance, post-hoc pairwise 
comparisons were conducted using Tukey's Honestly Significant Differ­
ence (HSD) test using software IBM SPSS Statistics R27.0.1.0 (IBM, 
USA). Specific control groups are defined in respective method sections 
and figure captions.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Synthesized ZnO NPs show photothermal properties
First, ZnO NPs were synthesized using a microwave-assisted hydro­
thermal method. As the morphology of ZnO NPs is highly sensitive to 
synthesis temperature(Polsongkram et al., 2008), the effect of reaction 
temperature (60–90ºC) on ZnO NP morphology and photothermal 
properties was investigated. The microwave-assisted hydrothermal 
synthesis method is efficient, cost-effective, and energy-saving 
compared with conventional approaches (Sharma et al., 2011). Micro­
waves enhance the reaction rate and selectivity by promoting chemical 
and physical transformations through molecular heating. Microwaves 
also accelerate collision efficiency via the mutual orientation of polar 
molecules involved in the reaction(Sharma et al., 2011).
The formation of ZnO NPs was proved by FTIR analysis (Fig. 1c). A 
characteristic peak representing ZnO between 400 and 800 cm−1 
(Madhan et al., 2021) was observed. The ZnO NPs exhibited a pre­
dominantly spindle-like morphology, with a length ranging from 115 to 
454 nm and a diameter between 42 and 144 nm (Fig. 1a). ZnO NPs 
synthesized at temperatures (60 and 70◦C) were larger and thicker than 
those synthesized at higher temperatures (80 and 90◦C), consistent with 
previous studies(Polsongkram et al., 2008; Wojnarowicz et al., 2018). 
This suggests that the ZnO crystal grew more along the 〈2 1 1 
0〉direction, rather than the〈0 0 0 1〉lengthwise direction (Fig. 1d) 
(Polsongkram et al., 2008). This behavior is likely due to the 
temperature-dependent reactivity of the precursor (Zn (OH)4
2-). Typi­
cally, lower temperatures lead to fewer nucleation sites and larger 
particles(Wojnarowicz et al., 2018). In contrast, higher temperatures 
promote system polarization, facilitate nucleation, and result in smaller 
particles(Wojnarowicz et al., 2018). Additionally, ZnO NPs synthesized 
at 60◦C formed flower-like morphologies (Fig. 1a). Similar phenomenon 
was previously reported(Wojnarowicz et al., 2018). At lower tempera­
tures, (Zn (OH)4)2−) precursors aggregate and form active sites after 
nucleation. They adsorb to ZnO surfaces due to electrostatic force(Khan 
et al., 2014). This facilitates anisotropic crystal growth along the c-axis, 
towards the〈0 0 0 1〉facet. As a result, ZnO NPs with morphologies of 
slender needle-like and broad arrow-like (flower-like) are formed(Khan 
et al., 2014).
Next, the photothermal performance of the ZnO NPs was evaluated 
(Fig. 1e). The light-to-heat conversion ability of the ZnO NPs highly 
depended on their morphology. Under light irradiation (808 nm NIR, 
power density of 1.0 W/cm2 for 3 min), ZnO NPs synthesized at 80◦C 
(ZnO-80, diameter 42.4 ± 11.2 nm, length 195.9 ± 58.1 nm) exhibited 
the highest temperature increase (~11.1◦C), while those synthesized at 
60, 70, and 90◦C exhibited lower temperature increases of approxi­
mately 9.1, 5.9 and 4.1◦C, respectively. Furthermore, the temperature 
profile of ZnO-80 NPs remained stable after four cycles of laser on/off 
treatment, indicating their good photothermal stability (Fig. 1f).
3.2. Chitosan/gelation coating shows photothermal properties
First, to evaluate the photothermal properties of the coating, ZnO-80 
NPs (5–15 wt%) were incorporated into a chitosan/gelatin (1:1) solu­
tion to develop a CS/G/ZnO coating in its liquid state. The coating was 
subjected to light irradiation (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2 for 5 min) and the 
corresponding temperature profile was recorded (Fig. 2a). All coating 
samples exhibited a temperature increase ranging from 5.3 to 14.2◦C. 
The temperature rise increased with ZnO NP concentration, approaching 
that of ZnO NPs alone as observed in 3.1. This result suggests that the

photothermal property of the ZnO NPs was largely retained within the

When ZnO NP concentration exceeded 5 wt%, the nanoparticles 
aggregated, which significantly compromised the coating's transparency 
and mechanical properties. Similar findings were reported in previous 
studies (Yu et al., 2021). Therefore, a ZnO NP concentration of 5 wt% 
was chosen for coating fabrication (CS/G/ZnO) in subsequent experi­
ments. The CS/G/ZnO coating had good photothermal stability, as 
indicated by its stable temperature profile after four cycles of laser 
on/off treatment (Fig. 2b).
Next, the interaction between ZnO NPs and the chitosan/gelatin 
coating in solid form (film, Fig. 2c) was investigated using DSC (Fig. 2d, 
e). Chitosan and gelatin exhibited an onset glass transition temperature 
(Tgonset) of 38.6 and 40.4◦C, and a mid-point glass transition tempera­
ture (Tgmiddle) of 62.0 and 58.0◦C, respectively. The CS/G composite had 
higher Tgonset and Tgmiddle values than its individual components. The 
addition of ZnO NPs lowered both Tgonset and Tgmiddle of the CS/G 
composite by 25.8% and 29.5%, respectively. This is probably because 
ZnO NPs disrupted polymer structures, facilitated chain movement, and 
reduced polymer crystallinity (Yang et al., 2023). Furthermore, the 
presence of ZnO NPs generated free volume near the particles in the 
matrix, enhancing the chain mobility of the surrounding polymers 
(Doumeng et al., 2022). These findings are consistent with previous 
studies (Carrion et al., 2007). The addition of antibacterial agent PHMG 
slightly increased the glass transition temperature of the CS/G/ZnO 
composite, probably due to intermolecular interactions (e.g., hydrogen 
bonding) between PHMG and the CS/G matrix. This change in the 
thermal behavior of the CS/G matrix would affect the PHMG release as 
discussed later in 3.3.
Furthermore, the photothermal effect of the CS/G/ZnO coating in a 
solid form was evaluated using DMA (Fig. 2f). Overall, the storage 
modulus (E') of the coating samples (with or without light irritation) 
decreased with increasing temperature, with a noticeable step between 
approximately 45–65◦C. This is consistent with the DSC analysis, where 
both chitosan and gelatin exhibited a glass transition between approx­
imately 38–62◦C. Similar DMA results from previous study on chitosan 
films were reported (Khouri et al., 2019). In the temperature range of 
45–65◦C, the slope of ΔE'/ΔT of the light-irradiated coating increased 
significantly, while the slope of the non-irradiated coating remained 
relatively unchanged. As the temperature of the CS/G/ZnO coating 
increased from ~30–40◦C under light irradiation, which falls in the Tg 
ranges of the CS/G/ZnO coating (39.8–42.3◦C), the photothermal 
heating was sufficient to approach or exceed the Tg locally. This suggests 
that the presence of ZnO NPs accelerated the polymer chain movement 
in the chitosan/gelation composite due to localized heat generated 
under light irradiation.
3.3. Light accelerates release of encapsulated antibacterial agent
Based on above analysis, we hypothesized that at room temperature 
(20–23.5◦C in winter and 23–25.5◦C in summer) (Burroughs & Hansen, 
2020), when the CS/G/ZnO coating is exposed to light for a period of 
time, ZnO NPs can induce a local temperature increase (ranging from 5.3 
to 14.2◦C). The generated heat can cause chitosan/gelatin to soften and 
transition to a glassy state (starting at approximately 40◦C). As a result, 
antibacterial agent encapsulated within the coating can be released 
under light exposure.

3.4. Coating shows light-responsive antimicrobial performance
We next evaluated the light-responsive antibacterial properties of the 
coatings. Overall, light irradiation had no significant effect on microbial 
growth in the control group (Fig. 4). This suggests that the light used in 
this experiment had minimal impact on microbial growth. The CS/G 
coating showed no significant antibacterial activity against E. coli or S. 
aureus regardless of light exposure. Similar findings were reported by 
Rodrigues et al., where chitosan films did not exhibit significant bacte­
riostatic activity against S. aureus or E. coli in an agar diffusion test 
(Rodrigues et al., 2020). This may be attributed to the weak positive 
charge of chitosan, which prevents its antibacterial function from 
migrating into the culture medium (Li & Zhuang, 2020; Rodrigues et al., 
2020; Yilmaz Atay, 2019).
Without light irradiation, the addition of ZnO NPs did not signifi­
cantly enhance the antibacterial performance of the coating (CS/G/ZnO 
vs. CS/G). The presence of PHMG without light irradiation slightly 
inhibited the growth of both E. coli and S. aureus (CS/G/PHMG vs. CS/ 
G), suggesting that PHMG did not exhibit strong antibacterial activity in 
this study. This could be due to the low concentration of PHMG used, 
which was chosen to focus on how the photothermal properties of ZnO 
NPs influence PHMG release and the antibacterial performance of the 
coating. If PHMG had been added at a higher concentration, its inherent 
antibacterial activity might have overshadowed the light-enhanced 
antibacterial effect resulting from the accelerated PHMG release due 
to the photothermal effect of ZnO NPs.
With light irradiation, the presence of ZnO NPs inhibited E. coli 
growth (CS/G/ZnO vs. CS/G). As CS/G/ZnO did not inhibit E. coli 
growth without light irradiation, the observed inhibition under light can 
be attributed to the ROS generated by ZnO NPs. Interestingly, CS/G/ZnO 
had a minimal effect on S. aureus growth under light. This could be 
because S. aureus, a Gram-positive bacterium, is more resistant than the 
Gram-negative E. coli (Cebri´an et al., 2017; Fisher & Mobashery, 2020; 
Sondi & Salopek-Sondi, 2004). In contrast to E. coli, the thick peptido­
glycan and anionic teichoic acids in S. aureus create a dense barrier that 
more effectively restricts the diffusion of released PHMG and scavenges 
ROS, contributing to its higher resistance under the light-triggered 
antimicrobial mechanism. Additionally, the relatively large particle 
size of the ZnO NPs used in this study (diameter 42.4 ± 11.2 nm, length 
195.9 ± 58.1 nm) could contribute to their low antibacterial activity 
(Raghupathi et al., 2011). Light irradiation did not significantly affect 
the antibacterial performance of CS/G/PHMG.
In the presence of both ZnO NPs and PHMG, light irradiation 
significantly enhanced the antibacterial activity of CS/G/ZnO/PHMG 
against both E. coli and S. aureus. The light-enhanced antibacterial ac­
tivity of CS/G/ZnO/PHMG was substantially higher than that of CS/G/ 
ZnO alone. This suggests that the accelerated release of PHMG, driven by 
the photothermal effect of ZnO NPs, rather than the antibacterial ac­
tivity (ROS) of ZnO NPs, played a dominant role in inhibiting microbial 
growth. Specifically, the diffusion of PHMG was accelerated under light 
exposure as the chitosan/gelatin matrix softened due to the localized 
heat generated by the ZnO NPs, leading to enhanced antibacterial ac­
tivity. The light-responsive antibacterial activity of CS/G/ZnO/PHMG 
increased as the light exposure time was extended from 5 to 15 min and 
plateaued thereafter. After 20 min of light exposure, the antimicrobial 
rate of CS/G/ZnO/PHMG against E. coli and S. aureus reached 97.3% and 
99.4%, respectively, and remained stable thereafter (Fig. 4d, e).

4. Conclusions
Environmentally responsive antibacterial food packaging is an 
effective way to enhance food quality and safety. In this study, we 
present a new light-responsive antibacterial coating based on a chito­
san/gelation composite, which was fabricated using a cost-effective, 
simple, and industrially viable method. A new strategy was developed 
to trigger and enhance the antibacterial activity of the coating by uti­
lizing the photothermal effect of large-sized ZnO NPs (> 100 nm).
Large-sized ZnO NPs with spindle-like morphology and photo­
thermal properties were synthesized and incorporated into the chitosan/ 
gelatin composite as light-to-heat converters. The composite had a low 
glass transition temperature and could easily soften when exposed to the 
heat generated by the ZnO NPs under light. This resulted in the 
controlled release of the encapsulated antibacterial agent, PHMG, from 
the coating matrix.
The light-responsive coating demonstrated significant effectiveness 
in preventing microbial growth under light exposure. Furthermore, the 
coating notably extended the shelf life of blueberries stored under light, 
high temperature, and high humidity conditions, highlighting its po­
tential for practical food packaging applications.
The primary strength of this work lies in its innovative, safety- 
focused strategy: In contrast to prior work utilizing small ZnO NPs 
(<100 nm) primarily for their intrinsic ROS-mediated antibacterial ac­
tivity which raises safety concerns, our approach strategically employs 
larger, photothermally-active ZnO NPs (>100 nm) as triggers. The 
constructed light-responsive antibacterial platform addresses the 
toxicity concerns associated with small, ROS-generating nanoparticles. 
Furthermore, the design synergy between the photothermal agent and a 
thermally responsive biopolymer matrix enables on-demand release of 
antimicrobials. This shifts the antibacterial action from a continuous, 
potentially hazardous mode to a controlled, light-responsive release of 
encapsulated agents, offering a green and intelligent solution for 
extending food shelf life.
A primary limitation of this study is that it does not quantify the 
potential generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by the ZnO 
nanoparticles under light, measure the specific migration levels of 
PHMG into food simulants, or assess the sensory impact on packaged 
blueberries. These aspects were beyond the scope of this proof-of- 
concept work, which focused on establishing the novel light-triggered 
release mechanism. Future research could evaluate ROS production, 
conduct comprehensive migration tests, and perform sensory analyses to 
fully address safety and quality considerations, ensuring the technol­
ogy's viability for real-world food packaging applications.

Cerium oxide nanozymes (CeO2NZs) are attracting vast attention due to their antioxidant and catalytic
properties and mimic the activities of multiple endogenous enzymes. However, as is the case for nano-
medicines in general, the success in showing their unique medical applications has not been matched by
an understanding of their pharmacokinetics, which is delaying their implementation in clinical settings.
Furthermore, the data of their modiﬁcations in body ﬂuids and the impact on their activity are scarce.
Herein, two types of widely used CeO2NZs, electrostatically stabilized and coated with a mesoporous
silica shell, were exposed to simulated saliva and lung, gastric and intestinal ﬂuids, and cell culture media.
Their physicochemical modiﬁcations and bioactivity were tracked over time up to 15 days combining the
data of diﬀerent characterization techniques and biological assays. The results show that the biocompat-
ibility and antioxidant activity are retained in all cases despite the diﬀerent evolution behaviors in diﬀerent
ﬂuids, including agglomeration. This work provides an experimental basis from a pharmacokinetic per-
spective that supports the therapeutic eﬀectiveness of CeO2NZs observed in vivo for the treatment of
many conditions related to chronic inﬂammation and cancer, and suggests that they can be safely admi-
nistered through diﬀerent portals of entry including intravenous injection, oral ingestion or inhalation.
Introduction
The potential of nanomaterials with enzyme-mimetic activities
is opening a new area of research in nanomedicine.1–3
Research on these materials, known as nanozymes (NZs), and
their application in diﬀerent areas of medicine is continuously
growing.4–6 Amongst them, cerium oxide NZs (CeO2NZs) are
some of the most widely studied due to their potential of
mimicking the activity of several key endogenous enzymes
such as superoxide dismutase (SOD),7 catalase,8 and peroxi-
dase.9 CeO2NZs have been reported to have protective and
therapeutic eﬀects in diﬀerent medical conditions such as
hepatic diseases,10 neurodegeneration,11 retinal degeneration,12,13
cardiopathies,14 ischemia,15,16 and diﬀerent tumours.17–19 Their
enzyme-mimetic behaviour is attributed to their ability to par-
ticipate in redox reactions through a well-described regenerative
cycle between the Ce-oxidized (Ce4+) and Ce-reduced (Ce3+)
states due to the presence of oxygen vacancies in the crystal
lattice when the size of the particle is reduced to the nanometer
scale.20–22
Despite the high potential of CeO2NZs, and NZs in general
for medicine, a common aspect that delays their eﬀective
implementation for patient benefit is concerns regarding their
toxicity and safety, and the lack of translatability consider-
ations in many research papers.23 In the case of CeO2NZs,
along with the reports praising their therapeutic potential,24
there are others pointing out their potential toxicity.25 We have
recently reviewed the diﬀerent reasons for these apparently

materials are dispersed in biological media are well described
in the literature in terms of agglomeration of the particles,
their dissolution, and the adsorption of proteins onto their
surfaces. It has been often described that the agglomeration of

also displaying toxic eﬀects.26,27 In vitro, these studies are
mostly performed for nanomaterials incubated in cell culture
media (CCM) or serum, while the data of these modifications

The case of CeO2NZs is not an exception and data on their
evolution in body fluids are practically nonexistent. One of
the few works is the study of Yokel et al.29 which investigated
the

of
CeO2NZs

statically stabilized with citric acid after two, three and six

fluids. These authors observed the formation of large agglom-
erates which did not significantly compromise cell viability in
A549 and Caco-2 cells. Other works showed the physico-
chemical interactions of CeO2NZs with specific molecules
such as hemoglobin and insulin, among others.30 The
agglomeration of NZs in CCM is the phenomenon more
widely investigated. For instance, Casals et al. characterized
the formation and time evolution of the protein corona (a
layer of adsorbed proteins on the NZ surface) in diﬀerent
electrostatically stabilized metal oxide nanoparticles, includ-
ing 5–10 nm CeO2NPs.31 In that work, the initial formation of
small agglomerates (ca. 30 nm) and of a soft protein corona
(with loosely attached proteins) is followed by a hardening of
this protein corona during the following 48 h, when the
protein corona is definitive. Ju et al. also characterized the
evolution of the protein corona in polymer-coated CeO2NZs,
and showed that they preserved 90% of their SOD and cata-
lase-mimetic activities in CCM.32
From the aforementioned papers and other works, it is
important to note the diﬀerences in the nanomaterials
studied in terms of morphology and surface structure, and
that diﬀerent physiological fluids have varying compositions
in terms of pH, ionic strength and macromolecular compo-
sition, and other factors. This can lead to diﬀerent evolution
behaviors of diﬀerent CeO2NZs in diﬀerent fluids which may
aﬀect the intended therapeutic action.33 pH is one of the
factors that may influence CeO2NZ activity. They exhibit
more catalase-like activity under alkaline conditions while
oxidase and peroxidase-like behaviours are more prevalent
in acidic environments.34,35 Another factor is the high ionic
strength of physiological fluids, which not only destabilizes
electrostatically stabilized CeO2NZs but can also introduce
the so-called “poisoning eﬀect” of CeO2NZ catalytic sites.36
For instance, McCormack et al. showed that the increased
concentration
of
phosphate
salts
in
aqueous
solutions
decreases the catalytic activity of CeO2NZs due to the inhi-
bition of oxygen capture and release on their surfaces.37
Surface modifications during the CeO2NZ synthesis pro-
cedure have been proposed to reduce their dissolution or
aggregation potential, such as coating them with polymers
(e.g., dextran, poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), PEG) or liposomes.
Another strategy proposed is the development of core–shell
structures. For instance, we have recently shown that nano-
composites based on CeO2NZ cores embedded in a meso-
porous silica shell (mSiO2) exhibited improved colloidal
stability of CeO2NZs and increased multi-enzymatic and
anti-inflammatory
activities
in
metabolic
comorbidities
associated with obesity.38

Results and discussion
Nanozyme synthesis and simulated body fluid preparation
The CeO2NZs used in this work were synthesized by chemical
precipitation at basic pH of cerium nitrate hexahydrate (Ce
(NO3)3·6H2O; 10 mM) with ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH;
27 mM) as previously reported.39,40 The CeO2NZs@mSiO2
nanocomposites were prepared using a one-pot synthesis
method in aqueous media and at room temperature based
on the adjustment of diﬀerent parameters of the MCM-41
process for silica shell formation, which allows the obtention
of colloidally stable and monodisperse nanocomposites from
50 to 200 nm with the CeO2NZs homogeneously distributed
in the mSiO2 shells.38,40 As discussed by Zeng et al.,40 this is

The chemical and oxidation states of both NZs, electro-
statically stabilized CeO2NZs and those embedded in the
mSiO2 shell, were studied by the analysis of the Ce 3d core
level region of the XPS spectra, which is particularly complex
because of its associated peak structure. The Ce 3d peak con-
sists of a satellite peak at 916.51 eV which is a fingerprint of
Ce4+ compounds and two groups of peaks corresponding to
the 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 spin orbitals.43 One group of two peaks at
901 eV corresponds to the 3d3/2 spin orbital and another group
of two peaks at 882 eV corresponds to the 3de spin orbital.43,44
These peaks are asymmetric, which is attributed to the
mixture of Ce3+ and Ce4+ in the CeO2 structure. Fig. 1G and H
show the analysis of the XPS spectra. The peaks in blue are
related to the presence the Ce3+ oxidation state and described
as v0, v′, v″, and v′′′ and the peaks in yellow are related to Ce4+
and described as u0, u′, u″, u′′′, u′′′′, and u″′′′. The relative
content of Ce3+ and Ce4+ in the case of CeO2NZs was deter-
mined as 31.3% and 68.7%, respectively (Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio of
0.46), and similar values were obtained in the case of
CeO2NZs@mSiO2 (30.7% of Ce3+ and 69.3% of Ce4+; Ce3+/Ce4+
ratio of 0.44).
Both CeO2NZs and CeO2NZs@mSiO2 were exposed to four
diﬀerent simulated body fluids including saliva, gastric fluid,
intestinal fluid (fasted state) and lung fluid (Gamble’s solu-
tion, which represents the interstitial fluid deep within the
lungs), and to CCM, up to 15 days. In preclinical settings,
simulated body fluids are frequently used to simulate the phys-
iological conditions, providing a controlled and standardized
environment for testing. Table 1 lists the composition and
main characteristics of these fluids, including protein and
saline contents, as well as their respective pH values. These
diﬀerent biological media were chosen as they are representa-
tive of the diﬀerent portals of entry of nanomaterials. They
were prepared following pharmacological standards employed
for the study of drug administration and pharmacokinetics
and oﬀer advantages in preclinical studies because they are tai-
lored to mimic certain aspects of real body fluids, such as the
ionic composition or pH, while maintaining consistent and
reproducible conditions.45,46 It is worth noting that while the
use of simulated body fluids is a common practice in pre-
clinical settings since they oﬀer controlled and reproducible
conditions to serve as a basis for the transition to real body
fluids in clinical studies, the use of real body fluids will be an
essential aspect for validating the safety and eﬃcacy of the
herein studied NZs for the ultimate goal of their clinical trans-
lation for the benefit of patients.

The increase of DH once the nanomaterials are dispersed in
biological fluids is commonly explained due to the bio-
molecular corona formation and/or the agglomeration of the
particles.47–49 The formation of a biomolecular corona and its
hardening has been discussed elsewhere.50,51 To summarise
briefly, once nanomaterials enter a biological fluid a soft bio-
corona is formed, consisting of loosely attached macro-
molecules present in the media which are adsorbed onto the
nanomaterials’ surface (mainly proteins if dispersed in serum
and known as a protein corona). This soft biomolecular
corona, through a series of spatial rearrangements, becomes
stable (hard) over time. However, agglomeration takes place at
shorter times compared to the biomolecular corona for-
mation.52 Thus, while in the case of CeO2NZs@mSiO2 the
increase of DH can be attributed exclusively to the bio-
molecular corona formation on the mSiO2 surface; non-coated
CeO2NZs are first agglomerated in the simulated biological

exposure in the media (2 h, and 1, 2, 4, 7, 10 and 15 days) are
shown in Table S1.† No other appreciable eﬀects on the zeta
potential were observed in our experiments.
Regarding dissolution, this is a phenomenon observed at a
higher extent when the size of the particles decreases, due to
the increase in surface energy. In our experiments, the leach-
ing of Ce ions from the CeO2NZs and CeO2NZs@mSiO2 com-
posites in the diﬀerent fluids was measured by inductively
coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). In all cases, it
has been observed less than 5% of Ce with respect to the
initial Ce mass concentration in the CeO2NZs, with the excep-
tion of CeO2NZs in the simulated intestinal fluids (up to 8%).
For the CeO2NZs, the presence of Ce ions in the supernatants
after removing the particles by centrifugation is observed from
the 2-day timepoint and slightly increased in the 15 day time-
point, while for CeO2NZs@mSiO2 no Ce was detected in the
2-day timepoint and less than 1% was detected at the 15 day
timepoint for all the simulated media (Fig. S3†). The minimal
presence or absence of Ce ions leaching in our experiments
can be explained by the fast agglomeration of CeO2NZs after
exposure to the biological media. The agglomeration of the
particles decreases the surface-to-volume ratio of the particles
and hence prevents them from dissolution. This change can
also be attributed to the protective eﬀects of the silica shell in
the case of CeO2NZs@mSiO2, as shown previously.40 In the
case of CeO2NZs dispersed in the intestinal fluid an 8% dis-
solution is observed in the 15 day timepoint, which is some-
what consistent with the fact that agglomeration occurs to a
lesser extent in this medium compared with others as shown
by the DLS measurements. However, it is diﬃcult to conclude

These physicochemical modifications in biological media are

human

Impact of the physicochemical evolution in simulated body
fluids and CCM on the activity of CeO2NZs and
CeO2NZs@mSiO2 in cellular oxidative stress mitigation
The next step was to evaluate the preservation of ROS-scaven-
ging activity of CeO2NZs exposed to the biological fluids. It has
been reported that the smaller the size of CeO2 nanoparticles,
the higher the catalytic activity, hence the selection of 4 nm
CeO2NZs either electrostatically stabilized or embedded in the
mSiO2 shell.21,22 This activity is due to their low reduction
potential and the coexistence of both Ce3+ and Ce4+ ions on
their surface, acting as electron donors or acceptors,62 in a
process that has been described as “redox buﬀer” which
assists in the maintenance of the redox balance by scavenging
free radicals only when they are in excess.7,63 This is explained
due to the particular electronic structure of CeO2NPs. The elec-
tronic configuration of the Ce3+ state is [Xe]4f 15d06s0, and that
of Ce4+ is [Xe]4f 05d06s0 (and that of metallic Ce is [Xe]
4f 15d16s2). This indicates that the 4f electron is the labile one,
i.e., available for chemical reactions. The distinct feature of
rare earth elements, like Ce, is the presence of such 4f orbitals
shielded by 4d and 5p orbitals. Due to this shielding, the 4f
electrons are weakly bound to the nucleus. This characteristic
allows for the possibility of the presence of Ce3+/Ce4+, where
CeO2NZs can switch between these two oxidation states,
enabling the formation and occupation of oxygen vacancies,
which are essential for CeO2NZs oxygen buﬀering capabili-
ties.63 These capabilities make CeO2NZs an eﬀective catalyst
for both oxidation and reduction reactions (such as peroxi-
dase-like and SOD-like reactions).
For these experiments, CeO2NZs and CeO2NZs@mSiO2
were also exposed up to 7 days to the diﬀerent simulated body
fluids and CCM. Afterwards, they were recovered and purified
from the media and incubated with HepG2 cells treated with
H2O2 (400 µM) for 24 hours. Flow cytometric analysis of ROS
generation using a DCFH-DA fluoroprobe was performed.
According to what has been discussed previously regarding
cell viability tests, both CeO2NZs and CeO2NZs@mSiO2 were
used with 25 and 50 µg of CeO2 per mL. As the size of the
CeO2NZs is the same in both cases, it allows for a direct com-
parison between them.
As shown in Fig. 5 (flow cytometric analysis) and Fig. 6
(immunofluorescence staining observed by microscopy), H2O2-
treated cells showed a 39.6% increase in ROS generation with
respect to the untreated cells, and the cells further incubated
24 hours with CeO2NZs and CeO2NZs@mSiO2 showed only a
18.7% and 8.35% increased ROS generation, respectively.
Therefore, CeO2NZs@mSiO2 showed a higher decrease in the
ROS concentration (76.4% decrease with respect to the H2O2-
treated cells) than CeO2NZs (52.8%). Table 2 shows the levels
of ROS reduction with respect to the H2O2-treated cells. This is
consistent with a previous published work where it was shown
that the mSiO2 coating enhances the ROS-scavenging activity
of CeO2NZs probably due to their enhanced stability in CCM.40
Remarkably, our results showed a similar ROS-scavenging
eﬀect in the case of both CeO2NZs and CeO2NZs@mSiO2 that
were exposed for 7 days to the simulated body fluids and CCM,

to the H2O2-treated cells. This is important since, similarly to
what was observed for cell viability, the agglomeration or bio-
molecular corona formation is not detrimental to the biologi-
cal activity, probably because it does not modify the crystalline

Experimental
Materials and reagents
Unless otherwise stated, all nanomaterials employed in this
work were synthesized in the aqueous phase, using Milli-Q
grade water. All reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich
and used as received. For the synthesis of the NZs and peroxi-
dase assay determination, cerium(III) nitrate hexahydrate,
ammonium hydroxide, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide, tet-
raethyl orthosilicate, ammonium nitrate and 3,3,5,5-tetra-
methylbenzidine were employed; for the preparation of the
body fluids, all the reagents employed are listed in Table 1; for
cell in vitro assays, HepG2 cells were obtained from American
Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and
Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium, foetal bovine serum,
streptomycin, and penicillin were purchased from Sigma-
Aldrich.
Synthesis of CeO2NZs and CeO2NZs@mSiO2
Both nanomaterials were synthesized in an aqueous phase,
using Milli-Q grade water. All reagents were purchased from
Sigma-Aldrich and used as received. All the synthesis pro-
cedures are based on pre-existed ones available in the scienti-
fic literature with a few modifications to be adapted for further
mesoporous silica (mSiO2) coating.39,40 CeO2NZs of 4 nm were
synthesized by the chemical precipitation of cerium(III) nitrate
hexahydrate (Ce(NO3)3·6H2O) in a basic aqueous solution.
10 mM of Ce(NO3)3·6H2O was dissolved in 100 ml of Milli-Q
water at room temperature. Afterward, 3 ml of 1 M NH4OH was
added slowly at room temperature under vigorous stirring and
the mixture was allowed to continue under mild stirring over-
night.
Synthesis
of
CeO2NZs@mSiO2
was
performed
to
obtained CeO2NZs. 7 ml of CeO2NZs were mixed with 3 mL of
a solution consisting of 10 mg ml−1 cetyltrimethylammonium
bromide (CTAB) in EtOH : H2O (2 : 1 v/v ratio). CTAB solution
was added dropwise under sonication to obtain an appropriate
NZ core dispersion during the CTAB overcoating process. This
was maintained under stirring for 30 minutes, and 60 µL of
tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) was added dropwise. Afterward,
the solution was kept under stirring overnight to allow the for-
mation of a mesoporous SiO2 shell. Finally, the obtained core–
shell nanostructures were centrifuged and resuspended three
times with a solution of NH4NO3 in ethanol (20 g L−1) for the
removal of CTAB. The particles were washed and kept in EtOH
for further use.

Exposure to the media
Simulated biological fluids including cell culture media,
saliva, gastric fluid, intestinal fluid and lung fluid were pre-
pared using the formulations described by Marques et al.,46
which are widely used in pharmacology to test the biocompat-
ibility of drugs. The characteristics of these fluids are listed in
Table 1 of the Results and discussion section. Prior to the
exposure,
the
concentration
of
both
nanomaterials
was
adjusted at 1 mg ml−1 CeO2NZs (2 × 1015 NZs per ml).
Afterwards, the nanomaterials were dispersed in the diﬀerent
simulated biological fluids (1 : 10 by volume) and kept in an
incubator at 37 °C. At diﬀerent time points, an aliquot was
extracted for further characterization.
Purification
To avoid the interference from the biological media during the
characterization of the nanomaterials, centrifugation was
carried out for 15 min at 20 000g. The obtained pellets were
redispersed in a solution of 1 mM TMAOH, obtaining stable
colloids. Centrifugation is an eﬃcient and reliable way to seg-
regate particle-associated proteins from non-associated pro-
teins when performed with care under previously described
specific conditions.31,51,65
Characterization techniques
To characterize the NZs and to track the time evolution of
their physical and chemical changes, a combination of
diﬀerent instrumental techniques was applied, including the
following:
UV-Visible spectrophotometry
UV-Visible spectra were recorded with a Shimadzu UV-1900
(Japan) spectrophotometer. 1 ml of nanomaterial suspension
was added to a quartz cuvette and spectra were obtained with
scanning in the wavelength range from 190 and 700 nm.
Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential
A Nanotrac wave II (Macchique, USA) instrument operating at
a light source wavelength of 532 nm and a fixed scattering
angle of 173° was used to measure the NZ hydrodynamic size
and zeta potential value. Measurements were conducted in a
1 cm path cell and three independent measurements were
performed.
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
Nanomaterials were visualized using a high-resolution trans-
mission electron microscope (FEI Talos F200s). 40 µl of the
colloidal solutions were drop-casted onto a carbon-coated
200 mesh copper grid and left to dry at room temperature. Size
distribution was measured using ImageJ analysis software.
Inductively coupled plasma-mass spectroscopy (ICP-MS)
The cerium concentration in the supernatants after purifi-
cation of the exposed NZs in the simulated body fluids and
CCM was measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spec-
trometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7500; Agilent Technologies, California,
USA). Quantification was performed by interpolation in a stan-
dard curve obtained from a commercial 1000 ppm cerium stan-
dard (Sigma-Aldrich).
TMB oxidation test
The enzyme-mimetic activity (horseradish peroxidase (HRP)
and oxidase) of CeO2NZs was studied by catalysing the oxi-
dation of 3,3,5,5-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) in the presence
of H2O2. Similar to HRP and oxidase, CeO2NZs can catalyse
the oxidation of TMB in the presence of H2O2 to produce a
blue-coloured
product
with
a
maximum
absorbance
at

absorbance of a solution consisting of 0.1 mM TMB, 1 mM
H2O2 and CeO2NZs or CeO2NZs@mSiO2. Both nanomaterials
were previously incubated in the diﬀerent simulated biological
fluids and CCM for 7 days and recovered from them by purifi-
cation. The concentration of CeO2 was selected according to
cell viability tests and flow cytometry analysis, i.e., 50 µg of

variation of the reaction solution at 652 nm. Control experi-

Cell culture
HepG2 cells were obtained from American Type Culture
Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). This immortalized
human hepatocyte stable cell line can be repeatedly frozen,
thawed and propagated. HepG2 cells were seeded in 96-well
culture plates (5 × 105 cells per well) in Dulbecco’s Modified
Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), supplemented with 10% foetal
bovine serum (FBS), 50 U ml−1 penicillin and 50 μg ml−1 strep-
tomycin, under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C.
Afterward, cells were incubated for 24 hours with the as-syn-
thesized CeO2NZs or CeO2NZs@SiO2 and recovered after 7
days in the simulated body fluids and CCM. Varying doses of
NZs, from 6.25 to 100 μg of CeO2 per ml, were used for cell via-
bility and ROS-scavenging experiments.
Cell viability test
Cell viability was assessed using the MTS technique (CellTiter
96; Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufac-
turer’s instructions. After being treated with the NZs, the cells
were washed twice with Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS)
and CellTiter reagent was added to each well. After incubation
for 4 h at 37 °C to allow the cells to bioreduce MTS, the absor-
bance of formazan was measured at 490 nm (Shimadzu
UV-1900, Japan). The quantity of formazan is directly pro-
portional to the number of living cells in culture. Three inde-
pendent experiments were performed for each NZ type.

Conclusions
Despite the enormous potential of nanomedicine and the
success in developing new materials for unique medical appli-
cations, data on the evolution of such new materials in
diﬀerent body fluids and how this aﬀects their bioactivity are
often missing. To contribute to fill this knowledge gap, the
stability, morphology, and bioactivity of two types of CeO2NZs
(electrostatically
stabilized
and
the
core–shell
structure
CeO2NZs@mSiO2)
were
investigated,
exposing
them
to
diﬀerent simulated body fluids (saliva and gastric, intestinal
and lung fluids) and CCM for up to 15 days. The results of this
study showed that agglomeration of the NZs is the dominating
event in the case of electrostatically stabilized CeO2NZs upon
exposure to the simulated fluids, while colloidal stability and
biomolecular
corona
formation
are
observed
for
CeO2NZs@mSiO2. For the latter, the results shown in this
study can also serve as a guidance to study the evolution of
nanozymes with similar physicochemical characteristics such
as Fe3O4, MnO2, or CuO, among others, which also lack such
data. Remarkably, in all cases, both NZs incubated up to 7
days in the simulated body fluids and CCM did not present
significant toxicity or a decrease in their ROS-scavenging
capacity. These findings indicate that while a certain level of
agglomeration of CeO2NZs may occur in body fluids, it does
not aﬀect their biocompatibility and original catalytic activity.
This provides an experimental basis to explain from the phar-
macokinetic considerations the high therapeutic eﬀectiveness
of CeO2NZs observed in vivo in many conditions related to
chronic inflammation and cancer.

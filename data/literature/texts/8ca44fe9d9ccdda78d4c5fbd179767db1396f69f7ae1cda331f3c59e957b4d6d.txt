D R U G  D E L I V E R Y
Engineered exosomes with a photoinducible protein 
delivery system enable CRISPR-­Cas–based epigenome 
editing in Alzheimer’s disease
Jihoon Han1†, Jae Hoon Sul1, Jeongmi Lee1‡, Eunae Kim1, Hark Kyun Kim1§, Minshik Chae1,  
Jeein Lim1, Jongho Kim1, Chanhee Kim1, Jun-­Sik Kim1, Yoonsuk Cho1, Jae Hyung Park2,3,4,5,  
Yong Woo Cho4,6, Dong-­Gyu Jo1,3,4,5,7*
Effective intracellular delivery of therapeutic proteins can potentially treat a wide array of diseases. However, ef-
ficient delivery of functional proteins across the cell membrane remains challenging. Exosomes are nanosized 
vesicles naturally secreted by various types of cells and may serve as promising nanocarriers for therapeutic bio-
molecules. Here, we engineered exosomes equipped with a photoinducible cargo protein release system, termed 
mMaple3-­mediated protein loading into and release from exosome (MAPLEX), in which cargo proteins can be 
loaded into the exosomes by fusing them with photocleavable protein (mMaple3)–conjugated exosomal mem-
brane markers and subsequently released from the exosomal membrane by inducing photocleavage with blue 
light illumination. Using this system, we first induced transcriptional regulation by delivering octamer-­binding 
transcription factor 4 and SRY-­box transcription factor 2 to fibroblasts in vitro. Second, we induced in vivo gene 
recombination in Cre reporter mice by delivering Cre recombinase. Last, we achieved targeted epigenome editing 
in the brains of 5xFAD and 3xTg-­AD mice, two models of Alzheimer’s disease. Administration of MAPLEXs loaded 
with β-­site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1 (Bace1)–targeting single guide RNA–incorporated dCas9 
ribonucleoprotein complexes, coupled with the catalytic domain of DNA methyltransferase 3A, resulted in suc-
cessful methylation of the targeted CpG sites within the Bace1 promoter. This approach led to a significant reduc-
tion in Bace1 expression, improved recognition memory impairment, and reduced amyloid pathology in 5xFAD 
and 3xTg-­AD mice. These results suggest that MAPLEX is an efficient intracellular protein delivery system that can 
deliver diverse therapeutic proteins for multiple diseases.
INTRODUCTION
Despite the discovery of many potentially therapeutic proteins, in-
cluding genome editing enzymes, epigenetic modifying enzymes, 
and transcription factors, most clinically applicable protein thera-
peutics remain confined to monoclonal antibodies, peptide hor-
mones, and cytokines, which primarily function by binding to their 
respective receptors on the plasma membrane to either trigger or 
block downstream signaling cascades inside the cell (1–3). Many 
diseases are associated with specific protein deficiencies that could 
be treated with protein replacement; however, the inability to deliver 
proteins across the cell membrane limits the development of protein-­
based therapeutics.
Numerous approaches have been developed to internalize pro-
teins into cells. For instance, cell-­penetrating peptides (CPPs), short 
peptides consisting of 5 to 30 amino acids, have been used to trans-
locate recombinant proteins across cell membranes (4–7). However, 
exposure of CPP-­conjugated recombinant proteins to biological flu-
ids can result in rapid degradation or stimulation of the immune 
response (8, 9). Several lipid nanoparticle–based protein delivery 
systems have also been reported (10, 11). Unlike protein transduc-
tion, lipid nanoparticles can encapsulate and protect cargo proteins 
from degradation in biological fluids. Nevertheless, challenges such 
as rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, low cellular up-
take, and poor cargo protein separation from lipids remain limita-
tions for efficient intracellular protein delivery (12–14).
To overcome these challenges, we used exosomes, which are 
nanosized (30 to 150 nm) membrane vesicles that are naturally se-
creted by cells, as nanocarriers for protein delivery. Compared with 
protein transduction and lipid nanoparticles, exosomes have advan-
tages in stability, biodegradability, biocompatibility, and immuno-
compatibility (15, 16). Loading proteins into exosomes can be 
achieved by fusing cargo proteins with exosomal markers (17, 18). 
However, separation from exosomal markers must be completed for 
cargo proteins to enter recipient cells and function properly. The 
bond between the cargo protein and exosomal marker is therefore a 
double-­edged sword because it is necessary for protein loading yet 
unnecessary for protein delivery. Thus, we developed engineered 
exosomes equipped with a photoinducible cargo protein release sys-
tem, mMaple3-­mediated protein loading into and release from exo-
some (MAPLEX). We fused the photocleavable protein (PhoCl) 
mMaple3, a Clavularia-­derived green-­to-­red photoconvertible fluo-
rescent protein variant (19, 20), between the cargo protein and CD9, 
a tetraspanin exosomal marker, so that cargo proteins can be loaded 
into exosomes. After the loaded exosomes are purified, cargo 
proteins can be separated from CD9 by 405-­nm light-­induced

RESULTS
mMaple3 can be combined with an exosomal marker and a 
cargo protein to construct a photoinducible cargo protein 
release system
To produce cargo protein-­loaded exosomes equipped with a photo-
inducible cargo protein release system (Fig. 1A), mMaple3 was first 
conjugated to the N terminus of CD9. For in vitro imaging, blue 
fluorescent protein (TagBFP) was used as a cargo protein and was 
conjugated to the N terminus of mMaple3 (fig. S1). Because CD9 is 
a member of the tetraspanin family, TagBFP-­mMaple3-­CD9 fusion 
proteins were localized to the plasma membrane when overex-
pressed in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells (Fig. 1B). 
Upon 405-­nm light illumination, mMaple3 was cleaved, as indicat-
ed by the reduction in green fluorescence and increase in red fluo-
rescence (Fig. 1, B and C). Efficient cleavage of mMaple3 was further 
confirmed by immunoblotting, where full-­length (FL) fusion pro-
teins disappeared and TagBFP-­containing N-­terminal fragments 
(NTFs) and C-­terminal fragments (CTFs) accumulated during 405-­nm 
light illumination (Fig. 1D). The highest photocleavage efficiency 
was observed after 10 min of continuous 405-­nm light illumination 
at 40 mW/cm2 (fig. S2).
Cargo proteins can be released from the exosomal 
membrane into the intraexosomal space via 405-­nm 
light illumination
To produce TagBFP:MAPLEXs, HEK293T cells were transfected 
with the TagBFP-­mMaple3-­CD9 construct, and TagBFP:MAPLEXs 
were isolated from the conditioned medium (CM) using the tan-
gential flow filtration (TFF) method (Fig. 2A). The concentration 
and size distribution of TagBFP:MAPLEXs were measured using 
nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), revealing about 4 × 1010 par-
ticles/ml with a mode diameter of 132 nm (Fig. 2B). This size distri-
bution was further confirmed by dynamic light scattering (DLS) 
analysis (Fig. 2C). The zeta potential of TagBFP:MAPLEXs was de-
termined to be −19.3 mV via electrophoretic light scattering (ELS) 
(Fig. 2D). In addition, the morphology and size of TagBFP:MAPLEXs 
were verified via cryo–electron microscopy (cryo-­EM) analysis (Fig. 2E). 
Next, the photoinducible protein release system of the MAPLEXs 
was assessed (Fig. 2F). Immunoblotting and fluorescence intensity 
measurements confirmed that FL TagBFP-­mMaple3-­CD9 fusion 
proteins were safely loaded into MAPLEXs, and mMaple3 was effi-
ciently cleaved by 405-­nm light illumination, leading to the release 
of TagBFP from CD9 (Fig. 2, G to I). Exosomal markers such as 
apoptosis-­linked gene 2–interacting protein X (ALIX), heat shock 
protein 90 (HSP90), and CD63 were detected in TagBFP:MAPLEXs, 
whereas calnexin, an endoplasmic reticulum marker, was not de-
tected (Fig. 2G and fig. S3A). The presence of CD81, another exo-
somal marker, was also confirmed by flow cytometry (fig.  S3B). 
Because the highest photocleavage efficiency (90%) was observed 
after 5 min of continuous 405-­nm light illumination at 40 mW/cm2, 
it was determined to be the optimal condition for cargo protein 
release (Fig. 2H). Last, we investigated the exosomal location of 
TagBFP using a protease digestion assay. Non-­illuminated (OFF) or 
405-­nm light-­illuminated (ON) TagBFP:MAPLEXs were treated 
with proteinase K in the presence or absence of Triton X-­100 
(Fig. 2J). TagBFP and ALIX, an intraluminal exosomal marker, were 
protected from proteinase K in the absence of Triton X-­100, whereas 
lysosome-­associated membrane protein 2b (LAMP2B), an exosom-
al marker with an extracellular domain, was degraded by proteinase 
K (Fig. 2J), suggesting that the cargo protein was located within the 
exosome and therefore protected from external proteases. Protec-
tion of TagBFP and ALIX from proteinase K was also observed in 
TagBFP:MAPLEXs ON, suggesting that the lipid bilayer integrity 
of MAPLEXs was not altered by 405-­nm light illumination. The 
405-­nm light illumination also did not affect the size or zeta poten-
tial of MAPLEXs (fig. S4, A and B).
MAPLEXs are internalized by recipient cells
Next, we examined whether MAPLEXs could be safely taken up by 
recipient cells. Fluorescence-­activated cell sorting analysis and fluo-
rescence intensity measurements confirmed the dose-­dependent 
uptake of TagBFP:MAPLEXs by HEK293T cells (fig. S5, A and B). 
To further examine the uptake of TagBFP:MAPLEXs by recipient cells, 
HEK293T cells were incubated with HEK293T-­derived exosomes 
(293T Exo), TagBFP:MAPLEXs OFF, or TagBFP:MAPLEXs ON, and 
fluorescence was assessed using confocal microscopy (Fig. 3A, left). 
Cells incubated with TagBFP:MAPLEXs OFF showed blue and 
green fluorescence, whereas those incubated with TagBFP:MAPLEXs 
ON showed blue and red fluorescence (Fig. 3A, right). The same 
result was obtained using HeLa cells as recipient cells (fig. S6). Fluores-
cence intensities were confirmed using a microplate reader (Fig. 3B). 
FL TagBFP-­mMaple3-­CD9 fusion proteins were detected in HEK293T 
cells incubated with TagBFP:MAPLEXs OFF, whereas NTFs and 
CTFs of cleaved TagBFP-­mMaple3-­CD9 were detected in cells incu-
bated with TagBFP:MAPLEXs ON (Fig. 3C and fig. S7). These re-
sults suggest that both ON and OFF MAPLEXs can be successfully 
taken up by recipient cells.
Releasing cargo proteins from the exosomal membrane 
enhances their function after uptake by recipient cells

markers by cleaving mMaple3 with 405-­nm light could enhance the 
function of cargo proteins upon uptake by recipient cells. We hy-
pothesized that transcription factors, which localize to the nucleus 
and regulate the expression of specific genes, would be suitable car-
go proteins to assess the intracellular protein delivery efficiency 
of MAPLEX (Fig. 4A). Therefore, MAPLEXs were loaded with

essential for regulating pluripotency. The sizes of OCT4:MAPLEXs 
and SOX2:MAPLEXs were characterized by NTA, and 405-­nm light-­
induced cargo protein release was confirmed for OCT4:MAPLEXs 
(Fig. 4, B to E) and SOX2:MAPLEXs (Fig. 4, F to I) using immunob-
lotting and fluorescence intensity measurements. Then, we treated

target genes. Treatment with OCT4:MAPLEXs ON substantially

MAPLEX can induce in vivo gene recombination

in  vivo. We used genetically engineered Cre reporter mice (Ai9) 
containing a LoxP-­STOP-­LoxP (LSL) cassette upstream of the tdTo-
mato reporter gene. The LSL cassette prevents the transcription of 
the CAG promoter–driven tdTomato; however, once the LSL cassette 
is deleted by Cre-­mediated recombination, tdTomato expression is 
turned on, allowing the detection of recombined cells (Fig. 5A). We 
produced Cre:MAPLEXs with diameters in the range of 100 to

cleavage (fig. S10, C and D). After daily intranasal administration 
for 14 days, induction of tdTomato fluorescence was confirmed in 
the brains of Ai9 mice treated with Cre:MAPLEXs ON (fig. S11, A 
and B). To ascertain cell type specificity, brain sections from the 
cortex were counterstained with either neuronal marker (neuronal 
nuclei) and astrocyte marker (glial fibrillary acidic protein) or mi-
croglia marker (allograft inflammatory factor 1), and the percentage 
of tdTomato-­positive cells was quantified in each cell type. Our find-
ings revealed that about 15% of neurons were positive for tdTomato 
fluorescence, whereas about 4% of astrocytes and 6% of microglia 
exhibited Cre-­mediated recombination (fig. S11, C and D). These 
observations suggested that the MAPLEX system could cross the 
blood-­brain barrier and deliver cargo proteins to neurons in the 
brains of mice, leading to gene recombination.
MAPLEX can induce targeted epigenome editing in the 
brains of Alzheimer’s disease mouse models
Last, we evaluated the ability of MAPLEXs to deliver macromolecu-
lar cargos, such as Cas9 or deactivated Cas9 (dCas9) ribonucleopro-
teins (RNPs), which are widely used to induce genome editing and 
targeted epigenome editing. We attempted to regulate the expres-
sion of the target gene by delivering a fusion protein consisting of 
single guide RNA (sgRNA)–incorporated dCas9 and the catalytic 
domain of DNA methyltransferase 3A (DNMT3A) (fig. S12A). 
sgRNA-­incorporated dCas9 can guide the DNMT3A catalytic do-
main (D3A) to the promoter of the target gene, where D3A can then 
induce DNA methylation, leading to the repression of target gene 
expression (28, 29). β-­Site amyloid precursor protein (APP) cleaving 
enzyme 1 (Bace1), which encodes an enzyme that cleaves the APP to 
produce the neurotoxic amyloid-­β (Aβ) peptide (30–32), therefore 
playing a crucial role in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease 
(AD), was selected as the target gene. We first assessed whether 
Bace1 expression was regulated by DNA methylation of its promot-
er. We identified 28 CpG sites in the promoter of Bace1 (fig. S12B) 
that were highly methylated in mouse BV2 microglial cells and de-
methylated in mouse N2a neuroblastoma cells (fig. S12C). Conse-
quently, BACE1 protein was detectable in N2a cells but not in BV2 
cells (fig. S12D). This is consistent with previous studies that report-
ed BACE1 expression predominantly in neurons and not in microg-
lial cells either in vitro and in vivo (33, 34). We then tested whether 
dCas9-­D3A could be used to specifically methylate CpG sites in the 
promoter of Bace1 in N2a cells. We designed nine sgRNAs targeting 
different CpG sites in the promoter of Bace1 and a nontargeting con-
trol sgRNA (fig. S13). Along with nontargeting dCas9-­D3A (Non-­
dCas9-­D3A), Bace1-­targeting dCas9 fused to catalytically inactive 
DNMT3A (mD3A) was used as another negative control (fig. S14A). 
Among the nine sgRNAs, sgRNA3 and sgRNA7 were the most effec-
tive in repressing the expression of Bace1 in N2a cells; however, the 
degree of repression was mild because of the limited transfection 
efficiency, probably due to the large size of the construct. To observe 
dCas9-­D3A–mediated Bace1 repression, transfected cells were sorted 
on the basis of enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) fluores-
cence, which was possible owing to the presence of T2A-­EGFP at 
the C terminus of dCas9-­D3A (fig. S14, A and B). Both sgRNA3-­
dCas9-­D3A and sgRNA7-­dCas9-­D3A repressed the expression of 
Bace1 compared with Non-­dCas9-­D3A and Bace1-­targeting dCas9-­
mD3A (fig. S15A). In contrast, Bace2 expression was not affected by 
Bace1-­targeting dCas9-­D3A (fig. S15B), supporting the specificity 
of sgRNA3 and sgRNA7 for Bace1. In addition, we confirmed the 
reduction of BACE1 protein expression by both sgRNA3-­dCas9-­
D3A and sgRNA7-­dCas9-­D3A (fig.  S15C). Next, we examined 
whether the observed repression of Bace1 was the result of Bace1-­
targeting dCas9-­D3A–mediated methylation of the target CpG sites. 
The methylation percentage of the targeted CpG sites was increased 
from 8.9 to 41.1% by sgRNA3-­dCas9-­D3A and from 6.0 to 36.8% by 
sgRNA7-­dCas9-­D3A (fig.  S15, D to G). Bace1-­targeting dCas9-­
mD3A also repressed the expression of Bace1 to some extent com-
pared with nontargeting dCas9-­D3A (fig. S15A), perhaps because 
endogenous wild-­type DNMT3A could have formed a heterotetra-
mer with mD3A and partially methylated the targeted CpG sites in 
the promoter of Bace1. Although the methylation percentage of the 
targeted CpG sites was increased by sgRNA3-­dCas9-­mD3A and 
sgRNA7-­dCas9-­mD3A (to 17.8 and 11.58%, respectively), it was 
substantially lower than that of Bace1-­targeting dCas9-­D3A (fig. S15, 
D to G). Although the degree of methylation induction with D3A 
was comparable between sgRNA3 and sgRNA7, we observed unex-
pectedly high methylation induction at CpG site 19 with sgRNA3-­
dCas9-­mD3A. Therefore, we selected sgRNA7 as the Bace1-­targeting 
sgRNA for further experiments. After confirming dCas9-­D3A–mediated 
Bace1 promoter methylation and gene repression, we applied the 
MAPLEX system to deliver Bace1-­targeting dCas9-­D3A to the 
brains of the 3xTg-­AD and 5xFAD mouse models of AD. To load

the C terminus of dCas9-­D3A (Fig. 6A). It has been previously re-

one of the most promising nanocarriers for bioactive molecules

to deliver functional proteins across the target cell membrane (40). 
Previous studies have focused on loading cargo proteins into exo-
somes using either light-­ or chemical-­induced heterodimerization 
systems (41, 42). However, loading proteins into exosomes can also 
be achieved by fusing cargo proteins directly with exosomal mark-
ers. With this method, the challenge lies not in loading but rather 
in releasing cargo proteins from exosomal markers post-­isolation. 
To address these limitations, we engineered exosomes equipped 
with a photoinducible cargo protein release system, called MAPLEX 
(fig. S23).
Using this system, we successfully delivered soluble proteins 
across the target cell membrane, allowing the induction of compli-
cated cellular events including transcriptional regulation, gene re-
combination, and epigenome editing in vitro and in vivo (fig. S23). 
Our results showing that MAPLEXs can be used to control OCT4 
and SOX2 signaling suggest the possibility of virus-­free reprogram-
ming of induced pluripotent stem cells via exosomal delivery. Intra-
venously injected MAPLEXs accumulated in hepatocytes, consistent 
with a report using exosomes without a targeting moiety (43), where 
they were able to facilitate gene recombination in Cre reporter mice, 
suggesting that the MAPLEX system might be effective in the treat-
ment of diseases associated with the liver. Moreover, MAPLEX 
could theoretically be equipped with targeting moieties, such as ra-
bies virus glycoprotein, tumor-­penetrating peptides, or chondrocyte-­
affinity peptide (44–46), to widen its potential applications.
Last, we showed that in vivo epigenome editing can be induced 
in the brains of AD mouse models via MAPLEX (fig. S23). Intrana-
sal administration, which has previously been used to deliver exo-
somes to the brain (23, 47, 48), was used to localize MAPLEX to the 
brain without any targeting moiety. MAPLEX-­mediated delivery of 
sgBace1-­dCas9-­D3A successfully induced methylation at the tar-
geted CpG sites, reduced Bace1 expression, and reduced recognition 
memory impairment and amyloid pathology in both 3xTg-­AD and 
5xFAD mice. Furthermore, we confirmed that MAPLEX-­mediated 
epigenome editing can last for 3 weeks after the last injection. Al-
though a statistically significant increase in methylation percentage 
was observed at targeted CpG sites upon dCas9-­D3A delivery in 
both 3xTG-­AD and 5xFAD mice, the extent of this increase was 
relatively modest when compared with the reduction in Bace1 ex-
pression. This may be due to the inherent complexity of the brain, 
comprising diverse cell populations with varying basal methylation 
percentage. In addition, the presence of endogenous DNMT3A and 
DNMT3L, which function as a heterotetramer in the methylation 
process, could have influenced the degree of induced DNA methyla-
tion. In cases where the amounts of endogenous DNMT3A and DN-
MT3L may be lower than the delivered dCas9-­D3A, it is plausible 
that sgBace1-­dCas9-­D3A could act as a repressor, potentially ob-
structing RNA polymerase binding to the Bace1 promoter. In either 
case, our findings demonstrate proof of principle for the potential of 
the MAPLEX system to deliver dCas9-­D3A into the brain in AD 
mouse models and repress Bace1 expression.
Although our study demonstrated the efficacy of the MAPLEX 
system in delivering proteins across cell membranes and triggering 
desired cellular responses, some limitations and unanswered ques-
tions remain. First, although we primarily focused on the cortex, 
the specific brain regions taking up the intranasally injected exo-
somes require more comprehensive evaluation. Additional regions 
such as the olfactory bulb could potentially benefit from increased 
exosomal delivery. Second, although we extensively characterized 
our exosomes by following the guidelines from the minimal infor-
mation for studies of EVs (MISEV2023) (49), the amount of cargo 
protein within individual exosomes needs further evaluation for 
precise dosing. Last, the implementation of a precise targeting sys-
tem is essential for clinical translation. Although some targeting 
moieties have been reported, they are generally not selective enough 
to fully prevent off-­target effects. Because the type of exosome-­
producing cells and administration routes also affect the targeting 
of exosomes, further research to elucidate the appropriate targeting 
moiety, exosome-­producing cells, and administration route is re-
quired to enhance the targeting capability of the MAPLEX system. 
The development of more sophisticated and selective targeting sys-
tems is the next step to improve the clinical potential of the MA-
PLEX system, which may open new avenues for genetic therapeutics 
and be applied to various diseases associated with specific protein 
deficiencies or gene mutations.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Study design
The aim of this study was to develop an exosome-­based intracellular 
protein delivery system that can be applied to multiple types of dis-
eases. We hypothesized that, by fusing a photocleavable protein be-
tween a cargo protein and an exosomal membrane marker, the cargo 
protein could be released from the exosomal membrane upon 405-­
nm light illumination, allowing its proper function in the target cell 
after exosomal uptake. Engineered exosomes, equipped with a pho-
toinducible protein release system, were characterized using NTA, 
immunoblotting, fluorescence intensity measurements, and cryo-­
EM. For the in vitro evaluation of our photoinducible protein deliv-
ery system, we delivered transcription factors (OCT4 and SOX2) to 
BJ fibroblasts and measured the mRNA expression of their target 
genes via qPCR. For the in vivo evaluation of our protein delivery 
system, we delivered Cre recombinase to Ai9 mice and measured 
tdTomato fluorescence using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS). 
Cre-­mediated deletion of the LSL cassette from gDNA was exam-
ined by PCR, and tdTomato protein was detected by immunoblot-
ting. Last, to examine whether our system could be applied to 
disease models, we delivered Bace1-­targeting sgRNA-­incorporated 
dCas9 fused with the catalytic domain of DNMT3A to 3xTg-­AD 
and 5xFAD mice. We conducted an NOR test to assess recognition 
memory impairment in AD mice. We also performed qPCR and im-
munoblotting to measure the mRNA and protein expression of 
Bace1 in the brains of AD mice and pyrosequencing to measure the 
methylation percentage in the promoter of Bace1.
For in vivo studies, the number of animals was selected on the 
basis of variability observed in pilot experiments and the availability 
of mice. Animals were randomized to groups [Ai9: three mice per 
group (three males), 3xTg-­AD: eight mice per group (three males 
and five females), and 5xFAD: four mice per group (three males and 
one female)], and no mice were excluded from the experiment. The 
NOR test was performed by an operator blinded to the mouse type 
involved in the test. In the NOR data analysis, three mice from the 
Non-­dCas9-­D3A:MAPLEX–treated 3xTg-­AD group and one mouse 
from the sgBace1-­dCas9-­D3A:MAPLEX–treated 3xTg-­AD group 
were excluded from the analysis because of the lack of exploratory 
activity (less than 7-­s exploration time of both objects within a 5-­
min period).

Exosome isolation and characterization
Exosomes were isolated by TFF as described previously (18, 50, 51). 
Twenty-­four hours after transfection with an appropriate construct, 
HEK293T cells were maintained in fresh fetal bovine serum (FBS)–
free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 1% 
penicillin-­streptomycin. After 24 hours, the first CM was collected, 
and fresh FBS-­free DMEM containing 1% penicillin-­streptomycin 
was again added to the HEK293T cell. After 24 hours, the second 
CM was collected, and all collected CMs were centrifuged at 1500g 
for 10 min at 4°C to remove cellular debris. Subsequently, soluble 
proteins were removed from the supernatant of the CM by using a 
0.22-­μm bottle top filter (Millipore Sigma). To remove soluble pro-
teins from the CM and concentrate exosomes, the filtered CM was 
subjected to TFF. Briefly, a 300-­kDa MWCO (molecular weight cut-
off) ultrafiltration membrane filter capsule (Pall Corporation) was 
used to perform a continuous circulation at a 2 ml/min operation 
speed. Contaminants below 300 kDa were removed, and exosomes 
were obtained in a final volume of ~5 ml. Exosomes were quantified 
using a micro bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay (Thermo 
Fisher Scientific) and NTA (LM-­10, Malvern Instruments Ltd.) 
equipped with a 642-­nm laser module at room temperature (RT). 
Samples were introduced into the chamber and video-­recorded for 
1 min. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. The size distri-
bution and zeta potential of the exosomes were measured by DLS 
and ELS (Zetasizer ZS90, Malvern Instruments Ltd.).

(Genicom, Daejeon, Korea) was used to measure the LED illumi-

Protease digestion assay

inactivate proteinase K. Sample buffer (4×) containing lithium do-

In vivo gene recombination by Cre delivery
Ai9 mice were fed an alfalfa-­free diet (Raonbio Co. Ltd.) for 7 days 
to reduce the autofluorescence. Cre:MAPLEXs were isolated as de-
scribed above, and 200 μl of Cre:MAPLEXs (1.5 mg ml−1) was admin-

manely euthanized, and the major organs (heart, liver, lung, kidney, 
and spleen) were excised. Cre:MAPLEXs were isolated as described 
above, and 24 μl of Cre:MAPLEXs (2.4 mg ml−1) was administered 
into Ai9 mice by intranasal injection every day for 14 days. Mice 
(n = 3 per group) were humanely euthanized, and the major organs 
(brain, heart, liver, lung, kidney, and spleen) were excised. Ex vivo 
fluorescence was imaged using an IVIS Lumina system (PerkinElmer) 
with the following excitation and emission filters: 535/575 to 650 nm 
for tdTomato and 465/575 to 650 nm for the tissue autofluorescence. 
The autofluorescence background filter image was then subtracted 
from the tdTomato filter image using the image math tool. All im-
ages were acquired under the same field of view using autoexposure, 
which corresponded to a pixel binning of 8 and f/stop of 2. gDNA 
was extracted from the liver using a total DNA extraction kit (BI-
ONICS) and PCR-­amplified using primers 26F and 26R to confirm 
LSL cassette deletion (table S4). Total proteins were extracted from 
the liver using the radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer 
(Merck) and subjected to Western blotting.

conversion kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. K1461) following

were purified and subjected to pyrosequencing analysis using se-
quencing primers 31F, 32F, and the PyroMark Q48 Autoprep System 
(QIAGEN) (table S6).

Statistical analyses
Statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism soft-
ware v.9.5.1. Significant differences between the two groups were 
calculated using a two-­tailed Student’s t test, and those among mul-
tiple groups were calculated using one-­way analysis of variance 
(ANOVA) with either Tukey’s post hoc test or Dunnett’s post hoc 
test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Appropriate methods 
are indicated in the legends, with significant differences marked in 
all figures. Values and error bars are expressed as means ± SEM or 
SD as noted in the text and figure legends.

Engineered exosomes with a photoinducible protein delivery system enable
CRISPR-Cas–based epigenome editing in Alzheimer’s disease
Jihoon Han, Jae Hoon Sul, Jeongmi Lee, Eunae Kim, Hark Kyun Kim, Minshik Chae, Jeein Lim, Jongho Kim, Chanhee
Kim, Jun-Sik Kim, Yoonsuk Cho, Jae Hyung Park, Yong Woo Cho, and Dong-Gyu Jo
Sci. Transl. Med. 16 (759), eadi4830.  DOI: 10.1126/scitranslmed.adi4830
Editor’s summary
Many diseases could potentially be treated with protein-based therapeutics, but an inability to deliver proteins
across cell membranes limits their development. Here, Han et al. engineered an exosome-based delivery system
called MAPLEX, where cargo proteins are fused with the photocleavable protein mMaple3, which is then fused to
an exosomal marker protein. The fusion product is overexpressed in cells in vitro and accumulates in exosomes,
which are then purified and exposed to 405-nm light, cleaving mMaple3 and releasing the cargo protein into the
intraexosomal space. Intranasal administration of MAPLEXs carrying a dCas9 ribonucleoprotein complex reduced
A# by epigenetic editing of the Bace1 promoter in cortical neurons of two mouse models of Alzheimer’s disease,
highlighting the potential of the MAPLEX system for delivery of therapeutic proteins. —Melissa L. Norton

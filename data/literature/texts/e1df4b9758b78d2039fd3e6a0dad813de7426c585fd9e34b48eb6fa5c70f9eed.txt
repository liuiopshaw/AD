A B S T R A C T
Silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) are effective broad-spectrum fungicides but their applications are restricted by high 
cost and cytotoxicity. Here, ultrasmall (~3 nm) and monodispersed silver-coated copper nanoparticles (Cu@Ag 
NPs) were synthesized for antibacterial nanocomposite fibers using high-gravity technology combined with in- 
situ modification, achieving a Cu/Ag mass ratio of 2, which is the smallest reported for this system. Small size 
contributes to the high antibacterial properties and low cytotoxicity. By reducing particle size from 16 nm to 3 
nm, inhibition zones against E. coli and S. aureus increase by 15 % and 38 % respectively, while mouse fibroblast 
cells viability rises from 68 % to 95 %. Moreover, the core-shell architecture enhances Ag–Cu interfacial electron 
transfer and •O2
−generation, further promoting antibacterial efficacy. The prepared Cu@Ag/polyvinyl alcohol 
(PVA) nanocomposite fibers exhibit 4× (E. coli) and 3× (S. aureus) higher antibacterial performances than Ag/ 
PVA counterparts, while maintaining excellent biocompatibility. These findings highlight the great potential of 
Cu@Ag NPs for low-cost and wide-ranging applications in wound dressings and other medical consumables.
1. Introduction
The threat of bacterial infections is insidious and widespread, 
constantly challenging the global public health defense [1]. A report 
released by The Lancet shows that in 2019, approximately 13.7 million 
people worldwide died from bacterial infections, and about 7.7 million 
of these deaths were closely related to pathogens such as Staphylococcus 
aureus (S. aureus) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) [2]. In light of these 
challenges, advancing antibacterial material research has become 
indispensable for mitigating infection risks and ensuring sustainable 
healthcare systems. Although conventional organic antibiotics exhibit 
rapid initial antibacterial effects and broad-spectrum ability, their 
overuse has led to the emergency of drug resistance and biotoxicity [3].
With the rapid advancement of nanotechnology, inorganic antibac­
terial nanomaterials, particularly based on metals [4–6], metal oxides 
[7–10] and metal complexes [11–13], have attracted extensive attention 
due to their sustained antibacterial efficacy and reduced tendency to 
induce microbial resistance. Among these materials, Ag nanoparticles 
(NPs) stand out as a broad-spectrum fungicide, exhibiting high antimi­
crobial efficacy even at low dosages [14,15]. Moreover, they demon­
strate environmental independence [16–19], making it a focal point of 
current research. However, the large-scale application of Ag NPs is 
significantly hindered by high production costs. To overcome this 
challenge, researchers have explored integrating other antimicrobial 
metals with Ag NPs. Cu emerges as an excellent candidate due to its dual 
role as an essential human trace element and a potent antibacterial agent 
[20]. Therefore, Ag-coated Cu (Cu@Ag) bimetallic antibacterial mate­
rials with a core-shell structure have been developed [21,22]. This 
structure not only decreases application cost, but also enhances the 
stability of Cu NPs by shielding them from direct environmental expo­
sure with the Ag shell [23–25], thereby prolonging the antimicrobial 
duration. Furthermore, synthesizing Cu@Ag NPs with small particle 
sizes is a critical strategy for further cost reduction and minimization of 
metal-induced cytotoxicity. The size of Cu@Ag NPs is inversely corre­
lated with the processes of bacterial intracellular uptake, transport, 
accumulation and subsequent biological interactions [26,27]. Specif­
ically, smaller NPs possess higher surface areas and more active sites, 
leading to enhanced membrane permeability, elevated intracellular 
reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and significant disruption of 
adenosine triphosphate (ATP) balance [28–32]. Under the same anti­
bacterial efficacy, the usage amount of small-sized Cu@Ag NPs will 
markedly decrease, which not only reduces antibacterial costs but also

Currently, the various methods, such as chemical reduction [33,34], 
in-situ reconstructing [35], self-assembly process [36], electrochemical 
deposition [37], have been used to synthesize NPs with core-shell 
structure. Among them, chemical reduction, including aqueous-phase 
and oil-phase methods, stands out as the preferred method for Cu@Ag 
NPs synthesis, primarily due to its simple and scalable process, which 
enables the attainment of both high yield and high purity [38,39]. 
However, in aqueous systems, the rapid ionization rates of metal salts 
and subsequent reaction kinetics are difficult to control, resulting in 
larger diameter and broader particle size distribution of the production 
in period studies [40–43]. Although oil-phase synthesis effectively 
mitigates this issue of uneven particle size distribution by regulating the 
ionization reaction rate, its long reaction periods and stringent inert 
atmosphere requirements significantly increase production costs 
[44,45]. Meanwhile, excessive consumption of organic solvents in this 
process can also trigger a range of environmental problems. Therefore, 
the development of an aqueous synthesis platform capable of enhancing 
mass transfer efficiency and nucleation kinetics is of great significance 
for the efficient preparation of ultrasmall and monodisperse Cu@Ag 
NPs.
High gravity technology, a novel process-intensification method 
based on rotating packed bed (RPB), offers a transformative solution 
[46]. During the precipitation process of nanoparticles, the intense 
mass-transfer ability in RPB induces instantaneous supersaturation, 
triggering explosive nucleation while suppressing crystal growth via 
rapid consuming of metal salt solution [47]. This enables the formation 
of ultrasmall nanoparticles with narrow size distributions [48,49]. At 
the same time, the superior micromixing performance of RPB ensures 
uniform dispersion of surface modifiers, resulting in monodisperse 
nanoparticles with enhanced colloidal stability. To date, our research 
group has not only achieved successful synthesis of various inorganic 
nanoparticles with small sizes and narrow size distribution, including 
Ag, ZnO, SiO2, CeO2, CaCO3 by high-gravity technology [50–55], but 
also established a solid technological platform for the preparation of 
ultrasmall nanoparticles with precise size control and high yield.
In this study, high gravity technology combined with the in-situ 
modification method was employed to successfully prepare ultrasmall 
and monodispersed Cu@Ag NPs, with copper sulfate as the copper 
source, silver nitrate as the silver source, gum arabica as the modifier, 
and hydrazine hydrate as the reducing agent. By precisely adjusting the 
synthesis condition, Cu@Ag NPs with different size distributions could 
also be fabricated. Through in vitro antibacterial assays and cytotoxicity 
tests, the antibacterial properties and cell compatibility of Cu@Ag NPs, 
as well as the relationship between particle size and these two aspects 
were investigated. Coating a silver layer on the copper surface not only 
enhanced the antibacterial stability of copper but also further boosted 
the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), attributed to electron 
transfer at the Ag–Cu junction. Concurrently, this coating markedly 
reduced the cytotoxicity associated with copper. Furthermore, obtained 
by compounding Cu@Ag NPs with polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), Cu@Ag/ 
PVA nanocomposite fibers exhibit not only excellent long-lasting anti­
bacterial capabilities but also favorable biosafety. These findings suggest 
that Cu@Ag NPs and Cu@Ag/PVA nanocomposite fibers are promising, 
cost-effective antimicrobial agents with strong efficacy and safety, of­
fering potential solutions to global challenges in bacterial infections.
2. Experimental section
2.1. Experimental materials
Copper sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4⋅5H2O; Macklin; China), gum 
arabica (GA; Macklin; China), sodium chloride (NaCl; Macklin; China), 
Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS; Macklin; China), potassium dichromate 
(K2Cr2O7; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.; China), 2,2-Diphenyl- 
1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH; Macklin; China), Hydrazine hydrate (85 %, 
N2H4⋅H2O; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.; China), silver nitrate 
(AgNO3; Beijing Tong Guang Fine Chemicals Co., Ltd.; China), ammonia 
(NH3⋅H2O; Beijing Tong Guang Fine Chemicals Co., Ltd.; China), yeast 
extract (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.; China), peptone (Thermo Fisher 
Scientific Inc.; China), agar (Beijing Biotopped Technology Co.; China), 
SYTO 9/PI Live/Dead Bacterial Double Stain Kit (Shanghai FuShen 
Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; China), Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium 
(DMEM; Cytiva Bio-technology Co. Inc.; China), mice (SPF; Beijing 
Weitong Lihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co., LTD; China) and 
deionized water (H2O; laboratory deionizer) were used in the study.
2.2. Synthesis of monodispersed Cu@Ag NPs
First, a typical synthesis procedure of Cu NPs was carried out within 
the RPB. GA (0.6 g) was dissolved in 100 mL of water. CuSO4⋅5H2O (0.6 
g) was dissolved into the above solution to form the precursor solution 
with a concentration of 24 mM. N2H4⋅H2O (4.24 g) was dissolved in 50 
mL of water to form reducing solution. The two solutions were then 
simultaneously injected into the RPB. The reaction was carried out at 
room temperature with a rotation speed of 2500 rpm. The reaction so­
lutions were sprayed into the rotator. The mixing and residence of the 
reactants in the RPB reactor were nearly instantaneous producing Cu 
NPs. A portion of the as-synthesized Cu NPs product was recirculated 
back into the RPB for 10 min. The nanodispersion collected from the 
outlet of the RPB immediately flowed into the beaker and kept stirring 
for 90 min to obtain wine-red Cu nanodispersion.
Then the synthesis process for Cu@Ag NPs with a particle size of 3.1 
nm is as follows. Cu nanodispersion was centrifuged with ethanol, then 
redispersed in 150 mL of water. GA (0.1 g) was added into the suspen­
sion. Meanwhile, 0.204 g of AgNO3 was dissolved in 150 mL of water 
under stirring, followed by dropwise addition of NH3⋅H2O until the so­
lution turned transparent. Both solutions were simultaneously pumped 
into the RPB. A portion of the reaction products were recirculated into 
the RPB to intensify reaction. After 1 min, the products were collected 
and stirred for 30 min to form black dispersion of monodisperse Cu@Ag 
NPs.
The resultant Cu@Ag NPs with varying sizes were synthesized using 
an identical process and maintaining the same feed molar ratio, while 
adjusting the volume of the Ag+ solution. Specifically, when the volume 
of the Ag+ solution is set at 100 mL, the size of the prepared Cu@Ag NPs 
is approximately 5.7 nm. In contrast, when the volume of the Ag+ so­
lution is decreased to 25 mL, the size of the Cu@Ag NPs increases to 16 
nm.
As a control sample, during the synthesis of Cu@Ag NPs in a con­
ventional stirred tank reactor (STR), the preparation methods of the 
precursor and reducing solutions were consistent with those in the RPB 
synthesis process. Then the reducing solution was added dropwise to the 
copper complex solution at room temperature, followed by continuous 
stirring for 90 min. The STR product was then collected through 
centrifugation (12,000 rpm for 15 min). After redispersion, GA and 
silver ammonia solution were added sequentially, and the mixture was 
continuously stirred for 30 min to obtain the Cu@Ag nanodispersion. 
The prepared Cu@Ag nanodispersion was utilized for subsequent 
experimental characterization and performance evaluation.
2.3. Synthesis of nanocomposite fibers
A specified amount of PVA was dissolved in deionized water. The 
nanodispersion was then introduced into above solution to synthesis Ag, 
Cu, and Cu@Ag electrospinning solution. The concentration of PVA was 
maintained at 10 wt%, while the mass ratios of main components added 
were controlled at 0.5 wt%, relative to the mass of PVA. During elec­
trospinning, the spinning voltage was adjusted to 20 kV, and the dis­
tance between the grounded collector and the nozzle was maintained to

2.4. Characterization of nanoparticles and nanomaterials films
The morphology of both the nanoparticles and those embedded in 
fiber composites was studied using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM, 
Japan), Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM, Japan) and high- 
resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM, Japan). The 
crystalline structures of the monodispersed nanoparticles were charac­
terized using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Netherlands). Elemental 
composition was investigated using X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy 
(XPS, USA); peak deconvolution for compositional analysis was 
accomplished using AVANTAGE software. The element distribution was 
characterized by Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Japan) and 
High Angle Annular Dark Field (HAADF, Japan). The surface plasmon 
resonance (SPR) of nanoparticles was characterized using Ultraviolet- 
Visible Spectrophotometer (UV–Vis, Japan) Thermal stability of the 
nanoparticles was determined through Thermogravimetric Analysis 
(TGA/DSC3+, Mettler Toledo International Inc.).
2.5. In vitro antimicrobial activities
The antibacterial properties of the samples against Gram-negative 
E. coli (ATCC 25923) and Gram-positive S. aureus (ATCC 25922) was 
evaluated using the zone of inhibition method. Briefly, 100 μL of the 
bacterial suspension of either E. coli or S. aureus (1 × 105 CFU⋅mL−1) was 
spread evenly on an agar plate. Next, oxford cups containing 100 μL of 
the sample dispersion were placed uniformly on the agar plate. The plate 
was then incubated at 37 ◦C for 12 h, after which photographs were 
taken and the zone of inhibition was measured.
Furthermore, the quantitative assessment of antibacterial efficiency 
was conducted using the colony-forming unit (CFU) counting method on 
agar plates. Specially, 1 mL of the bacterial suspension of either E. coli or 
S. aureus (1 × 105 CFU⋅mL−1) was mixed with the sample dispersion and 
incubated at 37 ◦C for 5 min. Subsequently, 100 μL of the resulting 
mixture was spread evenly onto solid agar plates and incubated for 12 h 
to determine bacterial growth. The calculation formula for antimicrobial 
rate is as follows. 
Antimicrobial Rate (%) = N0 −Nt

where N0 represents the colony count of the blank control group (bac­
teria treated with PBS), and Nt represents the colony count of the test

2.6. Bacterial structural damage assay
The structural damage of bacterial cells was assessed by the staining 
kit. 1 mL suspension (1 × 108 CFU⋅mL−1) of E. coli or S. aureus was 
prepared by dilution with PBS, followed by staining according to the 
protocol provided in the SYTO 9/PI Live/Dead Bacterial Double Stain 
Kit. Finally, the viability status of the bacteria (live/dead) was observed 
using a confocal fluorescence microscope.
2.7. Bacterial morphology assay
The morphologies of bacterial cells were assessed by SEM. After a 30 
min exposure period, the mixture was centrifuged with PBS. Then, they 
were immersed in glutaraldehyde fixative at 4 ◦C for 4 h. After fixation, 
the bacteria were dehydrated using a series of ethanol solutions with 
increasing concentrations (30 %, 50 %, 70 %, 90 %, and 100 %).
2.8. Bacterial protein leakage assay
The leakage of proteins from bacteria was examined as follows. 1 mL 
bacterial suspension (1 × 108 CFU⋅mL−1) containing either E. coli or 
S. aureus was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and then washed three 
times with PBS. The samples were added to the bacterial suspension and 
incubated for 30 min at 37 ◦C. The absorbance of the supernatant was 
measured at 280 nm using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer.
2.9. Cell viability assay
Mouse fibroblasts (3T3) cells were cultured in DMEM under standard 
cell culture conditions (37 ◦C, 5 % CO2). Nanoparticles and nanofiber 
samples were immersed in DMEM, and incubated under the same 
standard conditions to allow the extraction of compounds from the 
samples into the medium for 24 h. The cytotoxicity of 3T3 was assessed 
using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Solarbio) assay. Briefly, the sam­
ples extracts were incubated with 3T3 (2 × 103 cells per well) in 96-well 
plates for 24 h. Subsequently, the CCK-8 reagent was replaced, and the 
plates were incubated for an additional 30 min. Absorbance was 
measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader. 
Cell viability Rate (%) = Dt −D0
Dc −D0
× 100% 
where Dt tests sample absorbance after action on 3T3, D0 is the absor­
bance of the blank group (drug-free DMEM), and Dc is the absorbance of 
the control group (3T3 cells).
2.10. Cellular morphology assay
The morphology of the 3T3 cells was examined using a confocal 
fluorescence microscope following staining with LIVE/DEAD™ 
Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit.
2.11. Hemolysis rate assay
To assess the blood compatibility of the nanoparticles and nanofiber, 
the hemolysis test was performed [56]. Mouse whole blood was 
centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min at 4 ◦C to separate red blood cells 
(RBCs). The isolated RBCs were then washed thrice with PBS, with the 
supernatant removed after each time. 4 % RBCs suspension was pre­
pared, and samples were incubated with this suspension for 2 h at 37 ◦C. 
After incubation, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation, and 
its absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 540 nm. The calculation 
formula for hemolysis rate is as follows. 
Hemolysis Rate (%) = At −An
Ap −An
× 100% 
where At is test sample absorbance after action on RBCs, An is negative 
control absorbance (RBCs treatment with physiological saline), and Ap is 
positive control absorbance (RBCs treatment with deionized water).
2.12. Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were conducted using Origin 2018. Data are 
expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group differences were 
also evaluated. Statistical significance was defined at the following P- 
value thresholds: *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001 and ****, P <
0.0001.

size distribution of nanoparticles synthesized by RPB and STR (Fig. 1i

3.2. In vitro antibacterial activity and cytocompatibility evaluation of 
Cu@Ag NPs with different particle sizes
The antibacterial efficacy of nanomaterials is predominantly influ­
enced by their physicochemical characteristics, of which particle size is 
a particularly crucial factor. The size-dependent antibacterial activity 
and cytotoxicity of Cu@Ag NPs in vitro are investigated. As illustrated in 
Fig. 3a and Fig. S3, Cu@Ag NPs with different sizes exhibit excellent 
antibacterial activity against E. coli. It is noteworthy that the inhibition 
zone area of E. coli decreases from 3.1 cm2 to 2.7 cm2, while that of 
S. aureus from 3.9 cm2 to 2.8 cm2 (Fig. 3b and Fig. S4), respectively, as 
the particle size increases from 3 nm to 16 nm. This phenomenon can be 
attributed to the enhanced penetration capacity of smaller particles, 
which facilitates their disruption of bacterial cell membranes through 
increased interaction with membrane components, corroborating size- 
dependent efficacy trends reported previously [13,70,71]. Similarly, 
Cu@Ag NPs synthesized via the RPB method exhibit superior 
antibacterial performance compared to their 10 nm counterparts pre­
pared by the STR approach, with a 23.6 % increase in the antibacterial 
rate against E. coli (Fig. 3c and Fig. S5). The minimum inhibitory con­
centration (MIC) was determined by observing the lowest concentration 
of Cu@Ag NPs that prevented visible bacterial growth, while minimum 
bactericidal concentration (MBC) was identified as the lowest concen­
tration that killed 99.9 % of the initial bacterial inoculum. For the ul­
trasmall Cu@Ag NPs synthesized via RPB, the MIC values are 
determined to be 3.13 ppm against E. coli and 1.56 ppm against 
S. aureus, as illustrated in Fig. S6. Furthermore, the MBC values of 
Cu@Ag NPs against both bacterial strains are 6.25 ppm (Fig. S7). 
Additionally, the antioxidant capacity evaluated by analyzing the ability 
to scavenge DPPH free radicals also demonstrates size-dependent 
radical-scavenging activity, with smaller particles showing superior 
performance (Fig. 3d). The cytocompatibility of Cu@Ag NPs of various 
sizes against RBCs and 3T3 cells is evaluated. Fig. 3e indicates that the 
hemolysis rate of RBCs rises as the particle size increases. Notably, when 
considering the concentration associated with the same level of anti­
bacterial efficiency, the hemolysis rate of 16 nm Cu@Ag nanoparticles 
exceeds the 5 % safety threshold. In 3T3 cells viability assays (Fig. 3f), 
smaller Cu@AgNPs sustained cell viability above 80 % across tested 
concentration, demonstrating low cytotoxicity. These results underscore 
the size-dependent regulation of both antimicrobial activity and 
biosafety.

3.3. Comparative evaluation on the in vitro antibacterial activity and 
cytotoxicity of Cu@Ag NPs versus Cu NPs and Ag NPs
Furthermore, the synergistic interaction of Cu and Ag within Cu@Ag 
NPs was systematically studied. Firstly, the inhibition rates of E. coli and 
S. aureus are higher when treated with Cu@Ag NPs compared to those 
treated with Ag NPs or Cu NPs alone (Fig. 4a). Specifically, the anti­
microbial rates against E. coli improved by up to 16.8 % and 11.9 %, 
while those against S. aureus are enhanced by up to 4.2 % and 2.8 %, 
calculated as A-B (where A is Cu@Ag NPs group, B is control group). To 
study the enhanced antibacterial effect’s mechanism, electron- 
scavenging experiments were done. Results in Fig. S8 showed electron 
acceptor greatly affects the antibacterial performance of Cu NPs and 
Cu@Ag NPs, but not Ag NPs. Furthermore, the electron transfer between 
copper and silver enhances their synergistic antibacterial action. The 
experimental results reveal the existence of efficient interfacial charge 
transfer at the Ag–Cu junction, which induces a synergistic antibacte­
rial effect.
Notably, Cu@Ag NPs demonstrate superior inhibition against 
S. aureus compared to E. coli, likely attributable to the higher affinity of 
Ag for the thicker peptidoglycan layer in the cell walls of S.aureus 
[72–74]. To further evaluate the extent of membrane structural damage, 
the protein content in the bacterial suspension after nanoparticles 
treatment is measured. Higher protein content after treatment with Cu 
NPs suggests the most extensive damage to bacterial structures, under­
scoring the advantages of incorporating Cu (Fig. 4b). Meanwhile, the 
dose of protein leakage induced by Cu@Ag NPs was greater than that 
caused by Ag NPs. Similarly, S. aureus exhibited a higher degree of 
protein leakage compared to E. coli.
To thoroughly analyze the types of reactive oxygen species (ROS), 
such as superoxide anions (•O2
−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hy­
droxyl radicals (•OH), generated during the antibacterial process of 
Cu@Ag NPs and their influence on the antibacterial performance, ROS 
scavengers’ experiments are conducted. The results demonstrate that 
the antibacterial performance of Cu@Ag NPs displays significant 
changes upon the addition of specific ROS scavengers (Fig. 4c) [75]. 
Specially, the antimicrobial efficacy of scavengers targeting •O2
−, H2O2, 
and •OH against E. coli decreases to 22.4 %, 26.7 % and 34.7 %, 
respectively, from an initial 90.4 %. Significantly, the addition of the 
•O2
−scavenger leads to the most pronounced reduction in the antibac­
terial activity of Cu@Ag NPs against E. coli. Furthermore, Ag NPs and Cu 
NPs are more sensitive to H2O2. This difference arises from the electron 
transfer at the Ag–Cu junction, which alters the redox properties of the 
system and facilitates the reduction of oxygen species on the nano­
particle surface to •O2
−[76].
To elucidate the underlying antibacterial mechanism, the morpho­
logical changes of bacteria are observed. From the SEM images in Fig. 4d 
and Fig. S9, E. coli and S. aureus are relatively smooth, and their struc­
tural morphologies are intact before the treatment with nanoparticles. 
However, after treatment with Ag NPs and Cu NPs, nanoparticles are 
adhered to the bacterial surface, causing a slight shrinkage of mem­
brane. Nevertheless, when E. coli and S. aureus are treated with Cu@Ag 
NPs, many wrinkles and depressions appear on their surfaces, and even 
structural collapse occurs. These phenomena strongly suggest that 
Cu@Ag NPs can disrupt the permeability barrier function of the bacte­
rial cell wall, leading to the leakage of intracellular substances and ul­
timately bacterial apoptosis [77].
To further explore whether the bacterial membrane is ruptured, 
E. coli after being treated with nanoparticles is stained with SYTO 9 and 
PI [78]. SYTO 9, a green fluorescent cytoplasmic dye, can penetrate live 
bacteria with intact cell membranes, while PI can only enter bacteria 
with damaged membranes. In the control group, E. coli showed promi­
nent green fluorescence with no red fluorescence, indicating a high 
number of live bacteria (Fig. 4e and Fig. S10). After treatment with Ag 
NPs and Cu NPs, E. coli exhibits weak green fluorescence and moderate 
red fluorescence, reflecting the relatively limited antibacterial 
capabilities of these two types of nanoparticles. In contrast, E. coli in the 
Cu@Ag NPs group and the 75 % ethanol group displays intense red 
fluorescence, indicating damage to the bacterial membrane structure, 
which allowed substantial PI influx. This fully demonstrates the 
remarkable antibacterial efficacy of Cu@Ag NPs, which is highly 
consistent with the conclusion obtained from method above. The anti­
bacterial mechanism of Cu@Ag NPs can be encapsulated as follows. The 
surface characteristics of Cu@Ag NPs endow them with the ability to 
adhere tightly to the bacterial surface. Subsequently, they launch an 
assault on the bacterial membrane structure, culminating in the 
disruption of the cell structure. Meanwhile, the unique electronic 
properties at the Ag–Cu interface facilitate the injection of an electron 
flux into the bacterial cytoplasm. This influx of electrons significantly 
perturbs the redox homeostasis within the bacteria, potently inducing 
oxidative stress. Under the dual onslaught of membrane structure 
damage and oxidative stress, the physiological functions of the bacteria 
are severely disrupted, ultimately leading to bacterial death (Fig. 4f) 
[79–82].
After confirming excellent antibacterial performance of Cu@Ag NPs 
and its efficient ROS scavenging capacity, the effect on biocompatibility 
was then investigated. In the antioxidant activity assay, although 
Cu@Ag NPs exhibited a lower DPPH radical scavenging rate than Cu 
NPs, their scavenging rate still increased from 15.11 % (Ag NPs) to 
20.13 % (Fig. 4g). The primary reason for the stronger DPPH free radical 
scavenging ability of Cu@Ag NPs is presented as follows. In the core- 
shell structure, electron transfer occurs between Ag and Cu, altering 
the electron cloud distribution of the system and generating electrons 
with strong reducing ability. These electrons provide paired electrons 
for DPPH free radicals, facilitating the reduction and scavenging of 
DPPH free radicals [83]. To gain deeper insights, blood compatibility 
and cell compatibility of Cu@Ag NPs were investigated. Although the 
hemolysis rate for Cu@Ag NPs is slightly elevated compared to Cu NPs 
and Ag NPs, the hemolysis rates of three nanoparticles remain below the 
5 % safety threshold (Fig. 3h). Further, Fig. 3i reveals that Cu NPs exert 
the most pronounced cytotoxic effect on 3T3 cells, whereas Ag NPs 
exhibit the least toxicity. Especially, Cu@Ag NPs demonstrate an in­
termediate level of cytotoxicity. These findings collectively provide 
evidence supporting the protective role of the Ag shell in attenuating Cu- 
induced cellular damage. In a word, the above findings demonstrate the 
practicality of the design principle for core-shell structures. The core 
structure reduces the cytotoxicity of the core material, and due to the 
interactions between the core and the shell, the antibacterial perfor­
mance is significantly enhanced.
3.4. Synthesis of Cu@Ag nanocomposite fibers and in vitro antibacterial 
activity and cytocompatibility
Given the severe challenges that wound infection poses in clinical 
treatment, Cu/PVA, Ag/PVA, and Cu@Ag/PVA nanocomposite fibers 
were successfully prepared by electrospinning. Subsequently, a 
comprehensive assessment was carried out to evaluate their antibacte­
rial properties and biocompatibility, with the results presented in Fig. 5. 
The SEM images prove that Cu@Ag NPs can significantly enhance the 
orientation of PVA (Fig. 5a and Fig. S11) while having minimal impact 
on the diameter of PVA (Fig. S12). Additionally, an investigation into the 
antibacterial efficacy of various nanocomposite fibers against two bac­
terial strains reveals a relationship between the concentration of the 
antibacterial agents and the antibacterial rate (Fig. 5b, c). Remarkably, 
the antimicrobial rate of Cu@Ag/PVA nanocomposite fibers is superior 
to those of Ag/PVA and Cu/PVA. Specifically, when exposed to a 4 ppm 
concentration for 5 min, Cu@Ag/PVA nanocomposite fibers inhibits the 
growth of E. coli by 99.6 % and S. aureus by 99.99 %. Under identical 
experimental conditions, Ag/PVA nanocomposite fibers only achieve 
inhibition rates of 19.3 % against E. coli and 21.1 % against S. aureus, 
while Cu/PVA nanocomposite fibers with inhibition rates of 74.3 % for 
E. coli and 95.9 % for S. aureus. This finding contradicts the general rule

Fig. 5f reveals that the hemolysis rate exhibits a concentration-

nanocomposite fibers and Ag/PVA nanocomposite fibers maintain he­

molysis. The aforementioned findings reveal that Cu@Ag/PVA nano­
composite fibers exhibit outstanding bactericidal activity and favorable 
biosafety, rendering it an exceedingly promising candidate for the 
development of antimicrobial wound dressings and coatings applicable 
to implantable medical devices.
4. Conclusion
In summary, ultrasmall and monodispersed Cu@Ag NPs with an 
average particle size of 3 nm and Cu@Ag/PVA nanocomposite fibers 
have been successfully synthesized via high-gravity reduction technol­
ogy combined with in-situ surface modification. Size optimization re­
veals that decreasing particle size of Cu@Ag NPs from 16 nm to 3 nm 
significantly improve antibacterial efficacy, with inhibition zone areas 
against E. coli and S. aureus increasing by 15 % and 38 %, respectively. 
Critically, these ultrasmall Cu@Ag NPs demonstrate superior biocom­
patibility, exhibiting hemolysis rates below safety thresholds and 
maintaining over 90 % viability of mouse fibroblast cells. Compared to 
STR method, the RPB approach achieves smaller diameter, narrower 
particle size distribution and better dispersion of Cu@Ag NPs, enhancing 
antibacterial activity against E. coli by 24 %. Moreover, the core-shell 
architecture of Cu@Ag NPs facilitates the interfacial electron transfer 
at the Ag–Cu junction and promotes the generation of •O2
−, further 
improving the antibacterial performance. When benchmarked against 
Ag NPs, Cu@Ag NPs display 12 % greater efficacy against E. coli. Upon 
incorporation into PVA, the Cu@Ag/PVA nanocomposite fibers achieve 
99.6 % and 100 % inhibition of E. coli and S. aureus, respectively, within 
5 min at 4 ppm, while delaying exponential growth phase by 4 h and 8 h. 
Notably, its antibacterial performance surpasses that of Ag/PVA nano­
composite fibers by 4 times and 3 times. At the same time, Cu@Ag/PVA 
nanocomposite fibers display superior cytocompatibility. Overall, the 
Cu@Ag core-shell strategy presents an exceptional solution for patho­

Abstract
Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection has emerged as a serious risk factor for global human health. Current 
antibiotic-based clinical eradication therapies still have many challenges, including inadequate drug permeability 
across the gastric mucus barrier, H. pylori biofilm-induced antibiotic resistance and recurrent infections, as well 
as gut dysbacteriosis. Herein, we report a probiotic-nanozyme hybrid system (LP@FeTA/CuPt), which is formed 
by coupling polyphenol-coated Lactobacillus plantarum (LP@FeTA) and CuPt nanozymes. LP@FeTA effectively 
penetrates the gastric mucus barrier via autokinetic movement, and facilitates the targeted delivery of CuPt 
nanozymes within H. pylori biofilms by “eating” extracellular polymeric substances (EPS). CuPt nanozymes respond 
to the acidic and H2O2-rich biofilm microenvironment to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and release Cu 
ions, thereby achieving multi-target eradication of biofilms by interfering with H. pylori flagellar self-assembly, 
urease activity, outer membrane function and energy metabolism. Importantly, LP@FeTA/CuPt can be degraded 
by transferrin at the infection site, allowing the released CuPt nanozymes to reactivate the antibacterial immune 
functions of macrophage, thus eliminating biofilm-escaped bacterioplankton to prevent recurrent infections. 
Notably, LP@FeTA/CuPt also modulates gut microbiota homeostasis. This study provides a promising non-antibiotic 
therapeutic strategy for H. pylori infection.

Introduction
A probiotic-nanozyme hybrid system (LP@FeTA/CuPt) is 
developed by coupling polyphenol-coated Lactobacillus 
plantarum and CuPt nanozymes for the clinical challenge 
of persistent drug-resistant H. pylori biofilm infections. 
LP@FeTA/CuPt can penetrate gastric mucus barrier, 
infiltrate and degrade biofilm. Simultaneously, LP@FeTA/
CuPt also eliminates biofilm-escaped bacterioplankton 
by reactivating the macrophages immunity to prevent 
recurrent infections, as well as modulates gut microbiota 
homeostasis. As the recognized etiologic agent of chronic 
gastritis, peptic ulcers and gastric cancer, Helicobacter 
pylori (H. pylori) infection remains a critical public health 
concern, posing a significant threat to human health [1, 
2]. According to data from the World Health Organiza­
tion (WHO), H. pylori infection is estimated to affect 
approximately 50% of the global population [3]. Antibi­
otic triple therapy and bismuth-containing quadruple 
therapy remain the primary treatment modalities [4]. 
However, their eradication rates are remarkably compro­
mised by various influencing factors. On the one hand, 
the gastric acid environment interferes with the efficacy 
of antibiotics, and gastric mucus layer hinders effective 
penetration of antibiotics at the site of H. pylori infection, 
resulting in low local drug concentrations and subopti­
mal therapeutic outcomes. On the other hand, the anti­
biotic resistance associated with H. pylori biofilms is an 
essential factor contributing to the decreased eradication 
rates [5]. Studies have indicated that the biofilm coverage 
rate in peptic ulcer patients with positive urease tests can 
be as high as 97.3% [6]. The dense biofilm structure and 
the extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) contain­
ing polysaccharides and nucleic acids exhibit inherent 
resistance and impermeability to antibiotics [7]. Simul­
taneously, the acidic and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-rich 
biofilm microenvironment inhibits macrophage immune 
responses, compromising the prompt elimination of 
bacterioplankton that escape from mature biofilms and 
subsequently resulting in recolonization and persistent 
infections [8, 9]. Additionally, antibiotic treatment has 
a non-negligible impact to gastrointestinal microbiota, 
while the microbial barrier is essential for the host’s 
defense against foreign pathogenic bacteria [10, 11]. 
Overall, there is an urgent need for developing the more 
effective and safer non-antibiotic treatment strategies for 
H. pylori infection that can penetrate the gastric mucus 
barrier, eliminate biofilms and scaped bacterioplankton, 
as well as regulate the structural homeostasis of the gut 
microbiota.
Chemodynamic therapy (CDT) is an emerging non-
antibiotic antibacterial treatment strategy that achieves 
highly efficient sterilization by utilizing Fenton/Fenton-
like metal nanocatalysts to catalyze the generation of 
highly toxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH) from H2O2 in the 
infected microenvironment, thereby reducing the likeli­
hood of inducing drug resistance [12, 13]. Copper-based 
nanocatalysts demonstrate superior advantages in anti­
bacterial applications, as they can not only eliminate 
bacteria through CDT but also induce copper overload 
within bacterial cells, thereby achieving multi-target 
eradication of bacteria [14, 15]. However, it remains elu­
sive whether a definitive conclusion can be reached on 
copper-based nanocatalysts for eradicating H. pylori bio­
films and the action mechanisms [9, 16]. In light of this 
uncertainty, we hypothesize that copper-based nano­
catalysts could effectively eradicate H. pylori biofilms, 
and a critical question persists: how could copper-based 
nanocatalysts penetrate the gastric mucus barrier and 
target the H. pylori biofilm? The development of engi­
neered probiotic delivery systems presents a promising 
approach to addressing the aforementioned issues. By 
employing chemical engineering techniques to modify 
probiotics, the engineered probiotic prepared can adapt 
to the gastrointestinal environment and effectively target 
the delivery of drugs or functional nanomaterials to the 
lesion site, thereby facilitating disease treatment [17, 18]. 
Studies have demonstrated that Lactobacillus plantarum 
colonizes the intestinal mucosa through its motility and 
adhesion properties, as well as utilize polysaccharides 
to produce organic acids, therefore inhibit the growth 
of harmful bacteria and regulate intestinal microbiota 
homeostasis [19–21]. Theoretically, Lactobacillus plan­
tarum may penetrate the gastric mucus barrier and con­
sume the polysaccharides in EPS to degrade the biofilm 
structure, which is anticipated to facilitate the targeted 
delivery of copper-based nanocatalysts within the H. 
pylori biofilms.
Herein, we designed a probiotic-nanozyme hybrid sys­
tem (LP@FeTA/CuPt) formed by coupling polyphenol-
coated Lactobacillus plantarum (LP@FeTA) and CuPt 
nanozymes (Scheme 1a), with the following advantages 
(Scheme 1b): LP@FeTA/CuPt showed resilience in the 
gastric acid (Ⅰ) and effectively penetrated the gas­
tric mucus barrier (Ⅱ). (Ⅲ) LP@FeTA/CuPt infiltrated 
into the H. pylori biofilms by degrading EPS, allow­
ing the CuPt nanozymes to catalyze the production of 
reactive oxygen species (ROS) from hydrogen perox­
ide (H2O2) within the biofilm microenvironment, while 
simultaneously releasing Cu ions. This process achieved 
multi-target eradication of H. pylori biofilms by damag­
ing flagella-related DNA, inhibiting urease activity, dis­
rupting the outer membrane function and hindering 
ATP synthesis. (Ⅳ) LP@FeTA/CuPt could be degraded 
by the elevated transferrin at the infection site, and the 
released CuPt nanozymes reactivated the antibacterial 
immune response of macrophages, thereby engulfing 
biofilm-escaped H. pylori to prevent recurrent infection. 
(Ⅴ) LP@FeTA/CuPt also improved the structure of the

This system presented a hopeful strategy for the clini­

Synthesis of CuPt Nanozyme
CuPt nanozymes were initially synthesized according to 
previously reported methods with a little modification. 
In brief, 13.09 mg Cu(acac)2, 19.67 mg Pt(acac)2, 50 mg 
Benzoic acid and 80  mg PVP were dissolved in 10 mL 
of benzyl alcohol under magnetic stirring for 15  min. 
The resulting mixture was then transferred into a 25 mL 
Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 180℃ for 12  h in 
a vacuum oven. The synthesized CuPt nanozymes were 
precipitated with acetone and washed multiple times via 
centrifugation (14000  rpm, 30  min) using a mixed sol­
vent of ethanol and acetone (1:1, v/v). Finally, the purified 
CuPt nanozymes were freeze-dried and stored at 4℃ for 
further use.
Preparation of LP@FeTA/CuPt
Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (LP) cultured overnight 
were harvested and washed three times with normal 
saline via centrifugation (4000  rpm, 3  min) to remove 
the culture medium. The bacterial pellet was then resus­
pended in 5 mL of normal saline to achieve a concentra­
tion of 1 × 109 CFUs/mL. Then, 50 µL of FeCl₃ solution 
(10 mg/mL) was added to the bacterial suspension and 
mixed thoroughly, followed by the addition of 50 µL of 
TA solution (30  mg/mL). The mixture was stirred for 
5 min to facilitate the formation of the FeTA complex on 
the bacterial surface. The resulting LP@FeTA were col­
lected by centrifugation, washed with normal saline and 
resuspended in 5 mL of normal saline. Subsequently, 
100 µL of CuPt nanozymes solution (2  mg/mL) was 
introduced into the LP@FeTA suspension and allowed 
to react for 10 min. After the reaction, free CuPt nano­
zymes were removed through centrifugation and washing 
steps, yielding the final composite LP@FeTA/CuPt. The 
obtained LP@FeTA/CuPt was lyophilized and used for 
subsequent characterization.
Gastric Acid Resistance Assessment in Vitro
LP and LP@FeTA/CuPt (1 × 109 CFUs) were separately 
suspended in 2 mL of PBS and simulated gastric fluid 
(SGF) filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, which were 
further incubated at 37℃ for 2  h with gentle shaking. 
The resulting suspensions were then washed with PBS 
and resuspended in 2 mL of fresh PBS. A 100 µL aliquot 
of each bacterial suspension was appropriately diluted, 
spread evenly onto MRS agar plates and incubated at 
37℃ for 24  h. Colony enumeration was subsequently 
performed.
Mucus Penetration Capability Evaluation in Vitro and 
Exvivo
The mucin-penetrating capabilities of LP and LP@FeTA/
CuPt were evaluated in vitro using a Transwell system 
(12-well, 8 μm ). 100 µL of mucin extracted from exvivo 
porcine stomach was added to the upper chamber, fol­
lowed by 200 µL of LP or LP@FeTA/CuPt suspension 
(1 × 109 CFUs/mL). The lower chamber was filled with 1 
mL of PBS. The system was then incubated at 37℃ with 
gentle shaking. After incubation, 100 µL of solution from 
the lower chamber was collected, appropriately diluted 
and evenly plated onto MRS agar plates, which were 
subsequently incubated at 37℃ for 24 h prior to colony 
counting. To further assess the mucin-penetrating abil­
ity of LP@FeTA/CuPt, FITC-labeled LP@FeTA/CuPt was 
co-incubated with exvivo mouse gastric tissue. Briefly, 
the mouse stomach was sectioned along the central axis 
and gently rinsed with normal saline. One half of the tis­
sue was placed in a 12-well plate and incubated with 2 
mL of FITC–LP@FeTA/CuPt solution. After incubation, 
the tissue was gently washed with PBS and subjected to 
immunofluorescence staining.
Detection of Mucoadhesion
(i) The adhesive capacity of LP and LP@FeTA/CuPt to 
gastric mucosal epithelial cells (GES-1) was investigated. 
Thawed GES-1 cells were cultured in 1640 medium sup­
plemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicil­
lin-streptomycin for 24  h. GES-1 cells were seeded in 
24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and 
incubated overnight until adherence. Subsequently, 10 
µL of Hoechst 33,342 and 10 µL of DiD fluorescent mem­
brane dye were added and incubated for 15 min. Then, 
100 µL of FITC-labeled LP or LP@FeTA/CuPt suspen­
sion (1 × 106 CFU/mL) was introduced into the cells and 
incubated for 1  h in the dark. Imaging was performed 
using an inverted fluorescence microscope. (ii) The in 
vivo adhesion and retention capacity of LP@FeTA/CuPt 
was further evaluated. Mice fasted for 12 h were orally 
administered with 200 µL of Cy5-labeled LP or LP@
FeTA/CuPt (1 × 108 CFUs/mL). After 5  h, the stomachs 
were excised and subjected to imaging using a small ani­
mal imaging system.

Brucella agar plates supplemented with 7% sheep blood 
and 0.5% antibiotic mixture. The plates were then incu­
bated in a tri-gas incubator under microaerophilic con­
ditions (5% O2, 10% CO2, 37 °C) for 72 h. The cultivated 
H. pylori were harvested using an inoculating loop and 
resuspended in Brucella broth to achieve a density of 
1 × 109 CFUs/mL. 1 mL of the bacterial suspension was 
transferred into a 24-well plate and further cultured for

polarization status.
Phagocytic capability of macrophages
RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells 
per well in a 6-well plate containing coverslips and cul­
tured for 24  h. The culture medium was then replaced 
with fresh medium containing CuPt nanozymes, fol­
lowed by an additional 12 h of incubation. Subsequently, 
the cells were infected with H. pylori at a multiplicity of 
infection (MOI) of 100 for 6 h. To eliminate extracellu­
lar bacteria, the cells were further incubated for 2 h in 
medium containing gentamicin (100 µg/mL). Afterward, 
the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and per­
meabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 30 min. The cover­
slips were then incubated with 50 µL of primary antibody 
against H. pylori at 4℃ overnight. Following washing 
with PBST, the samples were treated with 50 µL of fluo­
rescent secondary antibody and incubated for 60 min in 
the dark. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI, and 
the samples were examined under a CLSM.
Evaluation of H. pylori infection treatment efficacy in Vivo
Animal experiments were conducted in accordance 
with the guidelines and approved by the ethics commit­
tee of Nanchang University (Approval number: NCU­
LAE-20221228028). C57BL/6 mice were purchased from 
GemPharmatech. C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided 
into five groups: Health, PBS, LP, LP@FeTA/CuPt and 
OAC (omeprazole + amoxicillin + clarithromycin). Mice 
in the Health group were gavaged with Brucella broth, 
while mice in the remaining four groups received intra­
gastric administration of 200 µL H. pylori suspension 
(1 × 109 CFUs/mL) every two days for two weeks. After 
four-week infection, H. pylori colonization in gastric tis­
sues was assessed using a H. pylori Giemsa staining kit. 
Subsequently, the corresponding oral treatments were 
given, and fresh feces of mice were collected for 16  S 
rRNA sequencing (Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Tech­
nology Co., Ltd.). Two days following the final adminis­
tration, the mice were euthanized. Gastric tissues were 
harvested and divided into three portions for subsequent 
analyses. The first portion was homogenized and resus­
pended in PBS, and the bacterial load of H. pylori in the 
stomach was quantified using the plate colony-counting 
method. The second portion was allocated for qPCR and 
western blot analysis. The third portion was fixed in for­
malin, followed by dehydration and paraffin embedding. 
Sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared using a micro­
tome and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) 
staining as well as immunohistochemical analysis.
Statistical analysis
All experiments data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0, 
evaluated as mean ± standard deviation based on at least 
three tests, and contrasted via One-way ANOVA. Aprob­
ability value (p-value) of < 0.05 was considered statisti­
cally significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001).
Result and discussion
Synthesis and characterization of LP@FeTA/CuPt
Considering the motility of Lactobacillus plantarum (LP) 
and its capacity to modulate the gut microbiota, it was 

Page 7 of 19
Zhang et al. Journal of Nanobiotechnology          (2026) 24:423 
selected as the delivery vehicle for the targeted transport 
of CuPt nanozymes into H. pylori biofilms. To address 
the detrimental effects of the gastric acidic environ­
ment on LP viability, a protective polyphenol coating was 
first self-assembled on its surface via the coordination 
between ferric (Fe) ions and tannic acid (TA), resulting in 
the preparation of LP@FeTA. LP was labeled with FITC, 
while the polyphenol coating was tagged using Cy5-con­
jugated TA. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) 
images demonstrated that the surface of LP (green chan­
nel) was coated with a polyphenol network (red channel) 
(Fig. 1a). To enhance the eradication efficacy against H. 
pylori biofilms, the LP@FeTA was further modified with 
CuPt nanozymes. Initially, CuPt nanozymes were syn­
thesized according to a modified version of a previously 
established method [22]. The as-prepared CuPt nano­
zymes exhibited a monodisperse spherical morphology 
with an average size of 5.8 nm (Figure S1). Subsequently, 
positively charged CuPt nanozymes (6.72 ± 1.00 mV) 
was immobilized onto the negatively charged LP@FeTA 
surface (− 14.27 ± 0.78 mV) via electrostatic adsorption, 
yielding LP@FeTA/CuPt (Fig. 1b). Transmission elec­
tron microscopy (TEM) images revealed a clearly vis­
ible additional layer on LP@FeTA/CuPt compared with 
the unmodified LP, and the higher-magnification TEM 
images confirmed the presence of adsorbed CuPt nano­

Furthermore, elemental mapping analysis of Fe, Cu, and 
Pt in LP@FeTA/CuPt confirmed the presence of the 
polyphenol coating and CuPt nanozymes (Fig. 1d). The 
ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectrum of LP@
FeTA/CuPt exhibited characteristic peaks of LP at 264.5 
nm and FeTA at 336.5 nm (Fig. 1e). Fourier transform 
infrared (FTIR) spectra displayed stretching vibration 
peaks at 1520 cm− 1 and 1732 cm− 1, corresponding to 
the aromatic C = C bonds and carbonyl groups of TA, 
respectively, indicating successful encapsulation of the 
polyphenol coating (Fig. 1f). X-ray diffraction (XRD) pat­
terns revealed that the crystalline peaks of the synthe­
sized CuPt nanozymes matched the standard reference 
[23], and characteristic peaks of CuPt nanozymes were 
also observed in the lyophilized LP@FeTA/CuPt, con­
firming the adsorption of CuPt nanozymes onto the LP@
FeTA surface (Fig. 1g). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy 
(XPS) analysis further verified the existence of Cu, Pt and 
Fe elements in LP@FeTA/CuPt (Fig. 1h). High-resolution 
XPS spectra of Cu 2p and Pt 4f were shown in Fig. 1i and 
j, the Cu 2p1/2 peak (950.48 eV) and Cu 2p3/2 peak (930.78 
eV) each split into two peaks, suggesting the coexistence 
of Cu(0) and Cu(II) species. Similarly, the Pt 4f7/2 peak 
(70.18 eV) and Pt 4f5/2 peak (73.38 eV) also split into two 
peaks, demonstrating the presence of both Pt(0) and 
Pt(II) states [24]. The above results indicated the success­
ful preparation of LP@FeTA/CuPt.
Gastric acid resistance, mucus penetration and 
mucoadhesion of LP@FeTA/CuPt
 H. pylori typically colonizes beneath the mucus layer 
[25]. The gastric acid environment and mucus layer bar­
riers significantly affect the efficacy of clinical antibiotic 
therapies. Therefore, the LP@FeTA/CuPt must withstand 
gastric acid, penetrate the mucus layer and adhere to 
the gastric mucosa (Fig. 2a). The gastric acid tolerance 
of LP@FeTA/CuPt was first evaluated via plate colony-
counting method, and the results indicated no signifi­
cant difference in colony forming units (CFUs) between 
LP and LP@FeTA/CuPt in PBS. However, the CFUs of 
LP@FeTA/CuPt were obviously higher than those of LP 
in simulated gastric fluid (SGF), demonstrating that the 
polyphenol coating and CuPt nanozymes modification 
did not adversely affect LP viability while enhancing its 
acid resistance (Figs. 2b and S3). Then, we validated the 
mucus penetration capacity of LP@FeTA/CuPt using a 
Transwell system (Fig. 2c). As illustrated in Fig. 2d, both 
LP and LP@FeTA/CuPt could traverse the mucus layer 
into the lower chamber, while the CFUs of LP@FeTA/
CuPt were slightly reduced compared with LP, poten­
tially due to the polyphenol coating-induced enhance­
ment of mucus adhesion and concomitant impairment 
of LP motility. Further, FITC-labeled LP@FeTA/CuPt 
was co-incubated with exvivo gastric tissue, and fluo­
rescence staining of gastric tissue exhibited green fluo­
rescence within the mucus layer, which also verified the 
mucus-penetrating ability of LP@FeTA/CuPt (Fig. 2e). 
Finally, the mucoadhesion capacity of LP@FeTA/CuPt 
was evaluated, FITC-labeled LP and LP@FeTA/CuPt 
were co-cultured with gastric mucosal epithelial cells 
(GES-1). Fluorescence imaging revealed stronger muco­
sal adhesion of LP@FeTA/CuPt compared with LP (Fig. 
2f). In vivo fluorescence imaging and quantitative analy­
sis further confirmed the enhanced retention capacity of 
LP@FeTA/CuPt in murine gastric mucosa relative to LP 
(Fig. 2g and h). This enhanced adhesion might be attrib­
uted to non-covalent interactions (e.g., hydrogen bond­
ing) between phenolic hydroxyl groups in the polyphenol 
coating and active groups on the gastric mucosal surface 
[26].

Mechanism of H. pylori biofilm eradication by LP@FeTA/
CuPt
To further investigate the potential mechanism of LP@
FeTA/CuPt groups in eradicating H. pylori biofilms, RNA 
sequencing (RNA-seq) based prokaryotic transcrip­
tomics was performed. The biofilm gene expression pro­
files of the Control and LP@FeTA/CuPt were analyzed. 
Violin plots depicting gene expression distribution and 
heatmaps illustrating inter-sample correlation indicated 
relatively uniform gene quantities and strong correla­
tions between samples within each group (Figures S9a 
and S9b). As shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 4a), LP@
FeTA/CuPt treatment induced 208 significantly differ­
entially expressed genes (DEGs) of H. pylori compared 
with the Control group, comprising 107 upregulated and 
101 downregulated genes. These DEGs underwent Gene 
Ontology (GO) annotation analysis, revealing that LP@
FeTA/CuPt significantly enriched genes primarily associ­
ated with cellular processes, cellular anatomical entities 
and catalytic activity (Fig. 4b). Kyoto Encyclopedia of 
Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was 
proceeded to evaluate functional pathway alterations in 
H. pylori following LP@FeTA/CuPt treatment. Bubble 
plots demonstrated that LP@FeTA/CuPt significantly 
suppressed H. pylori pathways related to lipopolysaccha­
ride biosynthesis, oxidative phosphorylation, pyruvate 
metabolism and flagellar assembly (Fig. 4c). This sup­
pression was primarily attributed to ROS and Cu ions 
released by LP@FeTA/CuPt within the biofilm microen­
vironment. ROS impair H. pylori outer membrane func­
tion by disrupting lipopolysaccharide synthesis [30], 
which can lead to an influx of Cu ions into the H. pylori 
cells. Cu ions likely inhibit ATP synthesis by interfering 
with oxidative phosphorylation and pyruvate metabo­
lism [31]. Simultaneously, Cu ions also competitively 
bind to the active site of nickel-dependent enzymes, 
thereby compromising urease activity. Urease is critical 
for H. pylori survival and colonization under acidic con­
ditions [32]. Additionally, ROS probably damage flagel­
lum-associated DNA, given that flagella are involved in 
H. pylori biofilms assembly and contribute to maintain­
ing its structural integrity [33]. Subsequent functional 
classification of DEGs and the corresponding heatmap 
following LP@FeTA/CuPt treatment were presented in 
Figs. 4d and S10, the results showed that genes associ­
ated with outer membrane function, ATP generation and 
flagellum assembly were significantly downregulated, 
whereas genes related to metal ion transport were mark­
edly upregulated.
We further validated the potential mechanism derived 
from the aforementioned prokaryotic transcriptome 
analysis. H. pylori outer membrane permeability was 
assessed using the fluorescent probe N-phenyl-1-naph­
thylamine (NPN). As illustrated in Fig. 4e, the LP@FeTA/
CuPt group exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity, 
indicating a significant increase in membrane perme­
ability. Consistent result was obtained from the protein 
leakage assay (Figure S11). Resazurin assay results dem­
onstrated that LP@FeTA/CuPt significantly reduced the 
metabolic activity of H. pylori within biofilms, which 
could be primarily attributed to Cu ions interfering with 
intracellular ATP synthesis (Fig.  4f). Urease activity 
assays revealed that LP@FeTA/CuPt markedly reduced 
H. pylori urease activity, which likely resulting from Cu 
ions interference with nickel transport Fig. 4g). Further­
more, bacterial ROS detection showed that LP@FeTA/
CuPt treatment increased intracellular ROS levels in H. 
pylori, which led to flagella-associated genes damage, 
consequently inhibiting biofilm formation by disrupt­
ing the flagellar self-assembly process (Fig. 4h). Through 
integrated transcriptomic sequencing and validation data 
analysis, the mechanism of LP@FeTA/CuPt action on H. 
pylori biofilms was comprehensively elucidated in Fig. 4i. 
LP@FeTA/CuPt responded to biofilm microenvironment 
to generate ROS and release Cu ions, thereby achieving 
multi-target eradication of H. pylori biofilm. These multi-
target effects include: (1) disrupting the outer membrane 
structure of H. pylori; (2) inhibiting ATP synthesis by 
interfering with oxidative phosphorylation and pyruvate 
metabolism; (3) reducing urease activity by impairing 
nickel transport; and (4) suppressing flagellar self-assem­
bly by damaging flagella-associated genes.

Gut microbiota regulation study of LP@FeTA/CuPt
 H. pylori infection influences the structure of the gas­
trointestinal microbiota, potentially leading to the devel­
opment of gastrointestinal diseases [38, 39]. Antibiotic 
therapy represented by OAC frequently disrupts intesti­
nal microbial balance [40]. We investigated the modula­
tory effects of LP@FeTA/CuPt on the gut microbiota in 
H. pylori infected mice. Alpha diversity analysis assessed 
the richness and diversity of microbial communities in 
murine fecal samples. Results from the Chao1 and Ace 
indices revealed a significant reduction in microbial rich­
ness in the PBS and OAC groups compared with the 
Health group, indicating substantial impacts of both H. 
pylori infection and OAC treatment on gut microbiota 
structure. In contrast, microbial richness was restored in 
the LP and LP@FeTA/CuPt groups (Figs. 7a and S13a). 
Shannon and Simpson index results demonstrated sig­
nificantly reduced microbial diversity in the OAC group, 
while LP@FeTA/CuPt treatment effectively restored 
diversity (Figs. 7b and S13b). Beta diversity analysis eval­
uated structural changes in microbial communities. As 
shown in Figs. 7c and S13c, the OAC group exhibited 
distinct separation from the Health group in multidimen­
sional space, whereas the LP@FeTA/CuPt group clus­
tered closely, indicating its ability to maintain microbial 
community structure.
Subsequent in-depth analysis of microbial composition 
revealed that LP@FeTA/CuPt modulated the abundance 
of Bacteroidota. Conversely, OAC treatment significantly 
reduced Bacteroidota while increasing Proteobacteria 
and Verrucomicrobiota (Fig. 7d) [41]. Heatmap and venn 
diagrams analyses of community composition at the fam­
ily level showed species counts in the LP and LP@FeTA/
CuPt groups comparable with the Health group, while 
the OAC group exhibited markedly reduced species 
numbers (Figs. 7e and S14a). Analysis of relative micro­
bial abundance at the genus level indicated that LP@
FeTA/CuPt modulated genera critical for maintaining 
gut microbiota equilibrium, including Muribaculaceae, 
Lactobacillus, and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136 (Fig. 7f) 
[42]. Additionally, linear discriminant analysis effect size 
(LEfSe) confirmed LP@FeTA/CuPt also regulated gut 
microbial structure, characterized by significant enrich­
ment of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group (Figure S14b). 
The comprehensive analysis of the microorganisms 16 S 
sequencing results revealed that LP@FeTA/CuPt could 
enhance intestinal microbial diversity, improve gut 
microbiota structure and maintain microbial homeosta­
sis in mice.
Biosafety assessment of LP@FeTA/CuPt
Finally, we evaluated the biocompatibility of LP@FeTA/
CuPt both in vitro and in vivo. Hemolysis assays revealed 
no significant hemolysis in red blood cells treated with 
LP@FeTA/CuPt (Figure S15a). We co-cultured gastric 
mucosal epithelial cells with varying concentrations of 
LP@FeTA/CuPt. As shown in Figure S15b, high concen­
trations of LP@FeTA/Pt induced a mild reduction in cell 
viability, likely attributable to trace amounts of organic 
acids generated by the material. Live/dead cell fluores­
cence staining yielded similar findings (Figure S15c). 
Furthermore, H&E staining of major organs (heart, 
liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) from mice subjected 
to different treatments demonstrated that LP@FeTA/
CuPt administration caused no observable pathological 
alterations in these tissues (Figure S16). We determined 
the copper ion metabolism in major organs (liver, kid­
ney) of mice using ICP-MS. As shown in Figure S17, the 
metal ions in LP@FeTA/CuPt were primarily metabo­
lized through the liver and kidneys. Collectively, these 
results indicated that LP@FeTA/CuPt exhibits favorable 
biocompatibility.
Conclusion
This study developed a probiotic-nanozyme hybrid sys­
tem, designated LP@FeTA/CuPt, and demonstrated its 
potential as a therapeutic agent against H. pylori infec­
tions for the first time. The prepared LP@FeTA/CuPt 
effectively penetrates the gastric mucus barrier by uto­
kinetic movement, and infiltrates the biofilm interior 
via targeted degradation of EPS. Then, LP@FeTA/CuPt 
responds to the biofilm microenvironment to generate 
ROS and release Cu ions, thereby achieving multi-target 
eradication of H. pylori biofilms. Moreover, LP@FeTA/
CuPt can be degraded by transferrin at the infection site, 
allowing the released CuPt nanozymes to reactivate the 
antibacterial immune functions of macrophages, thus 
eliminating biofilm-escaped bacterioplankton to prevent 
recurrent infections. In vivo experimental results demon­
strate that LP@FeTA/CuPt exhibits efficacy comparable 
to triple therapy in eradicating H. pylori while also con­
ferring beneficial regulatory effects on the gut microbi­
ota. In summary, this work provides novel insights and a

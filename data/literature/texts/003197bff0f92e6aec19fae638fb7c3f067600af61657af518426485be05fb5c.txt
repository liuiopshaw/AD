Ultrasmall Gold Nanocluster Based Antibacterial Nanoaggregates
for Infectious Wound Healing
Aswathy Ravindran Girija+,[a] Sivakumar Balasubramanian+,[b] Richard Bright,[b]
Allison J. Cowin,[a] Nirmal Goswami,*[b] and Krasimir Vasilev*[a, b]
Abstract: Wound infections are a growing challenge and
significantly delay the healing process. To overcome this
challenge, nanotechnology-inspired treatments have recently
emerged as promising alternatives to conventional drugs and
antibiotics. Here, we introduce a new type of antimicrobial
nanoaggregate that integrates the intrinsic properties of
ultra-small gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) with a biocompatible
antimicrobial polymer, namely, chitosan (CS). The synergetic
combination created from the AuNCs and CS in the nano-
aggregates resulted in superior antimicrobial activity against
Gram-negative Escherichia coli and Gram-positive Staphylo-
coccus aureus bacterial strains, when compared with their
individual components. Furthermore, the nanoaggregates
also facilitate rapid wound healing without exhibiting any
cytotoxic effects in mammalian cells. These nanoaggregates
may become a potent component of innovative wound
healing therapies.
Introduction
Wound healing is a complex, tightly orchestrated process
comprising the balanced integration of various tissue and cell
types where the major steps include haemostasis, inflammation,
proliferation and remodelling.[1,2] Amongst the various chal-
lenges associated with wound healing, the incidence of
contamination of multi-antibiotic-resistant bacteria is a major
challenge.[3] The consequences could be devastating, leading to
chronic and recurrent infections which significantly hinder the
healing process. Such infections are generally treated by
employing
surgical
techniques
and/or
the
application
of
prophylactic antibiotic medications.[4] However, the complica-
tion of surgery together with the rise in antimicrobial resistance
hinders the overall therapeutic outcome and at the same time
highlights the importance of discovering new alternative
antimicrobial agents which can resolve the magnitude of the
clinical challenge concerned with infected wounds.
In this context, nanomaterial based therapeutic agents are
attractive due to their unique physiochemical properties.
Typically, they offer a greater number of functional sites
compared to commercially available drugs/antibiotics which
enhance the interaction with a given microbe. In the past few
years, several antimicrobial nanomaterials have been developed
which are proven to be highly effective against various multi-
drug resistant bacteria triggered wound infection.[5–8] The
efficacy of these nanomaterials can be further improved by
reducing their size.[9–11] However, the inherent toxicity associ-
ated with these nanomaterials at the ultra-small size regime
raises safety concern and limits their clinical acceptance.[12]
The incorporation of ultra-small nanoparticles in a cyto-
friendly antibacterial dressing could be an excellent strategy to
inhibit bacteria-associated wound infections as well as to
prevent the adverse side effects arising from those nano-
particles (NPs). An ideal wound dressing material should have
high antimicrobial efficacy against a wide range of multi-drug
resistant bacteria, good biocompatibility and biodegradability,
and low cytotoxicity. In fact, the development of ultra-fine NPs
which are inert and highly stable, yet, have the capacity to
eradicate bacteria, followed by their subsequent incorporation
into
the
wound
dressing
material
could
be
a
strategic
advantage. Gold (Au) is an ideal material which does not
dissociate easily into ions as in the case of silver, which often
leads to toxicity in mammalian cells.[13] In fact, few previous
studies have reported the wound healing activity and anti-
microbial property of large sized AuNPs (3–100 nm).[14–17] These
AuNPs facilitated healing of infectious wound by several
proposed mechanisms including (a) by altering the membrane
potential of bacterial cells, resulting in the depletion of ATP,
eventually disrupting the metabolism and death of bacterial
cells,[18] (b) through the generation of reactive oxygen species
(ROS),[19] and (c) by regulating anti-inflammatory cytokines and
supporting angiogenesis.[20–22] Furthermore, the combination of
AuNPs with polymers resulted in substantial increase in the
wound healing activity.[23]
The purpose of this work is to explore the potential in
infected would healing of AuNPs of ultra-small size i.e., below
3 nm.[24,25] In this size regime, these NPs are often known as
nanoclusters (NCs). AuNCs exhibit several molecular like proper-
ties
such
as

To
achieve
this
goal,
we
developed
nanoaggregates
composed of ultra-small thiolate ligand protected AuNCs and
the biocompatible antibacterial polymer, chitosan (CS). CS has
been widely used as a wound dressing material owing to
several beneficial features. For instance, CS itself exhibits
antibacterial behaviour and has a low cytotoxicity toward
mammalian cells.[32] The mechanism of antibacterial property of
CS stems from its physicochemical properties.[32,33] The widely
accepted antimicrobial mechanism is the electrostatic interac-
tion between the polycationic CS (below its pKa value) and
negatively charged macromolecules at the bacterial cell surface
resulting in the changes in permeability of the membrane,
disrupting osmotic balance and eventually inhibiting the micro-
bial growth.[32] Another mechanism supports the penetration of
CS to the nuclei of microbe, interaction with microbial DNA and
subsequent inhibition of mRNA and protein synthesis resulting
in the antimicrobial effect.[32] We found that the combination of
the physiochemical properties of CS with the intrinsic nanoscale
properties of AuNCs in the nanoaggregates could not only
produce a synergistic antibacterial effect but also promote
wound healing without generating any cytotoxicity to the
mammalian cells.
Results and Discussion
Here, we chose mercaptosuccinic acid (MSA) as thiol ligands for
the synthesis of ultra-small AuNCs. These MSA-protected AuNCs
(will be referred as AuNCs@MSA henceforth) were prepared
using sodium borohydride (NaBH4) as reducing agent (details of
the synthetic procedure can be found in the experimental
section). A key strategy for our synthesis method is to introduce
small amount of sodium hydroxide in the NaBH4 solution, which
slows down the hydrolysis of NaBH4 and thus provides a mild
reduction kinetics. The purified NCs were first analysed by UV-
vis spectroscopy. As shown in Figure 1a, the purified AuNCs@M-
SA displayed a featureless spectrum, suggesting the presence
of small sized NCs. The lack of surface plasmon peak in the UV-
vis spectrum also ruled out the possibility of the presence of
large sized AuNPs. The colour of the AuNCs@MSA was reddish
brown under ambient light (inset of Figure 1a). Furthermore,
our transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed
that the as-synthesized AuNCs@MSA were below 2 nm in size
(Figure 1b & inset). All the above results clearly indicate that our
NaBH4 mediated synthesis protocol successfully resulted in the
formation of ultra-small AuNCs.
Following the successful synthesis of AuNCs@MSA, we
introduced them to the CS solution (pH~6) for the fabrication
of
antibacterial
nanoaggregates.
We
anticipated that
the
negatively charge carboxylate groups of the MSA ligands in
AuNCs will be counterbalanced by the positively charged amine
groups of CS, and subsequently spatially confine and self-
assemble to form AuNCs/CS nanoaggregates due to the electro-
static attraction between these two oppositely charged materi-
als (Figure 2a). We maintained pH of ~6 during the reaction,
based on our previous work where we reported the synthesis of
AuNCs impregnated nanogels.[34]
To verify our hypothesis, we performed dynamic light
scattering (DLS) and TEM studies. Both DLS and TEM are known
to be the primary tools for the characterization of NPs, nanogels
or nanoaggregates. The DLS spectrum exhibited a single peak
indicating an average hydrodynamic diameter of the AuNCs
containing nanoaggregates of 55 nm, (Figure 2e). Another peak
around ~365 nm could be due to slight aggregation of these
nanoaggregates in water, which may originate due to their
positively charged surface (zeta potential is 14.9 mV; Figure S1).
Since the zeta potential of purified AuNCs was   31.8 mV
(Figure S1), the resulted positive zeta potential of nanoaggre-
gates indicated the presence of CS on the outer surface of
nanoaggregates. We have also verified the stability of the
nanoaggregates in water, phosphate buffer saline (PBS) and in
cell media (DMEM). Both the size and the surface charge
remained unchanged (Figure S1&S2) even after one day storage
in water, indicating their high stability in practical solution. We
observed a slight reduction in size for the nanoaggregates in

their highly negative zeta potential value), we also anticipate
ROS dependent antibacterial activity of the AuNCs/CS nano-
aggregates against various bacteria used in our study.
To ensure the safe application of our nanoaggregates, we
first investigated their cytotoxic effect on mammalian cells.
Mammalian cells treated with either AuNCs or CS were used for
comparison and tissue culture plate was used as positive
control. We treated mouse fibroblast cell line (3T3) with three
different dilutions of treatments (10:90, 20:80 and 30:70 of CS;
AuNCs; and AuNCs/CS: culture media) up to 48 h. From our
experimental results, both concentration and time dependent
proliferation patterns can be observed for AuNCs (Figure 4a).
No significant difference in the cell viability could be observed
between the nanoaggregates and/or CS (up to the highest
concentration used in our study) and that of the control.
However, there was a slight reduction in viability at higher
concentration of AuNCs which, upon the formation of nano-
aggregates, was significantly improved.
To verify the synergistic effect of nanoaggregates in wound
healing, monolayer scratch assay was performed (see exper-
imental section for details). In this assay, wound treated with
AuNCs/CS was completely healed between 24-36 h, while the
treatment of CS had no significant model wound closure effect
compared to the control (Figure 4b–c). The wound remained
open even by 48 h (Figure 4b) in the case of AuNCs, which also
correlates with the proliferation studies where we noticed
apoptotic type cellular behaviour that might have instigated by
generated ROS, leading to the slowing of cellular migration.
Based on our results, we suggest that the synergistic effect
of AuNCs/CS has a significant role both in cellular proliferation
and promotes wound healing in a model assay. The fact that
even the highest concentration of AuNCs/CS used in our study
did not adversely affect the cellular migration and proliferation
signifies their potential in wound healing as it resulted in rapid

Conclusion
In conclusion, we report antibacterial nanoaggregates based on
the self-assembly of ultra-small AuNCs and chitosan. The
resulting nanoaggregates show enhanced antibacterial activity
against both Gram-negative Escherichia coli and Gram-positive
Staphylococcus aureus bacterial strains compared to individual
components i.e., AuNCs and CS. Importantly, the nanoaggre-
gates were found to not affect the viability of mammalian cells.
Furthermore, compared to the individual components, these
nanoaggregates showed better in vitro wound healing proper-
ties. The present study not only demonstrated a strategy to
design and develop antimicrobial nanoaggregates for treatment
of infected wound but it also provides pathways to rationally
combining other ligands, nanomaterials and/or drugs that will
benefit patients and improve medical outcomes.
Experimental Section
Materials: Hydrogen tetrachloroaurate (HAuCl4·xH2O), MSA, and
chitosan (medium molecular weight) from Sigma Aldrich; sodium
hydroxide (NaOH), hydrochloric acid from Merck were used as
received without further purification. All aqueous solutions were
prepared with ultrapure Millipore water.
Instrumentation: Solution pH was monitored by a pH meter from
SmartCHEM, TPS Australia. UV-vis absorbance spectra were re-
corded on a Shimadzu spectrophotometer. TEM images were
obtained using a JEOL-2100F microscope operating at an accelerat-
ing voltage of 200kV. DLS and zeta potential measurements were
recorded on Malvern-Zetasizer Nano ZS.
Synthesis of MSA Protected AuNCs: In a typical synthesis, HAuCl4
(20 mM, 2.5 mL) and MSA (5 mM, 20 mL) were mixed in water
(23.5 mL), leading to the formation of Au(I)-MSA complexes. An
aqueous NaOH solution (1 M, 3 mL) was then introduced into the
reaction mixture, followed by the addition of 1 mL of NaBH4
solution (prepared by dissolving 43 mg of NaBH4 powder in 10 mL
of 0.2 M NaOH solution). After 3 h, the solution colour changed to
deep brown indicating the formation of MSA protected AuNCs.
Finally, the NCs solution was purified by dialysis (Pur-A-Lyzer™
Maxi Dialysis Kit; MWCO 3.5 kDa) and used for further character-
ization. The concentration of the purified AuNCs was 150 μg/mL
(on the basis of gold atoms).
Synthesis of Nanoaggregates: Chitosan solution (2 ml, 5 mg/mL),
MilliQ water (4 mL) and as synthesised AuNCs (4 mL) were mixed in
a vial and the pH of the reaction mixture was adjusted to ~6. The
reaction mixture was allowed to stir for1 h at 50° C. The synthesized
chitosan coated nanoaggregate sample was purified by dialysis and
used for further studies.
Antibacterial Study: To evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of
AuNCs/CS, we tested against gram-negative (Escherichia coli) and
gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus). Briefly, E. coli and S.
aureus were inoculated into 5 ml of tryptic soy broth (TSB, Thermo
Fisher, MA USA) overnight at 37°C. The overnight cultures of E. coli
and S aureus were diluted to approximately 5×107CFU/ml in TSB.
Four separate incubations were setup for each bacterium; 900 μl of
5×107CFU/ml culture and 100 μl of either AuNCs, CS, AuNCs/CS or
PBS control, then incubated for 3 hours at 37°C. Bacteria were
stained using using Baclight™Bacterial Viability Kit (Molecular
Probes, ThermoFisher). Equal amounts of the dye mixture (3.34 mM
SYTO/20 mM Propidium iodide) were mixed together, and 2 μl
were added per sample, and incubated at 25°C in the dark.
Samples were then centrifuged and washed with PBS twice, and
resuspend in 50 μl of phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Cells were
mounted between a glass slide and coverslip, then imaged using
Olympus FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope. The excita-
tion/emission maxima for STYO9 480/500 nm and propidium iodide
490/635 nm. Viability was expressed as a percentage of live cells
counted in three separate 500×500 pixel areas (MeanSD). Green
or red fluorescence indicates live or dead/compromised cells
respectively.
Cell Culture: 3T3 cells (Mouse fibroblast cells) were maintained in
Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium, DMEM (Life Technologies,
Australia), supplemented with 10% foetal calf serum (Sigma-Aldrich,
Australia) supplemented with penicillin/ streptomycin (100 U/ml)
(Life Technologies, Australia) in humidified 5% CO2, 95% air
atmosphere at 37°C. For experimental purposes, 3T3 cells were
grown in plated in 96-well plates (for the proliferation assay) and in
image lock 96 well plates for scratch wound.
Cell Proliferation Assay: Cell proliferation assays were performed
with metabolic substrate WST-1 according to manufacturer’s
protocols (Roche Applied Science, Penzberg, Germany). Briefly,
10 μL of WST-1 reagent was added to the cells incubated with
different concentrations of treatments and left at 37°C for 30 min.
The presence of the formazan product was quantified using a dual
absorbance of 460 and 620 nm using a BMG FLUOstar OPTIMA
Microplate Reader.
Scratch Assay: We performed monolayer scratch assay using the
IncuCyte ZOOM™live cell imaging system (Essen BioScience, MI
USA) that measures scratch closure in real time and calculates
automatically relative wound density within the initially-vacant/
wound area at different time point. The relative wound density
represented as the ratio of the occupied area to the total area of
the original wound region. Cells were cultured in DMEM medium
(Life Technologies, Australia) in 10% foetal calf serum (Sigma-
Aldrich, Australia), with 100 u/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomy-
cin (Life Technologies), in plastic flasks (Corning Life Sciences, Asia
Pacific) in 5% CO2 and air at 37°C. Cells are removed from the
monolayer using Trypsin (Life Technologies) in phosphate buffered
saline, resuspended in medium and seeded at a density of 25,000
cells per well in 96-well Image Lock plates (Essen BioScience). After
seeding, cells are grown overnight to form a uniform monolayer.
We used a WoundMaker™(Essen BioScience) to create uniform
scratches in all the wells of a 96-well plate. After creating the
scratch, the medium was aspirated and the wells washed twice
with PBS to remove any cells from the scratched area. Following
the washes, different dilutions of treatments in growth medium (CS

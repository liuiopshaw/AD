Crystal structure of a NIR-Emitting DNA-Stabilized Ag16 Nanocluster
Cecilia Cerretani, Hiroki Kanazawa, Tom Vosch,* and Jiro Kondo*
Abstract: DNA has been used as a scaffold to stabilize small,
atomically monodisperse silver nanoclusters, which have
attracted attention due to their intriguing photophysical
properties. Herein, we describe the X-ray crystal structure of
a DNA-encapsulated, near-infrared emitting Ag16 nanocluster
(DNA–Ag16NC). The asymmetric unit of the crystal contains
two DNA–Ag16NCs and the crystal packing between the DNA–
Ag16NCs is promoted by several interactions, such as two
silver-mediated base pairs between 3’-terminal adenines, two
phosphate–Ca2+–phosphate
interactions,
and
p-stacking
between two neighboring thymines. Each Ag16NC is confined
by two DNA decamers that take on a horse-shoe-like
conformation and is almost fully shielded from the solvent
environment. This structural insight will aid in the determi-
nation of the structure/photophysical property relationship for
this class of emitters and opens up new research opportunities
in fluorescence imaging and sensing using noble-metal clusters.
DNA oligomers allow the creation of atomically monodis-
perse silver nanoclusters (DNA–AgNCs), usually below 30
Ag atoms that, depending on the DNA sequence, can result in
bright emitters.[1] Since their introduction in 2004,[2] attempts
have been made to characterize the exact composition and
structure of these DNA–AgNCs using techniques such as
mass-spectroscopy,[1a] extended X-ray absorption fine struc-
ture (EXAFS),[3] X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS),[4]
and more recently the first atomic structure information
appeared using X-ray diffraction.[5] In the study of Huard
et al.,[5] the cationic silver–DNA precursor was first crystal-
lized and then chemically reduced. This crystal structure
contains a silver cluster with novel Ag–Ag interactions and
a segment with cationic silver-mediated base-pair interac-
tions, similar to a previously reported silver–DNA hybrid
nanowire.[6] Here, we obtained the crystal structure of
a HPLC-purified magenta-colored near-infrared (NIR)-emit-
ting DNA–AgNC that was formed in solution before crystal-
lization.
AgNCs are known for their remarkable fluorescence
properties in comparison to other noble-metal clusters,[7] and
DNA–AgNCs have so far primarily found applications as
fluorescence-based sensors.[8] Given the increasing interest in
thiol ligands for stabilizing monodisperse noble-metal nano-
clusters,[9] we present here a DNA sequence that is a biocom-
patible water-soluble alternative scaffold for creating an
Ag16NC.
The NIR-emitting DNA–Ag16NC was formed in solution
by two DNA decamer fragments 5’-CACCTAGCGA-3’,
purified by HPLC and then crystallized by the hanging-drop
vapor-diffusion method in an environment containing 3-(N-
morpholino)propanesulfonic
acid
(pH 7),
spermine,

used machine learning tools for prediction of sequences that
stabilize fluorescent AgNCs with designed colors.[1c] In order
to verify that the crystal structure of the DNA–Ag16NC did
not dramatically differ from the solution structure, fluores-

Having established that we crystallized the solution-state
emitter, we proceeded with crystal-structure determination.
X-ray data was collected at 100 K by using a X-ray beam with
l = 2.0 c, which is the longest wavelength available at the
beamline, in order to obtain a strong anomalous signal of the
silver atoms. The measured crystal belongs to the space group
P21 and contains two DNA–Ag16NCs in its asymmetric unit.
The initial phase was determined by the single-wavelength
anomalous dispersion (SAD) method, then a molecular
model of the crystal was constructed and finally refined (R-
factor/Rfree = 12.6/14.6%) at a resolution of 1.8 c.[14] The
atomic coordinate and experimental data have been depos-
ited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) with the accession code
6JR4. (For further details, see the Supporting Information
and Table S1 in the Supporting Information).
The crystal structure of the NIR-emitting DNA–Ag16NC
is shown in Figure 2A (see Figure S4 in the Supporting
Information for a 2 j Fo j- j Fc j map drawn at 1s). Two DNA
decamers take on a horse-shoe-like conformation and confine
the Ag16NC, whose diameter and height are 7 c and 15 c,
respectively. Surprisingly, no canonical Watson–Crick[15] base
pairs are observed. The two DNA strands are mainly linked
via silver-atom-mediated interactions and only a few hydro-
gen bonds.

Figure 2B. The first region (Figure 3A) comprises four Ag
atoms surrounded by four cytosines: two cytosines (C1 and C3)
from the first strand and the same bases from the second
strand, as well as one adenine A2 (not shown). It is important
to note that these four Ag atoms are two by two on the same
plane, as shown in Figure 2B. Every Ag atom forms a coor-
dination bond with N3 of the cytosine and interacts with the
carbonyl group O2 of the neighboring cytosine. The Ag@
N3(C) and Ag@O2(C) distances are in the range of 2.2–2.3 c
and 2.4–2.8 c, respectively. The Ag–N1(A) distance between
the A2 base and the closest Ag is 2.5 c. Furthermore, each Ag
atom is in close contact with neighboring Ag atoms (d = 2.7–
2.8 c). Similar Ag–Ag atom interactions were also observed
in the green-emitting silver nanocluster by Huard et al.[5] The
structure is further stabilized by hydrogen bonds (d = 2.8–
3.1 c) between the carbonyl group O2 and amino group N4 of
the neighboring cytosine bases. As a result, these four Ag
atoms are isolated from the solvent region.
The second section (Figure 3B) is composed of four Ag
atoms that interact with two cytosines and two guanines: one
cytosine C4 and one guanine G9 from the first fragment and
the same bases from the second strand. More precisely, two
Ag atoms form a coordination bond with N1 of the guanines
and two Ag atoms interact with the cytosines via N3. The
close distance between Ag and N1(G) suggests that guanine
residues must be deprotonated at the N1 position. Binding of
Ag to deprotonated adenine and cytosine residues was also
suggested in the crystal structure of the green-emitting silver
nanocluster by Huard et al.[5] The Ag@N3(C)/N1(G) distances
are in the range of 2.3–2.4 c. Furthermore, all four Ag atoms
interact with each other (d = 2.8–3.1 c), as well as with the
carbonyl group of the neighboring nucleobase. A network of
hydrogen bonds (d = 2.6–3.4 c) is also present thanks to the
interaction between the carbonyl group of a base and the
primary amine of the closest base.
The third region (Figure 3C) is different from the
previously described sections. In fact, it includes six Ag
atom positions and two cytosines C8 from the two different
strands. Two of the six Ag atoms (magenta spheres in Figure 2
and Figure 3C) do not interact with the DNA bases, but only
with other Ag atoms. In the anomalous difference Fourier
electron density map, the electron density of these two Ag
atoms was relatively poor compared to the density of the
other Ag atoms, and their occupancies were refined to 0.31–
0.36. This means that these two Ag atoms are more unstable
compared to the other 16 Ag atoms (occupancy = 1) that
interact at least with one DNA nucleobase. Therefore, we
decided to define the structure of the overall nanocluster as
an Ag16 cluster. We will comment below on these two
positions, as well as an additional Ag with low occupancy at
the adenine A10 position.
Regarding the other four Ag atoms, two of them interact
with the cytosines via N3 and two are coordinated by the
oxygen O2 of two distinct carbonyl groups. The Ag@N3(C)
and Ag@O2(C) distances are 2.3 c and in the range of 2.7–
2.9 c, respectively. Furthermore, these fixed Ag atoms
interact with each other as described above (d = 2.7–2.9 c).
The last section (Figure 3D) is formed by four Ag atoms
and four bases: two guanines and two adenines; one adenine
A6 and one guanine G7 from the first strand and the same
bases from the second strand. Besides the interactions among
the Ag atoms (d = 2.7–2.9 c), two Ag atoms interact with two
guanines and two Ag atoms form coordination bonds with
two adenines, in both cases via N7 (d = 2.3 c). Moreover, the
carbonyl group O6 of the guanines interacts with the two Ag
atoms (d = 2.3–2.4 c) that form a coordinate bond with the
adenines. At the same time, oxygen atoms of the phosphate
group of the adenines coordinate the Ag atoms (d = 2.3–
2.4 c). The structure is further stabilized by hydrogen bonds
(d = 2.8–3.1 c) between the amino group N6 of one adenine
and a phosphate oxygen of the adenine on the opposite
strand. All 16 Ag atoms interact with each other, as can be
seen in Figure S5 in the Supporting Information.
The Ag@Ag distances (mostly 2.7–2.9 c, the shortest is
2.6 c and a few up to 3.1 c, see Figure S5 in the Supporting
Information) are far shorter than the sum of their van der
Waals radii (3.44 c)[16] and are similar to or rather shorter
than their metallic radii (2.88 c).[17] This observation agrees
with the fact that the metallic bond distance between two
neighboring atoms involved in a cluster tends to be shorter
than that observed in bulk metal.[7b,18] However, it was also
shown previously that argentophilic interactions lead to Ag+–
Ag+ distances shorter than the van der Waals radii.[19] Despite
the fact that numerous reports seem to indicate that the silver
nanoclusters are usually highly oxidized,[1a,3b,20] we speculate,
based on the Ag@Ag bond distances, that the Ag16NC
contains a significant number of neutral Ag atoms. Future
mass spectroscopy and X-ray absorption near edge structure
(XANES) studies could shed light on the actual charge of the
Ag16 nanocluster.
The Ag16NC is sealed with one adenine A2 on the top
(interacting with one Ag from plane 1) and with two adenines
A6 at the bottom. Two residues T5 and A10 make no
coordinate bonds to the Ag16NC but are involved in crystal
packing interactions described in detail in the next section.
The thymine plays also another important role: it enables the
required folding of the DNA strand around Ag atoms,
promoting the formation of the cluster. Deoxyribose rings
of six residues (C1, A2, C3, T5, G7, and A10) take a general C2’-
endo sugar pucker and those of other residues take an
alternative C3’-endo conformation. While DNA can flexibly
change its sugar pucker between C2’-endo and C3’-endo
conformations, RNA with ribose ring takes solely C3’-endo
pucker in principle. Therefore, it is impossible to replace the
present
DNA
sequence
with
the
corresponding
RNA
sequence.
The asymmetric unit of the crystal contains two DNA–
Ag16NCs characterized by a non-crystallographic two-fold
symmetry (Figure 4). The crystal packing between DNA–
Ag16NCs (Supporting Information, Figure S6) is promoted by
several interactions, such as two silver-mediated base pairs
between 3’-terminal adenines (A10–Ag+–A10), two phos-
phate–Ca2+–phosphate interactions, and p-stacking between
two neighboring thymines (T5, Supporting Information, Fig-
ure S7).

In conclusion, we have presented the atomic structure of
a NIR-emitting DNA–Ag16NC, which exhibited similar
fluorescence properties both in crystalline and solution
states. This structural insight will enable the link between
the structure properties and emission features to be deter-
mined, and might help the design of a new range of
monodisperse, biocompatible, and water-soluble AgNCs
with fascinating photophysical properties.
Moreover, we demonstrated the prevalence of interac-
tions between nucleotides mediated by Ag atoms, as well as
the presence of loosely associated Ag+ ions and the role of
thymine in the folding and crystallization process. Interest-
ingly, the Ag16 nanocluster is so tightly embedded in the DNA
scaffold that it is almost fully shielded from the surrounding
solvent. We hope that the presented data will be the starting
point for future rational design of DNA–AgNCs.

ABSTRACT 
Atomically precise metal nanoclusters (MNCs) have emerged as tailorable luminescent materials with visible to near-infrared 
emission modulated by core (kernel) size, metal composition, and ligand engineering. These ultrasmall clusters exhibit discrete 
quantum-confined electronic states with strong spin–orbit coupling (SOC), enabling diverse emission pathways. Current research 
focuses on elucidating emission mechanisms and developing strategies to enhance fluorescence quantum yields. In this review, we 
emphasize structure–photoluminescence (PL) correlations and the underlying excited-state origins of luminescence: (i) coinage- 
metal clusters display multiple emissive channels—including prompt fluorescence, room-temperature phosphorescence, and 
TADF; (ii) the electronic gap and thus emission energy is directly governed by core size and metal identity, with core shrinkage and 
enhanced SOC generally inducing red-shifts; and (iii) ligand shell properties (identity/rigidity/packing) control charge-transfer 
pathways and nonradiative decay, while heterometal doping or rigidification modulates state ordering to brighten emission 
without necessarily shifting band positions. Importantly, many clusters exhibit dual-emission behavior. We propose a coupled 
core–shell emissive- state model in which one band originates from metal-core excitation and the other from a ligand- or motif- 
centered charge-transfer state. Finally, we outline future challenges: dissecting core versus shell contributions to PL and boosting 
quantum efficiency through targeted control of cluster composition and ligand shell. Progress on these fronts is crucial for the 
rational design of next-generation cluster emitters.

Introduction 
Atomically precise metal nanoclusters (MNCs) are ultrasmall, 
ligand-protected aggregates of typically tens to a few hundred 
metal atoms, placing them between discrete molecules and larger 
nanoparticles (Figure 1 ) [ 1–8 ]. In this regime, the electronic struc- 
ture is quantized rather than band-like: well-defined superatom 
shells and discrete HOMO–LUMO gaps give MNCs molecule- 
like optical properties (sharp absorption features and size-tunable 
emission) [ 9–12 ]. Crucially, a large fraction of the atoms lie 
at the surface, so that metal core states hybridize strongly 
with ligand-based orbitals. This core–shell interplay yields rich 
photoluminescence (PL) behavior [ 13–16 ]. Indeed, although bulk 
gold (Au) or silver (Ag) are nonluminescent, atomically precise 
Au, Ag, and Cu clusters can emit brightly (as fluorescence, 
phosphorescence or even delayed fluorescence) when rigidified 
by ligands and strong spin–orbit coupling (SOC) [ 17–19 ]. Because 
MNCs are small enough for full structural characterization 
and electron counting, yet large enough to exhibit collective 
interactions and packing effects, they provide a uniquely con- 
trollable platform to study emission at the molecule–material 
boundary [ 20 ].

MNCs luminescence is highly tunable: quantized electronic 
states and ligand–core coupling allow PL to be regulated by 
the number of atoms, metal composition, ligand identity, and 
supramolecular environment. For example, introducing a dopant 
or changing ligand bundles can switch emission color or effi- 
ciency by shifting core-based versus charge-transfer excited 
states. Such tunability has opened applications in sensing, 
bioimaging, lighting, and catalysis [ 22–25 ]. Bright metal-cluster 
emitters benefit from large Stokes shifts and long lifetimes 
(sub- µs to ms), enabling background-free detection and energy- 
harvesting schemes. Nonetheless, harnessing MNC lumines- 
cence also presents challenges: multiple excited-state pathways 
(metal-centered versus ligand-to-metal charge-transfer [LMCT] 
states), intersystem crossing (ISC), and vibronic/nonradiative 
losses all compete to determine the quantum yield. Recent studies 
have shown that aggregation-induced emission (AIE) and solid- 
state packing can dramatically enhance brightness by restricting 
nonradiative motion [ 21, 26–32 ]. However, deconvoluting the 
contributions of core-localized “super atom” states, ligand/surface 
states, and environmental effects remains difficult. 
To address these issues, this review builds a unified framework 
from atomic structure to emission. We first examine synthetic 
routes and structural motifs of luminescent clusters, linking 
core size and geometry, ligand ordering, alloying, and charge 
state to superatomic electron counts and mechanical rigidity. 
We then analyze the electronic origins of light emission by 
classifying the emissive manifolds (core-centered, shell/surface- 
related, and charge-transfer states) and by evaluating the compe- 
tition between radiative and nonradiative pathways, with explicit 
attention to SOC and ligand-field effects. Building on these foun- 
dations, we highlight strategies that enhance emission (such as 
ligand crosslinking, host-matrix confinement, and atom-by-atom 
alloying) and discuss how they modulate core/shell electronic 
states and suppress losses. We conclude by distilling emerging 
design principles and delineating outstanding challenges, includ- 
ing efficiency ceilings in the red and NIR regimes, operando 
mapping of structure–function relationships, and scalable, com- 
positionally precise synthesis. Throughout, we anchor each topic 
in recent literature so as to connect atomic-level design with 
photophysical outcomes and to guide the rational development 
of brighter, application-ready MNCs emitters.

Synthesis and Structural Benchmarks of 
Luminescent Nanoclusters 
Synthesizing atomically precise, luminescent MNCs relies on 
bottom-up reduction/size-focusing or electrochemical routes 
to reach magic-size compositions, complemented by tem- 
plate/confinement or alloying strategies that harden the surface 
and tune the superatom electron count. Across Au, Ag, Cu, 
and alloyed kernels, control over nuclearity, dopant identity, 
and ligand architecture translates directly into color, lifetime, 
and quantum yield, enabling functions from bright molecular 
emitters to integrated photonic elements. This section surveys 
representative synthetic pathways and the resulting photophys- 
ical benchmarks, organizing the discussion by metal family and 
interweaving several nodal studies. 
2.1 
Nanoclusters 
Au nanoclusters (NCs) are model luminophores whose emis- 
sion arises from coupled core states, charge-transfer mani- 
folds, and interfacial hybrids, with strong SOC in Au making 
mixed fluorescence-phosphorescence common. Au NCs exhibit 
molecule-like PL from this balanced interplay; as a result, room- 
temperature phosphorescence or mixed singlet–triplet emission 
often appears. Color and efficiency are set by nuclearity/electron 
count, kernel geometry, surface “staple” motifs, and ligand- 
imposed rigidity. Thus, Au NCs span visible–NIR emission 
with lifetimes from nanoseconds to microseconds, and PLQY 
tunable from sub-percent to near unity via atomic- and surface- 
level design [ 33–36 ]. These principles, consolidated across spec- 
troscopy, computation, and synthesis, establish Au NCs as model 
luminophores at the molecule–material boundary [ 37–39 ].

Early studies set benchmarks for Au-cluster luminescence and 
showed that ligand chemistry can systematically raise brightness. 
From the early 2000s, Link et al. reported visible-to-IR emission 
from a ∼ 28-atom Au cluster (later assigned as Au25 (SG)18 ) [ 40 ]. 
Xie et al. showed a simple, biocompatible route to fluorescent 
BSA-stabilized Au25 , which demonstrated that atomically pre- 
cise Au clusters exhibit intrinsic fluorescence under ambient 
conditions—catalyzing subsequent advances in bioimaging and 
mechanism-driven emitter design [ 41 ]. Subsequent chemical 
strategies further boosted brightness: Wu et al. enhanced the 
PL quantum yield (PLQY) of Au25 (SG)18 by ∼ 1.8-fold through 
ligand exchange with electron-rich thiols, emphasizing that the 
ligand shell (alongside the Au core) can be tuned to strengthen 
oscillator strength and radiative rates [ 24 ]. Similarly, Pradeep 
and coworkers improved the PLQY of [Au25 (SBB)18 ]– to ∼ 1.8% by 
incorporating S(AuSBB) complexes, illustrating another ligand- 
centered (LC) route to increase emission efficiency [ 42 ]. Struc- 
tural innovations also yielded ultrabright emitters. These results 
emphasized the ligand shell, alongside the Au core, as a lever to 
strengthen oscillator strength and radiative rates. 
Surface rigidification and kernel-surface coupling emerged as 
central levers for red/NIR tuning and large PLQY gains. Luo 
and coworkers connected AIE in Au(I)–thiolate complexes to 
the emergence of ultrabright Au(0)@Au(I)–thiolate core–shell 
clusters [ 43 ]. Koshevoy’s group unveiled intensely luminescent 
homoleptic alkynyl Au(I)10 and cationic phosphine-protected 
Au(I)8 clusters with O2 -insensitive emission, establishing a robust 
molecule-like Au platform where precise core topology directly 
translates to high PL—thereby catalyzing further ligand- and 
structure-driven emitter design [ 44 ]. This drive for atomic pre- 
cision continued as Yu and Xie identified a red/NIR-emissive 
Au22 (SG)18 with precise composition, providing a clean structure–
luminescence correlation for thiolate-protected Au clusters [ 45 ]. 
By 2015, Pyo et al. rigidified the shell of Au22 (SG)18 using tetraocty- 
lammonium, suppressing low-frequency motions and elevating 
the room-temperature PLQY above 60%—a dramatic efficiency 
boost for an undoped Au emitter [ 46 ]. Further studies linked 
controlled growth with optical response: Yao et al. mapped a size 
evolution from [Au25 (SR)18 ]– to [Au44 (SR)26 ]2–, tying structural 
enlargement (kernel and extra staples) to emergent emissive 
states and providing a structural basis for tuning luminescence 
as clusters grow [ 47 ]. In parallel, variations in core packing were 
found to govern excited-state dynamics: Jin’s group showed that 
simply changing the crystal phase of the Au kernel (e.g., 30-atom 
hcp vs. 38-atom bcc) shifts excited-state lifetimes from ∼ 1 ns to 
4.7 µs, linking tighter bcc packing to suppressed nonradiative 
decay and thus much longer-lived emission [ 48 ]. Wu et al. 
later showed that shortening Au(I)–thiolate staples (Au10 to 
Au25 cores) red-shifts PL into NIR-II and switches the dominant 
radiative channel without changing overall composition [ 49 ]. 
Luo et al. deconvoluted the emission of an Au38 (PET)26 NC into 
distinct fluorescence, thermally activated delayed fluorescence 
(TADF), and phosphorescence components, achieving an overall 
PLQY above 90% at 80 K and providing a detailed kinetic 
picture of how radiative channels compete in one molecular-sized 
Au cluster (Figure 2A–C ) [ 50 ]. Collectively, these PL-focused 
milestones established a strong foundation of bright, tunable Au 
NCs. Building on this progress, a parallel line of inquiry has 
focused on controlling not just the intensity and color of emission, 
but also its polarization—specif ically by developing Au clusters 
that exhibit circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) and thus 
possess intrinsic chiroptical activity. 
Recent advances indeed show that chiral structure and assembly 
can endow Au NCs with robust CPL. Han et al. synthesized 
atomically precise enantiomeric Au4 clusters and found that 
aurophilicity-driven aggregation activates bright CPL ( ≈ 14% 
PLQY in the aggregated state), demonstrating a strategy for 
amplifying CPL through controlled cluster packing [ 51 ]. Building 
on this, Luo and coworkers reported an enantiomeric pair of 
Au13 NCs with a quasi-C2 -symmetric core that exhibit intense 
CPL in solution, achieving a high CPL quantum yield ( ∼ 61%) 
and directly linking the clusters’ chiral superatomic structure to 
their pronounced chiroptical response (Figure 2D ) [ 52 ]. Beyond 
inherent cluster chirality, supramolecular coupling has emerged 
as another lever for CPL control: Zhai et al. used hydrogen-bond- 
mediated intercluster coupling to reversibly switch between blue 
and red CPL states, enabling encryption-type responses [ 53 ]. Such 
advances in cluster chirality and assembly have also translated 
into practical devices. For example, Zang’s group fabricated 
hierarchical architectures that maximize both the luminescence 
dissymmetry (glum ) and the PLQY of cluster emitters, enabling 
functional outputs like enantioselective photopolymerization and 
circularly polarized light-emitting diodes [ 54 ]. Notably, the origin 
of CPL in these systems is the creation of chiral emissive states 
via ligand-induced asymmetry. In other words, even if a cluster’s 
metal core is initially symmetric or achiral, an asymmetric 
(especially enantiopure chiral) ligand shell can remove inversion 
symmetry and split the excited-state manifold into nonsuper- 
imposable mirror-image configurations. The excited electron’s 
transition dipoles become chiral, resulting in CPL output. This 
strategy is evidenced by recent successes in ligand-induced cluster 
chirality: for example, exchanging achiral ligands with chiral 
thiols and/or doping a symmetric Au14 Cd1 core yielded a chiral 
Au12 Cd2 cluster that exhibits strong CPL [ 55 ]. In essence, the 
ligand’s asymmetry is imprinted onto the cluster’s electronic 
states, inducing a glum in emission. 
External pressure provides a reversible, orthogonal dial to 
brighten emission by tuning packing and low-frequency motion. 
Indeed, applying pressure has emerged as a unique strategy 
to boost Au-cluster emission. Zang and coworkers reported 
stacking-dependent piezoluminescence in polymorphic Au8 NCs: 
under hydrostatic compression, one form (Au8 -C) showed 
enhanced PL while a polymorph (Au8 -P) became quenched. 
In situ structural and spectroscopic measurements (ADXRD, 
Raman, fs-transient absorption (TA), time-resolved photolu- 
minescence [TRPL]) revealed that anisotropic compression of 
Au8 -C increases its core aspect ratio and rigidifies the clus- 
ter (suppressing low-frequency vibrations), thereby establishing 
a clear pressure-induced brightening mechanism via packing- 
programmed cluster mechanics [ 56 ]. Complementing this poly- 
morph study, Zang group demonstrated pressure-activated NIR 
luminescence in well-defined Au11 clusters: applying high pres- 
sure “turned on” an intense NIR emission and reshaped the 
excited-state dynamics, further evidencing high pressure as a 
clean, reversible dial to enhance radiative output in atomically 
precise Au NCs [ 57 ]. These findings highlight how mechanical 
compression, like chemical and chiral strategies, can serve as 
a powerful and tunable lever for maximizing the luminescence 
of Au NC emitters. For Au NCs, achieving a closed ‑shell

These structure–photophysics rules now translate into applica- 
tions that exploit large Stokes shifts, long lifetimes, and matrix- 
tolerant emission. In biomedical imaging and sensing, Au NCs 
provide bright, photostable emission from visible to NIR-II, with 
lifetime-encoded readouts [ 58 ], and a 2025 critical review out- 
lines preclinical NIR-II visualization strategies using molecular 
Au clusters [ 59 ]. For device applications, electroluminescent 
LEDs employing Au NC emitters have advanced beyond proof- 
of- concept: triple-ligand engineering achieves ≈ 5.1% external 
quantum efficiency and > 103 cd m− 2 luminance in solution- 
processed devices [ 60 ], with earlier work establishing white Au- 
NC LEDs as eco-friendly color converters [ 61 ]. Meanwhile, the 
millisecond-scale phosphorescence of aggregated/rigidified Au 
NCs enables time-gated anticounterfeiting and security printing 
by overcoming short-lived background interference [ 62, 63 ]. Col- 
lectively, these emerging applications demonstrate how atomic 
precision in monometallic Au clusters enables functionality 
beyond fundamental optics. While Au NCs have been widely 
studied for their strong SOC, Ag NCs offer an interesting contrast 
due to their relatively weaker SOC, which significantly impacts 
their luminescent behavior. 
2.2 
Ag NCs 
Ag NCs provide a complementary luminescent testbed to Au 
systems, extending the design rules established for Au clus- 
ters. Compared with Au, Ag NCs exhibit stronger superatomic 
shell effects, sharper visible–NIR absorption features, and more 
rigid multishell kernels, enabling efficient emission when sur- 
face dynamics are restrained. Their photophysics is dominated 
by size-quantized, cluster-centered electronic manifolds that 
hybridize with interfacial states. Consequently, tailoring the 
kernel geometry (such as icosahedral vs. dodecahedral shells) 
and enforcing tight ligand bundling can open long-lived NIR 
emissive channels with sizable PLQY. Recent reviews have 
summarized how bottom-up thiolate syntheses and templated 
methods have yielded robust Ag NC platforms for optical 
studies [ 64, 65 ]. 
Early structure–property benchmarks established quantitative 
links between precise Ag-core architectures and red/NIR emis- 
sion. Building on foundational insights, Li et al. established the 
intensely red-emitting [Ag62 S13 (St Bu)32 ]4 + —resolving its atom- 
ically precise core–shell architecture and correlating valence 
electron count with kernel–shell charge-transfer-driven PL in 
both solution and solid states. This provided a quantitative 
template for monometallic Ag cluster design [ 66 ]. The 2013 disclo- 
sure of ultrastable [Na4 Ag44 (p-MBA)30 ] established an 18-electron 
superatom configuration featuring a crystallographically resolved 
Ag12 @Ag20 hollow core with six Ag2 (SR)5 staples, directly linking 
optical bands and stability to kernel–staple topology [ 67, 68 ]. In 
parallel, Jin and coworkers compared [Ag62 S12 (SBut )32 ]2 + with 
[Ag62 S13 (SBut )32 ]4 + , showing how valence-electron count and a 
central sulfide govern kernel–shell charge transfer and emission 
pathways (fs-TA), thereby defining topology-optics relationships 
(Figure 3A–C ) [ 69 ].

Prototype Ag29 frameworks highlight how peripheral coordina- 
tion and interfacial coupling tune lifetimes, triplet formation, 
and NIR brightness without alloying. [Ag29 (BDT)12 (TPP)4 ]3–
emerged as a prototypical NIR-emissive thiolate/phosphine Ag 
cluster: thermodynamic and photophysical analyses revealed 
microsecond lifetimes and strong sensitivity of emission to 
secondary-ligand binding equilibria, clarifying how peripheral 
coordination governs triplet formation and nonradiative loss. 
Excited-state engineering on the same Ag29 scaffold via periph- 
eral Ag(I) complexes produced a bright NIR triplet emitter, 
providing a clean monometallic route to intensify NIR PL 
(Figure 3D,E ) [ 70, 71 ]. Mechanistically, Weerawardene et al. 
established that absorption and emission in Ag44 (SR)30 are 
governed by kernel-localized superatomic states and interfacial 
coupling, providing a quantitative foundation for distinguishing 
CC and charge-transfer contributions in Ag NCs [ 38 ]. In par- 
allel, coordinating-solvent/ligand-bundle studies uncovered why 
[Na4 Ag44 (p-MBA)30 ] resists oxidation and how solvation tunes its 
near-surface electronic structure-knowledge that guides emission 
retention under processing [ 72 ]. 
Rigid frameworks and chiral design have delivered near-unity 
PLQY and CPL, underscoring the role of structural locking in 
suppressing nonradiative loss. Han et al. reported ultrastable, 
atomically precise chiral Ag NCs with PLQY > 95% at 300 K, 
clarifying how rigid frameworks and interfacial coupling sup- 
press nonradiative decay [ 18 ]. Wang and colleagues resolved 
homochiral Ag6 enantiomers from racemates built with nonchi- 
ral ligands, achieving stimuli-responsive CPL (acid/base and 
solvent switching) while preserving atomic precision—thereby 
delivering a modular Ag platform for chiroptical encoding [ 73 ]. 
Beyond thiolates, Ag chalcogenolate clusters reached near-unity 
PLQY at room temperature through sulfide-induced rigidifica- 
tion, underscoring that suppressing low-frequency motions is a 
general recipe for highly efficient monometallic Ag emitters [ 74 ]. 
Additionally, Zhang et al. synthesized chiral Ag NCs (R/S ─Ag29 
and R/S ─Ag16 ) through asymmetric transformation and demon- 
strated NIR-II CPL (circularly polarized phosphorescence) from 
R/S ─Ag29 enantiomers at ∼ 975 nm. This represents the first 
example of NIR-II CPL from an atomically precise metal cluster 
[ 75 ].

NCs: from the structural elucidation of Ag62 -based luminescent

Ag NCs now operate as functional light-active components in

For biomedicine, DNA-templated AgNCs (AgN-DNAs) provide

toward white-LED platforms [ 78 ]. For security photonics, room- 
temperature Ag64 S clusters with near-unity PLQY enable time-

these application tracks translate kernel electron count, rigid sur-

with integration into waveguides, metasurfaces, and hybrids to 
follow [ 65 ]. 
2.3 
Cu NCs 
Extending the synthesis–structure–photophysics paradigm from 
Au and Ag to a more earth-abundant metal, Cu NCs offer a 
cost-effective platform for luminescence. The emission of Cu 
NCs can be dramatically enhanced by rigidifying the Cu(I)–
ligand interface and tuning the cluster’s electron count and 
geometry. However, Cu’s inherently weaker SOC and more 
labile metal–ligand bonding (relative to Au) make nonradiative 
decay a greater challenge. Therefore, suppressing low-frequency 
vibrational modes—through robust alkynyl/thiolate ligand shells 
or host-matrix confinement—becomes crucial to stabilize bright 
triplet or thermally mixed excited states. These design princi- 
ples, highlighted in recent reviews, frame the development of 
high-performance Cu NC emitters. 
Early studies established rigidification and ordered assembly 
as primary levers to brighten Cu-cluster emission. Wu et al. 
pioneered assembly-induced emission brightening in thiolate- 
capped Cu NCs, demonstrating that ordered supramolecular 
organization dramatically enhances luminescence and acti- 
vates mechanochromism—crucially blueprinting rigidification- 
induced emission (RIM) and AIE concepts for Cu clus- 
ters (Figure 4A ) [ 15 ]. High-yield bulk synthesis of atomically 
precise Cu29 (coprotected by cyclohexanethiolate and triph- 
enylphosphine) provided a scalable model linking composi- 
tion, kernel architecture, and photophysics [ 79 ]. A single- 
crystal-resolved [Cu11 (TBBT)9 (PPh3 )6 ]2 + then delivered a clean 
structure–photophysics correlation, with bright red/NIR PL 
(685/675 nm) and solid-state PLQY ≈ 22% [ 80 ]. Chiral pack- 
ing and solvent-driven surface stiffening show that ligand-level 
control alone can raise kr and PLQY at room temperature. 
Zhang and coworkers established chiral, atomically precise 
Cu(I) alkynyl clusters that display aggregation/crystallization- 
triggered bright emission and intense CPL, linking rigid packing 
to enhanced kr and offering a stereochemical handle for Cu- 
cluster photonics [ 81 ]. A parallelepiped-like Cu10 alkynyl cluster 
reached PLQY ≈ 35% with τav ≈ 8.4 µs, critically, the same 
work showed that solvent-induced structural transformation 
can further stiffen the shell and enhance radiative yield—an 
instructive demonstration that surface rigidification and kernel 
reorganization are powerful, ligand-only levers in Cu (Figure 4B ) 
[ 82 ]. Li et al. recently demonstrated Förster energy transfer at the 
cluster–molecule interface by cocrystallizing Cu8 and Cu10 . This 
strategy rigidly fixes donor–acceptor distances and orientations, 
experimentally validating design principles for Cu-based emitters 
while highlighting critical structure–photophysics relationships 
in cluster systems [ 83 ]. 
These ligand- and assembly-level controls that brighten prompt 
emission also compress the singlet–triplet gap ( ΔEST ) and sta- 
bilize triplet manifolds. Building on this foundation, Cu NCs 
now extend beyond conventional PL into thermally TADF: a 
single-crystal-resolved Cu12 NC unambiguously exhibits TADF 
via a small S1 –T1 gap and efficient triplet harvesting [ 84 ], while 
a structurally defined anionic Cu40 pushes the spectral reach 
into the NIR-II window with deep-red emission and intrinsic 
photoredox activity under blue-LED excitation—underscoring 
how electron count, hydride inclusion, and dense ligand pack- 
ing cooperatively stabilize long-wavelength, functionally active 
excited states [ 85 ]. For Cu NCs, rigidifying the Cu(I)–ligand 
interface and controlling electron ‑ count geometry are essen- 
tial to suppress nonradiative decay and enable bright NIR 
emission. 
Translational applications of atomically precise Cu NCs now 
span NIR emitters, chiroptical photonics, solid-state lighting, and

2.4 
Alloy NCs 
Alloy NCs use heterometal incorporation to tune electronic struc- 
ture and spin dynamics, producing predictable changes in emis- 
sive behavior. Alloy NCs extend the design logic of monometallic 
systems by introducing heterometals into the cluster framework. 
Through single-atom doping or multimetal kernels, alloying can 
tune superatomic electron counts, adjust SOC strength, and mod- 
ify interfacial electric fields. These atom-level changes re-order 
excited states and rebalance the competition between radiative 
and nonradiative pathways [ 90, 91 ]. In practice, atom-level edits 
(such as center-site dopants or shell substitutions) have delivered 
step-changes in PLQY, unlocked polarized emission suitable for 
waveguides, and pushed near-unity efficiency in the NIR, while 
template libraries enable systematic mapping of composition–
optics relationships across mono- to tetrametallic families. Mech- 
anistically, the alloying-induced brightness gains often stem from 
spin-dynamics control via the heavy-atom effect: introducing 
high-Z dopants (such as Au, Pt) increases SOC, which facilitates 
singlet-to-triplet ISC and more effectively populates triplet states. 
The enhanced triplet channel strengthens phosphorescence and 
frequently extends emission lifetimes, often without shifting band 
positions. 
Stepwise exemplars show how atom-by-atom edits create large, 
quantifiable gains in brightness. A concise trajectory of advances 
illustrates how atom-by-atom edits translate into step changes 
in performance. A seminal proof of concept is the Ag-for-Au 
substitution in Au25 , where controlled metal exchange to form 
Agx Au25 − x (1 ≤ x ≤ 13) lifted the PLQY from ∼ 0.1% (parent Au25 ) 
to ≈ 40%—a ∼ 400-fold boost attributed to heterometal-induced 
reorganization of the superatomic manifold and suppression of 
nonradiative decay (“the 13th Ag atom matters”). This work 
established alloying as a first-order lever for brightening atom- 
ically precise emitters (Figure 5A ) [ 22 ]. In a complementary 
direction, center-atom and shell reconstructions in Pt1 Ag24/28 
families demonstrated that swapping a single kernel atom can 
measurably reshape excited-state dynamics and PLQY (Pt1 Ag28 , 
PLQY ≈ 4.9 vs. 0.1% for Pt1 Ag24 ), providing a clean, atomistic link 
between dopant position, structure, and luminescence [ 23 ]. To 
generalize such composition control beyond isolated exemplars, 
a 21-member M29 materials library (mono- to tetrametallic) 
was constructed via in-situ and forced metal exchange on a 
common template, enabling systematic, site-specific editing of 
kernel/surface metals and rapid correlation of alloying pat- 
terns with optical outputs (Figure 5B–E ) [ 92 ]. Bootharaju et al. 
synthesized uniform Au-doped Ag clusters via Ag25 -templated 
galvanic exchange, where single-atom center substitution sub- 
tly perturbs absorption, luminescence, and ambient stability, 
yielding 25-fold luminescence enhancement [ 93 ]. Importantly, 
where a heterometal dopant is placed in a cluster can dictate 
the excited-state behavior. Core-site doping (replacing an interior 
metal atom) often perturbs the HOMO–LUMO landscape and can 
introduce strong SOC—for instance, replacing the central Ag in 
an Ag25 cluster with Pt or Au significantly altered the emission 
intensity and dynamics [ 94 ]. Such core dopants tend to accelerate 
ISC and can prolong triplet lifetimes. On the other hand, surface 
or staple-site doping primarily influences the LMCT pathways 
and the cluster’s structural flexibility. Distributing dopants over 
multiple equivalent sites can either delocalize the excitation 
(enhancing radiative recombination) or, if it induces lattice 
strain, open nonradiative decay channels. We now recognize 
dopant positioning as a design lever: by choosing specific atomic 
sites for alloying, one can optimize radiative rates and sup- 
press nonradiative decay. This insight is reinforced by recent

3 
Electronic Origins of Luminescence 
With atomically precise architectures established, we now delin- 
eate how these structural blueprints govern luminescent phe- 
nomena, specifically unpacking the electronic origins of emission 
through superatomic HOMO–LUMO states, aggregation-induced 
rigidity, LMCT, and the governing kinetics of brightness. These 
electronic landscapes are rationalized via complementary mod- 
els: a molecular orbital (MO) perspective emphasizing HOMO–
LUMO transitions, and a superatom paradigm founded on 
discrete 1S/1P/1D shells and electron-counting rules—together 
elucidating the quantized spectra and size-tunable gaps char- 
acteristic of coinage-MNCs [ 106–108 ]. Brightness then follows 
from the competition between radiative channels (fluorescence, 
phosphorescence, and—when singlet–triplet gaps are sufficiently 
small—TADF) and nonradiative losses set by electron-vibration 
coupling (EVC) and interfacial relaxation; heavy-atom SOC 
promotes triplet formation, while increased surface rigidity sup- 
presses vibrational quenching [ 109, 110 ]. With these principles, 
we classify emissive manifolds, introduce an overlap-centric 
descriptor that unifies several control knobs, and show how 
aggregation/crystallization and metallophilicity-driven electron- 
cloud contraction tune radiative versus nonradiative rates. 
3.1 
Excited-State Classification and Transitions 
This section classifies the principal emissive manifolds in 
coinage-MNCs and explains how core–ligand coupling governs 
color, lifetime, and quantum yield. Despite the chemical diversity 
of atomically precise MNCs, their PL typically arises from a small 
set of excited-state manifolds. The orbital origin and the degree of 
core–ligand coupling in these manifolds largely determine emis- 
sion energy, lifetime, and efficiency. Over the past two decades, 
three primary emission mechanisms have become prevalent in 
ligand-protected coinage-metal clusters: (i) CC (core) emission, 
(ii) ligand- or shell-centered emission, and (iii) ligand-to-metal 
(or related) charge-transfer emission. 
3.1.1 
(Core) Emission via HOMO–LUMO Transitions

sitions delocalized over the metal kernel. Upon photoexcitation, 
the cluster relaxes to its lowest excited state and then emits. 
Size-quantized absorption bands red-shift as core size grows, 
consistent with the jellium “super atom” model. Solvent effects 
are usually small because the transition is confined to the 
inorganic core. Vibronic coupling and slight symmetry break- 
ing in the core may broaden emission lines and influence 
lifetimes, but they do not fundamentally change the kernel- 
localized character of the emission. A representative example 
is the [Ag25 (SR)18 ]– NC, whose luminescence has been assigned

steady-state emission peaks coincide. For example, in Au25 (SR)18 ,

core–shell excited state with LMCT character, distinct from the 
longer-wavelength core-based phosphorescence [ 118 ]. Similarly,

3.1.3 
Ligand-to-Metal (or Related) Charge-Transfer 
Emission Emission 
A third category involves charge-transfer excited states, where the 
electron and hole of the excitation are separated between the lig- 
and shell and the metal core. In this mechanism, photoexcitation 
promotes an electron from an orbital primarily located on a ligand 
(or surface complex) into an acceptor orbital on the metal core (or 
vice versa, in metal-to-metal or metal-to-ligand CT cases). After 
excitation, the cluster relaxes to a charge-separated excited state 
and can then emit a photon as the electron recombines with the 
hole. LMCT-type emission often features very large Stokes shifts 
and can be sensitive to both ligand coverage and the cluster’s 
charge state. 
This mechanism has been the subject of intense debate, especially 
in the case of thiolate-protected Au clusters. Early studies by Jin 
et al. on [Au25 (SR)18 ]q ( q = –1, 0) suggested that the fluorescence 
in these clusters originates from an LMCT process, with electron 
density shifting from the S ─Au ligand bonds into the metal core 
upon excitation (Figure 8A,B ) [ 23 ]. This proposal came in part 
to explain observations that neither a pure jellium model nor 
a pure LC model could fully account for the emission trends. 
According to the metal-core HOMO–LUMO transition theory, 
one would expect that increasing the size of a Au cluster (adding 
more metal atoms) should systematically red-shift the emission 
wavelength (as the superatomic gap narrows). However, Negishi 
et al. discovered that for a series of monodisperse, homoligand- 
protected Aux (SR)y clusters spanning a range of sizes, the 
fluorescence peak position remained essentially constant even as 
the HOMO–LUMO gap (and formal electron count) changed with 
size. In other words, larger clusters did not produce the expected 
red-shifted emission, defying the predictions of a purely core- 
based emission mechanism [ 119 ]. On the other hand, one might 
postulate that the emission comes from a ligand-based excited 
state (as in mechanism ii above). Yet theoretical calculations by 
Aikens et al. and others indicated that if the emissive state were 
located on the organic shell, one would typically need to directly 
excite those high-lying ligand orbitals, which requires higher 
photon energy. In practice, fluorescence is observed even when 
exciting at energies corresponding to the cluster’s HOMO–LUMO 
transition (which is lower in energy than the LC transitions). This 
is inconsistent with a purely LC emission model, since low-energy 
excitation of the metal core somehow still leads to luminescence 
[ 120 ]. Thus, the LMCT model was proposed: the cluster under- 
goes a core ‑localized excitation, followed by ultrafast transfer of 
the excited electron (or hole) between core and shell, yielding a 
charge ‑separated emissive state. Radiative decay then occurs once 
the state has stabilized (e.g., after ligand/core relaxation and in 
Au clusters, possibly after spin–orbit mixing to yield some triplet 
character). 
A representative LMCT-based emitter is the binuclear cluster 
Au2 Cu6 , which exhibits a relatively high room-temperature 
PLQY ( ∼ 11.7%) with an emission mainly originating from an 
excited state in which electron density has shifted from the 
aryl phosphine ligands to the Cu(I)–A u2 metal core (Figure 8C–
E ) [ 95 ]. The rigid aromatic ligand framework in that system 
serves to both donate electron density (facilitating LMCT) and 
to restrict intramolecular rotations (enhancing radiative decay 
by reducing nonradiative losses). Substituting ligands with dif- 
ferent phosphine derivatives can modulate the LMCT strength 
and increase PLQY to ∼ 17.7%, demonstrating the sensitivity of 
LMCT to frontier orbital alignment between ligand and metal. 
In some cases, metal-to-metal charge transfer (MMCT) between 
different metal sites can likewise produce an emissive state. 
For instance, Au NCs capped with 11-mercaptoundecanoic acid 
(AuNCs@MUA) show a > 300 nm Stokes shift, with absorption 
peaks near 280, 360, and 390 nm, but PL excitation (PLE) 
is dominated by a band at ∼ 285 nm. Exciting at ∼ 285 nm 
produces a lower-energy emission, suggesting an MMCT pathway 
where excitation leads to electron relocation between metal sites 
or metal and ligand. Chemical modulation of AuNCs@MUA 
by ligand removal or exchange toggles the emission between 
MMCT and LMCT, depending on cluster size and ligand 
coverage [ 121 ]. 
Crucially, none of the above three mechanisms alone can uni- 
versally explain the wide range of emission behaviors in NCs. 
In fact, many clusters likely have multiple emissive channels 
operating simultaneously. For example, certain Au and Ag clus- 
ters exhibit dual emission peaks (a phenomenon detailed in 
the next section), or extraordinarily large changes in intensity 
with minor chemical modifications, which suggests that core- 
based and shell-based excited states may coexist and interact 
[ 119 ]. The observed PLQYs of clusters can vary by 1–2 orders of 
magnitude depending on surface chemistry (ligand type, doping, 
etc.), even when the emission wavelength is unchanged—a clear 
indication that the radiative process and the dominant emitting 
state are sensitive to the cluster’s interface and environment 
[ 93 ]. Determining the true origin of a cluster’s emission is 
therefore not merely an academic exercise; it is fundamental 
to developing design strategies for highly luminescent clusters. 
In the following sections, we describe how electronic structure 
models help identify these emissive states and how experi- 
ments have revealed an interplay between core- and shell-based 
luminescence.

3.2 
Electronic Structure Descriptions and Dual 
Emissive States 
To understand why a given MNC emits at a certain wavelength 
and how its luminescence responds to size, composition, or 
surface modifications, we must connect the emissive state to a 
concrete description of the cluster’s electronic energy levels. In 
atomically precise clusters, the relevant frontier orbitals arise 
from two sources—(i) the delocalized metal kernel orbitals and 
(ii) the ligand or interfacial orbitals—which often hybridize 
with each other. The ordering and mixing of these orbitals 
are primarily dictated by the cluster’s total electron count 
(which determines superatomic shell filling) and the symmetry 
of the metal core, and are further modulated by SOC and the 
strength/geometry of metal–ligand bonding. External factors like 
the dielectric environment or crystal packing can also shift orbital 
energies and couplings by changing electrostatic screening or 
introducing lattice strain. A useful electronic structure model 
must therefore accommodate pure core states, pure ligand states, 
and anything in between, because the character of the lowest 
excited state (whether it is core-centered [CC], ligand-centered 
[LC], or charge-transfer [CT]) directly influences the radiative 
versus nonradiative pathways available. 
Two complementary models, the MOs and the superatom shell 
picture, provide practical lenses for assigning emissive states. 
In the MO view, the HOMO–LUMO gap and electron–hole 
overlap largely set color and brightness. MO or “HOMO–LUMO”
view: here, the cluster is treated akin to a large molecule. 
The focus is on the highest occupied and lowest unoccupied 
MOs (HOMO and LUMO) and nearby frontier orbitals, which 
collectively define the optical gap ( ΔE ) and the nature of low- 
lying excitations. In this view, the emission wavelength is roughly 
determined by the HOMO–LUMO gap, the oscillator strength 
of the transition depends on the spatial overlap of the HOMO 
and LUMO electron densities, and one judges whether an excited 
state is CC, LC, or CT in character by inspecting how much 
of the HOMO and LUMO (or the involved natural transition 
orbitals [NTOs]) reside on the metal core versus the ligands. 
This picture is especially informative when ligand or interfacial 
orbitals dominate the frontier (e.g., in clusters where the HOMO 
is ligand-based or the LUMO has significant ligand character) and 
in situations where solvent or polarity effects (solvatochromism) 
are significant. In practice, TD-DFT with appropriate functionals 
(especially range-separated hybrids that handle CT states better) 
and inclusion of SOC for heavy elements is used to calculate 
the optical spectra of clusters within this MO framework. Such 
calculations can predict excitation energies, assign absorption 
bands to specific orbital transitions, and estimate radiative rates 
(oscillator strengths). For example, Aikens and collaborators have 
provided detailed TD-DFT analyses of thiolate-protected Au and 
Ag clusters, illustrating how frontier orbital compositions corre- 
late with observed optical properties [ 122 ]. Qian and coworkers 
have also discussed how controlling the HOMO/LUMO character 
through ligand choices can tune Au cluster emission [ 123 ]. 
The superatom model treats the metal kernel as an atomic- 
like entity whose delocalized electrons occupy quantized shells 
(1S, 1P, 1D, 2S, 1F, . . . ) arising from the collective potential of 
the core; shell fillings at “magic numbers” (2, 8, 18, 34, . . . ) 
predict stability, discrete absorption bands, and a pronounced 
HOMO–LUMO gap. Optical excitation corresponds to promo- 
tions between these superatomic shells (e.g., 1P →1S), which gives 
small clusters resolvable transitions distinct from nanoparticle 
plasmons. Building on this framework, a brief electron-count

and Ag-DNA motifs) form a distinct visible–NIR spectral class, 
while other counts such as 6e exhibit different absorption/PL

and underpins modern assignments in atomically precise Au/Ag

In reality, most MNCs are described by a hybrid of these two 
pictures. The low-lying orbitals of a ligand-protected cluster 
consist of the superatomic orbitals of the metal core perturbed 
by and mixed with the orbitals of the metal–ligand interface 
(thiolate staples, phosphine ligands, and so on). The extent of this 
mixing depends on factors like how close the ligand orbitals are 
in energy to the metal core states, the geometry of binding, and 
the overall cluster charge. Generally, a closed-shell superatomic 
electron count (such as 8 or 18) biases the lowest excited state 
to be CC, since there is a substantial gap to the next shell and 
the excitation tends to stay within the metal core. In contrast, an 
electron count that is one or two electrons beyond a closed shell 
(or just before one) can result in frontier orbitals that are closer 
together in energy and more easily hybridize with ligand orbitals, 
yielding a lowest excited state that has significant CT (core–
shell mixed) character. Recent overviews of luminescent clusters 
emphasize that this continuum from purely CC to increasingly 
CT character is the rule rather than the exception in coinage- 
metal clusters—very few systems are completely one or the other.

emission toward CT-like states; together with electronegativity- 
governed electron-cloud contraction/expansion, these effects 
rationalize the substantial PLQY increases often seen upon 
alloying. 
Significantly, these considerations also clarify why small changes 
to surface motifs can cause major shifts in PL behavior. The 
lowest excited states in many clusters already sit near a boundary 
between core-centered and CT character, with their frontier 
orbitals strongly shaped by the metal–ligand interface. Modest 
adjustments to staple geometry, ligand substituents, or counter- 
ions therefore perturb the energies and composition of these 
interfacial orbitals, which can switch the identity of the emissive 
state, alter electron–hole overlap, and rebalance radiative versus 
nonradiative channels. At the same time, motif-level changes 
often modify shell rigidity and packing, directly influencing EVC 
and hence knr . Consequently, seemingly minor edits to surface 
motifs, such as tuning aromaticity or steric bulk on Au–thiolate 
staples, or changing halides/counter-ions in alloy clusters, can 
drive order-of-magnitude changes in PLQY with only modest 
shifts in emission wavelength. (iv) Symmetry breaking by external 
stimuli offers postsynthetic control of cluster electronics. In 
Au25 (SR)18 , high pressure shortens Au–Au distances by fractions 
of an ångström and blue-shifts absorption/emission [ 126 ]. The 
pressure lifts certain degeneracies (splitting the superatomic P 
orbitals, e.g.) and can change a cluster from a dual-emission 
situation to a single-emission one by stabilizing one state over 
the other. Notably, the increased rigidity under pressure also 
reduced nonradiative decay, leading to brighter emission. Simi- 
larly, forming cocrystals or applying electrostatic fields can break 
symmetry: for example, sequential addition of cations to anionic 
Au25 clusters has been shown to “squeeze” the ligand layer 
electrostatically and pull the electron density slightly outward or 
inward, thereby tuning the relative energy of core versus shell 
states. In one case, binding of protic cations and hydrogen-bond 
donors to [Au25 (SR)18 ]– resulted in a dramatic enhancement of its 
PLQY (to near unity) along with a shift of the emission to a more 
core-like character [ 127 ]. Essentially, external pressure or fields 
can be used as reversible dials to manipulate state mixing in situ, 
without changing the cluster’s nuclear structure. 
We underscore that the structural rigidity of the metal kernel 
is crucial for emissive-state stability. An excited-state distortion 
(such as bond elongation or Jahn–Teller deformation within the 
core) tends to localize the excitation and facilitate nonradiative 
decay through vibrational coupling. Conversely, if the kernel 
is geometrically constrained from distorting, the excited state 
remains more delocalized and is forced to decay radiatively. 
For instance, Jin et al. observed that a 38-atom Au cluster 
with a rigid bcc-core lattice had an emission lifetime in the 
microseconds, whereas a 30-atom hcp-structured analogue with 
higher susceptibility to slight bond-length distortions showed 
only nanosecond emission [ 48 ]. The difference was attributed to 
suppressed core deformation in the bcc cluster, which “trapped”
the exciton in a radiative state. In general, symmetry-breaking in 
the cluster due to thermal motion or structural strain can mix 
bright and dark states: if such distortion funnels the excitation 
into a dark (trap) state, luminescence efficiency drops, whereas 
if it merely perturbs the state slightly, it might borrow intensity 
and even enhance phosphorescence. This dual role of distortion 
highlights why maintaining a stiff, symmetric kernel through 
methods like crystal packing or ligand constraints is key to 
localizing the emissive state on the entire cluster (preventing a 
single-site trap) and preserving high radiative yield.

From these intriguing cases, two general principles emerge: (1) 
Doping localized in the metal core (and not disrupting the ligand 
shell or staple motif) tends to leave the emission color unchanged 
even though it shifts the HOMO–LUMO energies of the core. 
This implies that the emissive state is not a pure core state 
but is instead associated with the surface or interface (where 
the structure was unchanged). The core’s electronic gap can be 
modulated by doping, but if the emission originates from an 
interface-centered state (e.g., a staple or LMCT state), its energy 
will remain constant, as observed. (2) The electronegativity 
of the dopant versus host metal correlates with the emission 
efficiency: introducing a more electronegative metal into a less 
electronegative metal core usually increases PLQY, whereas the 
reverse (introducing a less electronegative atom into a more 
electronegative host) tends to decrease PLQY. For instance, Au 
( χ ≈ 2.54) doping into an Ag ( χ ≈ 1.93) cluster core draws electron 
density inward toward the Au atom (since Au attracts electrons 
more strongly). This “pulling” of electron density into the core 
can reduce the electronic coupling between the core and the 
surface, in effect isolating the surface orbitals. The result is often 
enhanced luminescence, as the emissive shell-based state is less 
quenched by the core electrons. Conversely, Ag doping into a 
Au cluster pushes electron density outward (Ag holds electrons 
more loosely, so valence electrons delocalize toward the cluster 
periphery). This can increase overlap between the metal core 
electrons and the ligand shell, facilitating nonradiative pathways 
that quench shell-based emission, thereby lowering PLQY. 
In summary, doping redistributes the cluster’s delocalized “free 
electrons.” Confining these electrons within the core (electron- 
cloud contraction) typically enhances luminescence, whereas 
allowing them to spill into the surface (electron-cloud expansion) 
promotes nonradiative loss. Two Ag superatoms illustrate this 
control: closed-shell [Ag62 S13 (St Bu)32 ]4 + is intensely emissive [ 66 ], 
while open-shell [Ag62 S12 (St Bu)32 ]2 + is dark; restoring the central 
sulfide removes the extra delocalized electrons, localizes charge, 
and recovers bright emission [ 69 ]. A similar trend appears in 
[Au25 (SR)18 ]–, where decreasing the number or delocalization 
of free electrons—either by introducing π-accepting arylthio- 
lates or by partial oxidation—substantially increases PLQY [ 24 ]. 
Together, these results show that restricting core–surface electron 
overlap is a direct route to stronger emission. 
Synthesizing the above insights, we arrive at a unifying picture: 
ligand-protected MNCs often possess two coupled luminescent 
centers—one in the metal core and one in the surface ligand shell. 
The core-centered emission corresponds to superatomic HOMO–
LUMO transitions and generally shifts with core size (red-shifting 
for larger cores) but typically has low quantum yield that further 
drops with increasing size (due to increasing nonradiative decay). 
The ligand/shell-centered emission (often involving LMCT or 
excimer-like states on the surface) tends to occur at a relatively 
fixed energy (often in the desirable first biological window of 
∼ 700–900 nm for Au clusters) and can achieve higher quantum 
yields if the conditions suppress quenching. These two emissive 
centers are interdependent: synergistically, the metal core absorbs 
light and can funnel excited energy/electrons into the shell, while

3.3 
Radiative–Nonradiative Competition and 
Excited-State Dynamics 
The brightness of a cluster’s luminescence is governed not 
only by the nature of its emissive state (core vs. shell vs. CT) 
but also by the competition between radiative decay (photon 
emission) and nonradiative decay (heat or ground-state recovery 
without light) from that state. The radiative decay rate kr and 
the nonradiative rate knr together determine the excited state’s 
PLQY (PLQY = kr /( kr + knr )) and its lifetime. Understanding what 
factors control kr and knr in NCs is key to rationally improving 
their luminescence [ 130, 131 ]. 
Radiative channels in MNCs can span prompt fluorescence 
(singlet emission on the nanosecond scale), phosphorescence 
(triplet emission on the microsecond to millisecond scale, enabled 
by heavy-atom SOC), and TADF (a microsecond-scale delayed 
emission resulting from thermal up-conversion of triplets to 
singlets when the singlet–triplet gap is small). Which channel 
dominates, or whether multiple coexist, depends on the cluster’s 
electronic structure (especially ΔEST ) and composition (heavy- 
metal content). A recent time-resolved study of an atomically 
precise Au39 cluster exemplified this: at room temperature, Au39 
showed a multiexponential decay with an initial nanosecond 
fluorescence, a slower microsecond component attributable to 
TADF, and a dominant phosphorescence component on the order 
of tens of microseconds. The presence of Au with strong SOC in 
the kernel facilitated efficient ISC to the triplet, while a small 
singlet–triplet energy gap allowed some triplets to thermally 
repopulate the singlet (hence the TADF) [ 132 ]. Ag NCs, having 
weaker SOC, typically favor fluorescence unless a deliberate 
donor–acceptor design is introduced to shrink ΔEST . Impressively, 
through strategic surface design, even Ag clusters have achieved 
TADF emission in the NIR-II range: by aligning a CT state such 
that the singlet and triplet are nearly degenerate, researchers 
have observed efficient triplet harvesting in a well-defined Ag 
cluster system [ 133 ]. These examples highlight that clusters can 
be engineered to access the full gamut of emissive behavior (flu- 
orescence, phosphorescence, TADF) by tuning their electronic 
configurations and spin–orbit interactions [ 125 ]. 
The radiative rate kr of an electronic transition is proportional 
to the square of its transition dipole moment (| μ|2 ), which in 
turn depends on the spatial overlap between the electron and 
hole wavefunctions of the excited state. A useful quantitative 
descriptor in this context is the overlap of the NTOs representing 
the excitation. Generally, excited states that are more spatially 
localized—whether confined to the metal core or restricted to 
a ligand—exhibit reduced overlap between the excited electron 
and the corresponding hole. This diminished overlap results in 
a smaller | μ|2 and typically leads to a longer radiative lifetime. 
Conversely, when the electron and hole are delocalized over the 
same spatial region—such as in a symmetric excitation within the 
metal core or across a conjugated ligand framework—the | μ|2 is 
typically larger, resulting in a faster radiative rate. Importantly, 
reducing the overlap also lowers the exchange energy between 
electron and hole, which in many cases reduces ΔEST (the 
singlet–triplet splitting). Thus, there is an intrinsic trade-off: 
reduced electron–hole overlap favors TADF by bringing singlet 
and triplet states close in energy, but at the cost of lower oscillator 
strength and weaker prompt emission. In contrast, strong overlap 
enhances singlet emission brightness but typically yields a larger 
ΔEST , limiting access to triplet-based radiative channels. This 
overlap-dependent behavior has become a design cornerstone in 
purely organic TADF emitters and is equally applicable to cluster 
systems [ 134 ]. 
Radiative efficiency in ligand-protected NCs is set by electron–
hole overlap. In ligand-protected clusters, one can tune the 
overlap by structural means [ 135 ]. If the emissive state is core- 
centered, overlap is high and kr can be substantial. In ligand- 
to-core CT states, the electron and hole separate across the 
core–shell interface, which lowers overlap and slows radiative 
decay (but potentially making ΔEST small enough for TADF). In 
these cases, increasing the overlap without completely losing CT 
character is a way to boost brightness. Ligand electronics and 
structural rigidity are key levers: by choosing ligands that either 
donate or withdraw electron density, one can control how much 
of the electron (or hole) density sits on the core versus the ligand 
in the excited state. For example, a more electron-rich ligand 
might push the LUMO density onto the metal (increasing overlap 
with a metal-based HOMO), whereas an electron-poor ligand 
might pull the HOMO density outward. Additionally, making 
the ligand shell more rigid (through intra- or intermolecular 
constraints) can help because it keeps the electron and hole 
wavefunctions stationary relative to each other, preserving their 
overlap during the emission process and preventing excess 
reorganization that could lead to nonradiative decay. Recent 
experiment–theory studies have directly shown that the shape of 
the metal kernel and the structure of the surface ligands together 
control the emission overlap (Figure 10A–D ) [ 136, 137 ]. 
Coordinated edits to the core shape and surface staples modulate 
overlap and kr . In tailored series of Au clusters, the NIR-emitting 
state’s transition density was found to reside partly on the core 
and partly on the staples, and the fraction on the core versus the 
surface changed with deliberate modifications to the core shape 
and the staple geometry. Correspondingly, the emission lifetimes 
and radiative rates shifted, confirming that those structural edits 
effectively changed the electron–hole overlap and thus kr [ 128 ].

Intercluster distance and relative orientation can reconfigure 
radiative pathways by tuning electronic coupling and opening 
(or suppressing) aggregate-state channels. In densely packed 
solids or cocrystals, designed spacing and alignment between 
clusters can enhance dipole–dipole coupling to brighten singlet 
emission, or maintain orthogonal transition dipoles to favor 
long-lived, lower-overlap states that enable delayed emission 
(AIE/TADF-like behavior). Recent Ag-cluster studies show that 
ordered coassembly can simultaneously activate AIE and TADF- 
type signals by fixing donor–acceptor geometries at the lattice 
level, underscoring packing as a practical handle for rate control. 
Likewise, at a single-cluster level, bringing closed-shell metal 
atoms into close proximity (short d10 ⋅⋅⋅d10 contacts) via metal- 
lophilic attraction or externally applied pressure can concentrate 
the electron density and boost the effective SOC in the cluster. 
This electron-cloud contraction typically manifests as a blue-shift 
of emission (since bonding orbitals are stabilized and antibonding 
orbitals raised in energy) and an intensity increase (due to greater 
orbital overlap and reduced vibrational freedom) [ 140 ]. A vivid 
demonstration of this is the pressure-dependent luminescence 
of certain Au clusters: applying a few GPa of pressure to a 
crystal of a Au8 cluster led to ∼ 30 nm blue-shift in its emission 
and a noticeable intensity increase. When the pressure was 
released, the emission color and intensity reverted, confirming 
a reversible structural tuning of the electronic overlap [ 141 ]. 
Similarly, organometallic Au(I) complexes that are nonemissive 
in isolation often luminesce strongly when stacked cofacially, 
thanks to aurophilic contacts that create new excited states and 
rigidify the system. From a design perspective, these assembly- 
level effects (packing, pressure, metallophilic contacts) comple- 
ment the single-cluster optimizations (ligand choices, doping) 
by offering an external handle to manipulate kr and knr without 
changing the cluster’s inherent composition. For instance, one 
can envision engineering a solid-state emitter by first selecting 
a cluster with a desirable emissive state (placed appropriately 
in energy), then tweaking its ligands to set a favorable overlap 
and ISC rate, and finally organizing those clusters in a crystal 
or matrix to lock in the structure and possibly induce beneficial 
intercluster coupling.

closely related to Stokes shifts and reorganization energy); and 
(iii) ISC to a dark triplet or internal triplet–triplet transitions that

Ultrafast spectroscopy has captured some of these processes in

Temperature-dependent measurements also reveal that certain 
clusters have thermally activated nonradiative channels: as the 
temperature rises, vibrations can more easily enable nonradiative 
decay, quenching the emission (hence many clusters are far 
brighter at cryogenic temperatures). As mentioned, strong ISC in 
Au and Ag is a double-edged sword: it enables formally forbidden 
transitions (like phosphorescence) to occur at appreciable rates, 
but it also means that any symmetry-breaking (distortion of the 
cluster structure or environment) can mix states of different spin 
and character. Depending on the specifics, this can either open up 
a radiative path or introduce a new nonradiative decay path [ 143 ]. 
For instance, a symmetry-breaking ligand field might mix a bright 
singlet with a dark triplet, resulting in an overall lower PLQY due 
to the creation of a pathway to the dark state. Conversely, similar 
mixing might borrow intensity from a bright state to activate a 
previously forbidden transition, enhancing phosphorescence and 
raising PLQY. Thus, understanding and controlling symmetry 
and SOC effects is crucial, especially in Au-rich clusters where 
these effects are pronounced. 
Strategies to engineer radiative versus nonradiative rates in 
clusters largely flow from the above considerations: (i) spin 
management (enhance ISC/phosphorescence or enable TADF): 
to harness triplet emission pathways, incorporate heavy atoms 
or specific dopants to boost SOC. Au-rich or Au-doped clusters 
naturally have faster ISC, populating triplets efficiently. If the goal 
is phosphorescence, one can design for a large ΔEST so that the 
triplet is the clear lowest state (as in M@Au12 superatoms that 
phosphoresce). If the goal is TADF, then arrange donor–acceptor 
orbitals such that ΔEST is minimized, and pair them with mod- 
erate SOC so ISC proceeds while RISC remains efficient. Recent 
advances show that Ag clusters can be tuned into a TADF regime 
by this method. In either case, judicious alloying (adding Au 
or heavy elements) or bonding designs (separating electron and 
hole to tweak ΔEST ) are the tools. (ii) Rigidity and confinement 
(suppress vibrations/IC): a highly effective approach to reduce 
knr is to physically restrict the cluster and its ligands. This can 
be done by using bulkier or multidentate ligands that lock the 
structure, by crystallizing or aggregating the clusters (the essence 
of AIE, see next section), or by embedding clusters in rigid hosts 
like metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) or polymers. All these 
techniques remove low-frequency vibrational modes and raise 
the energy barrier for nonradiative decay. Numerous studies and 
reviews have documented dramatic PLQY enhancements when 
clusters are simply taken from solution and either aggregated or 
crystallized. The cluster’s emissive state is essentially “trapped” in 
a configuration that cannot easily distort, so it has no choice but 
to emit light. This strategy has been successfully applied to Au, 
Ag, and Cu clusters, which are often faint in solution but bright 
in the solid state [ 144 ]. (iii) Interfacial electrostatics and distance 
tuning (optimize core–shell coupling): the strength of core–
shell electronic coupling can be modulated by external fields or 
targeted chemical modification. Bringing ligand orbital energies 
closer to those of the core, or shortening the core–shell separation 
through gentle compression, accelerates the desired CT steps 
that populate the emissive state and reduces energy dissipation 
through structural reorganization. For example, adding a specific 
cation that binds to the ligand shell of [Au25 (SR)18 ]– caused the 
ligands to pack more tightly and increased the effective core–shell 
electronic coupling. The result was a near-unity quantum yield 
because the system quickly funneled excitation into a radiative 
channel with minimal loss [ 127 ]. Essentially, the cluster’s inter- 
face was “hardened” both electronically and mechanically by that 
cation, leading to faster radiative decay and slower nonradiative 
decay. In general, anything that reduces the freedom of the excited 
state to slosh between core and shell (unless intentionally for 
TADF) or to distort will tend to improve PLQY. 
To maximize emission efficiency, one must not only boost 
the radiative rate ( kr ) via SOC or higher overlap, but simul- 
taneously suppress nonradiative decay ( knr ) by rigidifying the 
cluster–ligand interface and engineering the vibrational land- 
scape. Subsequently, we explore concrete manifestations of these 
principles—demonstrating how aggregation and metallophilic 
interactions translate structural control into enhanced emission. 
3.4 
Structural and Environmental Strategies for 
Enhanced Emission 
Translating the above principles into practice, researchers have 
developed several strategies to brighten the emission of MNCs 
by modifying their structure or environment without changing 
their basic composition. The two major themes are: (i) increase 
structural rigidity to suppress nonradiative loss—typically via 
aggregation or host encapsulation, which blocks low-frequency 
motions and strengthens emission, and (ii) amplify orbital over- 
lap and spin–orbit coupling to raise radiative rates–most effec- 
tively by exploiting d10 ⋅⋅⋅d10 metallophilic proximity or applying 
gentle compression, which shortens M ⋅⋅⋅M contacts and blue- 
shifts while brightening the PL. In addition, carefully engineering 
the cluster’s environment (solvent, matrix, or host) can further 
modulate these effects. 
AIE and Rigidification : Many ligand-protected clusters exhibit 
much stronger luminescence in the aggregated or solid state than 
in solution. This classic AIE behavior is a consequence of RIM: 
when clusters pack together (or when their surface ligands inter- 
lock), the floppy vibrational modes that dominate nonradiative 
decay in solution are suppressed. The emissive state, now in a 
stiffer environment, has a longer radiative lifetime relative to 
nonradiative processes, resulting in higher PLQY. In the context 
of metal clusters, AIE can occur via cluster–cluster interactions or 
through intracluster ligand–ligand interactions. High-resolution 
spectroscopic studies have confirmed that, for example, gently 
drying a solution of a weakly luminescent Au25 (SR)18 cluster to 
form a thin film or crystals can increase its emission intensity by 
an order of magnitude or more, purely by removing solvent and 
forcing the clusters into a less dynamic lattice [ 90 ]. Essentially, 
the cluster’s surface “freezes,” and vibrational channels that 
previously quenched the excited state are no longer active. In 
some cases, aggregation can also alter the electronic structure 
slightly, such as by through-space interactions between the 
ligands or metal cores of neighboring clusters. This can create 
new, lower-energy excimer-like excited states or increase the

Three broad practical routes are commonly used to induce AIE in 
atomically precise clusters. First, Lock the ligand shell to impose 
rigidity. Chemical or electrostatic “locking” of the surface (using 
cross-linkers, multidentate ligands, or ionic matrices) stiffens the

intercluster coupling. Packing can either enhance singlet emis-

confinement, locking each cluster within the lattice. Third, Pro-

clamps,” suppressing low-frequency motions and stabilizing the 
emissive geometry. Ag(I)–thiolate clusters exemplify this strategy: 
solution-phase assemblies yield ordered arrays whose emission 
intensities rise by orders of magnitude because each cluster is 
held like a bead on a stiff string [ 147 ]. Across these AIE routes, 
photophysical control comes from supramolecular engineering 
while keeping the cluster composition unchanged. In practice, 
clusters can be synthesized for a target color, then embedded in 
films, crystals, or composites to maximize brightness, decoupling 
intrinsic chromophore design from postassembly rigidification. 
Metallophilicity and pressure serve as proximity-driven routes 
to luminescence brightening. In closed-shell d10 systems 
(Au+ /Ag+ /Cu+ ), short M ⋅⋅⋅M approaches arising from 
relativistically enhanced metallophilic attraction compress 
frontier electron density, strengthen orbital overlap, and increase 
the effective SOC. The resulting electron-cloud contraction 
elevates the transition dipole moment and can simultaneously 
accelerate fluorescence and phosphorescence, a principle 
long exemplified in luminescent Au(I) assemblies and now 
generalized to atomically precise clusters. Under external 
compression—or via packing that enforces closer contacts—
the same proximity effect can be dialed in without altering 
stoichiometry, yielding blue-shifts tied to subtle M–M shortening 
and sizable intensity gains. Recent polymorph-resolved and 
high-pressure studies on Au NCs make this causal chain 
explicit, linking anisotropic compression and shortened Au ⋅⋅⋅Au 
distances to enhanced emission, while broader reviews codify 
metallophilicity as a reliable handle for translating proximity 
into radiative-rate increases in metal-cluster photonics. Direct, 
structure-resolved evidence comes from single-crystal high- 
pressure studies on Au NCs, where a ∼ 0.1 Å Au–Au contraction 
produces a ≈ 30 nm PL blue-shift and brighter emission; theory 
attributes the blue shift to changes in orbital symmetry and the 
intensity increase to pressure-induced conformational locking 
at the metal–ligand interface, confirming in situ that modest 
compression shortens metal–metal distances, strengthens orbital 
overlap and SOC, and thereby increases the radiative rate 
[ 148 ]. Complementary stacking-dependent piezoluminescence 
in polymorphic Au8 clusters demonstrates that reversible 
packing/pressure can tune M ⋅⋅⋅M distances and light output in 
real time, providing a practical, external-field dial on proximity- 
driven photophysics. More broadly, these results distill a general 
design principle: any intervention that slightly strengthens 
metal–metal interactions in the emissive manifold (closer 
contacts via packing, stacking, or hydrostatic pressure) increases 
transition-dipole strength and can unlock otherwise hidden 
radiative channels, while the accompanying lattice stiffening 
curbs vibronic dissipation. Consequently, metallophilicity- 
driven packing and pressure become complementary to AIE-type 
rigidification—one focuses on proximity-enhanced overlap/SOC, 
the other on damping of motion—yielding cooperative gains in 
brightness and color control in atomically precise coinage-metal 
clusters. 
A clear connection between where the valence electrons reside 
and how brightly Ag-cluster superatoms emit has now been 
established. In the Ag25 framework, site-specific substitution at 
the kernel center (Pd, Pt, Au in MAg24 (SR)18 ) modulates PL 
without wholesale structural change, demonstrating that tight- 
ening electron density toward the core correlates with stronger 
emission, whereas a more peripherally distributed electron cloud 
yields dimmer output—an effect captured across crystal and 
solution measurements and consistent with kernel-localized 
superatomic transitions (Figure 11A,B ) [ 94 ]. Complementary X- 
ray/DFT analyses further show that charge distribution within 
Ag25 versus Au25 differs in a way that rationalizes these trends: 
Ag-S bonding is more polar and the Ag kernel donates electron 
density more readily than its Au analogue, so compositional 
edits that bias electron density inward (via a more electroneg- 
ative or heavier center) enhance radiative overlap | μ| and, in 
turn, kr , while outward delocalization promotes nonradiative 
loss. 
It is important to note that AIE-type rigidification and 
metallophilic contraction address different sides of the 
brightness equation: AIE predominantly cuts down knr , 
while metallophilicity boosts kr . Therefore, these approaches 
can be combined synergistically. For instance, one could 
embed a cluster in a MOF that simultaneously immobilizes 
the cluster (AIE effect) and applies a slight compressive 
strain (metallophilic effect), achieving a multiplicative 
enhancement of PLQY. In practice, many high-performance 
cluster-based emitters in the solid state likely benefit from a bit of 
both.

3.5 
Mechanistic Probing via Theory and 
Spectroscopy 
Identifying the exact origin of luminescence in a new NC requires 
a multipronged approach, combining advanced theoretical calcu- 
lations with diverse spectroscopic experiments. On the theoretical 
side, TD-DFT is the primary tool for studying optical excitations 
in atomically precise clusters, due to its balance of accuracy 
and feasibility for systems containing tens to hundreds of atoms. 
Special care is taken in these calculations to handle the peculiar- 
ities of clusters. For example, the choice of exchange-correlation 
functional is critical: standard functionals may underestimate the 
energy of CT states, so range-separated hybrids or long-range- 
tuned functionals are often employed to better describe LMCT 
or MMCT excitations. Including empirical dispersion corrections 
can also improve structural predictions, since ligand packing and 
weak interatomic forces play a role in cluster geometry. SOC 
is another key factor for Au and Ag NCs, where heavy-metal 
atoms introduce strong SOC that affects excited-state energies 
and transition probabilities. Modern TD-DFT codes can incor- 
porate SOC, either perturbatively or self-consistently, allowing 
direct computation of singlet–triplet energy gaps and radiative 
phosphorescence rates. This is essential for understanding phe- 
nomena like TADF or room-temperature phosphorescence in 
these clusters. For cases where DFT’s approximations falter (such 
as when an excited state has strong long-range CT character 
or when the system has near-degenerate states), higher-level 
many-body methods like the GW approximation for quasiparticle 
energies combined with the Bethe–Salpeter equation (BSE) for 
excitons can provide improved accuracy for excitation energies 
and electron–hole interactions, albeit at significantly higher 
computational cost [ 153 ]. These methods are not yet routine for 
large clusters but have been applied selectively to validate key 
states in smaller systems. 
Beyond static calculations, theoretical investigations also extend 
to excited-state dynamics simulations. Methods like fewest- 
switches surface hopping, Redfield density–matrix relaxations, 
or kinetic network models are used to simulate how an excited 
state decays via nonradiative pathways. Such simulations, while 
complex, have been performed on certain smaller clusters to 
estimate IC and ISC rates, complementing experimental lifetime 
measurements. Finally, transition density analysis provides an 
invaluable diagnostic of excited-state nature regardless of the 
electronic structure method. By examining NTOs or plotting 
the spatial distribution of the excited-state electron and hole, 
researchers can visually and quantitatively determine where the 
excitation is located. This analysis distills a possibly multiorbital 
excitation into a dominant hole-electron pair, revealing if an 
excited state is primarily core-centered, LC, or CT in nature 
[ 154 ]. One can further quantify the character by calculating 
the fraction of the NTO electron and hole density residing 
on the metal core versus on the ligands. A near-zero net 
charge separation indicates a locally excited state (core–core or 
ligand–ligand), whereas a large imbalance signifies a CT state. 
Similarly, the overlap between the electron and hole densities 
gives a metric correlated with oscillator strength (as discussed 
in Section 3.3 ). By reporting such metrics, theoretical studies 
provide clear labels for each excited state that aid in mechanism 
assignment. 
On the experimental side, a wide array of steady-state and time- 
resolved spectroscopic techniques is employed to unravel the 
emissive state and dynamics of clusters. Steady-state UV–vis 
absorption and PL spectra are the starting point. The absorption 
spectrum reveals allowed electronic transitions; comparing it 
with the emission spectrum can indicate whether the emitting 
state is the lowest excited state or a different state accessed after 
relaxation. A large Stokes shift often hints that the emissive 
state differs in character from the initially excited states. PLE 
spectroscopy adds further insight by measuring the emission 
intensity as a function of excitation wavelength. If the PLE 
spectrum (for a given emission peak) mirrors the absorption 
spectrum, it implies that excitation anywhere in the absorption 
band eventually relaxes into the same emitting state. In contrast, 
if the PLE spectrum is narrower or highlights only specific absorp- 
tion bands, it means that only certain excited states lead to the 
observed emission. For example, the well-studied [Au25 (SR)18 ]− 
(where SR = thiolate) cluster has a broad absorption onset, but 
the PLE corresponding to its 720 nm NIR emission shows a 
pronounced feature around 520 nm. This indicates that exciting 
the cluster at 520 nm populates a particular state that relaxes 
into the 720 nm emissive state (a core-localized triplet), whereas 
excitation at other wavelengths populates different states that 
emit at a different wavelength. 
Solvatochromism and environmental perturbation studies are 
another steady-state tool: by observing how the emission spec- 
trum shifts or changes intensity in different solvent environments 
or upon adding external species, one can infer the nature of the 
emitting state. A strongly solvent-dependent emission signals a 
polar excited state with CT character, since a polar environment 
stabilizes a separated charge distribution. For instance, if adding 
a small amount of a polar solvent or an electrolyte ion causes the 
cluster’s emission to red-shift or quench, it suggests the emissive 
state involves the cluster’s surface that is sensitive to external 
fields or hydrogen bonding. Temperature-dependent PL mea- 
surements provide complementary clues: a purely core-centered 
excited state typically shows an Arrhenius-type quenching with 
increasing temperature but undergoes minimal spectral shift. In 
contrast, an emissive state with CT character might not only 
change in intensity with temperature but also shift in peak wave- 
length or exhibit an equilibrium between emissive states, because

Time-resolved spectroscopies map the dynamics of excited-state 
relaxation in clusters with nanosecond-to-millisecond resolution. 
TRPL, acquired by time-correlated single-photon counting or fast 
digitizers, often resolves multiple decay components that sig-

TADF via triplet-to-singlet up-conversion. Discrimination relies

responds to magnetic fields, and is sensitive to isotopic substitu-

Ultrafast spectroscopic techniques like femtosecond TA and 
picosecond transient fluorescence up-conversion provide a real- 
time view of excited-state evolution immediately after photon 
absorption. In TA pump-probe experiments, a femtosecond 
pump depletes the ground state and the probe tracks spectral 
changes from femtoseconds to nanoseconds, revealing ground- 
state bleach and new bands from excited species or CT interme- 
diates. For Au25 (SR)18 , TA resolves an instantaneous bleach of the 
core absorption and a near-simultaneous new band assigned to a 
LMCT state within ∼ 1 ps, directly showing ultrafast shell-to-core 
electron transfer that seeds the emissive manifold. Fluorescence 
up-conversion complements TA by resolving prompt emissions 
on tens-of-picoseconds timescales that are invisible to slower 
techniques, capturing early singlet fluorescence before ISC chan- 
nels population into triplets [ 156–158 ]. Together, TA and time- 
resolved PL enable reconstruction of a quantitative Jablonski 
scheme for each cluster, including IC, ISC, radiative decay, and 
nonradiative relaxation with their characteristic time constants. 
Other specialized techniques are also employed as needed. For 
example, measuring the absolute PLQY verifies how efficiently 
the cluster converts absorbed photons into emitted light, which is 
an integral metric for brightness. If a cluster has multiple emissive 
states, quantum yield measurements under different conditions 
can indicate which state is dominant in contributing to emission. 
Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), especially time-resolved 
EPR, can directly detect the presence of long-lived triplet states or 
radical intermediates formed after excitation, lending support to 
proposed mechanisms like charge separation or triplet emission. 
Vibrational spectroscopies (infrared or Raman) can track how the 
cluster’s vibrational modes couple to its electronic transitions; 
for instance, certain Raman bands may change intensity upon 
photoexcitation, indicating which parts of the molecule are 
involved in the excited-state distortion or relaxation. While these 
techniques are valuable, the primary toolkit for mechanism prob- 
ing remains the combination of optical spectroscopy (absorption, 
PL, PLE, TRPL, TA) and quantum-chemical calculations, as they 
most directly reveal the electronic-state landscape and dynamics 
[ 159–162 ]. 
In practice, mechanism assignment follows a coupled theory–
experiment workflow. First, record absorption, emission, and 
PLE for each band to count emissive states and flag large Stokes 
shifts or unusual band-band correlations. Second, measure time- 
resolved PL at multiple wavelengths to expose distinct lifetimes 
and extract radiative times for each channel. Third, run TD-DFT 
with SOC when relevant to obtain energies, oscillator strengths, 
singlet–triplet gaps, and NTOs; classify each low-lying state as 
core-centered, LC, or CT. Fourth, map calculation to data: for 
example, a long-lived, solvent-sensitive band points to a CT 
state, whereas a weakly solvatochromic, very long-lived band 
indicates a core state. In [Au25 (SR)18 ]–, this approach assigns 
the ∼ 520 nm emission to ligand-to-core charge transfer and the 
∼ 720 nm emission to a pure core transition [ 118 ]. Fifth, validate 
by perturbation. Modify ligands to shift or quench shell-involved 
bands; alloy or dope the kernel to tune core bands through the 
superatomic gap; apply pressure or fields to adjust coupling.

By using this comprehensive approach, researchers have solid-

clusters. A case in point is the previously debated dual emission

that the ∼ 520 nm emission originates from a hybridized core–

∼ 720 nm NIR emission comes from a lower-lying pure metal 
core state. Perturbation tests align with this assignment: changing 
the ligand or solvent selectively shifts or quenches the 520 nm 
band, while the 720 nm band remains largely spectrally invariant 
and is affected mainly in intensity by thermal quenching. A 
complementary demonstration of interface control is the near- 
unity PLQY achieved when [Au25 (SR)18 ]– binds a specific cation 
at the ligand periphery: the imposed electrostatic field and steric 
locking increase core–shell orbital overlap, shift the emission 
to higher energy, and shorten the radiative lifetime–evidence 
that the emissive state becomes more core-dominated under 
controlled interfacial hardening [ 127 ].

the percentage of transition density on cluster components or 
the computed singlet–triplet gap. Similarly, experimental studies 
present decay lifetimes, quantum yields, and activation energies 
with appropriate error estimates and fitting models. This level

techniques to new systems, and it facilitates direct comparisons

3.6 
Design Principles for Bright Cluster Emitters 
(Place–Set–Stabilize) 
Building upon the detailed understanding of luminescence mech- 
anisms developed in the earlier sections, we can now formulate 
general design principles for creating bright and tunable emitters 
from atomically precise metal clusters. Here, we present a three- 
part strategy encapsulated as “place–set–stabilize.” In essence, 
one must first place the emissive state appropriately through 
thoughtful cluster composition and structure; next set the balance 
of radiative and nonradiative decay rates by engineering the 
cluster’s electronic properties (tuning orbital overlap, SOC, and 
rigidity via ligand selection, doping, etc., to maximize light 
emission efficiency); and finally stabilize that emissive state in 
practical operating conditions. This framework is informed by 
superatom electronic theory, the photophysical analyses of emis- 
sion processes, and the various case studies we have discussed. 
By applying “place–set–stabilize,” researchers can systematically 
optimize cluster luminophores rather than relying on trial-and- 
error discovery. 
Place the emissive manifold . Use electron counting and ker- 
nel symmetry to position the lowest excited state where you 
want it. Closed-shell superatoms (magic counts 2, 8, 18, 34, 
. . . ) favor core-centered lowest states with large gaps, narrow 
bands, and reduced environmental sensitivity; archetypes include 
[Au25 (SR)18 ]– (8e– on an Au13 core) and Au36 (SR)24 (18e–) [ 163, 
164 ]. By contrast, open-shell counts or motifs that mix core and 
surface orbitals bias the frontier toward the interface and promote 
CT or ligand/surface-based emission; neutral Au25 and Au22 (SG)18 
are illustrative, where the frontier density partly resides on 
staples, yielding red/NIR surface-involved states. In practice, 
composition (including site-specific dopants), size, and symmetry 
are selected to bias the emissive manifold either deep in the core 
for robustness or at the core–shell interface for CT character and 
large Stokes shift [ 165, 166 ]. 
Set the radiative versus nonradiative rates . After placing the lowest 
emissive state, tune its efficiency by increasing the transition 
probability while suppressing vibrational loss. Raise the radiative 
rate by enlarging electron–hole overlap through rational ligand 
electronics and subtle compositional edits: more donating or 
π-rigid ligands (and, where appropriate, judicious heterometal 
doping) shift frontier density toward the metal kernel, strengthen 
the transition dipole, and increase oscillator strength. In parallel, 
manage spin dynamics: in Au/Ag systems, strong SOC can be 
leveraged—via heavy-atom content or site-specific dopants—
to accelerate ISC and render triplet emission allowed, or to 
place the system in a TADF-friendly regime when the singlet–
triplet gap is small. At the same time, minimize nonradia- 
tive decay by suppressing low-frequency modes through shell 
rigidification (multidentate/ π-stiff ligands), controlled aggrega- 
tion/crystallization, confinement in rigid hosts, or electrostatic 
“locking” at the interface; these routes reduce EVC and stabilize 
the emissive geometry, often yielding orders-of-magnitude PLQY 
gains without changing stoichiometry. Together, overlap/SOC 
engineering (to boost kr ) and mechanical/electrostatic stiffen- 
ing (to depress knr ) provide a coordinated pathway to bright, 
room-temperature emission in atomically precise clusters [ 90 ]. 
Stabilize the emissive state under working conditions . Even a bright 
cluster can underperform in bulk or ambient settings unless its 
excited state is protected from quenchers and structural relax- 
ation. The practical remedy is to “lock” the interface by inducing 
controlled aggregation or embedding the cluster in a rigid host, 
thereby suppressing low-frequency motions and environmen- 
tal perturbations. In well-designed solids, many MNCs display 
aggregation- or crystallization-induced emission, where quantum 
yield rises sharply compared with dilute solution because the 
lattice constrains ligand librations and limits solvent-assisted 
dissipation. Matrix strategies achieve the same outcome: thin 
polymer or glassy films restrict structural freedom; host–guest 
scaffolds such as MOFs confine emitters in nanoscopic cages, 
raising PL efficiency and stability while preserving spatial isola- 
tion to avoid concentration quenching; and ionically engineered 
composites can template gentle aggregation without excitonic 
cross-talk, enabling device-ready color-conversion layers. In par- 
allel, proximity dials—short, directional metallophilic contacts or 
modest hydrostatic compression–can contract the electron cloud 
and strengthen core–shell coupling, often yielding blue-shifted, 
brighter emission by increasing orbital overlap and effective SOC. 
Taken together, these stabilization tactics translate the intrinsic 
high kr / knr achieved at the molecular level into durable perfor- 
mance in films and devices by hardening the interface, excluding 
quenchers, and maintaining favorable electronic coupling [ 145 ]. 
By applying the place–set–stabilize framework deliberately, the 
design of bright cluster emitters becomes systematic rather than 
ad-hoc. As a concrete illustration for NIR bioimaging, first place 
the lowest excited state in a cluster-shell CT manifold that 
naturally falls in the 700–800 nm window—for example, select 
an open-shell Au cluster paired with rigid, aromatic thiolates to 
bias LMCT at bio-window wavelengths. Next set the radiative 
balance by tailoring composition and surface: light Ag alloying 
in the Au core can nudge core–shell level alignment so the 
LMCT state gains enough metal character to radiate efficiently 
without drifting out of the target band, while multidentate or 
otherwise rigid ligands suppress low-frequency vibrations that 
feed nonradiative loss; if a triplet pathway is desirable (TADF 
or phosphorescence), introduce a judicious heavy-atom handle 
to enhance ISC. Finally stabilize the emitter in operation by 
encapsulating the cluster in a biocompatible polymer or silica 
matrix that immobilizes the interface and excludes quenchers, 
preserving high PLQY under physiological conditions. More 
broadly, this three-step playbook functions as a concise checklist: 
ensure the emissive state is correctly positioned (place), maximize 
the radiative rate while minimizing internal loss (set), and 
lock in those gains under real-world conditions (stabilize). In 
practice, many recent advances can be read through this lens: 
researchers choose a nuclearity/composition for the desired color, 
tune ligands or dopants to raise kr and curb knr , then embed or 
aggregate to maintain performance at scale. Notably, interfacial 
hardening via external ions exemplifies how “set” and “stabilize”
can merge: stepwise binding of cations to thiolate-protected

4 
Challenges and Future Perspectives 
The preceding sections have established a mechanistic design 
framework for luminescent clusters. Emission states are deter- 
mined by modulating cluster electron count and symmetry; 
excited-state transition rates are governed by SOC strength and 
structural rigidity; and emissive states are stabilized through 
interface engineering and environmental control. The critical 
next step involves translating these mechanistic principles into 
practical systems, transforming exemplar highly luminescent 
clusters into on-demand tunable, performance-stable emissive 
materials for functional devices. Achieving this goal faces several 
unresolved scientific challenges while simultaneously highlight- 
ing breakthrough directions for future research. The following 
sections outline core impediments hindering the application- 
oriented development of luminescent clusters and discuss inno- 
vative strategies and emerging opportunities to overcome these 
barriers. 
4.1 
Key Scientific Challenges 
Luminescence Efficiency Bottleneck : Radiative output in lumi- 
nescent clusters remains severely compromised by dominant 
nonradiative decay pathways, even when the lowest emissive 
state is strategically optimized. Low-frequency vibrational modes 
(e.g., cluster–core breathing vibrations, ligand–stalk oscillations) 
establish efficient IC channels at metal–ligand interfaces, result- 
ing in nonradiative dissipation of excitation energy [ 39, 138 ]. 
This challenge is particularly acute for red/NIR-emitting clusters 
(notably in the NIR-II regime), where narrowing energy gaps 
exponentially enhance nonradiative transition probabilities due 
to the energy gap law. CT-dominated excited states further 
induce interfacial reorganization energy losses. While heavy- 
atom-enhanced SOC facilitates triplet harvesting, suboptimal 
energy-level spacing may accelerate transitions to nonemissive 
states. Solid-state environments introduce additional quenching 
mechanisms including oxygen sensitization and defect-assisted 
deactivation, creating a persistent performance chasm between 
device operational efficiency and solution-phase PLQY measure- 
ments. 
Ambiguous Structure–Property Relationships : A fundamental 
challenge lies in establishing robust mechanistic mappings 
that reliably predict excited-state ordering and transition kinet- 
ics from atomic structures for numerous cluster systems. 
The lack of operando, time-resolved techniques for correlat- 
ing structural dynamics with photophysical responses in situ 
necessitates reliance on ex post facto crystallographic char- 
acterization and steady-state spectroscopy for emission mech- 
anism inference. Crucially, the genuine emissive states typ- 
ically correspond to transient geometries perturbed by pho- 
toexcitation and modulated by solvent/matrix environments. 
Concomitant limitations—including electron beam sensitivity, 
photo-/thermal-induced structural reconstitution, and ensemble 
averaging that masks metastable subpopulations and single- 
particle heterogeneities (such as spectral diffusion, intermittent 
blinking)—further obscure mechanistic interpretations. Con- 
currently, inconsistent reporting standards for critical parame- 
ters impede cross-study comparability and data-driven rational 
design, particularly regarding: effective electron count, singlet–
triplet energy splitting ( ΔEST ), CT character, nuclear-ligand 
shell distance/rigidity metrics, absolute PLQY, and lifetime 
components with associated uncertainties. This methodological 
variability fundamentally obstructs the development of predictive 
structure–property frameworks. 
Scalable Synthesis and Purity Barriers : Scaling luminescent 
cluster synthesis from milligram-level exploration to gram- 
scale manufacturing confronts narrow synthetic windows and 
purification challenges, where reactions exhibit acute sensitivity 
to trace water/halide impurities and require precise etching 
agent spiking protocols to achieve nuclearity focusing. Although 
sequential purification (e.g., ion exchange chromatography) 
yields monodisperse nuclear/charge-state products, this 
process invariably reduces yields while risking ligand shell 
alterations or charge-state modifications. Even after successful 
isolation, maintaining ligand shell integrity, ensuring dopant 
site-specificity, preserving original electronic counts, and 
stabilizing interfacial configurations remain fundamentally 
challenging. Purified clusters further face storage instabilities 
through ligand dissociation or redox degradation, processing 
incompatibilities during solution casting/printing that induce 
aggregation, and critically lack standardized batch-to-batch 
quality control measures—including mass spectrometry 
validation of core composition/charge states and standardized 
quantum yield/lifetime quantification protocols. Concurrently, 
reconciling performance optimization with sustainability 
imperatives necessitates employing earth-abundant metals, 
green ligands/solvents, and recyclable architectures without 
compromising atomic-precision features or luminescent 
efficacy. 
These 
interdependent 
constraints 
collectively 
constitute a multifaceted roadmap for practical cluster 
deployment. 
4.2 
Breakthrough Directions and Strategies 
Data-Driven Inverse Design : Transitioning beyond heuristic 
approaches, theory-guided inverse design, leveraging computa- 
tional modeling and data science, enables on-demand devel- 
opment of luminescent clusters. By quantifying mechanistic 
determinants—including electron count, SOC, structural rigid- 
ity, and interfacial fields–as tunable parameters, researchers 
implement multialgorithm workflows. For core-centered emis- 
sion, evolutionary algorithms optimize ligand scaffolds under 
steric/electronic constraints, while for LMCT or surface-centered 
emission, Bayesian optimization or active learning efficiently 
navigates multidimensional reaction spaces, guiding ligand mod- 
ifications or doping strategies. Surrogate models trained on 
limited high-fidelity computations (such as TD-DFT/GW-BSE- 
derived bandgaps, CT integrals, ΔEST , vibronic couplings) predict 
photophysical properties, directly aiding experimental synthesis 
and characterization of clusters. Reinforcement learning further 
proposes site-specific heterometal doping sites and core–shell

In Situ, Time-Resolved Structural Characterization : Because the 
emissive state is set by photoinduced, transient geometry and 
interfacial configuration, structure and photophysics must be 
probed simultaneously under relevant timescales. Emerging 
techniques like low-dose electron diffraction and 4D-STEM 
capture short-range order, strain, and orientation fields of 
both the kernel and staple-like surface motifs under reduced 
beam damage. Operando SAXS/WAXS and X-ray absorption 
(EXAFS/XANES) track core–shell distances, local coordination, 
and oxidation states under controlled ionic strength, tempera- 
ture, and atmosphere, thereby correlating structure with elec-

diffraction connects lattice dynamics—such as breathing modes 
and cooperative Jahn–Teller distortions—to early-time carrier 
redistribution. Temperature-dependent TRPL and TA provide 
detailed insights into radiative and nonradiative rates, as well 
as intersystem-crossing kinetics, enabling a direct map from 
structural perturbation to emission outcomes. Standardizing 
these techniques will provide a routine workflow for studying 
luminescent mechanisms in NCs. 
Integrated Multifunctional Luminescent Cluster Design : By tuning 
atom-level parameters (electron count, SOC, symmetry, and inter- 
facial fields), MNCs can be endowed with additional functions 
such as magnetism and catalysis while retaining high-quality 
emission. Magnetic luminescent clusters can be designed by 
doping heterometals at symmetry-privileged sites to introduce 
localized unpaired electrons, making the material magnetically 
addressable and emissive. Chirality engineering via macrocyclic 
ligands enables circularly CPL and creates catalytic “pockets”
for asymmetric transformations. Tuning metal–metal and LMCT 
states can couple robust photophysics with photocatalytic activity, 
where intrinsic emission serves as an in situ reporter for catalytic 
processes. Structurally, embedding atomically precise cluster 
units into ordered frameworks or polymers, and orienting them 
into microcrystals or MOF-based hybrids, preserves molecular- 
level uniformity while imparting macroscopic functions such 
as waveguiding, sensing, and catalysis. This integrated strategy 
enables simultaneous light transport and modulation, reaction 
driving and monitoring, and it does so without sacrificing 
atomic-scale controllability. 
In designing brighter clusters, strategies should be tailored to 
the emission mechanism. For core-centered emissive states, tight- 
ening the metal kernel and incorporating heavy-metal dopants 
can boost radiative decay (via stronger SOC) while suppress- 
ing distortion-related losses. In contrast, clusters with LMCT 
or surface-centered emission benefit from enhanced core–shell 
electronic coupling and a rigidified ligand environment—for 
example, cross-linking or aggregating ligands to immobilize the 
emissive state. Finally, systems exhibiting delayed or dual emis- 
sion (e.g., TADF or combined fluorescence/phosphorescence) 
demand balancing the singlet–triplet energy gap: moderate gap 
coupled with heavy-atom presence allows efficient ISC and 
reverse crossing, maximizing output. 
5 
Conclusions 
The collective evidence indicates that atomically precise MNCs 
emit light via multiple intertwined pathways. In general, lumines- 
cence can originate from electronic transitions within the metal 
kernel (a cluster- or core-centered process) or from the surround- 
ing ligand shell through aggregation-induced mechanisms, or 
from CT states involving ligands and metal atoms. For example, 
thiolate-bound Au25 (SR)18 clusters exhibit PL predominantly via 
LMCT along the Au-S bonds [ 38 ]. Similarly, restriction of ligand 
motion can induce AIE wherein surface motifs (e.g., Au(I)–
thiolate or Ag(I)–thiolate complexes) become primary emissive 
centers [ 145–147 ]. In some Cu(I) clusters the emission mechanism 
has been explicitly shown to combine a metal-CC excited state 
with a triplet metal–ligand charge transfer component [ 170 ]. 
Taken together, these insights support a dual-emission model 
in which MNCs possess coupled core and shell states. Under 
photoexcitation an MNC can access two distinct excited-state 
manifolds (such as a short-lived fluorescence and a longer-lived 
phosphorescence) that both contribute to the observed emission. 
This core–shell coupling rationalizes the frequent observation of 
dual or multipeak PL in Au, Ag, and Cu clusters, which can be 
sensitively tuned by solvent, temperature, or ligand environment. 
The rich structural chemistry of Au, Ag, and Cu clusters underlies 
their diverse photophysics. Au clusters often adopt well-defined 
Aun (SR)m cores with Au(I)–thiolate staple shells, and both the 
inner kernel and the outer shell can participate in light emission. 
For instance, engineering the surface staples of an Au28 (SR)20 pair 
dramatically altered its quantum yield by modulating the core 
electron relaxation dynamics [ 171 ]. Ag clusters, especially those 
stabilized by thiols or phosphines, frequently display intense 
AIE-type PL arising from Agx –ligand complexes or metal–metal 
interactions [ 18, 74 ]. Cu(I) clusters likewise emit across the 
visible spectrum—often via d10 -d10 cuprophilic transitions and 
LC triplet states—yielding multicolor luminescence and large 
Stokes shifts. Alloy clusters offer further tunability: for exam- 
ple, Au–Ag and Au–Cu doping can shift emission wavelengths 
and enhance ISC. Notably, recent work achieved near-100% 
NIR quantum yield in a doped Au16 Cu6 cluster by suppressing 
nonradiative decay and promoting triplet emission [ 100 ]. In 
general, size and composition dictate a cluster’s optical bandgap 
and excited-state lifetimes, providing structural benchmarks for 
luminescence: Au clusters tend to emit from visible to NIR-II 
as size increases, Ag clusters can display efficient blue-green 
to orange emission, and Cu clusters often occupy the orange- 
red region, all adjustable by ligand chemistry and heterometal 
incorporation. 
These tunable luminescence properties make MNCs attractive 
for many applications. Their atomically precise, molecular-like 
nature confers high stability, large Stokes shifts and long lifetimes. 
which are ideal for fluorescent probes. Accordingly, MNCs have 
been developed as biocompatible optical reporters for in vitro 
and in vivo imaging, bio-sensing, and diagnostics [ 172, 173 ]. They 
also serve as ratiometric and turn-on sensors (through dual- 
emission and FRET designs) for ions, molecules, and biological 
markers. In photonics and optoelectronics, cluster emitters have 
appeared in light-emitting devices and nonlinear optics [ 174, 
175 ]. For example, chiral Au–Cu clusters were incorporated into

Despite rapid advances, several key challenges remain. First, 
achieving bright emission in the deep-red and NIR-II window 
is still difficult for many clusters. Although doping strategies 
have yielded nearly unity quantum yields in specific NIR- 
emissive clusters, general design rules for high-efficiency long- 
wavelength luminescence are lacking. Second, fully mapping 
the structure–property landscape under real operating conditions 
is an open problem. Because cluster emission can be sensi- 
tive to environment and aggregation state, operando and in 
situ spectroscopy will be essential to connect atomic structure, 
electron dynamics, and photophysical output under realistic 
excitation and working conditions. Third, scalable synthesis 
with atomic precision remains elusive: large-scale, reproducible 
routes to yield grams of defect-free clusters (especially multi- 
metal variants) are still under development. Progress in stan- 
dardized ligand-design, flow or photochemical synthesis, and 
assembly methods will be critical for translation beyond the 
laboratory. 
In summary, recent studies have converged on a unified view that 
MNC luminescence arises from coupled core and shell excited 
states, mediated by CT interactions and AIE effects. This picture 
reconciles many prior observations and provides a framework 
for rational design. Going forward, closing the remaining gaps 
in emission efficiency (especially in the red/NIR-II), in operando 
structure–function understanding, and in scalable atomically 
precise fabrication will be key to unlocking the full potential of 
luminescent metal clusters in imaging, sensing, optoelectronics, 
and other photonic technologies.

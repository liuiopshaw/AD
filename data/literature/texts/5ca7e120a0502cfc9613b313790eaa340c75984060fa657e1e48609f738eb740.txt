A B S T R A C T
Pathogenic bacteria resistance to antimicrobial agents has emerged as a major health issue. Developing new
systems enabling inhibitory and bactericidal effects is crucial. Herein, layer-by-layer technique (LbL) was used to
fabricate microcapsules composed of biopolymers, cationic polycyclodextrin (P(CD+)) and anionic alginate
(alg−). This was achieved through successive adsorption of these polyelectrolytes onto calcium carbonate mi-
croparticles (CaCO3) used as template. Then, a chelating agent was added for removing the CaCO3 core, leading
to microcapsules. Quartz crystal microbalance and zeta potential measurements evidenced the successful al-
ternate adsorption of polyelectrolytes and related charge reversal, respectively, upon the LbL deposition. The
shape of the capsules was characterized by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and fluorescence microscopy.
The antibacterial activity of P(CD+) was first evidenced on two well-known bacteria: Staphylococcus aureus
(Gram-positive) and Escherichia coli (Gram-negative) using well diffusion method on Plate Count Agar (PCA)
medium. Then, an inhibitory effect on the bacteria growth has been observed for the P(CD+)-based micro-
capsules during bacterial culture in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth medium. Our results pointed out towards a strong
dependence of the extent of inhibition on the number of self-assembled polyelectrolytes' layers and capsules’
concentration. The effect increased with both concentration and number of layers. Acridine orange staining
evidenced the bactericidal effect of P(CD+)-based microcapsules. This contribution demonstrates un-
ambiguously that: i) aqueous solutions of P(CD+) exhibit antibacterial activity at physiological pH suggesting
that P(CD+) can act as a versatile alternative to the widely studied chitosan whose antimicrobial activity is
limited to acidic pH and ii) (P(CD+)/alg−)n hollow capsules can be exploited as an antibacterial agents with
additional host properties.
1. Introduction
Human beings are often impacted by microorganisms such as bac-
teria, fungi and viruses(Rosenberg, Koren, Reshef, Efrony, & Zilber-
Rosenberg, 2007). Furthermore, contamination by microorganisms
leads to severe problems in food and pharmaceutical industries where
microbes may contaminate raw materials, products and equipments
(Marambio-Jones & Hoek, 2010).
Antimicrobial agents can be regarded as materials that kill or simply
inhibit the growth of microorganisms with minimal or no harm to the
host. Various mechanisms of microorganism’ inhibition have been dis-
cussed to date, of which are intimately related to the chemical com-
position
and
structure
(organic,
inorganic,
charged
…)
of
the
antimicrobials, each exhibiting advantages and limitations(Batoni,
Maisetta, Lisa Brancatisano, Esin, & Campa, 2011).
The antimicrobial activity of cationic polymers is generally assigned
to the interaction of the positive charge moiety of the polymer with the
charged surface of bacteria cell walls. Natural and synthetic nanoma-
terials have also been widely employed due to their strong anti-
microbial properties inherent to their unique properties in terms of
adsorption and surface area (Chirkov, 2002; Wei et al., 1994). On this
basis, the self-assembly of polymers including cationic polyelectrolytes
(Kiss et al., 2012) or peptides (H. Wang et al., 2010) into nano-anti-
microbial agents has been proved efficient for infections treatment (Liu
et al., 2009; H.; Wang et al., 2010).

The loading and release properties can be easily adjusted by controlling

Because of their low toxicity toward mammalian cells, intrinsic
environmentally-friendly nature and effective antimicrobial properties,
biopolymers could be commonly used in consumer products (Cha &
Chinnan, 2004). Among biopolymers, chitosan has been widely used as
a bioactive polysaccharide taking advantage of its inhibitory and bac-
tericidal effect while exhibiting minimal toxicity (Sudarshan, Hoover, &
Knorr, 1992). However, the antibacterial activity of chitosan is pro-
moted only under its cationic form limiting its application to acidic
medium (Divya, Vijayan, George, & Jisha, 2017). The mode of action of
chitosan-based antimicrobial agents has been explained through dif-
ferent mechanisms. The first one is based on the electrostatic attraction
between polycationic chitosan and the negatively charged membrane of
the bacterial cell, ultimately leading to intracellular components dis-
ruption (Qi, Xu, Jiang, Hu, & Zou, 2004). The second one implies in-
hibition of the enzymatic activity and bacterial growth through chela-
tion of trace metals by chitosan (Rabea, Badawy, Stevens, Smagghe, &
Steurbaut, 2003). Antimicrobial efficiency of chitosan and chitosan-
based nanostructures is highly influenced by pH. Indeed, because
chitosan is soluble in acid pH, its use is restricted to a limited range of
pH and application.
In this context, we investigate herein the possibility to use a water-
soluble cationic polycyclodextrin (PCD+) bearing permanent qua-
ternary ammonium charges, as a new antimicrobial agent. PCD+ will
then be applied to the elaboration of microcapsules as a new approach
to prevent the growth of two models of pathogenic bacteria, Escherichia
coli and Staphylococcus aureus (Goy, Britto, & Assis, 2009). In the lit-
erature, two strategies are described to obtain cyclodextrin-containing
LbL assemblies. The first one involves the use of charged cyclodextrins
(CD), and the LbL assemblies are prepared through electrostatic inter-
actions (Sato, Suzuki, & Anzai, 2003, 2004; Yang, Johnson, Shi,
Holesinger, & Swanson, 1997). The second strategy for constructing CD-
containing LbL assemblies relies on host–guest complexation between
guest molecules and CD acting as the driving force for the assembly
build-up (Dubacheva et al., 2010; Kaftan et al., 2011; Suzuki, Egawa,
Mizukawa, Hoshi, & Anzai, 2002; Van der Heyden, Wilczewski, Labbe,
& Auzely, 2006). It deserves noting that ionic polymers of cyclodextrin
have already successfully been used for elaborating multilayer films on
flat supports (Jatupol et al., 2016; Junthip, Tabary, Leclercq, & Martel,
2015), (Jatupol et al., 2016), (Martin, Tabary, Chai, et al., 2013; Martin,
Tabary, Leclercq, et al., 2013), (Martin, Tabary, Leclercq, et al., 2013).,
(Pérez-Anes et al., 2015).

2. Experimental section
2.1. Materials
These chemicals were purchased and used as received: chitosan
(chi+, Sigma-aldrich), alginate (alg−, Sigma-aldrich), acetic acid (Alfa
aesar), tocopherol acetate (Sigma Aldrich), calcium chloride (CaCl2,
rectapur, 99%), sodium nitrate (rectapur, 99%), sodium 2-mercap-
toethanesulfonate (Sigma-Aldrich) and sodium acetate (Normapur).
2.2. Preparation of cationic polycyclodextrin (P(CD+))
The cationic polycyclodextrin (P(CD+)) was prepared by using the
procedure developed by Renard et al. (Renard, Deratani, Volet, &
Sebille, 1997) and Thuaud et al.(Thuaud, Sebille, Deratani, & Lelievre,
1991) The cationic cyclodextrin polymer was synthesized through the
crosslinking of β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) with epichlorohydrin (Renard
et al., 1997) followed by reacting the obtained copolymer with 2,3
epoxypropyltrimethylammonium
chloride
(Thuaud
et
al.,
1991).
Briefly, β-CD in NaOH solution (33%) was allowed to react with epi-
chlorohydrin for 210 min at 50 °C. After neutralizing the pH of the re-
active mixture, the polymer was concentrated by ultrafiltration to re-
move salt and further low molecular compounds and further recovered
through freeze-drying. The resulting β-CD polymer was chemically
modified with quaternary ammonium side groups through reaction
with 2,3-epoxypropyltrimethylammonium chloride in aqueous basic
solution. The resulting modified polymer was purified by a dialysis step
using a cellulose dialysis membrane (1 kDa cut off). The product was
isolated via freeze-drying to yield a white solid. The characteristics of
the involved polyelectrolytes are given in Table 1.
2.3. Elaboration of cationic-polycyclodextrin-based multilayered film

the polycation. After introducing in the QCM flow chamber, the surface
was flushed with the buffer for 10 min. The self-assembly of P(CD)+/
alg−was performed onto flat gold substrate via alternative contact with
the polyelectrolyte solutions for 15 min. A 10 min rinsing step was

2.4. Elaboration of biopolymer microcapsules based on cationic-
polycyclodextrin
The elaboration of the cationic-polycyclodextrin/alginate (P(CD+)/
alg−) bilayered capsules consists in the successive deposition of this
polyelectrolytes pair onto calcium carbonate microparticles followed by
the template removal. More precisely, CaCO3 microparticles were fab-
ricated
according
to
Volodkin
et
al.
(Volodkin,
Larionova,
&
Sukhorukov, 2004; Volodkin, Petrov, Prevot, & Sukhorukov, 2004)
Equal volume of 0.33 M calcium chloride (CaCl2) and sodium carbonate
(Na2CO3) solutions were mixed under a vigorous stirring for 3 min. This
method led to a rapid precipitation of the CaCO3 microparticles. A
centrifugation process of the suspension during 10 min allows removal
of the residual ions. Lastly, the resulting particles were rinsed several
times with water. Acetone was used to remove the water. Particles were
dried under a stream of filtered air. The LbL technique was used to
prepare
the
multilayer
films
from
1 mg mL−1
polycation
and
1 mg mL−1 alginate solutions. 2 mg of CaCO3 particles were incubated
in 1 mL of water. The first layer of P(CD+) was deposited onto the
surface of the calcium carbonate particles by adding 500 μL of this
polycation stock solution to the particles suspension. A stirring time of
15 min is sufficient to allow the polymer adsorption onto the surface of
the particles as deduced from the QCM investigations. The final volume
was always kept at 1 mL. The particles were then centrifuged (5 min,
10 000 rpm) and washed three times. The particles were suspended in
water and 500 μL of alginate was added to the particles suspension and
the mixture was stirred for 15 min for alginate deposition on the pre-
vious P(CD+) layer. Note that the centrifugation method is used to
separate the free polyelectrolyte from polyelectrolyte-coated micro-
particles, then after each polyelectrolyte adsorption step. The particles
were then rinsed three times. This overall process describes the as-
sembly of a single bilayer: (cationic-polycyclodextrin/alginate)1 and
was repeated to obtain different numbers of assembled bilayers. Finally,
the CaCO3 microparticles were removed with 0.2 M EDTA solution. To
do this, the coated CaCO3 microparticles were shaken for 1 h with the
EDTA solution. A dialysis method was applied to purify the capsules for
three days using a cellulose dialysis bag (3.5 kDa cut off) against dis-
tilled water. Water was refreshed every hour. The procedure is slightly
adapted when using chitosan instead of cationic-polycyclodextrin. In-
deed, chitosan is solubilized in 10 mM acetate buffer pH 4.
2.5. Microbial studies
Staphylococcus aureus ATCC6538 (Gram-positive) and Escherichia
coli ATCC25922 (Gram-negative) were used. Prior to the experiment,
bacteria were grown aerobically at 37 °C overnight in agitated
(150 rpm) liquid Luria-Bertani broth medium (LB, Sigma Aldrich).
2.5.1. Antibacterial activity of polymers and well diffusion method
Antibacterial activities of the studied polymers were evaluated
using well diffusion method in Petri dish on solid Plate Count Agar
(PCA). The diameter of inhibition zones (including the diameter of the
well) was measured in centimeter (cm). Samples from overnight cul-
tures of each bacteria were diluted in physiological solution (9 g.L−1
NaCl) to obtain a bacterial suspension of the two different strains
(OD620nm = 0.5). Note that for enterobacteria as E. coli (Escherichia coli)
and S. aureus (Staphylococcus aureus), 1 OD at 620 nm is equivalent to
8 × 108 CFU/mL (Willey et al., 2008). 100 μL of this suspension was
inoculated onto the surface of a PCA Petri dish. Four wells (dia-
meter = 6 mm) were done in the agar surface and were filled with
20 μL of antimicrobial samples (1 mg mL−1). The antimicrobial samples
were diluted from stock solution of each polymer. The stock solutions
are: P(CD) (10 mg mL−1), P(CD+) (10 mg mL−1), chi+(7 mg mL−1) and
alg−(10 mg mL−1). A total of four tests (three replicates of each sample
and one filled with physiological solution as reference (9 g.L−1 NaCl in
distilled water) was done into each plate. After 24 h of incubation at
37 °C in aerobic conditions, the diameters of inhibition around the well
were measured.
2.5.2. Antibacterial effect of (P(CD+)/alg−)n bilayer-based microcapsules:
inhibition of bacterial growth method
Inhibitory impact of microcapsules on growth of E. coli and S. aureus
was studied during kinetic growth under shaking in liquid Luria-Bertani
broth medium (LB) at 37 °C. For the assay, eight parallel wells of a 96-
well microplate were used (eight replicates). Microcapsules suspension
of each compound described above (section 2.4) at various concentra-
tions of 0.02 g.L−1, 0.06 g.L−1 and 0.1 g.L−1 was added to a same
bacterial suspension and then incubated at 37 °C. The final volume is
200 μL for each well. The initial optical density of bacterial suspension
was 0.05 at 620 nm. Sterile physiological distilled water was mixed
with a bacterial suspension as a control bacterial growth test. Bacterial
growth was followed periodically by reading the optical density of
bacterial suspensions at 620 nm. To confirm the results obtained
through optical density measurement, bacteria were counted with a
Mallassez cell.
2.5.3. Bacterial viability assay: microscopic observation
The effect of microcapsules on bacterial viability was assessed using
an orange acridine test followed by fluorescence microscopy observa-
tion. 10 μL of acridine orange (0.1 % wt) was added to 100 μL of bac-
terial suspension taken at the end of the antibacterial assay (described
in section 2.5.2). A 10 μL drop was then introduced on a cover slip to
observe the bacteria (Boulos, Prevost, Barbeau, Coallier, & Desjardins,
1999).
2.6. Loading of hydrophobic agent
A solution of tocopherol acetate (5 mg/mL) was added to a sus-
pension of (P(CD+)/alg−)n microcapsules (n = 1 to 4). Then, the sus-
pensions were stirred for 24 h to ensure effective tocopherol acetate
complexation. The samples were introduced in a dialysis bag (3.5 kDa
cut off) to remove unloaded tocopherol acetate. The loaded micro-
capsules were subjected to absorbance measurement at 292 nm.

3. Results and discussion
Fig. 1 illustrates the approach used to prepare CD-containing mi-
crocapsules. The surface of CaCO3 microparticles was modified through
alternate deposition of the positively charged cationic polycyclodextrin
and a layer of negatively charged alginate. The chemical structures of
the P(CD+), chitosan and alginate are presented in Fig. 2. Their char-
acteristics are reported in Table 1. For the alginate, the Mn value is 2.7
105 g mol−1, the Mw value is 3.8 105 g mol−1. For the cationic poly
(cyclodextrin), the Mn value is 6.5 103 g mol−1, the Mw value is 2.4
104 g mol−1 and the degree of substitution is 0.40 ± 0.05. For the
chitosan, the Mn value is 8.5 104 g mol−1, the Mw value is 2.0
105 g mol−1. The degree of acetylation of chitosan is 0.14 ± 0.02.
After each coating step, free or unbound polyelectrolytes are removed
by centrifugation to prevent the formation of inter-polyelectrolyte
complexes. After the LbL process, the calcium carbonate template is
selectively removed to ensure the capsule formation. Herein, we com-
pare the antimicrobial agent performances of the cationic poly-
cyclodextrin to the well-known chitosan. Thus, the antibacterial activ-
ities of P(CD+) and chitosan, under its cationic form, are initially tested
in aqueous solutions, to provide comparative data for the intrinsic an-
tibacterial activity of the said polymers. Then, P(CD+) and chitosan
were layer-by-layer assembled with alginate onto CaCO3 core, and the
resulting multi-layered capsules were subjected to antibacterial assay.
3.1. Characterization of microcapsules based on cationic polycyclodextrin
The assembly process was first monitored on QCM resonators to
evidence the mass increase after each deposition step of each poly-
electrolyte (Fig. 3). (P(CD+)/alg−) and (chi+/alg−) have been studied
as polyelectrolytes couples. From the QCM frequency shift plots, we
observe a decrease of the frequency shift which could be correlated to
an increase of mass due to the successive polyelectrolytes adsorption.
The final frequency as measured after the adsorption of 7 layers is
around −75 Hz when using P(CD+) as polycation. This value reaches
−225 Hz with chi+ as polycation.
Fig. 3 B presents the equilibrium frequency shift as measured after
each polyelectrolyte adsorption. Two growth regimes are evidenced
from these plots, an exponential growth and a linear one. The ex-
ponential behavior is observed by employing chitosan as polycation.
This result suggests the ability of the chitosan to diffuse “in” and “out”
of multilayer films (Lavalle et al., 2004). This is in agreement with the
work of Richert et al. (Richert et al., 2004) who studied chitosan/
hyaluronan films. In contrast, the multilayer film based on the cationic
polycyclodextrin shows a linear build-up process. Using this polycation
instead of chitosan may lead to a decrease in the diffusion phenomena
and consequently to a linear variation of the thickness with the number
of layer.

The impact of cationic polycyclodextrin on the two pathogenic en-
vironmental bacteria E. coli and S. aureus, was investigated in agar
plates and then in liquid LB medium. E. coli is a Gram-negative bac-
terium which could be found in gastrointestinal tract normal flora. S.
aureus is a Gram-positive bacterium that can induce skin infection and
pus.
Results from antibacterial tests showed no inhibition zone for the
unmodified native polycyclodextrin. Interestingly, the modification of
the polycyclodextrin (incorporation of cationic charge) leads to an in-
hibition zone for both bacteria. This could directly be correlated to the
incorporation of the quaternary ammoniums known to be responsible of
damage of the microbial membranes and of denaturation of the soluble
proteins. This denaturation can disrupt cell functions and induce bac-
terial death (Kügler, Bouloussa, & Rondelez, 2005). Diameter of in-
hibition zones was around 1.2 and 1.1 cm ± 0.1 for E. coli and 1.4 and
1.1 cm ± 0.1 for S. aureus with chitosan and cationic polycyclodextrin
respectively (Fig. 6).
The results obtained with chitosan, are in agreement with those
reported by Li et al. (Li et al., 2007) and Du et al. (Du, Zhao, Dai, &
Yang, 2009) These authors suggested that this polysaccharide usually
presents higher antibacterial activities against S. aureus than E. coli.
Compared to chitosan, P(CD +) shows a lower efficiency on S. aureus
(Fig. 6) and a same inhibitory effect against E. coli. P(CD +) provided
the same inhibition toward E. coli and S. aureus. This similar effect on
these two bacteria suggests that the structure of the bacterial wall is not
involved in inhibitory mechanisms. Like quaternary ammonium-
containing materials, P(CD +) may cause the denaturation of bacterial
cytoplasmic proteins (Willey et al., 2008). It is worth noting that no
antimicrobial effect is observed with the alginate.
The bacterial growth kinetics was followed in LB medium (initial
bacterial concentration 6.4 × 107 cell/mL) in the presence of different
composition and concentration of microcapsules. Each experiment was
performed in six replicates (Fig. 7). Note that no inhibition was ob-
served with bare calcium carbonate particles at the studied particle
concentration. The 7 h (420 min) experimental time for monitoring the
growth curve has been selected as it is assumed that the physico-
chemical conditions of the medium, such as pH, does not significantly
change during this period. Therefore, the presence of microcapsules is
the sole parameter which can impact the bacteria growth.
To investigate the influence of several particles parameters (num-
bers of layers, concentration of particles …) on the bacteria growth, the
percentage of bacterial inhibition at the end of the experiment was
estimated (see Table 2). Whatever the strain studied, (P(CD+)/alg−)3P
(CD+) particles inhibit the growth rate more than single-layer particles
(P(CD+)/alg−)P(CD+). For instance, at 0.02 g.L−1 the percentage of
inhibition for E. coli is 8.9% and 28.1% for (P(CD+)/alg−)P(CD+) and
(P(CD+)/alg−)3P(CD+) respectively. The same trend is observed for S.
aureus, i.e. 15.6% and 42.2% for (P(CD+)/alg−)P(CD+) and (P(CD+)/
alg−)3P(CD+) respectively (Table 2). The bacterial density reached at
the end of the experiment is lower in the presence of capsules. For E.
coli the following OD values are measured at the end of the experiment

tion and other protein denaturation (Nair et al., 2009). In the future,
biochemical analysis and electron microscopy would allow us to know

Note that for enterobacteria as E. coli and S. aureus, 1 OD at 620 nm
is equivalent to 8 × 108 CFU/mL (Willey et al., 2008).
From the above results, we have demonstrated that the micro-
caspules inhibit significantly the growth of both bacteria. The following
question is then raised, are the bacteria alive or not? Several research
works have demonstrated that the color of bacteria stained with acri-
dine orange (AO) allows discriminating between living bacteria and
dead bacteria (Francisco, Mah, & Rabin, 1973). This relationship be-
tween their activity and the color of acridine orange-stained bacteria is
based upon the in vitro spectrofluorescence of nucleic acids (DNA) with
AO (Rigler, 1969). Indeed, acridine orange is a cell-permeant nucleic
acid binding dye and is able to differentially double-stranded (ds) and
single-stranded (ss) nucleic acids. AO emits red fluorescence when
bound to ssDNA denatured DNA found in dead bacteria and green
fluorescence when bound to ds DNA characterizing living bacteria.
Herein, a red fluorescence is observed after the bacteria staining (Fig. 8)
evidencing that exposition to capsule (P(CD+)/alg−)P(CD+) or capsule
(P(CD+)/alg−)3P(CD+) induce the death of bacterial cells. It deserves
noting that a green fluorescence is observed for the S. aureus without
capsule (negative control, Fig. 8 A), pointing that the bacteria are alive
with no capsule. Note that the test the acridine orange is a qualitative
test of observations and not a quantitative one. The number of living
bacteria in suspension was then determined by counting them with a
Mallassez cell (Willey et al., 2008). It should be noted here that no
changes in shape of bacteria are observed suggesting that the effect of
the microcapsules is not exerted on the bacterial structure. Thus, herein
the quaternary ammonium-containing microparticles would rather in-
duce inhibition and denaturation of bacterial proteins (Willey et al.,
2008). Furthermore, the death of bacteria cells was qualitatively
checked. At the end of the experiment, we have inoculated bacterial
suspension on PCA solid medium for growth overnight in aerobic
conditions at 37 °C. No bacterial colonies were observed which confirms
the death of the bacteria due to their contact with microcapsules.
After clearly evidencing the interest in modifying polymer based on
cyclodextrin to bring antibacterial activity, we have investigated the
possibility to still exploit the host−guest complexation in the particle's
shell. The studied hydrophobic compound is the tocopherol acetate, an
antioxidant molecule. Interestingly, in water, the solubility of toco-
pherol acetate increased linearly (from 0 to 0.014 M) as the number of
the cationic-poly (cyclodextrin) layers increased (from 1 to 4) for a
concentration of microcapsules of 2 mg mL−1 (Fig. 9).
4. Conclusion
For antibacterial purposes, we first evidenced the possibility to use a
water-soluble cationic polycyclodextrin bearing permanently charged
quaternary ammonium groups rather than the cationic chitosan. This
latter has low solubility in water as well as in physiological conditions.
Then, P(CD+)-microcapsules were prepared through an easy and effi-
cient approach based on template-assisted self-assembly of polyelec-
trolytes in aqueous solutions without employing neither organic sol-
vents or surfactants. This process, followed by zeta measurements,
relied on the capacity of the cationic-polycyclodextrin to be layer-by-
layer assembled with alginate onto sacrificial carbonate microparticles.
The SEM and fluorescence microscopy results disclosed capsules for-
mation. These polyelectrolytes-coated particles have then been evi-
denced to inhibit growth and eliminate pathogenic bacteria.
The following conclusions were drawn from this work: (i) cationic
polycyclodextrin presents an antibacterial activity which could only be
correlated to the incorporation of positive charges in the poly-

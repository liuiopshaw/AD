ABSTRACT: Periodontitis, one of the most common, challenging,
and rapidly expanding oral diseases, is an oxidative stress-related
disease caused by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS)
production. Developing ROS-scavenging materials to regulate the
periodontium microenvironments is essential for treating periodonti-
tis. Here, we report on creating cobalt oxide-supported Ir (CoO−Ir)
as a cascade and ultrafast artificial antioxidase to alleviate local tissue
inflammation and bone resorption in periodontitis. It is demonstrated
that the Ir nanoclusters are uniformly supported on the CoO lattice,
and there is stable chemical coupling and strong charge transfer from
Co to Ir sites. Benefiting from its structural advantages, CoO−Ir
presents cascade and ultrafast superoxide dismutase-catalase-like
catalytic activities. Notably, it displays distinctly increased Vmax
(76.249 mg L−1 min−1) and turnover number (2.736 s−1) when
eliminating H2O2, which surpasses most of the by-far-reported artificial enzymes. Consequently, the CoO−Ir not only provides
efficient cellular protection from ROS attack but also promotes osteogenetic differentiation in vitro. Furthermore, CoO−Ir can
efficiently combat periodontitis by inhibiting inflammation-induced tissue destruction and promoting osteogenic regeneration.
We believe that this report will shed meaningful light on creating cascade and ultrafast artificial antioxidases and offer an
effective strategy to combat tissue inflammation and osteogenic resorption in oxidative stress-related diseases.
KEYWORDS: cascade enzyme-mimics, artificial antioxidases, reactive oxygen species, periodontitis, bone regeneration
INTRODUCTION
Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease with an
incidence of over 40% worldwide and has become one of the
most common, challenging, and rapidly expanding oral diseases
in recent years.1 It has been considered the main cause of tooth
loss and is also connected with several system diseases, such as
atherosclerosis and diabetes.2 Currently, the main strategy for
treating periodontitis is to prevent disease progression,
including mechanical debridement, antibiotics, anti-inflamma-
tory drugs, and surgical techniques.3 However, the recon-
struction of the periodontal tissue structure is hard to realize
since the local infection and inflammation are difficult to
eliminate, which will activate the immune cells to overproduce
abundant reactive oxygen species (ROS).4 The high-level ROS
will directly damage the cells and tissues through the
peroxidation of lipids, proteins, and DNA.5 Additionally,
ROS mediates many physiological and pathophysiological
signal transduction and hence proinflammation and osteoclas-
togenesis.6 For instance, the inflammatory environment
triggered by ROS will suppress osteogenic markers, such as
bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2), Runt-related tran-
scription factor 2 (Runx2), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) of
stem cells.7 Moreover, an increase in receptor activator of
nuclear factor-kappa ligand (RANKL)-induced osteoclasto-
genesis by ROS will break the homeostasis between bone
formation and bone resorption.8 Therefore, developing an
efficient strategy to regulate the ROS level in the periodontium
microenvironments for protecting stem cells and osteoblasts

In the past decade, abundant antioxidative defense strategies
based on natural enzymes, artificial enzymes, and antioxidants
have been developed to maintain the intracellular redox
balance.9−18 In the natural antioxidase systems, superoxide
dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) can efficiently eliminate
ROS by cascade reactions between SOD (transform ·O2
−into
H2O2) and CAT (transform H2O2 into H2O and O2).19
However, when utilized for clinical treatments, natural
enzymes are restricted to abundant intrinsic shortcomings,
such as difficult storage, high cost, low stability, and potential
immunogenicity.20 To tackle these limitations, artificial
enzymes with antioxidase-like biocatalytic properties have
aroused dramatically increased attention in recent years.21−26
Among them, the metal oxides-based materials exhibit a
balanced trade-off between biocatalytic activities and biode-
gradability, which have attracted extensive attention in
combating oxidative stress and inflammation-related diseases,
for instance, MnO2 nanoparticles,27 CeO2-based nanomedi-
cines,28,29 and CoOx-based nanomaterials.30 Although great
efforts have been made, further development of metal oxide-
based artificial enzymes is still encountering enormous
challenges, especially in catalytic activities and cascade reaction
properties, which display a rather poor ROS scavenging

It has been disclosed that the high electronegative metal
catalytic sites in metal oxides would result in poor reversible
redox properties when reacting with oxygen intermedi-
ates.31−34 Recent reports have revealed that cobalt oxides
can serve as stable and biocompatible support with a high
redox potential of Co3+/Co2+ for diverse biocatalytic
therapeutics.35−40 Meanwhile, originating from its redox-active
characteristics of cobalt oxides, the Co2+ sites can further
contribute electrons (Co2+ to Co3+) to the metal centers, thus
introducing charge transfer between the support materials and
active centers. Therefore, loading catalytic atoms on the cobalt
oxides and precisely modulating their electronic structures and
microenvironments of catalytic centers may offer promising
opportunities to construct efficient and cascade ROS-
scavenging artificial enzyme systems, while this remains
challenging and undiscovered.
Here, by taking advantage of the iridium (Ir) element with
abundant d-electrons and high redox stability with oxygen
intermediates,41−43 we report on creating cobalt oxides-
supported Ir (CoO−Ir) as cascade and ultrafast artificial
antioxidases to alleviate the local tissue inflammation and bone
resorption in periodontitis. The morphology, chemical and
electronic structures, ROS-scavenging activities, and perio-
dontium microenvironment regulations of this CoO−Ir
nanobiocatalyst have been systematically explored. We
demonstrate that the Ir nanoclusters are uniformly supported
on the CoO lattice, and there is stable chemical coupling and
strong interfacial charge transfer from Co to Ir sites, which may
promote its intrinsic catalytic redox properties for ROS-
scavenging. Benefiting from the advantages of CoO−Ir
structures, CoO−Ir presents cascade and ultrafast SOD-CAT
catalytic activities. Notably, it displays increased Vmax (76.249
mg L−1 min−1) and turnover number (2.736 s−1) when
eliminating H2O2, which surpasses most of the by-far-reported
artificial enzymes. Consequently, the CoO−Ir provides not
only efficient cellular protection of both bone marrow
mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and osteogenic precursor
cells (MC3T3-E1) from ROS attack but also promotes
osteogenetic differentiation in vitro. Furthermore, the CoO−
Ir can efficiently combat periodontitis by inhibiting inflamma-
tion-induced tissue destruction and promoting osteogenic
regeneration. We believe that this report will provide essential
guidance for creating cascade and ultrafast artificial anti-
oxidases for treating diseases with ROS-related inflammation
and osteogenic resorption.

annealing in an Ar atmosphere at 450 °C to generate CoO−Ir-
based artificial antioxidases (see Methods for details).
However, without the addition of IrCl3, a large rodlike CoO
was obtained as the reference group (Figure 1a and Figure S1).

To further investigate the catalytic microenvironments and
electronic structures of the as-obtained CoO−Ir nano-
biocatalysts, we performed X-ray absorption near-edge
structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine
structure (EXAFS) spectroscopies. The three XANES spectra
at the Ir L3-edge display similar characteristic features, except
for the relative intensities. The white line intensity of CoO−Ir
in Ir L3-edge spectrum is between Ir foil and IrO2, suggesting
an average cationic environment of Ir in CoO−Ir due to the
partial oxidization of Ir species (Figure 2e).53,54 As shown in
FT-EXAFS spectra (Figure 2f), the peak observed at
approximately 2.2 Å is in close proximity to the Ir−Ir bond
of the Ir foil,44 indicating that the predominant form of Ir
species present in CoO−Ir exists as Ir−Ir bonds. Based on this
finding, a detailed investigation into the ligand conditions of Ir
was conducted by wavelet transforming k3-weighted EXAFS
spectra to visualize structural information at both R-space and
k-space resolutions. Clearly, CoO−Ir exhibits a peak intensity
at 2.4 Å in R space and 8 Å−1 in k space, assigned to the Ir−Ir/
Co bonds and the relatively low-intensity region at 1.5 Å in R
space and 6 Å−1 in k space, attributed to the Ir−O bonds at the
interface of Ir nanoclusters and CoO substrates.55 Combined
with the above results, the chemical coupling between Ir
nanoclusters and CoO substrates and strong interfacial charge
transfer from Co to Ir sites may promote the stabilization and
catalytic redox properties of this CoO−Ir-based artificial
antioxidases.56
Antioxidase-like Biocatalytic Performance and Ki-
netics Analysis. After successfully characterizing the prep-
aration and chemical composition and structure of CoO−Ir,
we further tested and compared their SOD-CAT cascade
catalytic ROS-scavenging activities toward ·O2
−and H2O2
(Figure 3a). The SOD-like activity (·O2
−to H2O2) was
investigated by monitoring the elimination ratio of ·O2
−with
tetrazole nitrate sodium chloride blue (NBT) as a specific
indicator.57 As shown in Figure 4b, both CoO and CoO−Ir
reveal strong SOD-like activities, indicating that the Co sites
play a key role in the SOD-like activity. Meanwhile, it can be
seen from Figure 3c that the removal of ·O2
−by both samples
is dose-dependent, and the removal ratio of ·O2
−can reach
about 50% at a particularly low concentration (10 μg mL−1),
surpassing the performance of Co3O4 and Co3O4−Ir
synthesized under an air atmosphere (Figure S7a, b).
Thereafter, the CAT-like activity (H2O2 to O2 + H2O), the
second crucial step of the ROS-scavenging cascade system, was
monitored for CoO and CoO−Ir. In a time-dependent H2O2
scavenging assay, CoO−Ir shows a significantly increased
H2O2-eliminating activity (84.8%) compared to the pristine
CoO (∼5.0%) (Figure 3d). The generated oxygen was also
subjected to testing and exhibited a correlation with the
decomposition of H2O2. Figure S8 presents the O2 generation
rates from different H2O2 concentrations in the presence of
CoO and CoO−Ir. At the same concentration, CoO−Ir
exhibits much higher CAT-like activity (Figure 3e). Then, we
computed and compared the maximal reaction velocity (Vmax),
Michaelis constant (Km), and turnover number (TON, the
maximum number of substrate molecules converted per unit
active catalytic center), as shown in Figure 3f, g. Compared
with CoO (Vmax = 3.644 mg L−1 min−1), CoO−Ir displays
increased Vmax = 76.249 mg L−1 min−1 and TON = 2.736 s−1,
thus indicating that the CoO-supported Ir nanocluster exhibits
rapid kinetic elimination of ROS (as shown in Figure S9) and
demonstrates significant difference with the most advanced
ROS-eliminating nanobiocatalysts reported so far, including
Co3O4 nanoplates, Mn3O4 hexagonal plates, ultrasmall Cu5.4O,
Au24Cu1 cluster, etc. (Figure 3e and Table S2). Therefore, we
demonstrate that the CoO−Ir artificial antioxidases present
cascade and ultrafast SOD-CAT catalytic activities. Further-
more, we have expanded the same preparation approach to a
series of other noble metals; the CoO−Ir exhibits a distinct
ability to scavenge ROS (Figure S7c, d).
Biocompatibility and Cellular ROS Scavenging Abil-
ity. Inspired by the cascade and ultrafast antioxidase-mimetic
ROS elimination properties of CoO−Ir, we carefully evaluated
its medical potential in combating oxidative stress and
recovering physiological functions in inflammatory micro-
environments. The illustrative scheme of the biosafety test and
the oxidative stress model is presented in Figure 4a. First, the
cellular biocompatibility of nanobiocatalysts on stem cells
(BMSCs) and osteogenic precursor cells (MC3T3-E1) has
been examined. Both CoO and CoO−Ir exhibit no noticeable
cytotoxicity on cell viability and apoptosis at the working
concentrations (1 and 5 μg mL−1) (Figure 4b−d, Figure S10).
There are only very limited toxic effects of nanobiocatalysts on
cell proliferation when the concentration is lower than 10 μg
mL−1, while higher concentrations (25−100 μg mL−1) of
biocatalysts exert certain toxicity (Figure 4e, f).
Consequently, the 1 and 5 μg mL−1 as safe concentrations
were adopted in the 2,7-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-
DA) staining to verify the scavenging capability of the
intracellular ROS in the presence of H2O2. By measuring the
cell viability of BMSCs and MC3T3-E1 cells stimulated by
100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 μM H2O2, we determined the
establishment of an oxidative stress model by using the
concentration of 150 μM for 2 h (Figure S11). Representative
images detect that the pretreatment of H2O2-induced intra-
cellular ROS could be efficiently eliminated by CoO−Ir, and
there is no significant difference compared with the control
group. While apparent green signals are observed in the H2O2
and CoO groups, they thus indicate the high intracellular ROS
level (Figure 4g). The bright-field images reveal that cells show
distinct morphologies after different treatments (Figure 4h).
Since both 1 and 5 μg mL−1 CoO−Ir present excellent
antioxidant activity, 1 μg mL−1 was chosen for the subsequent
studies. It is believed that incorporating the Ir nanocluster
enhances the antioxidative activities by directly scavenging
excessive ROS production and thus protecting cells from
further damage.58

During the osteoblast mineralization, the early stage involves
increased Collagen I (Col 1) matrix, secretion of BMP-2 and
Runx2, and the later period is represented by high levels of
extracellular matrix proteins, osteocalcin (OCN) and osteo-
pontin (OPN). The regulation of BMSC differentiation into
osteoblasts by BMP-2 and Runx2 is necessary for bone
formation and expedites the repair of bone defects.63,64 As
shown in the immunofluorescence staining, enhanced signals
of BMP-2 and Runx2 can be observed in CoO−Ir, while
significantly reduced signals are observed in the high ROS level
media with CoO (Figure 5h−k). The semiquantitative analysis
further demonstrates that CoO−Ir can significantly promote
Runx2 secretion in BMSCs, even better than the control group
(Figure 5h, i). Runx2, as a particular transcription factor
required for the development of bone (especially chondrocyte
maturation), can upregulate the expression of matrix genes
(OCN, Col I, etc.).63,65 Runx2 is closely related to energy
metabolism during osteogenic differentiation and bone
formation. Mitochondria are the most important organelle
for energy metabolism, and ROS are mainly derived from the
electron transport chain of mitochondria in vivo.66 This
suggests that CoO−Ir can eliminate excessive ROS derived
from abnormal metabolism and directly promote the
osteogenic differentiation and function of stem cells.
Activation of BMP-2-Runx2 Axis of MC3T3-E1. In
order to confirm the antioxidative and osteogenic properties of
CoO−Ir, we further examined another cell type, MC3T3-E1,
which is a preosteoblast cell line. As an established model for
osteoblasts, MC3T3-E1 is a well-characterized murine
preosteoblast cell line. MC3T3-E1 cells and BMSCs are varied
in morphologies and functions, and both of them are
commonly used in bone research for continuous osteogenic
processes.67 After cell culture and staining, we carried out the
same measurements for MC3T3-E1 cells to verify the
antioxidation and osteoinductivity of CoO−Ir and the possible
mechanism (Figure 6a). Similar to the results of BMSCs,
CoO−Ir rescues more than half of the dead MC3T3-E1 cells
from H2O2-induced ROS attack (Figure 6b, Figure S15, S16).
While MC3T3-E1 at the high-ROS level exhibits poor skeleton
organization with round morphology, the CoO−Ir and control
group exhibit outspread and flattened shapes with well-
organized F-actin (Figure S17). The quantitative results of
the F-actin staining also disclose that the CoO−Ir group had a
larger spreading area and greater cell protrusions when
compared with the ROS condition (Figure 6c).
Next, we also evaluated the effect of CoO−Ir on the
osteogenic properties in MC3T3-E1 cells under oxidative
stress. The highest expression of ALP in the CoO−Ir-treated
group was observed over other groups (Figure 6d, e, and
Figure S18). For the immunofluorescence results of early
markers in osteogenesis, the highest expression of BMP-2 has
also been found in the CoO−Ir-treated group, which suggests
that CoO−Ir not only rescues the inhibition of excessive ROS
on bone formation but also has a direct osteogenesis-
promoting effect (Figure 6f, i). As one of the most powerful
cytokines that promote preosteoblast differentiation in vitro
and bone development in vivo, BMP-2 exerts its osteogenic
effects by activating Smad signaling and osteogenic genes
transcription, such as ALP, Col I, and OCN.64,68 Moreover,
BMP-2 is known to increase the expression level of Runx2
through Smad signaling. These results demonstrate the critical
role of the BMP-2-Smad-Runx2 axis in osteoblastogenesis.69,70
The Runx2 staining and its semiquantitative analysis further
demonstrated the activation of the BMP-2-Runx2 axis by
CoO−Ir in the oxidative microenvironment, reflecting its
osteoinductive potential (Figure 6g, j).
Accompanied by enhanced staining of osteoinductive
proteins, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was
then carried out to characterize the relative mRNA expression
of osteogenesis genes (ALP, Col-1, Runnx2, and OCN) on day
7 (Table S3). Interestingly, CoO−Ir not only exerts cellular
protection by eliminating ROS compared to H2O2 and CoO
groups but also endows cells with an exceeding performance in
osteogenic differentiation compared to the control level
(Figure 6h). The osteoinductivity of CoO−Ir may be
attributed to the cobalt ions, which have been considered a
potential alternative to growth factors and genetic approaches
because of their stimulating effects on angiogenesis and
osteogenesis in tissue engineering.38−40 Therefore, both the
literature data and our results suggest that cobalt-based
biomaterials have promising potential for tissue regeneration
applications.
Inhibition of Inflammatory Reaction and Bone
Resorption In Vivo. In light of CoO−Ir’s favorable effects
on BMSCs and MC3T3-E1 osteogenic development in an
oxidative environment, a rat ligation-induced periodontitis was
constructed as a model inflammatory disease to evaluate the
therapeutic efficacy of CoO−Ir. The left maxillary second
molars were ligated to establish a periodontal model, and 3
weeks of injection with different treatments were performed
after 1 week (Figure 7a). Micro-CT scanning and 3D
reconstructed images show the destruction of the alveolar
bone and resorption at the root bifurcation after ligation, which
indicates the inflammatory process of the periodontitis model.
After injection of CoO, the height of the alveolar bone was
slightly recovered but not as much as the CoO−Ir group
(Figure 7b). Additionally, measurement of the distance
between the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) and the alveolar
bone crest (ABC), which is an indicator of alveolar bone
destruction, consistently confirm that CoO−Ir rescued the
alveolar bone loss from inflammatory disease (Figure 7d, e).
Further analysis of bone mass around second molar regions
also shows greater trabecular bone volume (BV/TV) and
trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) after CoO−Ir injection, indicat-
ing the undefected trabecular factors (Figure 7f, g).
The active destruction of connective tissue and progressive
bone resorption that characterize chronic periodontitis result
from the presence of various stimulatory factors in plasma,
which stem from the respiratory burst of polymorphonuclear
neutrophils (producing excessive ROS). Various periodontitis
defense strategies, such as natural enzymes-based biocatalysts,
artificial antioxidases, and antioxidants, have caused attention
to exert anti-inflammatory effects by effectively eliminating
excess cellular ROS and maintaining intracellular redox
homeostasis.12,17,71,72 Along with the improvement of
destructive periodontal tissue, we speculate that CoO−Ir had
potential in vivo ROS scavenging potential in the inflammation
microenvironments. The ROS-dependent dihydroethidium
(DHE) fluorescence confirmed that both the control group
and the P + CoO−Ir treated group had a lower fluorescence,
and the P + saline and P + CoO groups exhibited higher
fluorescence signals, indicating that our artificial antioxidase
could emerge as efficient ROS scavengers in oxidative stress-
related diseases (Figure 7c). After treatment, neutrophils
(NEUT) and white blood cells (WBC) decreased to the

CONCLUSION
In summary, to efficiently regulate the high ROS and
inflammatory periodontium microenvironments, we have
created a cascade of ultrafast artificial antioxidases (CoO−Ir)
to alleviate the local tissue inflammation and bone resorption
in periodontitis, as illustrated in Scheme 1. We demonstrate
that the CoO−Ir presents ultrafast SOD-CAT cascade catalytic
activities. Notably, the CoO−Ir displays distinctly increased
Vmax and TON values when eliminating H2O2, which surpasses
most of the artificial enzymes reported thus far. Consequently,
the CoO−Ir provides not only efficient cellular protection
from ROS attack but also promotes osteogenetic differ-
entiation in vitro. Furthermore, the CoO−Ir can efficiently
combat periodontitis by inhibiting inflammation-induced tissue

METHODS
CoO−Ir was prepared according to the following procedures. Two
mmol of Co(NO3)2·6H2O, 0.2 mmol of IrCl3·xH2O, 6 mmol of
NH4F, and 10 mmol of urea were added into 70 mL of deionized
water and stirred for 30 min at room temperature. After that, the
prepared solution was sealed into a 100 mL Teflon-liner stainless steel
autoclave and placed into an oven at 120 °C for 8 h. After cooling to
room temperature naturally, the precursor was collected with
centrifugation and washed with deionized water and alcohol to
remove the impurities. Finally, the precursor was transformed into
The Ir0/Ir4+ codoped CoO through calcination at 450 °C for 2 h with
a heating rate of 2 °C min−1 under Ar flow. For comparison, CoO was
prepared by the same procedure without adding IrCl3·xH2O.

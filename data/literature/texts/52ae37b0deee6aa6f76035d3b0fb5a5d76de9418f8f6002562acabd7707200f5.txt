Engineering Functional Metal Materials at the Atomic Level
Qiaofeng Yao, Xun Yuan, Tiankai Chen, David Tai Leong, and Jianping Xie*
DOI: 10.1002/adma.201802751
1. Introduction
The history of mankind is progressing together with the 
ability of fine modulation of materials, and about half of these 
advanced materials are metal based. Therefore, customizing 
functional metal materials on atom-by-atom basis represents 
one of the most ambitious dreams of modern materials scien-
tists. Extensive efforts over the past decades have been devoted 
to modulate metallic materials’ structural attributes and thus 
their corresponding physical and chemical (physicochemical) 
properties at nanometer scale (≈1000 atoms or above).[1–6] 
Consequentially, a great diversity of metal nanomaterials with 
engineerable size, morphology, crystallinity, and hierarchy 
have been produced and explored in many sectors of practical 
applications, including catalysis, energy, biomedicine, and 
With continuous research efforts devoted into synthesis and characterization 
chemistry of functional nanomaterials in the past decades, the development 
of metal materials is stepping into a new era, where atom-by-atom customi-
zation of property-dictating structural attributes is expected. Herein, the 
state-of-the-art modulation of functional metal nanomaterials at the atomic 
level, by size- and structure-controlled synthesis of thiolate-protected metal 
(e.g., Au and Ag) nanoclusters (NCs), is exemplified. Metal NCs are ultrasmall 
(<3 nm) particles with hierarchical primary, secondary, and tertiary structures, 
reminiscent of natural proteins or enzymes. Given the proven dependence 
of their physicochemical properties on their size and structure, documented 
synthetic methodologies delivering NCs with atomic-level monodispersity 
and tailorable size and structural attributes at individual hierarchical levels 
are categorized and discussed. Such assured atomic-level modulation could 
confer metal NCs with novel application opportunities in diverse fields, which 
are also exemplified by their size- and structure-dictated catalytic and biomed-
ical performance. The precise synthesis and application chemistry developed 
based on the hierarchical structure scheme of metal NCs could increase the 
acceptance of metal NCs as a new family of functional materials.

The discovery and rapid development 
of thiolate-protected metal nanoclusters 
(NCs) in the past decades provide an ideal platform for sus-
taining the pursuit of atomically precise modulation in metal-
based materials.[11,12,17–30] Thiolate-protected metal NCs, or 
[Mn(SR)m]q, where n, m, and q denote the number of metal atom 
(M), thiolate ligand (SR), and net charge of individual cluster, 
are ultrasmall particles with a core size of <3 nm. They can be 
synthesized (by wet chemical approaches) and characterized (by 
advanced mass spectrometry and X-ray techniques) at atomic 
precision.[22,28,29,31,32] Due to strong quantum confinement effect 
and unique metal–thiolate (M–SR) bond in this size regime, 
metal NCs exhibit intriguing molecular-like properties, such 
as highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO)–lowest unoc-
cupied molecular orbital (LUMO) transition,[17,25] quantized 
charging,[21] intrinsic chirality,[33] strong luminescence,[34,35] and 
good catalytic activity and selectivity,[36–38] which are distinctly 
different from those of their larger counterparts (i.e., metal NPs 
> 3 nm). For example, the characteristic surface plasmon reso-
nance (SPR) of large metal NPs is absent in metal NCs, however, 
the latter (NCs) possesses well-defined molecular-like absorption 
and strong luminescence with single electron transition char-
acteristic.[14,39] These unique molecular-like properties render 
metal NCs as an emerging class of functional materials finding 
increasing acceptance in various fields, such as catalysis,[36,37] 
biomedicine,[40] energy,[41,42] and many others.[43] Moreover, the 
molecular-like properties of metal NCs are highly size-/structure 
dependent, where one or few atoms difference in NCs could 
result in obvious property changes.[22] Such size-/structure- 
sensitivity could on one hand provide a good means for diver-
sifying cluster properties for practical applications, and on the

Since the resolution of the first crystal structure of thiolate-
protected metal NCs ([Au102(SR)44]0) in 2007,[24] a few dozens of 
crystal structures of [Mn(SR)m]q (in a considerably broad size/
structure spectrum) have been resolved by X-ray crystallog-
raphy technique.[11] It has now become known that [Mn(SR)m]q 
adopts an universal core–shell structure, where a highly sym-
metric M(0) core is capped by a pattern of M(I)–SR protecting 
motifs. Intriguingly, the core–shell structure of metal NCs has 
comparable size and similar structural hierarchy as those of 
naturally occurring proteins. The structure of a protein mole­
cule has a three-level hierarchy: 1) primary structure describes 
the sequence of amino acids; 2) secondary structure depicts 
the repeating local structural motifs (e.g., α-helix and β-sheet); 
and 3) tertiary structure presents the spatial arrangement of 
secondary structures. Taking the most intensively investigated 
[Au25(SR)18]− as an example (Figure 1),[25] the type of metal 
atom and thiolate ligand corresponds to the primary structure 
of metal NCs. And packing of these metal atoms and thiolate 
ligands into basic structural units, e.g., center-occupied icosa-
hedral Au13 core and linear dimeric SR–[Au(I)–SR]2 protecting 
motif in [Au25(SR)18]− (Figure 1), can be regarded as secondary 
structure; while spatial arrangement of the icosahedral Au13 
core and SR–[Au(I)–SR]2 protecting motifs constitutes the ter-
tiary structure of metal NCs. Resembling natural proteins, 
the physicochemical properties and materials performance of 
metal NCs are cohesively dictated by the three-level hierarchical 
structure of [Mn(SR)m]q NCs.
In this report, we discuss recent progress in customizing the 
size and structural attributes of thiolate-protected metal NCs at 
atomic precision, according to the aforementioned hierarchical 
structure scheme. We first present a brief summary of synthetic 
progresses on atomically precise metal NCs, based on reduc-
tion-growth of M(I)–SR complexes (indicated by open circles 
in Figure 1) and conversion chemistry upon preformed parent 
NCs (solid circles in Figure 1). In particular, this report high-
lights the design principles and governing chemistry delivering 
state-of-the-art modulation (including monodispersity and tai-
lorability) of size and hierarchical structure of thiolate-protected 
metal NCs. The assured atomic-level capability of modulating 
size and structural attributes has conferred metal NCs with 
unique materials performances, which are also exemplified by 
their catalytic and biomedical applications in this report. We 
hope the fundamental and methodological discussions may 
serve as a catalyst to promote the translation of precisely tun-
able size and structure of metal NCs into fine-customizable 
practical applications.
2. Synthesis of Metal NCs via Reduction-Growth 
Strategy
The synthetic efforts of thiolate-protected metal NCs could be 
dated back to the seminal work by Brust et al.,[18] where small-
sized (2–5  nm) thiolate-protected Au NPs were prepared by 
reduction of Au(I)–SR complexes in a two-phase system. Such 
bottom-up reduction-growth synthesis methods were further 
extended into sub-2  nm regime (i.e., synthesis of [Mn(SR)m]q 
NCs) mainly by elevating the thiol-to-Au ratio.[20,22,44,45] Due to 
its accessible starting precursors, i.e., M(I)–SR complexes (could 
be facilely produced by direct mixing of commercially available 
metal salt like HAuCl4 and thiols), the reduction-growth strategy 
has been developed into the most widely used synthesis method 
for metal NCs with atomically controllable size and structure.

As shown in Figure 2a, a typical reduction-growth method 
consists of two stages: 1) reduction-powered size growth, where 
reduction of Au(I)–SR complexes yields mix-sized metal NCs; 
and 2) size-focusing of mix-sized metal NCs into molecularly 
pure metal NCs. This two-stage formation scheme has been 
experimentally supported by our recent mechanistic study on 
the formation process of [Au25(SR)18]−.[46] By employing a mild 
gaseous reducing agent, carbon monoxide (CO) to establish a 
mild and readily quenchable reduction kinetics, we were able to 
identify and track 29 Au(I)–SR complex or intermediate NC spe-
cies (see detailed formula in Figure 2b). Intriguingly, all iden-
tifiable Au(I)–SR complex or intermediate NC species feature 
an even-numbered valence electron count (N), which could be 
deduced based on the cluster formula [Mn(SR)m]q via the equa-
tion N  = n  − m  − q. The time-dependent abundance analysis 
(Figure 2b) further suggests the first 40 min (since CO introduc-
tion) as the Stage I, where reactive 0 e− Au(I)–SR complex spe-
cies (e.g., [Aun(SR)n]0 and [Aun(SR)n–pClp+1]−) are consumed to 
give rise to intermediate NC species with N > 0. Figure 2b also 
exhibits a temporal appearance sequence of intermediate NC 
species as 2 e− → 4 e− → 6 e− → 8 e− species, indicating that the 
formation and growth of Au(0) core in the Stage I is powered 
by a step-wise 2 e− reduction. Following the reduction-powered 
size growth (Stage I) is the size-focusing stage (Stage II). The 
mix-sized intermediate NC species are subsequently converted 
into molecularly pure [Au25(SR)18]− in an elongated period 
of ≈3 d via a series of isoelectronic addition, disproportionation 
and comproportionation reactions. It should be pointed out that 
balanced molecular-like reactions have been proposed for for-
mation and consumption pathways of individual intermediate 
NC species in our original publication,[46] constituting a step 
reaction map along the reduction growth of [Au25(SR)18]−. This 
may represent an important step toward introducing fine total 
synthesis chemistry into nanoscience, making a comprehensive 
step reaction map possible, which is previously only affordable 
in total synthesis of organic- or biomolecules.
On the basis of the above two-stage formation scheme, a 
diversity of kinetics or thermodynamics control measures have 
been reported for engineering the size and structure of metal 
NCs at atomic level.

In addition to substituting the strong reducing agent with 
milder ones, mild and controllable reduction kinetics could 
also be achieved by retarding the reducing power of NaBH4. It 
has been widely known that NaBH4 reduction could be facili-
tated by solvolysis in protic solvent.[51] The reducing ability of 
NaBH4 could thus be tailored by proper selection of solvent. By 
substituting the solvent from commonly used water with less 
polar ethanol, Wu et  al. synthesized [Ag7(DMSA)4]− (DMSA 
denotes meso-2,3-dimercaptosuccinic acid) by NaBH4-reduction 
of Ag(I)–DMSA complexes.[52] In an extreme case, Pradeep and 
co-workers developed a solid reaction route, where NaBH4 and 
Ag(I)–SR complexes were mixed and grounded at solid state, 
for high yield production of a series of atomically precise Ag 
NCs of [Ag9(SR)7]0,[53] [Ag32(SR)19]0[54] and [Ag152(SR)60]2+.[55] Our 
group also managed to retard the reducing power of NaBH4 
by introducing a proper amount of NaOH where the excess 
OH− could be a scavenger of H+ in the aqueous solution.[56] 
It should be noted that the external OH− could not only slow 
down the reduction kinetics of Au(I)–SR complexes, but also 
enhance the etching ability of free thiolate or residual Au(I)–SR 
complexes on the metal core of intermediate Au NCs. By using 
such NaOH-assisted NaBH4-reduction method, balanced reduc-
tion and core-etching kinetics could be achieved, giving rise to 
molecularly pure [Au25(SR)18]− in large quantity (at gram scale) 
and short reaction time (<3 h). Of note, a similar high alkaline

Besides addition of a proper amount of OH−, the size-
focusing process could also be facilitated by introduction of 
excess free thiols, elevating the reaction temperature and elon-
gating the aging time. This is because the size-focusing process 
relies heavily on relatively slow (in comparison with reduction 
reaction of M(I)–SR complexes) core-etching reaction of mix-
sized intermediate NCs by free thiols or M(I)–SR complexes, 
which eliminates less stable sizes and retains the most robust 
one. Due to the superior stability of [Au25(SR)18]−, molecu-
larly pure [Au25(SR)18]− NCs with variable thiolate ligands have 
been synthesized based on deliberate control over the size-
focusing process. For instance, water-soluble [Au25(SG)18]− 
(H-SG = l-glutathione) was produced by reacting a mixture of 
Aun NCs (n = 10–39) with excess H-SG ligands at 55 °C for 3 
h.[58] By implementing a SR-to-Au ratio (RSR-to-Au) of 5:1 in the 
reduction growth of [Au25(SR)18]− and aging the reaction mixture 
after NaBH4 reduction for >48 h, Parker et al. obtained molec-
ularly pure [Au25(S(CH2)2Ph)18]− in tetrahydrofuran (THF).[59]  
Dharmaratne et  al. further monitored the size-focusing pro-
cess of [Au25(S(CH2)2Ph)18]− in THF (RSR-to-Au = 1:6) by matrix-
assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI)-MS,[60] which 
clearly suggests that monodisperse [Au25(S(CH2)2Ph)18]− was 
evolved from a mixture of Aun NCs with n = 25–102 (Figure 3a). 
In addition to [Au25(SR)18]−, many other stable NCs of Au[11,61] 
and Ag[62,63] have been produced by enhanced core-etching reac-
tion (size focusing). Besides chemical evolution by core-etching 
reaction, molecularly pure metal NCs could also be isolated from 
mix-sized metal NCs by advanced separation techniques, such as 
solvent fractionation,[20] size exclusion chromatography (SEC),[64] 
high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC),[65] thin-layer 
chromatography (TLC),[66] and gel electrophoresis (GE).[22,45]
2.1.2. Size Tailorability in Reduction-Growth Strategy
According to the two-stage growth scheme, the size of final 
metal NCs could be tailored by either altering the size distribu-
tion or engineering the size-focusing thermodynamics of inter-
mediate NCs. Many experimental efforts devoted into synthetic 
chemistry of atomically precise metal NCs have demonstrated 
that a mixture of NCs with properly narrowed size distribu-
tion could be evolved into the most thermodynamically stable 
constituent size. One good example is the size-focusing syn-
thesis of [Au25(SR)18]− discussed above (Figure 3a). As shown 
in Figure 3b, by shifting the size-distribution of intermediate 
Aun NCs upward to n  = 38–102, which largely excluded the 
ultrastable [Au25(SR)18]−, Qian et al. obtained molecularly pure 
[Au38(SR)24]0 by treating these intermediate Aun NCs with 
excess thiols at 80 °C for 40 h (Figure 3b).[67] Moreover, the 
authors continued elevating the dominant sizes of intermediate 
Aun NCs to n ≥ 102, and carried out a core-etching reaction in 
the presence of excess thiols at 80 °C for 24 h.[68] The MALDI-
MS analyses unambiguously suggest that a cluster species cen-
tered at ≈34 kDa became dominant while the other sized NCs 
diminished (Figure 3c). The molecular formula of ≈34 kDa spe-
cies is further determined to be [Au144(SR)60]0 by ESI-MS.
An effective way to alter the size distribution of intermediate 
metal NCs and thus the size of final metal NCs is tuning the 
reduction kinetics of M(I)–SR complexes. This could be fac-
ilely achieved by deliberate control over the reducing power of 
the reducing agent. For example, Yu et al. managed to elevate 
the reducing power of CO by fine tuning pH in a range of 7 to 
11 in an aqueous solution of Au(I)–SR complexes saturated by 
CO, which gave rise to [Au10-12(SR)10-12]0 (pH 7), [Au15(SR)13]0 
(pH 9), [Au18(SR)14]0 (pH 10) and [Au25(SR)18]− (pH 11) at varied 
pH in solution.[69] Similarly, Zhu et  al. tamed the reducing 
power of NaBH4 by decreasing its dosage (one equivalent to 
Au) and mixing rate (slow stirring and dropwise addition) for 
synthesis of molecularly pure [Au20(SR)16]0, while fast addition 
of 10 equivalent NaBH4 into a similar reaction mixture under 
vigorous stirring led to extensive formation of [Au25(SR)18]−.[70] 
Other than reducing power, the reduction kinetics of M(I)–SR 
complexes could also be altered by the chemical states of M(I)–
SR complexes. The essential role of chemical states of M(I)–SR 
complexes on the reduction kinetics has been well documented 
in theoretical and experimental literature.[71–74] For example, 
Yao et al. demonstrated that the size of Au NCs could be cus-
tomized as [Au18(SR)14]0 and [Au15(SR)13]0 by changing the 
aggregation state of Au(I)–SR complexes via pH adjustment of 
reaction solution.[49]

With the increasing awareness of ligand effects on the size 
of metal NCs, tuning the size-focusing thermodynamics by 
appropriate design of ligands’ bulkiness has attracted intensive 
research interest. Bulky thiolate ligands (typically with rigid 
ring and cage moieties directly coordinated to S) were originally 
used in synthesis of metal NCs by Murray and co-workers[44,75] 
and their effects on the size and size distribution of metal NCs 
were investigated in-depth by Tracy and co-workers.[27] Another 
milestone work comes from Tsukuda and co-workers, in which 
[Au41(S-Eind)12]q′ (S-Eind denotes 1,1,3,3,5,5,7,7-octaethyl-s-hy-
drindacene-4-thiolate; q′ denotes unspecified charge) was pro-
duced by using bulky S-Eind as core-etching agent.[76] It should 
be noted that the extraordinarily low ratio of thiolate-to-metal 
(RSR-to-M = 12:41 = 0.29) of [Au41(S-Eind)12]q′ is rarely reported in 
similar-sized metal NCs protected by less bulky thiolate ligand 
like [Au25(S(CH2)2Ph)18]− (RSR-to-M = 0.72), [Au38(S(CH2)2Ph)24]0 
(RSR-to-M  = 0.63), [Au44(S(CH2)2Ph)28]0 (RSR-to-M  = 0.63) and 
[Au68(S(CH2)2Ph)34]0 (RSR-to-M  = 0.50), suggesting more cata-
lytic active site might be accessible on the surface of [Au41(S-
Eind)12]q′. More interestingly, Chen et al. highlighted the steric 
hindrance effects of thiolate ligands on the stable size of Au 
NCs in a recent report, where a subtle change in substituting 
site of methylbenzenethiol (MBT) could give rise to strikingly 
different stable sizes (i.e., [Au130(p-MBT)50]0, [Au104(m-MBT)41]0, 
and [Au40(o-MBT)24]0).[77] Based on such pivotal ligand effects 
on cluster sizes, a considerable number of NCs with novel sizes 
and structures (e.g., [Au21(S-Adm)15]q’,[78] [Au24(S-Adm)16]0,[79] 
and [Au30(S-tBu)18]0,[80] where S-Adm denotes 1-adamantanethi-
olate ligand) have been synthesized and fully characterized by 
X-ray crystallography up-to-date.
2.2. Structure Modulation in Reduction-Growth Strategy
2.2.1. Modulation of Primary Structure in Reduction-Growth Strategy
According to the structural scheme detailed in Figure 1, metal 
NCs possess hierarchical structure, where primary structure 
refers to the type of metal atom (i.e., M) and thiolate ligand 
(i.e., SR) in NCs with specific size (i.e., [Mn(SR)m]q with defined 
n and m). Due to their determining effects on the physico-
chemical properties and thus materials performance of metal 
NCs, extensive research efforts have been devoted into altering 
the composition of metal core and protecting ligand shell. In 
the frame of reduction-growth strategy, alloy or hetero-thiolate-
protected NCs could be produced by introducing different 
metal salts or thiols into the feeding mixture, respectively. For 
example, bimetallic [Au25−xM′x(SR)18]0/−, [Au38−xM′x(SR)24]0, [Au

(M′) have been synthesized by coreduction of corresponding 
M′(I)–SR or M′(II)–SR complexes together with Au(I)–SR 
complexes.[81,82] However, it should be noted that the dis-
tribution and alloying site of the heteroatom in the resultant 
alloying NCs are quite specific to the chemical identity of the 
heteroatom. Recent advances in X-ray crystallography analysis 
reveal that Pt and Pd heteroatoms prefer to replace the cen-
tral Au atom in the icosahedral Au13 core of [Au25(SR)18]−,[83,84] 
giving rise to monodoped [Au24M′(SR)18]0 (M′ = Pt or Pd). On 
the contrary, a distribution of [Au25−xM′x(SR)18]− (M′  = Ag or 
Cu) with variable x values were typically produced by a core-
duction method, which is most likely due to their preferential 
displacement with the vertex Au atoms in the icosahedral Au13 
core.[85,86] Similarly, due to a lack of symmetrically or chemi-
cally unique displacement site, hetero-thiolate protected metal 
NCs synthesized by coreduction of M(I)–SR complexes with 
different SR typically possess a spreading ligand distribution 
with a few exceptions.[56,87] For example, Yuan et  al. reported 
synthesis of bithiolate and trithiolate protected Au25 NCs by a 
NaOH-assisted NaBH4-reduction method from a rich library 
of thiolate ligands with different carbon chain length, struc-
ture and functionalities (Figure 4a).[56] Although [Au25(S(CH2)
7COOH)10(S(CH2)2COOH)8]−, [Au25(S(CH2)5COOH)10(S(CH2)

produced at optimized conditions (Figure 4b,c), other combina-
tions of ligands only yield hetero-thiolate protected Au25 NCs 
with unfixed population of individual ligands.
2.2.2. Modulation of Secondary Structure in Reduction-Growth 
Strategy
Tailorability investigation on the secondary structure of metal 
NCs has largely been evoked by recent discovery of structural 
isomerism in NCs.[88–96] Resembling isomerism in molec-
ular chemistry, structural isomerism of NCs refers to NCs 
with identical cluster formula but different metal core and 
M(I)–SR protecting motif structures. Such structural isom-
erism of NCs was observed for the first time by Tian et  al. 
in [Au38(S(CH2)2Ph)24]0.[88] By using an one-pot reduction-
growth method, the authors prepared a [Au38(S(CH2)2Ph)24]0 
(referred to as Au38T hereafter), showing distinctly dif-
ferent UV–vis absorption features (Figure 5c) from those of 
[Au38(S(CH2)2Ph)24]0 (referred to as Au38Q) previously reported 
by Qian et  al.[93] The X-ray crystallography analysis indicates 
that such changes in optical properties are originated from their 
altered secondary structure (Figure 5a,b).[88,93] Au38T possesses a 
capped-icosahedral Au23 core, which is further wrapped by two 
trimeric SR–[Au(I)–SR]3, three dimeric SR–[Au(I)–SR]2, three 
monomeric SR–Au(I)–SR protecting motifs and one bridging 
SR (Figure 5b). In contrast, Au38Q consists of a face-fused 
bi-icosahedral Au23 core capped by six dimeric SR–[Au(I)–
SR]2 and three monomeric SR–Au(I)–SR protecting motifs 
(Figure 5a). Besides the pair of Au38T and Au38Q, other structur-
ally isomeric metal NCs that could be synthesized by reduction-
growth strategy include a pair of [Au52(SR)32]0 isomers,[95,96] and 
a pair of [Au24(SR)20]0 vs [Au24(SeR)20]0 pseudo-isomers (due to 
changed protecting ligands).[90,92] It is worth noting that recent 
theoretical models suggest that [Au24(SR)20]0 adopts a sim-
ilar structure to [Au24(SeR)20]0 if the steric hindrance of SR is 
properly low.[91,97] Similar structural isomerism has also been 
observed in Ag NCs (e.g., [Ag44(SR)30]4−[98] and [Ag11(SR)7]3−[99]) 
after desolvation in gas phase of ion mobility MS.

3. Synthesis of Metal NCs via Cluster-Conversion 
Strategy
The increasing accessibility of atomically precise metal NCs 
has fueled a rapid growth of cluster-conversion chemistry in 
recent years. In the cluster-conversion strategy, atomically 
precise metal NCs are deployed as starting materials, which 
could easily transit the molecular purity of starting NCs to 
the product NCs. Apart from such inherent merit on mono-
dispersity control, cluster-conversion chemistry holds great 
potential to produce novel size and structure (in all hierar-
chical levels) of NCs, which are difficult (if not impossible) 
to be delivered by reduction-growth strategy.[11,12,103] This 
section aims to provide a concise overview on the synthesis 
methodologies delivering atomically precise metal NCs with 
tailorable size and structures based on cluster-conversion 
chemistry (Figure 7).
3.1. Size Modulation in Cluster-Conversion Strategy
The cluster size of metal NCs could be tailored by a variety 
of conversion reactions based on atomically precise parent 
metal NCs, including ligand exchange induced size conversion 
(LEISC, Approach I in Figure 7)[103] and redox induced size con-
version (Approach IV in Figure 7).[104] In a typical LEISC reac-
tion (Approach I in Figure 7), parent metal NCs are allowed 
to react with foreign thiolate ligands, which usually possess 
vastly different structure (e.g., steric hindrance in particular) 
from the thiolate ligands on NC surface. Upon proper optimi-
zation of experimental parameters (e.g., type and amount of 
foreign ligand, reaction time, and temperature), novel-sized 
metal NCs could be obtained with molecular purity suitable 
for single crystal growth.[103] Such LEISC reaction is thermo-
dynamically driven, and the size of product NCs is largely 
dictated by mutual fitness of the metal core and self-arranged 
thiolate ligands. For example, by treating [Au38(S(CH2)2Ph)24]0 
with excess H-SPh-tBu at elevated temperature (80 °C) for 12 h, 
Zeng et al. employed the LEISC method to obtain [Au36(SPh-
tBu)24]0.[105] It should be pointed out that [Au36(SR)24]0 was 
the first NC identified with face-centered-cubic (FCC) type

could also be driven by redox reactions (Approach IV,

[Au25(SR)18]− was used as cluster seed, whose further growth 
was fueled by CO reduction of external Au(I)–SR complexes. 
We then tracked the growth reaction by time-course ESI-MS, 
in which totally 35 Au(I)–SR complex and intermediate NC 
species were identified (Figure 9b). Interestingly, all these 
species feature an even-numbered valence electron count 
(N). This data readily means that the size growth reaction is 
driven by 2 e− boosting of the valence electron count of NCs, 
similar to our previous observation in the reduction-growth of 
[Au25(SR)18]−.[46] The time-dependent abundance of the interme-

converted into [Au25(SR)18]− by CO reduction. With the deple-
tion of those reactive [Aun(SR)n]0 species, the growth steps into 
Au25-mediated size growth stage (Stage I), where further size 
growth of [Au25(SR)18]− is initiated by its selective adsorption 
of CO. Such initiation mechanism is supported by successful 
capture of [Au25(SR)18CO]− adduct by ESI-MS. By the end of 
Stage I (t = 48 h), the majority of [Au25(SR)18]− NCs (N = 8) are 
converted to [Au44(SR)26]2− (N = 20) by 2 e− reduction. The final 
stage is size-focusing stage (Stage II), in which a few other-
sized intermediate NCs are converted into [Au44(SR)26]2− via a 
slow size-focusing process. Based on the detailed analysis on 
the time-dependent abundance of all 35 species, we were also 
able to propose molecular-like formation and consumption 
reactions for individual species, constituting a complete step 
reaction map along the formation route of [Au44(SR)26]2− from 
[Au25(SR)18]−.[104] As an important implication of the known syn-
thetic chemistry of [Au44(SR)26]2− at unprecedented molecular 
level, we have also attempted to trap the growth of [Au25(SR)18]− 
into another intermediate size, i.e., [Au38(SR)24]0.[104]
Besides the reduction-driven seeded-growth reaction, the size 
conversion of metal NCs could also be made possible by oxi-
dation of parent metal NCs. For example, Higaki et al. demon-
strated that the [Au23(SR)16]− could be consistently converted into 
[Au28(SR)20]0 upon oxidation by O2, H2O2, or Ce4+.[109] It has also 
been suggested that the oxidation process could induce a direct 
one-to-one size conversion from [Au23(SR)16]− to [Au28(SR)20]0, 
while the detailed molecular-like reaction equation is presently 
not clear. Similar observation of oxidation-induced size conver-
sion from [Au23(SR)16]− to [Au28(SR)20]0 (Cd(NO3)2 as oxidizing 
agent) was also reported in a recent report from Zhu et al.[110] 
Intriguingly, the authors also demonstrated that [Au23(SR)16]− 
could be converted into [Au20Cd4(SH)(SR)19]0 upon changing 
the oxidizing agent from Cd(NO3)2 to Cd(SR)2.[110] Taking the 
different electronic configurations of Au(0) ([Xe]4f145d106S1) 
and Cd(0) ([Kr]4d105S2) into account, [Au20Cd4(SH)(SR)19]0 (N = 
20 × 1 + 4 × 2 − 1 − 19 − 0 = 8) is isoelectronic to [Au28(SR)20]0 
(N = 28 × 1 − 20 − 0 = 8). This readily means that Cd(SR)2 may 
prompt a similar oxidation-induced size conversion reaction of 
[Au23(SR)16]− (toward [Au28(SR)20]0), which takes place concur-
rently with a substitution reaction of 2 Au → 1 Cd. Isoelectronic 
size conversion from [Au23(SR)16]− to [Au19Cd2(SR)16]− could 
also be accomplished via similar substitution of four surface Au 
atoms of the former by two Cd heteroatoms.[111]

3.2.2. Modulation of Secondary Structure in Cluster-Conversion Strategy
In addition to size and primary structure, the ligand exchange 
reaction could also alter the secondary structure of metal NCs 
(Approach II, Figure 7). For example, Chen et  al. recently 
reported a ligand exchange induced reversible quasi-isomeri-
zation of [Au28(SR)20]0.[89] The [Au28(SPh-tBu)20]0 was incubated 
in the presence of excess H-ScC6H11 at elevated tempera-
ture (80 °C) for 2 h. This incubation could completely convert 
[Au28(SPh-tBu)20]0 into [Au28(ScC6H11)20]0. Intriguingly, the X-ray 
crystallography analysis suggests that the [Au28(ScC6H11)20]0 pos-
sesses similar FCC Au20 core as that of [Au28(SPh-tBu)20]0, but 
different Au(I)–SR protecting motifs. The FCC Au20 core of the 
former is protected by two trimeric SR–[Au(I)–SR]3, two mono-
meric SR–Au(I)–SR protecting motifs and eight bridging SR 
ligands; while the latter consists of four dimeric SR–[Au(I)–SR]2 
protecting motifs and eight bridging SR ligands. Of particular 
note, the similar Au20 core structure of [Au28(SPh-tBu)20]0 and 
[Au28(ScC6H11)20]0 results in similar optical absorption profiles of 
these two NCs, where the most pronounced absorption features 
of [Au28(SPh-tBu)20]0 retain in [Au28(S-cC6H11)20]0 with some 
blue shifts. The most striking finding in this study is the revers-
ibility of such quasi-isomerization reaction. By similar incuba-
tion of [Au28(ScC6H11)20]0 in the presence of excess H-SPh-tBu 
at 80 °C for 2 h, [Au28(ScC6H11)20]0 could be transformed back to 
[Au28(SPh-tBu)20]0. The DFT calculation suggests that the revers-
ibility of such quasi-isomerization should be attributed to altered 
stable structure by thiolate ligands, where the intermolecular 
interaction between thiolate ligands is a dictating factor.
3.2.3. Modulation of Tertiary Structure in Cluster-Conversion Strategy
In stark contrast to structural isomers, enantiomers of metal 
NCs typically possess comparable stability, which is the root 
cause for the difficulty in asymmetric synthesis and/or separa-
tion of enantiomers. This, on the other hand, readily implies 
that the reverse process (i.e., racemization reaction; Approach 
III, Figure 7) may be facilitated by the entropy effects. For 
example, Knoppe et  al. carried out thermal treatment of 
enantio-separated Au38Q, which was made possible by the afore-
mentioned chiral HPLC,[100] at temperatures varied from 40 to 
90 °C for 30 min.[135] Such thermal treatment could consistently 
fuel the racemization reaction, as indicated by the decreasing 
ee with increasing incubation time. The Arrhenius plot made 
possible by kinetic analyses at varied temperature suggests 
the activation energy for the racemization reaction as ≈28 kcal 
mol−1, which is comparable to the strength of strong hydrogen 
bond. On the basis of this relatively low activation energy, and 
almost unchanged racemization kinetics in the presence of 
external thiols, the authors proposed a SN2 mechanism for the 
racemization reaction, which relied heavily on the flexible sur-
face of [Au38(SR)24]0. In a follow-up work, Barrabés et al. dem-
onstrated that doping [Au38(SR)24]0 with Pd could significantly 
enhance the surface flexibility of the resultant [Pd2Au36(SR)24]0, 
giving rise to a faster racemization kinetics of the latter.[136]
4. Catalytic and Biomedical Applications  
of Metal Nanoclusters
Through the sensitive dependence of cluster properties on their 
size and structure, the modulable size and hierarchical struc-
ture of metal NCs could be readily transited into reliably tun-
able electronic, optical, catalytic, and biological properties of 
NCs, making them useful in many practical sectors. Among 
these applicable fields of metal NCs, catalytic and biomedical 
applications are of particular interest, as the size and structural 
hierarchy featured by metal NCs are reminiscent of natural pro-
teins or enzymes. Therefore, we employ catalytic and biomed-
ical applications of metal NCs as paradigms to demonstrate 
how the size and hierarchical structure of metal NCs impact 
their performance in applicable setups.

Since the discovery of catalytic activity of nanosized Au by 
Haruta et  al., the catalytic activity of nanosized metal has 
been long attributed to its richness in low-coordinated surface 
atoms.[137] However, the remarkable catalytic activity and selec-
tivity of metal NCs in sub-3 nm regime should not be ascribed 
solely to their high specific surface area. It has now become 
known that other factors dictating the catalytic performance 
of metal NCs include HOMO–LUMO structure,[138] charge 
state,[139] fine surface arrangement,[140] and electron-transfer 
mediating property,[139] which are largely sustained by the clus-
ters in its entirety rather than specific surface atom. Due to 
these unique electronic and structural features, metal NCs (in 
both ligand-on and ligand-off states) hold demonstrated cata-
lytic applications in oxidation (e.g., CO,[141,142] styrene,[143,144] 
alcohol,[145,146] and borane[147]), reduction or hydrogenation (e.g., 
ketone or aldehyde,[148] nitro compound,[140,149] and alkynes[150]), 
CC coupling (e.g., Sonogashira coupling,[151] Ullmann cou-
pling,[152] and Suzuki coupling[153]), photochemical (e.g., water 
splitting[154]) and electrochemical (e.g., hydrogen evolution,[139] 
oxygen reduction,[155] and CO2 reduction[156]) reactions.
Owing to the strong luminescence, small size, rich surface 
chemistry, and designable physiological activity of metal core, 
metal NCs have attracted enormous interest as emerging can-
didates of bioprobes,[14] radiotherapy sensitizers,[157] and anti-
microbial agents.[158,159] For example, Shang et al. synthesized 
lipoic acid-protected Au NCs with temperature-dependent 
emission at 710  nm (excitation wavelength, λex  = 580  nm), 
which could be easily internalized by HeLa cell via endocy-
tosis process.[160] Based on the temperature-dependent emis-
sion intensity and lifetime, such lipoic acid-protected Au NCs 
could be used as intracellular thermometer, spatially mapping 
out intracellular temperature gradient with a resolution of 
0.3–0.5 °C in a temperature range of 14–43 °C. Our group have 
demonstrated that [Au10–13(SG)10–13]0,[161] [Au25(SG)18]−[162] and 
[Au29–43(SG)27–37]q’[163] NCs could efficiently escape reticuloen-
dothelial system (RES) and accumulate in tumor via enhanced 
permeability and retention (EPR) effect, resulting in enhanced 
cancer radiotherapy. Due to increasing demands for in vivo 
applications, interactions between metal NCs and organisms 
(e.g., clearance pathway[164,165] and cytotoxicity[166]) have become 
a center of recent biomedical explorations.
4.1. Effects of Size
Although the size dependence of catalytic activity could be quite 
reaction specific, a monotonically descending trend against 
increasing cluster size is statistically most profound in catalysis 
literature of metal NCs. Such trend is likely due to a combined 
effect of increasing specific surface area and altered electronic 
structure of metal NCs with decreasing size. For example, the 
calculated binding energy of O2 to [Au25(SR)18]−, [Au102(SR)44]0, 
and [Au144(SR)60]0 (after partial ligand removal) sequentially 
decreases with increasing cluster size (Figure 11a), indicating 
the activation of O2 by these NCs is less efficient at larger 
size.[138] Such decreasing capability of O2 activation is attributed 
to the narrowed HOMO–LUMO gap of larger-sized NCs, and 
could readily transit to reverse size-dependent catalytic activity 
toward aerobic oxidation reactions.[138] Zhu et  al. conducted 
aerobic oxidation of styrene (Figure 11c) in the presence of 
unsupported or supported (on SiO2 and HAP = hydroxyapa-
tite) [Au25(SR)18]−, [Au38(SR)24]0 and [Au144(SR)60]0, and 
documented deceasing conversion with increasing cluster size 
(Figure 11d).[143,167] For ease of presentation, a generic form of 
[Mn(SR)m]q/support or Mn/support is adopted hereafter to refer 
to supported ligand-on [Mn(SR)m]q or ligand-off Mn NCs, respec-
tively. Similar reverse size-dependent catalytic activity has also 
been observed in many other reactions catalyzed by thiolate-
protected Au NCs, including oxidization of methyl phenyl 
sulfide,[168] electroreduction of O2,[155] photocatalytic decomposi-
tion of methyl viologen,[41] and water splitting reactions.[169] A 
notable exception of such descending trend was reported by Liu 
et al. (Figure 11b).[170] They employed Aun/HAP (n = 10, 18, 25, 
39, and ≈85) to catalyze aerobic oxidation of cyclohexane. The 
turnover frequency (TOF) of Au NCs exhibits an initial growing 
and subsequent descending trend toward cluster size, with the 
catalytic activity maximized at n = 39.
Cluster size is also a dictating factor in diverse biomedical 
applications of metal NCs. Appropriately long blood retention 
time is a prerequisite for effective tumor uptake of metal NCs 
via EPR effect, conducive for their subsequent bioimaging, 
and therapy applications.[164] The blood retention time of metal 
NCs is largely dependent on their renal clearance efficiency, as 
renal clearance becomes a dominant elimination pathway for 
NPs <6–8  nm.[164] Intriguingly, opposite to common under-
standings that glomerular filtration barrier acts as an one-direc-
tional size cutoff slit rejecting NPs or biomolecules >6–8  nm 
for renal clearance, Du et al. recently revealed that glomerular 
filtration membrane could preferentially trap smaller-sized 
Au NCs in sub-nanoregime.[165] Such preferential reten-
tion of smaller-sized NCs by glomerular filtration membrane 
is unambiguously evidenced by electron microscopy (EM) 
analysis, reminiscent of SEC separation of NCs (Figure 12). 
Due to the unique bio-nanointeraction in sub-nanoregime, 
[Au10-11(SG)10-11]0, [Au15(SG)13]0, [Au18(SG)14]0, and [Au25(SG)18]− 
exhibit exponentially increasing renal clearance efficiency with 
increasing cluster size. In addition to renal clearance efficiency, 
the size of Au NCs also determines their antibacterial activity. 
In a recent contribution of our group, we demonstrated that 
the antibacterial activity of Au could be turned on upon the 
particle size dropped into sub-nanoregime.[159] Our semiquan-
titative analysis suggests that [Au25(MHA)18]− (MHA denotes 
6-mercaptohexanoic acid ligand) shows remarkable killing 
efficiency toward both gram-negative (e.g., Staphylococcus 
aureus, 95%) and Gram-positive (e.g., Escherichia coli, 96%) 
bacteria after 2 h treatment. Such killing efficiency is remark-
ably higher than those delivered by ≈6 nm MHA-protected Au 
NPs (3% and 2%, respectively) and Au(I)–MHA complexes 
(5% and 3%, respectively). The enhanced antibacterial activity 
of [Au25(MHA)18]− is attributed to an imbalanced metabolism 
induced by [Au25(MHA)18]−, where simultaneous upregulating 
pro-oxidative enzymes and down-regulating reductive enzymes 
generate excess reactive oxygen species (ROS).

Ligand effect is another focus of catalytic exploration of 
metal NCs, primarily due to the discovery of uncompromised 
and even higher catalytic activity of ligand-on metal NCs in 
selected reactions. For example, Jin and co-workers reported 
beneficial impact of ligands on catalytic oxidation of CO and 
styrene.[141,173] By thermal treatment of supported [Aun(SR)m]q 
at ≥200 °C (thiolate desorption temperature determined by 
thermogravimetric analysis, TGA), the authors observed sup-
pressed conversion of CO (for Aun/CeO2, n = 25 and 38)[141,173] 
and styrene (for Aun/SiO2 or HAP, n  = 25, 38, and 144),[167] 
respectively, in their aerobic oxidation reactions. Such benefi-
cial effects are attributed to the preserved structural integrity 
of NCs by the thiolate ligands. In a follow-up work, Wu et al. 
investigated the ligand effects of [Au25(SR)18]−/CeO2 into great 
details by combined experimental and theoretical means.[174] 
By using in situ infrared (IR) and X-ray absorption spectros-
copy to monitor the surface structure changes of [Au25(SR)18]−/
CeO2 in O2 and CO thermal treatment, the authors concluded 
that the thermal treatment at a temperature lower than that of 
thiolate desorption temperature determined by TGA (≈200 °C) 
could induce partial thiolate ligand removal. Such partial ligand 
removal and subsequent structure rearrangement could open 
active sites for CO activation while keeping the integrity of M–S 
framework largely retained, boosting the catalytic activity of 
[Au25(SR)18]−/CeO2 catalyst. The density functional theory (DFT) 
calculations by these authors further suggest that CO would 
preferentially adsorb on the open Au sites in the Au(I)–SR pro-
tecting motifs after partial ligand removal, conducive for their 
activation. In a separate work, Lopez-Acevedo et al. considered 
the leaving species in the partial removal process as SR–[Au(I)–
SR]2 due to their robust structure and rich abundance on the 
surface of [Au25(SR)18]−.[138] Their DFT calculations indicate 
that a CO and an O2 could coadsorb on neighbored vertex Au 
atoms of the icosahedral Au13 core, which become accessible 
after removal of two SR–[Au(I)–SR]2. The authors further 
spotted that the activation of adsorbed O2 was made possible 
by single electron transfer from the cluster’s superatom orbital 
to 2π* orbital of O2, facilitating their reaction with coadsorbed 
CO. Based on the above discussion, we may now conclude that, 
in catalytic reactions dependent on the molecular-like electronic 
structure of metal NCs, partial removal of thiolate ligands, 
which could open more active sites while preserving the integ-
rity of cluster structure, is favorable. It should also be noted that 
there exist many reactions which rely dominantly on the active 
surface atoms rather than molecular-like electronic structure of 
intact NCs, where complete ligand removal is favored as long 
as obvious sintering is not observed.[37,38] Of particular interest, 
the increasing awareness of the importance on controlling the 
extent and harshness (to avoid unfavorable sintering) of ligand 
removal has fueled development of several mild ligand removal 
methods, including oxidation- and reduction-assisted stripping 
approaches.[175,176]

selectivity.[152,178]
The thiolate ligands represent the interface between the 
metal NCs and biological system, and the design of thiolate 
ligands on the NC surface is another equally important factor 
(other than the aforementioned size) determining the interac-
tions of NCs with organisms. For example, due to extensive 
presence of various proteins in serum/plasma, the dynamic 
adsorption of these proteins on the surface of metal NCs 
could lead to the formation of protein corona, which could 
significantly increase the hydrodynamic diameter of NCs and 
trigger their uptake by macrophage.[164] In order to elongate 
the blood circulation time of functional metal NCs and thus 
enhance their EPR-induced passive tumor targeting, hydro-
philic polymers (e.g., polyethylene glycol)[179] and zwitteri-
onic thiolate ligands (e.g., GSH)[180] are usually incorporated 
into the ligand shell of metal NCs for a weakened interac-
tion with plasma proteins. In parallel to passive tumor tar-
geting, active tumor targeting could also be achieved facilely 
by conjugation of metal NCs with active targeting moieties 
(e.g., folic acid).[181] In addition to active targeting molecules, 
other functional molecules could also be incorporated into 
the ligand shell to enhance or diversify the biomedical activity 
of metal NCs. For example, our group reported antibacterial 
synergism between molecular antibiotics and Ag NCs, which 
was made possible by covalently conjugating daptomycin (a 
common molecular antibiotic) with antibacterial Ag NCs.[158] 
Of particular note, the daptomycin–Ag NC hybrid exhibits 
higher killing efficiency than that of a physical mixture of 
identical ingredients. In a very recent contribution, Zheng 
et al. further demonstrated that the surface charge could also 
affect the antibacterial performance of [Au25(SR)18]− NCs.[182] 
By tuning the types and number of non-antibacterial thiolate 
ligands, we were able to tune the ζ-potential of [Au25(SR)18]− 
from −21 to −37  mV, where more negatively charged NCs 
exhibited higher killing efficiency toward Staphylococcus 
aureus. Such negative surface charge promoted antibacterial 
performance is relevant to the enhanced ROS generation 
by more negatively charged Au NCs. Of additional note, the 
type of ligand as well as metal atoms could also be tuned 
to enhance the luminescence properties of metal NCs for 
bioimaging or biosensing applications. This topic has been 
intensively reviewed elsewhere.[12,14,34,183]
4.3. Effects of Secondary Structure
Given that the secondary structure of thiolate-protected metal 
NCs predicts the arrangement of both thiolate ligand and 
metal atom, its essential impact on the catalytic performance 
of metal NCs is expected. Exciting experimental supports to 
this hypothesis come with recently discovered structural iso-
mers of [Au38(SR)24]0,[88] as well as [Au28(SR)20]0.[89] It should 
be reminded that [Au38(SR)24]0 possesses a pair of structural 
isomers, Au38T and Au38Q, where the former features a less 
densely packed surface (Figure 5 and the corresponding struc-
ture discussion).[88] Due to such “loose” surface, Au38T shows 
enhanced catalytic activity toward hydrogenation of 4-nitro-
phenol at low temperature (e.g., 0 °C). It has been found that 
incubation of 4-nitrophenol, 10 equivalent of NaBH4 and 0.001 
equivalent of Au38T in THF at 0 °C for 30 min could convert 
44% of 4-nitrophenol into 4-aminophenol. However, the cata-
lytic activity disappears when Au38T is substituted by Au38Q or 
[Au25(SR)18]0. Similarly, Chen et al. compared the catalytic per-
formance of [Au28(ScC6H11)20]0 and [Au28(SPh-tBu)20]0 toward 
CO oxidation.[89] These two NCs could be regarded as a pair of 
structural pseudo-isomers due to their varied Au28S20 frame-
works. It has also been found that [Au28(ScC6H11)20]0 with a 
more open surface exhibits higher catalytic activity. In contrast 
to experimentally demonstrated catalytic dependence, the sec-
ondary (and tertiary) structure effects of metal NCs on their 
biomedical activity have rarely been reported.
4.4. Effects of Tertiary Structure
The exposure and accessibility of metal atom is decisively deter-
mined by the spatial arrangement of M(0) core and M(I)–SR 
protecting motifs. The space-filling model suggests that the 
arrangement of six dimeric SR[Au(I)–(SR)]2 motifs on the ico-
sahedral Au13 core could create two solvent accessible pocket-
like cavities on the surface of [Au25(SR)18]−, which are widely 
accepted as active sites for many catalytic and surface reac-
tions.[36,134] More importantly, such pocket-like cavity could be 
regarded as a spatially confined nanoreactor, offering selec-
tivity to specific reactions or reaction pathways. For instance, 
Nasaruddin et  al. recently tracked the catalytic hydrogenation 
of 4-nitrophenol on the surface of [Au25(SR)18]− by time-course 
UV–vis absorption and ESI mass spectra.[140] Surprisingly, our 
optical absorption and mass spectra consistently suggest that 
the hydrogenation of 4-nitrophenol could occur via an azoben-
zene intermediate (bottom pathway in Figure 13a). Such reac-
tion pathway is different from that commonly reported in NP 
literature (top pathway in Figure 13a), and should be attributed 
to the unique surface structure of [Au25(SR)18]−. As shown in 
Figure 13b, the pocket-like cavity could well accommodate two 
4-nitrophenol molecules together with a BH4− ion, facilitating 
the formation of azo-bond between two substrate molecules. 
Similar observation was also reported by Li et  al., where the 
fine structure of pocket-like cavity gave rise to high conversion 
and selectivity of Sonogashira cross-coupling reaction between 
p-iodoanisole and phenylacetylene.[151]

5. Conclusion
To sum up, the past two decades have witnessed great advances 
in synthetic chemistry of thiolate-protected metal NCs, where 
their size, primary, secondary, and tertiary structures could be 
effectively modulated with atomic resolution. Such fine modu-
lation could first be made possible by rational controls over the 
reduction-powered size growth and thermodynamically con-
trolled size-focusing processes in a typical reduction-growth 
strategy. In addition to reduction-growth strategy, the size 
and hierarchical structures of metal NCs could also be effec-
tively tailored via conversion reactions based on preformed 
parent NCs. Such conversion reaction could be induced by 
thermal treatment, metal ions, SR ligands, M(I)–SR complexes, 
reducing or oxidizing agents, and foreign NCs. It should be 
pointed out that modulation of secondary and tertiary struc-
tures is a latest discovery in cluster research and thus they 
are less developed, in comparison to that of size and primary 
structure (i.e., composition). Therefore, future research efforts 
may focus on methodological and mechanistic investigation on 
structural isomerization (i.e., modulating secondary structure) 
and asymmetric synthesis (i.e., modulating tertiary structure) 
of metal NCs.
Beyond primary, secondary, and tertiary structures describing 
intramolecular or intracluster arrangements is quaternary struc-
ture, which depicts intermolecular arrangement of proteins or 
NCs. As intercluster interactions could provide an alternative 
means to diversify or enhance the materials performance of 
metal NCs, the self-assembly chemistry governing the quater-
nary structure of metal NCs is a rising topic in cluster research 
community.[186–190] For example, our group discovered that the 
luminescence of metal NCs could be vastly enhanced in assem-
bled NCs via an aggregation-induced emission (AIE) mecha-
nism, which relies heavily on inter-cluster interactions blocking 
the nonirradiative relaxation pathways of excited electrons.[34,191] 
Similar AIE phenomena have recently been observed by many 
other groups in assembled or crystallized mono- or bimetallic 
NCs.[192,193] In addition to such practical importance, self-
assembly (especially crystallization) exploration could also 
greatly add to fundamental understandings of metal NCs. It has 
been widely recognized that crystallizing metal NCs into super-
crystals for X-ray crystallography examination is the most reli-
able way to reveal the atomic structure of NCs.[11] More impor-
tantly, recent advances in crystallization chemistry of metal NCs 
indicate that the fine hierarchical structure of individual NCs, 
which is absent in metal atoms, could stimulate novel cluster 
packing modes (e.g., 6H left-handed helical close packing) that 
is not possible in bulk metals.[189] Due to short development 
history, the exploration of self-assembly chemistry of metal NCs 
is much less sophisticated in comparison to their synthesis 
chemistry, requiring more research efforts in the near future.
In addition to methodological and mechanistic exploration 
on the synthesis and self-assembly chemistry, the applicable 
investigation could be another center of future research efforts. 
Arising from the precisely tunable size and structural attributes 
are diverse molecular-like optical, electronic, catalytic, and phys-
iological properties, which render thiolate-protected metal NCs 
as a novel family of functional materials. More importantly, 
due to precisely known size and structure, thiolate-protected 
metal NCs could be used as an ideal platform for revealing 
underlying chemistry governing their material performances. 
Although considerable achievements have been made toward 
establishing reliable structure-property relationship at all hier-
archical levels, the structure–property exploration is still in its 
infant age. Particular interest may be placed on unique catalytic 
opportunities (e.g., asymmetric catalysis) brought about by ter-
tiary and quaternary structures of metal NCs, which are inter-
esting but almost unexplored.
On the basis of atomically precise tailorability discussed in 
this report, the development of metal materials is stepping into 
a new era, where atom-by-atom engineering of metal mate-
rials for practical applications would be possible. Besides such

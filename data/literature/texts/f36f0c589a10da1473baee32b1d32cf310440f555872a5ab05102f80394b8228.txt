ABSTRACT: Drug repurposing presents a workable strategy in tackling
antibiotic resistance. Many known drugs have been repurposed for their
applications against different targets. Antihistamines that are usually used
to treat allergy symptoms can be combined with nanoscale materials to
enhance their efficiency. Herein, we explored the antimicrobial properties
of a common antihistamine drug, promethazine, in Gram-positive and
Gram-negative bacteria. Being positively charged, promethazine was
easily incorporated into the mannose-conjugated bovine serum albumin-
stabilized promethazine hydrochloride gold nanoclusters. Capping with
D-mannose helped in targeting the bacteria by inhibiting their adhesive
appendage called pili. Following their uptake, drugs released inside the
bacteria caused reactive oxygen species production and membrane
permeability alteration, ultimately resulting in bacterial inhibition.
Additionally, they were also explored for biofilm eradication. As observed
through staining assays, the number of dead cells increased with increasing concentration of drug-loaded gold nanoclusters in the
biofilm mass. Therefore, the as-synthesized mannosylated gold nanoclusters incorporated with promethazine were analyzed for
potential antibacterial and antibiofilm applications.
KEYWORDS: repurposing, antihistamine, nanoclusters, mannosylation, antibacterial, antibiofilm
■INTRODUCTION
The need for developing potential antimicrobial agents to
combat multidrug resistance is universally recognized. The
majority of the antibiotics that were discovered decades ago1
have already become resistant to a variety of pathogens. Apart
from plazomicin in 20182 and lefamulin in 2019,3 the
discovery of new antibiotics has been slow.4 Therefore, the
unavoidable Darwinian consequence of rampant antibiotic
usage has led scientists to explore multiple strategies that are
becoming increasingly expensive and untenable using conven-
tional routes of developing antibiotics.5 The medical and
research establishments are thus constantly on the lookout for
a creative and sustainable approach towards the identification
of new antimicrobials. Drug repurposing or repositioning is
one such strategy that focuses on the identification of newer
targets for existing drugs.6 The idea behind such an approach is
backed by two concepts. One is that a single drug interacts
with multiple targets.7,8 Another is that a target associated with
a particular disease is generally relevant to a number of other
pathogenesis.9 Researchers have been studying this for years
against a variety of infectious and noninfectious diseases.
Studies have revealed the antimicrobial efficiency of pro-
chlorperazine, an antipsychotic drug against 103 strains of both
Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.10 Antineoplastics
like raloxifene11 and zoledronic acid12 have also been studied
against Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Chlamydia pneumoniae,
respectively. Repurposed drugs pose as attractive choices with
lower developmental risks that translate to lesser time and cost.
They are also particularly suitable for combination doses that
lower the risk of monotherapy resistance and side effects
associated with a higher dosage. It has been found that
nonantibiotic drug classes such as neuroleptics, antihistamines,
antidepressants, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs
(NSAIDs) act in multiple ways on microbial growth and
hence may have a greater degree of broad-spectrum
antibacterial activity.13 One such class is the antihistaminic,
which has been explored primarily against infectious diseases.
Antihistamines are known to be inverse agonists of
histamine (H1, H2, H3, and H4) receptors.14 Depending on
their chemical structure, H1 antihistamines are segregated into

six classes: alkylamines, ethanolamines, ethylenediamines,
phenothiazines, piperazines, and piperidines.14 There have
been studies where antihistamines belonging to different
classes showed both bacteriostatic and bactericidal activities
against a broad range of bacteria.15 For example, chlorproma-
zine, thioridazine, and triflupromazine have been tested against
Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and MRSA.16 Addition-
ally, another antihistamine, terfenadine, was repurposed against
S. aureus,17 while diphenhydramine was found to potentiate
levofloxacin activity against methicillin-resistant S. aureus and
P. aeruginosa.18 Moreover, since phenothiazines have similarity
to acridine’s chemical structure, they may also intercalate into
bacterial plasmids.19 However, several such potential drugs
including antibiotics suffer from low half-life, poor release
profiles, and premature clearance from the circulation system,
lowering their overall efficiency.20 There are multiple
approaches to amplify the benefits of drugs, both repurposed
and otherwise, that include drug modification, administration
route modification, and conjugation to target specific ligands.21
Apart from them, nano-based delivery systems too may prove
to be quite appropriate for consideration at this juncture.
Nanotechnology may be utilized to circumvent issues related
to solubility, stability, specificity, and ADME (absorption,
distribution, metabolism, and excretion).22 They can also be
utilized to detect bacteria; one of the most preferred methods
in the sensing technology is luminescence-based detection.
Metal-like gold nanoclusters have exceptionally high photo-
luminescence, photostability, and lower toxicity.23 There have
been studies where gold nanoclusters synthesized using
vancomycin as a stabilizer were utilized to determine Fe3+ in
various water sources.24 Another group had generated
vancomycin-immobilized gold (Van-Au) nanoparticles that
were explored for potential antimicrobial applications against
antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains.25 Gold nanoclusters
conjugated with ligands like amino acids, peptides, antibiotics,
enzymes, and DNA have been used extensively for therapeutic
purposes.26 Ligands that qualify as small molecules27 (>900
Da) are preferable, as they can regulate biological pathways
and are more permeable into cells.28 In this context,
carbohydrates play vital roles in the development and
differentiation of living organisms and disease pathogenesis.29
It is well known that the mammalian cell surface is covered
with glycocalyx, a dense glycoconjugate coating that pathogens
often utilize to initiate an infection.30 Understanding this
interaction has helped develop an alternate strategy called anti-
adhesion therapy.31 It hampers bacterial attachment to its host
receptors, thus interfering with the early stages of infection.
Combining nanomaterials with carbohydrates can further
heighten their potential biological applications.32,33 Generally,
carbohydrates are known to have less affinity for their
receptors. However, the higher surface area-to-volume ratio
of nanoconstructs can allow attachment of multiple carbohy-
drate moieties, thus increasing the binding affinity to their
receptors.32 Hence, carbohydrate-conjugated delivery systems
may excellently mimic cell surfaces.
In this study, we have reported the development of
mannose-coated bovine serum albumin (BSA)-stabilized gold
nanoclusters encapsulating promethazine. The as-synthesized
nanoclusters were found to be fluorescent and stable in nature.
Promethazine hydrochloride, a known antihistamine drug, was
selected after intense experimental screening of different drugs
(promethazine, ranitidine, ebastine, levocetirizine, cetirizine,
and pheniramine maleate), followed by a similar procedure
using its pure form. It belongs to the phenothiazine class
(Figure S1) and is the generic name for (2RS)-N,N-dimethyl-
1-(10H-phenothiazin-10-yl)propan-2-amine hydrochloride.
Although used for symptomatic relief from allergy-like
conditions and motion sickness, promethazine is already
known to alter bacterial cell membrane permeability causing
irreversible damage. The gold nanoclusters not only enhanced
the drug delivery, but their fluorescent property could also be
utilized for biological tracking. As they fluoresce in the NIR
range, the chances of background interference and light
scattering in biological systems are greatly reduced. Moreover,
the encapsulation also facilitated sustained drug release.
Finally, functionalization with a bacteria-binding biologically
active functionality like
D-mannose helped in amplifying
specificity for the target organism. The nanocomposites
could successfully generate ROS and caused membrane
damage in Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. They
also eradicated biofilms of the respective bacteria, which was
confirmed through lipid content detection and live/dead
staining. Hence, the potential of the synthesized nano-
composites is highlighted through our studies.
■MATERIALS AND METHODS
Chemicals. The experimental protocols followed include the use
of the following chemicals: hydrogen tetrachloroaurate trihydrate
(HAuCl4.3H2O; Sigma-Aldrich Co., USA), BSA (Sisco Research
Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., India), sodium hydroxide (NaOH; Merck,
India), Luria-Bertani (LB) broth (Himedia), nutrient broth (NB)
(Sigma-Aldrich), brain heart infusion (BHI) broth (Sigma-Aldrich),
and agar powder (bacteriological grade, Himedia). Milli-Q grade
water (>18 MΩ cm−1, Millipore) was used in all the experiments.
Synthesis of BSA-AuNCs. BSA-stabilized gold nanoclusters were
synthesized by modifying a reported protocol.34 Briefly, 30 mg/mL of
BSA was dissolved in 5 mL of deionized water. Next, 5 mL of aqueous
HAuCl4 solution (20 mM) was added, and the reaction was heated at
50 °C for 1 min. Following this, as the reaction cooled to room
temperature, 1 M NaOH solution was used to adjust the pH to 12.0.
The above reaction was then allowed to stir vigorously for 15 min at
60 °C, resulting in the formation of a deep brown solution.
Conjugation of D-Mannose to BSA. D-Mannose and BSA were
conjugated through the Maillard reaction by modifying a known
protocol.35 0.5 g of D-mannose and BSA each was dissolved together
in 50 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.0) and stirred at
room temperature until a homogeneous solution was formed. It was
then stirred at 45 °C for 3 h. The solution was cooled to room
temperature and then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C to
remove unreacted products. The final solution was lyophilized for
future use.
Synthesis of (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs. Herein, we used the
previously conjugated Man-BSA instead of BSA. Briefly, 30 mg/mL
of lyophilized Man-BSA was dissolved in 5 mL of deionized water. 1
mL of aqueous promethazine hydrochloride (20 mM) solution was
added to it and stirred at room temperature. Next, 5 mL of aqueous
HAuCl4 solution (20 mM) was added, and the reaction was heated at
50 °C for 1 min. Following this, as the reaction cooled to room
temperature, 1 M NaOH solution was used to adjust the pH to 12.0.
The above reaction was then allowed to stir vigorously for 15 min at
60 °C, resulting in the formation of a deep orange solution. The
resultant solution was centrifuged at 15000 rpm for 40 min at 4 °C to
remove the unreacted drug. The final pellet was redispersed in water.
The reaction was performed at three different pH values (10.0, 12.0,
and 14.0) to check the difference in the fluorescence intensity of the
formed clusters.
Measurement of Browning. Browning and intermediate
products of BSA glycation were determined by measuring the peak
absorbance at 420 and 294 nm, respectively, with UV−visible
spectroscopy (Jasco V-630 spectrophotometer).

Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS). The amount of gold

UV−Vis Spectroscopy. The absorbance characteristics of the as-

Transmission Electron Microscopy. The as-synthesized nano-
composites were analyzed under a transmission electron microscope,
operated at a maximum accelerating voltage of 200 keV (JEM 2100;
JEOL, Peabody, MA). The sample was drop-cast on a carbon-coated
copper grid and air-dried overnight.
Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy. The morphol-
ogies of both control and treated bacteria were studied using field
emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) (JEOL, JSM-
7610F). Bacteria were treated with the minimum inhibitory
concentration (MIC) of (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs. Both control and
treated bacteria were incubated for 10 h at 37 °C (180 rpm), followed
by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 1 min. The resultant pellet was
washed and redispersed in water. 20 μL of the diluted sample was
again drop-cast on an aluminum foil-covered glass slide and air-dried
overnight.
Zeta Potential. The zeta potential of promethazine hydrochloride
and (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs was recorded using a Malvern Zetasizer
Nano ZS.
Antibacterial Studies. The following strains were used for the
antibacterial studies: Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus MTCC 96 and
Bacillus subtilis MTCC 1305) and Gram-negative bacteria (P.
aeruginosa MTCC 2488 and E. coli DH5α). S. aureus MTCC 96
was grown in BHI at 37 °C (180 rpm) for 12 h. B. subtilis MTCC
1305 and P. aeruginosa MTCC 2488 were grown in NB at 37 °C (180
rpm) for 12 h. E. coli DH5α was grown in LB at 37 °C (180 rpm) for
12 h. The bacterial strains were treated with varying concentrations of
free promethazine hydrochloride and (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs. The
bacterial growth was monitored by recording the optical density
(OD) at 595 nm using the UV−visible spectrophotometer (Jasco V-
630). The measurements were recorded in triplicates.
Further, the MIC and the minimum bactericidal concentration
(MBC) were established by plating the bacteria on nutrient agar
media. Here, 100 μL of bacteria treated with the MIC and MBC of
free promethazine hydrochloride and (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs was
spread-plated on the media using an L-rod and incubated at 37 °C for
12 h.
Growth Kinetics Study. The growth curve was analyzed for S.
aureus MTCC 96 and P. aeruginosa MTCC 2488 after treating the
bacteria with the MIC and MBC of (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs and free
promethazine hydrochloride. Their growth was monitored for 12 h at
37 °C (180 rpm) with an interval of 2 h.
Leakage Study. Overnight grown bacterial cultures were used to
reinoculate into the respective medium and incubated for 12 h.
Aliquots of bacterial suspensions treated with sub-MICs of free
promethazine and (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs were incubated at 37 °C
for 12 h. A control was included in each case containing untreated
bacteria. The suspensions were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 2 min,
and the supernatant was passed through 0.45 μ filters. The absorbance
of the clear supernatant was estimated at 260 and 280 nm using a
UV−vis spectrophotometer.
DCFDA Assay. The ROS generated after treating the bacterial
cells was measured through a 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate
(DCFDA)-based assay. Overnight grown cultures were adjusted to
105 cfu/mL. The sub-MIC of free promethazine and (Man-BSA)-PH-
AuNCs was used to treat them for 6 h at 37 °C and 180 rpm.
Following incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm/10 °C
for 30 min for disruption. The supernatant was collected and treated
with 100 μM DCFDA for 1 h in the dark. Fluorescence was detected
at an excitation filter of 485 nm and an emission filter of 523 nm using
a PerkinElmer fluorescence spectrophotometer.
Bacterial Biofilm Assay. The bacteria were diluted to 1 × 106
cfu/mL using the NB (for P. aeruginosa) and BHI (for S. aureus
MTCC 96) media from which 100 μL of bacteria was added to a 96-
well plate and incubated at 37 °C in static conditions. After 24 h, the
medium was removed, and fresh medium was added. To this, different
concentrations of free promethazine hydrochloride and (Man-BSA)-
PH-AuNCs were added and incubated for 12 h. After incubation, the
medium was removed, and the biofilm was stained with 0.01% crystal
violet. The stained biofilm was incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. The
crystal violet was dissolved using 95% ethanol, and the absorbance (in
triplicate) was measured at 595 nm using the microplate reader.

■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Preliminary Screening of Antihistamine Drugs for
Potential Antibacterial Activity. We used six different types
of antihistamine drugs that are available over the counter
(OTC) for the initial screening in P. aeruginosa, E. coli, and S.
aureus strains. Their chemical classes are listed in Table S1.
The details of the procedure are described in the Supporting
Information. Among all the drugs, promethazine displayed
activity against all three strains with zones of radii as high as 6
mm for Gram-positive and 4 mm for Gram-negative bacteria
(Figures S2 and S3). Hence, based on the spread plate method,
promethazine was selected for its best inhibitory activity
against all the three strains at the given concentrations (Table
S2).
Development and Characterization of Mannose-
Coated BSA-Stabilized Gold Nanoclusters Encapsulat-
ing Promethazine Hydrochloride ((Man-BSA)-PH-
AuNCs). Nanotechnology-based antimicrobial targeting has
become an important strategy in tackling off-target hits,
biocompatibility, stability, and solubility issues. To reap even
better benefits of nanocarriers, functionalization is a well-
known approach. While the gold nanoclusters aided with
tracking, the D-mannose coating helped increase the carriers’
overall specificity toward the target. They can also help reduce
toxicity and prolong circulation time. Following the con-
jugation of BSA and D-mannose, we performed the following
experiments to confirm the same.
In a Maillard reaction, a reducing sugar like mannose reacts
with the free amino groups in proteins to form brown complex
polymers that contain nitrogen.35,36 These brown products are
often used to validate the reaction. Therefore, to confirm the
conjugation of D-mannose to BSA, absorbances at 420 and 294
nm for free BSA and Man-BSA were compared. An increase in
the A420 value after glycation indicated that browning had
occurred. Similarly, an increase in A294 of Man-BSA compared
to BSA is an indication of intermediate reaction products
(Figure 1A). Such significant changes in the absorbance values
indicated the successful conjugation of D-mannose to BSA.
Additionally, we also compared the fluorescence spectra of
BSA and Man-BSA. The fluorescence spectrum of BSA showed
λmax at 356 nm, but after glycation with D-mannose, it had
moved to 352 nm (Figure 1B). This blue shift can be
attributed to the attachment of the monosaccharide to the
protein interior, thus masking the tryptophan residues. In

The vibrations at 1652−1405 cm−1 (Figure 1D) indicated the
attachment of carbonyl (C=O) from mannose, while the peak
at 870 cm−1 was the resultant of the carbon−nitrogen (C−N)
bond, a characteristic of any glycoprotein conjugate. Based on
the above results, it may be concluded that D-mannose was
successfully conjugated to BSA, thus forming the glycation
products. Finally, we measured the concentration of mannose
through anthrone-based estimation of carbohydrates. The
(Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs were found to have a mannose
concentration of 383 μM (Figure S4).
BSA was selected as both the stabilizing and protective
agent. It is widely known as a model protein because the
disulfide bond and the free SH groups of BSA provide steric
protection, thus stabilizing the cluster.37 The as-synthesized
(Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs appeared transparent and deep orange
in color. Under UV light, they exhibited a strong red emission.
When excited at 365 nm, they had a λem of 650 nm, while BSA-
AuNCs had a λem of 656 nm (Figure 2C). TEM images
revealed (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs with an average size of 3.57
± 0.5 nm (Figure 2A,B). Similarly, the average size of BSA-
AuNCs was found to be 2.5 ±0.9 nm (Figure S5A). The size
calculations were carried out using the ImageJ software. Thus,
from the TEM images, the successful synthesis of the (Man-
BSA)-PH-AuNCs was confirmed. The selected area electron
diffraction pattern for BSA-AuNCs did not show any
significant pattern (Figure S5C), which is in correlation with
the XRD data obtained (Figure S5F). Additionally, the UV−vis
graph of the (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs revealed a feeble peak at
248 nm for promethazine and 280 nm for BSA. This is in
concurrence with promethazine’s UV−vis spectral peak at 248
nm and a weak shoulder at 297 nm (Figure S5D). However,
there was no peak in the visible region for gold, thus
discounting the possibility of the formation of surface plasmon
resonance active gold nanoparticles in the reaction medium.
The zeta potential analysis confirmed that the BSA-AuNCs
have a surface charge of −22.5 mV (Figure S5E), while (Man-
BSA)-PH-AuNCs have a surface charge of −17.5 mV (Figure
2D). Surface functionality is a critical parameter regulating the
biodistribution and pharmacokinetics of nanoparticles. The
negative charge on the nanocarrier is preferable for drug
delivery as positively charged drugs can be loaded into it
through electrostatic interaction. Also, negative charge ensures
significantly prolonged circulating half-lives, while highly
cationic nanocarriers are rapidly cleared off from the
circulation.38 Furthermore, they have reduced plasma protein
adsorption and lower nonspecific uptake.39 To explore the
effect of pH during synthesis, we synthesized the (Man-BSA)-
PH-AuNCs at three different pH values (10, 12, and 14). It
was observed that with increasing pH, there was a slight blue
shift and reduction in the fluorescence intensity (Figure S6).
Following this, we performed atomic absorption spectros-
copy (AAS), and the concentration of gold in the (Man-BSA)-
PH-AuNCs was found to be 76.4 μg/mL (Figure S7).
Additionally, the elemental composition was supported by
mapping performed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectrosco-
py (Figure S8). Then, using UV−vis spectroscopy, the loading
efficiency of promethazine was found to be 62%, and
consecutively, the concentration of promethazine loaded was
calculated to be 12.4 mM. The encapsulation of promethazine
into the gold nanoclusters might be a result of the covalent
bond between the gold and sulfur interface.40 The formation of
the gold−thiolate bond requires the sulfhydryl group to be
deprotonated, creating a thiyl radical (RS·), whereas the
protonated SH group can interact with gold only by weaker
coordination bonds through sulfur’s lone-pair electrons. Since
the thiolate−gold (RS−Au) bond has a strength close to that
of the gold−gold bond, it can modify the gold−gold bonding
at the gold−sulfur interface.

Antibacterial Effect of (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs against
Gram-Negative and Gram-Positive Bacteria. The anti-
bacterial activity of (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs was determined
on Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus MTCC 96 and B. subtilis
MTCC 1305) and Gram-negative bacteria (P. aeruginosa
MTCC 2488 and E. coli DH5α). The MIC and MBC values
obtained for promethazine and (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs are
listed in Table S3. Upon treatment, the MIC value for B.
subtilis (459 μM) was the lowest, followed by S. aureus (678
μM), P. aeruginosa (687 μM), and E. coli (698 μM). The
results were also further substantiated through the spread plate
method, where bacteria were plated on their respective agar
and then treated (Figure S10). A lesser number of colonies
compared to negative control (lawn-like appearance) were
observed in MIC, while at MBC, no colonies were observed.
We have also determined the zones of radii of inhibition of
(Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs on spread-plated bacteria and com-
pared them with respective positive and negative controls

Mechanism of Antibacterial Activity. To decipher the
effect of (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs on bacterial membrane
permeability, a leakage study was performed. The increase in
the absorbance values (Figure 4) at 260 and 280 nm of (Man-
BSA)-PH-AuNCs- and promethazine-treated bacteria com-
pared to untreated bacteria is an indication of membrane
damage. Because of this, cellular components like protein and
genetic material leak out of the cells and seep into the media
that gives the higher absorbance.
At sub-MICs, a phenothiazine class of drugs depolarize the
membrane and increase its permeability and fluidity.49 This
may lead to changes in the cellular structure and inner and
outer membrane-bound protein functionality.50 The posses-
sion of certain surfactant-like characters, owing to a tertiary
amino group and a bulky aromatic structure, could be linked to
their membrane-altering effects.51 However, the increase in
leakage of (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs-treated bacteria compared
to free promethazine can be attributed to the combinatorial
effects of promethazine and gold nanoclusters52,53 delivered
through a nanocarrier.

Generation of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS). To
determine whether treatment with the as-synthesized (Man-
BSA)-PH-AuNCs generated reactive oxygen species (ROS) in
bacteria, 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA) dye, a
cell-permeable nonfluorescent precursor of DCF, was used for
its detection. Endogenously, cells produce ROS, which
eventually is converted to hydrogen peroxide through
superoxide dismutases and catalases.54 However, under stress
such as hypoxia, there can be excessive ROS accumulation.
This may damage proteins, DNA, and intracellular systems
such as respiratory systems.
Initially, through spectrophotometry, we quantified the
fluorescence in treated vs untreated bacteria. The intensity of
fluorescence is directly proportional to the amount of oxidized
DCFH-DA to DCF. Intracellular esterases can cleave DCFH-
DA at its two ester bonds and produce H2DCF. The
subsequent oxidation of this accumulated product yields the
highly fluorescent DCF. It was observed that upon treatment,
there was a significant increase in the fluorescence intensity at
523 nm compared to the untreated bacteria (Figure 6).
The suggestive production of ROS in treated bacteria may
be attributed to a combination of factors. First, AuNCs in the
as-synthesized (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs are known to cause
oxidative stress in bacterial cells.55 This may be because even
though bulk gold is chemically inactive, the ultrasmall gold
nanoclusters undergo a drastic change in their physical and
chemical properties. Second, studies show that phenothiazine
derivatives like methylene blue can inhibit bacteria via
photodynamic inactivation.56 Promethazine being a member
of the same chemical class may also have functioned in a
similar manner once inside the cell. Hence, the treatment may
have caused an intracellular imbalance, leading to ROS
generation and subsequent bacterial inhibition.
Inhibition of Bacterial Biofilms. Following the determi-
nation of bactericidal activity of (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs, we
proceeded to utilize it against bacterial biofilms. These well-
structured communities of adherent bacterial cells are enclosed
in a self-produced extracellular polysaccharide matrix (EPS).57
Biofilms are known to pose a serious threat to life forms
through nosocomial infections58 related to medical implants58
and chronic infections like cystic fibrosis.59 Gram-negative
bacteria like P. aeruginosa can arm themselves with biofilm
formation to survive in hostile environments.60 Similarly,
Gram-positive S. aureus are also known to form biofilms on
animate and inanimate surfaces, thus posing complications,
which is rapidly becoming a case of concern.61
The antibiofilm studies carried out on P. aeruginosa, E. coli,
and S. aureus confirmed 50% inhibition (MBIC50) by (Man-
BSA)-PH-AuNCs at concentrations of 8, 6.9, and 4.2 mM,
respectively (Figure 7A−C(II)). The same study carried out
with only free promethazine hydrochloride inhibited 50% of
the biofilms at 2.8, 2.9, and 1.4 mM, respectively (Figure 7A−
C(I)). Altogether, these results were found to be in coherence
with studies on planktonic bacteria.
Staining of Biofilms. The AO/PI dual staining study
revealed that the (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs could successfully
reduce the biofilm mass in the case of both Gram-negative P.
aeruginosa and E. coli and Gram-positive S. aureus. AO could
permeate the control cells and they emitted green fluorescence
(Figure 8A−C(I−III)).
The emission of red fluorescence due to PI was negligible in
control biofilms of both P. aeruginosa and S. aureus possibly
because of the smaller number of dead cells. In the case of the
biofilm treated with sub-MBIC50 (Figure 8A−C(IV−VI)) and
MBIC50 of (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs (Figure 8A−C(VII−IX)),
a prominent red fluorescence was observed that increased with
an increase in the concentration of the composite. The red
fluorescence is a result of the penetration of PI dye through the
damaged bacterial membrane. The green fluorescence emitted
from live cells was very limited at the highest treatment
concentration. For comparison, P. aeruginosa and S. aureus
biofilms were also treated with MBIC50 concentration of free
promethazine (Figure S14).
The as-synthesized Man (BSA-PH-AuNCs) antibiofilm
activity was further characterized through confocal microscopy
where Nile red dye was used to study the lipid content in the
Gram-negative bacterial biofilm (Figure 9). In the case of the
control biofilm, the confocal microscopy images revealed a
higher lipid content over the entire individual well in a 96-well
plate emitting a higher red fluorescence (Figure 9A,B(I−II)).
However, upon treatment with sub-MBIC50 (Figure 9A,B(III−
IV)) and MBIC50 of (Man-BSA)-PH-AuNCs (Figure
9A,B(V−VI)), there was a concomitant reduction in red
fluorescence with fragments of the degraded biofilm visible in
the bright-field and merged images. This suggested the
successful inhibition of the biofilm mass, as indicated by the
diminished lipid content. The confocal images of MBIC50 of
promethazine-treated and Nile red-stained biofilms are also
added in the Supporting Information (Figure S15).
■CONCLUSIONS
With increasing resistance toward common antibiotics,
infectious diseases are predominantly becoming an impossible
wall to break. Towards exploring alternate options, the
antihistamine drug promethazine was repurposed for potential
activity against Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria and
their respective biofilms. A phenothiazine class of drugs, it is
known to alter bacterial membrane permeability and
intercalate into plasmids. Promethazine was successfully loaded
onto mannosylated BSA-stabilized gold nanoclusters for better
stability, specificity, and prolonged circulation time. The as-
synthesized drug-loaded clusters were nano-sized, luminescent,
and stable in nature. Although promethazine has been known
to affect the pili of Gram-negative E. coli, it was found to have a
superior activity against Gram-positive S. aureus and B. subtilis,
possibly due to their lack of an outer membrane that allowed
easy entry into the cells. The mannosylation of the gold
nanoclusters helped in better targeting of the Gram-negative
bacteria as well, especially E. coli. D-Mannose can also bind to
the Gram-negative pili and inhibit them. Furthermore, the
growth kinetics curve of all four strains of treated bacteria
showed an arrest. Upon treatment, the consequent drug release
may have resulted in cell death following intracellular ROS
generation and cell membrane damage. Additionally, the
sustained release of promethazine from the nanoclusters also
helped to inhibit bacterial biofilms of P. aeruginosa, E. coli, and
S. aureus. As promethazine is a clinically approved drug, its
safety profile has been established. Drug delivery via carriers
over free drugs has become the preferred approach lately.62,63
Utilizing gold nanoclusters for the same is backed by their use

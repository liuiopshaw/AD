ABSTRACT: This work represents an important step in the quest
for creating atomically precise binary semiconductor nanoclusters
(BS-NCs). Compared with coinage metal NCs, the preparation of
BS-NCs requires strict control of the reaction kinetics to guarantee
the formation of an atomically precise single phase under mild
conditions, which otherwise could lead to the generation of multiple
phases. Herein, we developed an acid-assisted thiolate dissociation
approach that employs suitable acid to induce cleavage of the S−C
bonds in the Cu−S−R (R = alkyl) precursor, spontaneously
fostering the formation of the [Cu−S−Cu] skeleton upon the
addition of extra Cu sources. Through this method, a high-nuclearity
copper sulfide nanocluster, Cu50S12(SC(CH3)3)20(CF3COO)12 (ab-
breviated as [S-Cu50] hereafter), has been successfully prepared in
high yield, and its atomic structure was accurately modeled through single-crystal X-ray diffraction. It was revealed that [S-Cu50]
exhibits a unique double-shell structural configuration of [Cu14S12]@[Cu36S20], and the innermost [Cu14] moiety displays a rhombic
dodecahedron geometry, which has never been observed in previously synthesized Cu metal, hydride, or chalcogenide NCs.
Importantly, [S-Cu50] represents the first example incorporating mixed Cu(II)/Cu(I) valences in reported atomically precise copper
sulfide NCs, which was unambiguously confirmed by XPS, EPR, and XANES. In addition, the electronic structure of [S-Cu50] was
established by a variety of optical investigations, including absorption, photoluminescence, and ultrafast transient absorption
spectroscopies, as well as theoretical calculations. Moreover, [S-Cu50] is air-stable and demonstrates electrocatalytic activity in ORR
with a four-electron pathway.
■INTRODUCTION
Atomically precise nanoclusters (NCs) possess ultrasmall
dimensions (less than 3 nm) and hold a unique position
between small molecules and large bulk materials, which
enables them to display a variety of intriguing physicochemical
properties.1,2 Usually, they are deemed as an important class of
metastable intermediates in the course of crystal growth from
small complexes to large nanoparticles.3,4 Therefore, they
inherit low-symmetry structures from small molecules and at
the same time start to exhibit embryonic structural features of
the corresponding bulk solids. From the kinetics perspective,
the acquisition of atomically precise NCs offers opportunities
to gain deep insights into the growth mechanisms for larger
nanomaterials.5,6 From the view of designing novel nanoma-
terials, NCs themselves are quite unique in various properties
because of the strong quantum confinement.7,8 Importantly,
NCs are positioned on the boundary of the size scale to access
the atomic structures through single-crystal X-ray diffraction,
since it would become extremely challenging to grow single
crystals of suitable quality for larger nanoparticles.9,10
Atomically precise coinage metal NCs, especially Au, Ag and
Cu, are the main focuses in this research subject, and great
success in the development of these NCs has clearly shown
significant value in both fundamental science and applications
in several aspects.11,12 For example, general preparation
approaches have been established to obtain atomically precise
metal NCs,11,13 which affords atomic-level precision for the
NCs’ structure to ensure the accuracy of the structure−
property relationship,14,15 and new materials with novel
properties are being continuously discovered or created.16,17
With the rapid progress in the development of coinage metal
NCs, isolating more complex atomically precise binary
semiconductor NCs (BS-NCs) is emerging as one of the
most promising directions. This is because (i) BS-NCs have

distinct electronic structures, which are the determining factor
for their overall properties.19 (iii) As opposed to the metallic
skeleton in metal NCs, BS-NCs feature a coordinate covalent

the coordination modes between the metal centers and anions
greatly enhances the diversity of their structures and
compositions.20 Copper sulfides belong to one of the most
commonly investigated BS materials with p-type conductivity,
and they have a wide spectrum of applications in photo-
catalysts, transistors, solar cells, etc.21,22 Their versatility in
applications has been partially ascribed to the multiple
oxidation states for copper, including Cu(0), Cu(I), Cu(II),
and even Cu(III),23 and the subtle change of the oxidation
state may result in significant differences in properties. For
example, in 2021 Gao and co-workers prepared a Cu2−xS
ultrathin film, whose optical properties can be effectively
modulated between fluorescent and plasmonic by tuning the
oxidation state of Cu.24
Relatively mature synthetic approaches have been estab-
lished for the preparation of coinage metal NCs. Generally,
metal NCs can be effectively prepared through a two-step
reaction pathway, that is, the acquisition of monomeric/
polymeric metal−ligand complexes, and the formation of a
thermodynamically favorable metallic skeleton after the
reduction process.1 The merits of such a preparative method
lie in its mild operating conditions and controllable kinetics,
which result in atomically precise NCs whose atomic structures
can be characterized by single-crystal X-ray diffraction.
However, the preparation strategy for metal NCs cannot be
directly adopted by the binary metal chalcogenide NCs on
account of the quite different bonding natures between the
metal−metal and metal−chalcogenide bonds. In order to
design an appropriate synthetic approach for the preparation of
the desired binary metal chalcogenide NCs, two aspects should
be carefully taken into account: (i) in most cases, the metal−
metal bond formation for coinage metal NCs (e.g., Au, Ag, or
Cu NCs) is a naturally occurring outcome upon the addition of
reducing reagents at room temperature, while the creation of
BS-NCs usually requires extra driving forces to dissociate the
starting reagent (in order to release anions) and overcome the
energy barriers to form the metal−chalcogenide bonds.25,26 (ii)
The half-cell reduction potentials for metals dictate the
easiness of forming the metallic skeleton for metal NCs, and
their resultant atomic structures are mainly dependent on the
surfactant ligands used in synthesis,27−29 whereas the choice of
organic ligands, the kinetics for the dissociation of the anion
source, and the binding energy for metal−chalcogenide bonds
could all be involved independently or synergistically into the
construction of the final binary architectures. The former issue
raises a contradictory problem that during the synthesis of
metal chalcogenide NCs, heating is often a necessary operation
to effectively decompose the starting materials and release the
anions,25,30 but at the same time leads to an uncontrollable
crystal growth process that makes the preparation of atomically
precise NCs problematic. The latter factor imposes complex-
ities in designing the syntheses of NCs with target structures.
Therefore, compared with metal NCs, the acquisition of BS-
NCs demands more intensive development of their synthetic
routes.
Encouragingly, there have been a number of atomically
precise BS-NCs obtained heretofore, and they demonstrate a
host of compelling chemophysical activities. For example,
Brandi and co-workers employed highly reactive phosphane as
a reagent to prepare In37P20(O2CCH2Ph)51.25 Feng and Zheng
have successfully prepared a series of cadmium sulfide
superlattices (featuring multiple tetrahedron units denoted as
Tn) through a solvothermal method.31,32 For the preparation of
copper chalcogenide NCs, several interesting structures have
been successfully obtained, such as Cu32S5(StBu)(hfac)6
33 and
Cu74S15(2-PET)45.34 In addition, D. Fenske and co-workers
have systematically developed an effective synthetic approach
that employs coinage metal phosphane complexes (e.g.,
[MX(PR2R′)] or [MX(dppR)x]y, M = coinage metal, X =
halide) and reactive R″E(SiMe3) or E(SiMe3)2 (E = S, Se or
Te, R″ = organic group) to prepare a series of chalcogenide-
bridged Cu(I) NCs which can be generally formulated as
Cu2E.35,36 Interestingly, this method is particularly suitable for
copper selenide NCs, because the preliminarily formed
phosphane complexes [CuX(PR2R′)x]y or [CuX(dppR)x]y
display comparable reactivity to that of Se(SiMe3)2, so that
the dissociation of Se(SiMe3)2 to yield Se2−and the
construction of the Cu−Se framework could occur via a
reasonable kinetic pathway, ensuring the creation of atomically
precise Cu−Se NCs.20
Our group is interested in the design of atomically precise
metal sulfide nanomaterials and their applications. Herein, we
report the synthesis, isolation, and characterization of an air-
stable copper sulfide nanocluster Cu50S12(SC(CH3)3)20-
(CF3COO)12 (abbreviated as [S-Cu50] thereafter) through a
two-step reaction process. The [S-Cu50] demonstrates a highly
symmetric geometry with a rare double-shell atomic structure.
Moreover, the [S-Cu50], to the best of our knowledge, is the
first atomically precise copper sulfide NC featuring a Cu(II)/
Cu(I) composition reported to date.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Synthesis of [S-Cu50]. The synthesis of [S-Cu50] was
started off from the Cu(I) thiolate precursor obtained by the
reaction of Cu2O and HStbutyl in pyridine (SI, eq S1), aiming
to achieve three goals: (1) obtaining a soluble and reactive Cu-
containing precursor; (2) providing sulfur for the formation of
sulfide (S2−); (3) introducing passivating ligands that direct
the crystal growth afterward. The mixture of Cu2O and
HStbutyl was first suspended in pyridine and stirred at room
temperature for 12 h (more information can be found in the
SI). The reddish-brown, cloudy suspension gradually turned
into a clear orange solution. After that, the solvent was
evaporated off under vacuum, and the resultant powder was
further dried under vacuum at 60 °C for 12 h. A high-purity
Cu(I)-thiolate precursor (SI, Figure S16) can be obtained on a
large scale and safely stored in an inert atmosphere for future
use. Of note, performing the reaction in acetone, alcohols, and
tetrahydrofuran (THF) was found to be extremely slow;
therefore, it can be speculated that pyridine plays an important
role in proton transfer to facilitate the reaction of thiol and
Cu2O (SI, Scheme S1).37 In the next step, the Cu2O and
Cu(I)-thiolate precursor were mixed and suspended in THF,
followed by addition of excess CF3COOH, which is one of the
key steps with two purposes: (1) inducing bond cleavage of the
S−C from the terminal −Stbutyl groups to facilitate the
formation of a Cu−S−Cu framework and (2) dissolving Cu2O
(forming a Cu−OOCCF3 complex) to afford more copper

To account for the cleavage of the C−S bond and the
generation of Cu(II), we proposed a simplified reaction
mechanism that illustrates the key steps leading to the
formation of the [S-Cu50] assembly (SI, Scheme S2 and eq
S2). Given the preparation of [S-Cu50] was performed in an
inert atmosphere, it can be deduced that the oxidation of
Cu(I) to Cu(II) was realized through a disproportionation
reaction on the Cu(I)−SC(CH3)3 precursor, which is
accompanied by the cleavage of the C−S bond to yield S2−.
The proton may play a role in stabilizing the intermediate to
foster the above process. This mechanism predicts that the
innermost Cu atoms should feature a high oxidation state,
which agrees well with the theoretical calculation of the metal
charge distribution (will be discussed in the Theoretical
Calculations section). In addition, the mechanism is partially
supported by the detection of the new alkane products by
NMR (SI, Figure S21).
Structure and Composition Determination. Single-
Crystal Structure. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis
reveals that [S-Cu50] crystallizes into the cubic space group
Fm3̅c, and its formula can be written as Cu50S12(SC(CH3)3)20-
(CF3COO)12 (SI, Table S1 and Figure S1), indicating the
incorporation of Cu(I) and Cu(II) valences. [S-Cu50] displays
a relatively isotropic geometry with a diameter of about 1.2 nm
(Figure 1a,d) and exhibits a novel double-shell atomic
structural configuration of [Cu14S12]@[Cu36S20] with a cavity
located in the center of the assembly. Briefly, the inner shell for
[S-Cu50] is a [Cu14S12] layer, which can be further separated
into a [Cu14] rhombic dodecahedron (Figure 1b) and a S12
icosahedron (Figure 1c). Although the 12 S atoms also build a
definitive icosahedral geometry, we do not deem it as an
independent shell, because there are no chemical interactions
between the sulfur atoms (average distance for S···S is 4.148
Å). The outer shell is a [Cu36S20] layer with a dimension of
about 1.2 nm (Figure 1d). It is notably different from the
[Cu14S12] shell in which the S atoms are all S2−and purely
bonding with Cu, while the 20 sulfurs in the outer [Cu36S20]
shell originate from the surfactant thiolate ligands. During the
refinement process, it was found that there are highly
disordered electrons remaining in the Fourier map of electron
density. Those electrons can be rationally defined as neutral
THF molecules, provided no counterions were introduced in
the synthesis.
Detailed structural analysis reveals that the inner shell is
made up of 14 copper and 12 sulfur atoms. The 14 copper
atoms organize into an ideal rhombic dodecahedron with 12
congruent [Cu4] rhombic faces (Figure 2a), and all Cu−Cu
bond distances are equal at 2.707(15) Å, which falls into the
range of the van der Waals length for a Cu−Cu metallic bond
(2.80 Å).38,39 The [Cu14] rhombic dodecahedron is subject to
the intrinsic symmetries of the ideal mathematical model,
which contains two different 2-fold axes, a 4- and a 6-fold axis
(SI, Figures S2 and S3). We have carefully examined the single-
crystal X-ray diffraction data and did not find any obvious
positive electron residues inside the innermost [Cu14] rhombic
dodecahedron, confirming the hollow character for the [S-
Cu50]. In fact, it has been long accepted that compared with
the hollow structure, the solid cores are usually more
energetically favorable.40 For example, in Au or Ag metal
NCs featuring an icosahedron kernel, the [M13] configuration
(M = Au or Ag) is more favorable than the hollow [M12]
kernel, which can be attributed to the extra stability provided
by the 12 M−M bonds originating from the central atoms
(also see SI, Table S2, [Cu13] icosahedron and [Cu12] hollow
icosahedron).41,42 In this respect, [S-Cu50] belongs to a
relatively rare NC family possessing a hollow core structure
and represents the first example with a rhombic dodecahedron
geometry among atomically precise Cu metal or chalcogenide
NCs reported to date. By virtue of the geometric perfection of
the [Cu14] rhombic dodecahedron, it allows one to easily
calculate several important geometrical parameters for such a
convex polyhedron. For example, the volume (V) of the
rhombic dodecahedron is 61.1 Å3, and the radius (ri) of the
inscribed sphere is about 2.21 Å (SI, Figure S4). In-depth
structural scrutinization reveals that the inner shell is not only
composed of a metallic copper skeleton but also has 12 outer
sulfur atoms to yield an overall [Cu14S12] layer. Each sulfur
atom is hanging over a congruent rhombic face and closer to
one of the vertices as shown in Figure 2b. Each sulfur atom is
chemically bonded to three Cu vertices of the rhombus,
resulting in a distorted pyramidal geometry (Figure 2b), and
the bond distances for S1−Cu1 and S1−Cu2 are equally
2.320(18) Å, while S1−Cu3 displays a shorter bond length of
2.157(3) Å (SI, Figure S5 and Table S3). Moreover, the 12
sulfur atoms form an icosahedral geometry (Figure 2c) even
though they are not chemically bonded. Instead, the 12 sulfur
atoms act as an interstitial anionic bridge to the outer shell
through Cu−S−Cu interactions, showing an overall μ4-
bridging pattern (SI, Figure S5). The outer shell for [S-
Cu50] is a [Cu36S20] spherical layer, which is built up from six
congruent “double-star” motifs of [Cu10S8] (Figure 2d). Such a
geometry can be considered as the connecting of the two
distorted stars by sharing two corners. The two stars are not
coplanar but exhibit a dihedral angle of about 64.87° (SI,
Figure S7). In addition, the overall “double-star” geometry
contains a 2-fold axis and two vertical mirror planes (Figure
2d), belonging to the symmetry group of C2v. The six “double-

The [S-Cu50] shows a cubic geometry with regiospecific
faces that guide NCs to self-assemble into a simple cubic
packing pattern in the crystal lattice (SI, Figure S11). Such a
packing mode is directed by both face-to-face noncovalent
intercluster interactions and geometrical matching. The former
factor is associated with regiospecific faces which bind with
each other via the hydrogen bonding of C−H···F (specifically,
there are eight C−H···F hydrogen bonds 2.676 Å in length
between each contacting of the faces), and the latter driving
force can be attributed to the tendency for the cubic geometry
to extend by packing the subunits in a face-to-face fashion.
Interestingly, the hydrogen bonding directs a 90° rotation for
the adjacent cluster to match the C−H···F interaction,
resulting in a 3D superlattice composed of clusters in
alternating 90° orientations (Figure 3c,d). It should be noted
that NCs with a small size usually retain the structural natures
of small molecules with low symmetry,10,43 and the resulting
geometries for such NCs are commonly spherical, rodlike, or
nondefinite shapes.5,17,28 The rectangular hexahedral (cubic or
cuboidal) packing patterns are rare, which may be because of
the difficulty for the maximum space filling compared with
close packings like face centered cubic (fcc) or hexagonal
closed packed (hcp). In 2021, Bakr and co-workers reported
the
first
high-nuclearity
polyhydridocopper
NC,
[Cu23(PhSe)16(Ph3P)8(H)6]·BF4, with a cuboidal geometry,
which further assembles into a quasi-simple cubic (q-SC)
geometry.44 The [S-Cu50] obtained in the current work
exhibits a similar packing pattern, but it extends from the cubic
clusters to an overall cubic superstructure. Such a self-assembly
behavior at atomic-level precision can span over several orders
of magnitude, reflecting in the shape of the resultant
macroscopic solids (Figure 3e,f): immediately after the
completion of the reaction, all solid products were immediately
imaged by scanning electron microscope (SEM), and most of
the solids display definitive cuboidal or cubic shapes (SI,
Figures S13, S14, and S15), which is a consequence of the self-
assembly behavior discussed above. The solids were further
investigated by small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) (Figure
3g), which shows strong scattering signals, indicating the
presence of long-range-ordered superstructure. All SAXS peaks
can be successfully indexed to the corresponding crystal planes
in a cubic system with the lattice length a = 40(1) Å (SI,
Figure S12 and Table S5; see SI for more information). This
aligns well with the lattice length from single-crystal X-ray
diffraction (cubic crystal system with a = 39.2248(10) Å).
Therefore, it can be concluded that the bulk polycrystalline [S-
Cu50] solids are all following the same cubic arrangement at
the macroscopic scale. It should be mentioned here that such a
crystallization process for atomically precise structures,
including molecules, NCs, or even larger particles, is certainly
a self-driven, spontaneous process upon the evaporation of
solvent (this is how single crystals are grown), but it is usually
quite vulnerable to disturbances of the surrounding environ-
ment, which may cause indefinite morphology for the solids. In
this work, it seems the self-assembly of the [S-Cu50] is quite
kinetically and thermodynamically favorable, so that the
atomic-level self-assembly pattern can span several orders of
magnitude for length scales, finally bringing about macroscopic
solids with a cubic or cuboidal morphology.

Determination of the Oxidation States for Cu. To
accurately determine the oxidation states for the metals is an
important step in assessing the electronic structure of a
nanomaterial, and it is helpful in establishing the relationship
between the atomic structure and chemophysical properties.
However, this is not trivial work for NCs, complicated by the
coexistence of multiple metal centers with different chemical
environments and the possible charge delocalization across the
nanostructure. In [S-Cu50], the 50 total copper cations are
charge-balanced with 12 S2−(there are no S−S bonds in the
single-crystal structure), 20 −SC(CH3)3, and 12 CF3COO−,
which give rise to an average oxidation state of +1.12 for Cu
from the formula, indicating the mixed valences of Cu(I)/
Cu(II), with the monovalent copper being the dominating
species.

An XPS survey scan (SI, Figure S22) shows the characteristic
peaks for C, O, F, S, and Cu elements without other
unexpected signals, which is consistent with the atomic
structure modeled from single-crystal X-ray diffraction. A
high-resolution scan applied to the Cu 2p orbitals (925−960
eV) unveils two obvious peaks centering at 952.88 and 932.98
eV, which can be assigned to Cu 2p1/2 and Cu 2p2/3 orbitals,
respectively. However, the execution of the one-phase
simulation immediately turned out to be unsuccessful, as
small features locating at the higher binding energy areas
cannot be satisfactorily fitted. Therefore, the second phase was
introduced in the simulation, which gave rise to a better-fitting
spectrum, as shown in Figure 4a. The result suggests that Cu
metal centers are of mixed valence, and the minor phase shows
a higher oxidation state for Cu (higher binding energy). Taking
into account the average oxidation state (+1.12) calculated
from the formula for Cu, the major and minor phases can be
rationally assigned to Cu(I) and Cu(II). In addition, broad
shakeup satellites ranging from 942.0 to 950.0 eV can be seen
in the spectrum, in which there is a small but observable bump
located at about 943 eV (Figure 4a). This corresponds well to
the characteristic satellite peak for Cu(II).50 Overall, the XPS
investigation infers the presence of Cu(I) and Cu(II) in the [S-
Cu50] assembly. As mentioned previously, it is not so simple to
confirm the presence of Cu(II) from XPS, especially for the
copper-chalcogenides. This might be because the Cu−S or
Cu−Se bonds are easily detrapping charges from specific metal
atoms,51 resulting in a relatively delocalized charge distribution
over the whole cluster and eventually bringing ambiguities to
the XPS fingerprints for Cu(I) and Cu(II).52 Interestingly, XPS
evidence for the presence of Cu(II) is clear in clusters where
Cu atoms are bonding with more electronegative oxygen. For
example, in 2021, Zang and co-workers reported a [Cu54] NC
(formulated as [Cu54S13O6(SC(CH3)3)20(C(CH3)3SO3)12])
with a S@(Cu12O6) core,53 and the (Cu12O6) component
consists of divalent Cu. In the XPS spectra, the Cu(I) and
Cu(II) 2p orbitals are evidently split (e.g., 932.18 and 935.04
eV for Cu(I) and Cu(II) 2p1/2 peaks, respectively), and strong
Cu(II) shakeup satellites are clearly shown. On the contrary,
copper sulfides usually display similar XPS features for all
valences of Cu, which impose difficulties in distinguishing
different valent copper components.54 In this regard, it implies
significant differences in the bonding natures for Cu−S and
Cu−O.
Electron Paramagnetic Resonance Spectroscopy
(EPR). In order to unambiguously confirm the presence of
the divalent copper in the [S-Cu50] structure, EPR spectros-
copy has been carried out. EPR is a technique that is able to
sensitively detect unpaired electrons and their variations in
surrounding environments by interfering with the magnetic
field to generate characteristic EPR signals. Based on this,
divalent Cu with the electronic configuration of 3d9 should
demonstrate an EPR response, while the Cu(I) is signal-silent
since it has a full 3d orbital (3d10). Here, [S-Cu50] single
crystals were ground into polycrystalline powder and placed in
a magnetic field at 100 K to record its EPR spectrum (Figure
4b). It was revealed that [S-Cu50] demonstrates strong EPR
signals with the g factor at about 2.073 (SI, Figure S24), which
is significantly larger than that for the isolated single electron
(ge = 2.0023), clearly confirming the presence of the Cu(II) in
the [S-Cu50] structure. In addition, the magnetic moment
caused by the spin of the unpaired electron can also interact
with the magnetic moment resulting from the spin of the
positively charged nucleus (spin−orbital coupling), generating
more EPR features called hyperfine coupling. Theoretically,
Cu(II) could produce four hyperfine lines before the major
EPR signals,55 but they are not observable in [S-Cu50],
impeding the successful assignment of the orbital for the
unpaired electron (dx

powder in THF, and it shows a similar profile to the solid EPR
spectrum (SI, Figure S23). The vague features for the

X-ray Absorption Near-Edge Spectroscopy. X-ray
absorption near-edge spectroscopy (XANES) is a powerful
technique to determine the average oxidation state of Cu in [S-
Cu50].28 Here, Cu(0), Cu(I)2S, and Cu(II)S were used as
references due to their Cu−Cu or Cu−S binding nature similar
to that of [S-Cu50] (Figure 4c). It shows that the [S-Cu50]
displays the typical K-edge absorption features for Cu, which
can be assigned to the 1s →4p electronic transition (Figure 4c,
inset). The accurate K-edge energies for all samples have been
extracted from their corresponding first-derivative plots (SI,
Figure S25) and the average oxidation state of Cu in [S-Cu50]
was determined to be +1.3 (Figure 4c) from the linear
calibration curve established with the reference samples. The
XANES results further confirm the presence of Cu(II) and
Cu(I) in [S-Cu50].
Theoretical Calculations. To understand the electronic
structure of [S-Cu50], density functional theory (DFT)
calculations have been performed using the hybrid PBE
functional at the def2-TZVP(Cu)/def2-SVP level. After fully
relaxing the [S-Cu50] structure, the molecular symmetry was
expectedly reduced (SI, Figure S37 and Table S7), which can
also be visualized from the wave functions of the frontier
molecular orbitals (Figure 5a). Consistent with the crystal
structure, the protecting ligands of −SC(CH3)3 and CF3COO−
are covalently bonding to the surface Cu. The frontier orbitals
for HOMO−1, HOMO, LUMO, and LUMO+1 are primarily
localized on the outer [Cu36S20] shell, while the wave functions
for HOMO−2 and HOMO−3 are more concentrated on the
inner [Cu14S12] shell. The LUMO+2 and LUMO+3 states are
both concentrated on the inner shell but feature different
orbital components. Specifically, the LUMO+2 is dominated
by d orbitals from Cu−Cu in the innermost [Cu14], while the
LUMO+3 is mainly composed of s and p orbitals from the
Cu−S in the inner [Cu14S12] shell. The projected density of
states shows that the HOMOs are dominated by d orbitals
from Cu and p orbitals from S and O (SI, Figure S38). The S
from −SC(CH3)3 ligands provides more influence on the wave
functions (>85%) compared to the O from CF3COO−in the
high-energy HOMOs, indicating the different binding
strengths for the two surfactant ligands. The LUMOs for [S-
Cu50] are mainly composed of s and p orbitals from Cu.
Interestingly, the three lowest LUMOs comprising d orbitals of
Cu and p orbitals of S are similar to those in HOMOs, which is
clearly revealed in the Kohn−Sham molecular energy diagram
(Figure 5b). It should be addressed here that the calculated 3d
shells for Cu display partial empty character, indicating their
divalent nature, which agrees with the previous XPS and EPR
investigations. In addition, a narrow band gap between the
HOMO and LUMO (0.82 eV) is expected for the mixed-

To ascertain the charge distribution for Cu in the [S-Cu50]
assembly, Mulliken charge was calculated on each Cu atom
(Figure 5c and SI, Table S6). Generally, the Cu atoms in the
inner [Cu14S12] shell are more positively charged than those in
the outer [Cu36S20] shell (av 0.934 vs 0.216). In particular, 8 of
14 Cu atoms in [Cu14S12] possess much higher charges
(1.282), and the Mulliken charges for the remaining 6 Cu
atoms (0.374−0.629) are also obviously higher than those in
[Cu36S20] (0.216). The charge separation between the inner
[Cu14S12] shell and the outer [Cu36S20] shell is accompanied
by the elongation of the Cu−Cu bond, consistent with our X-
ray structural analysis (SI, Figures S9 and S10). This can also
be verified by the reduction of bond order from an average of
2.1 in the inner shell to an average of 0.9 in the outer shell, as
revealed by the Mayer bond valence (Figure 5c and SI, Table
S6). The calculated Mulliken charge distribution is also
consistent with the proposed reaction mechanism, which
predicts that the coppers located in the innermost shell have
higher valences (SI, Scheme S2).

Oxygen Reduction Reaction. Copper-based nanomateri-
als are potentially good candidates for electrocatalysts with
high catalytic efficiency and durability.29 The electrocatalytic
activities of [S-Cu50] were evaluated through the oxygen
reduction reaction (ORR) (Figure 7 and SI, Figures S29−
S36). The active electrode was fabricated by mixing [S-Cu50]
with carbon black (10 wt % loading) to improve the overall
conductivity (more information can be found in the SI). The
electrochemical experiment was conducted on a three-
electrode setup, where Ag/AgCl served as a reference
electrode, a carbon rod served as the counter-electrode, and
0.1 M KOH acted as the electrolyte. With the calibration of
Ag/AgCl vs RHE (SI, Figure S29), all potential values have
been converted to those relative to RHE.
The ORR investigation shows that without the saturation of
O2 (the solution was purged with Ar for 0.5 h), the CV plot of
[S-Cu50] (SI, Figure S30) still shows redox signals at 0.63 V
(reduction) and 0.77 V (oxidation), which can be attributed to
the intrinsic oxidation/reduction process between the Cu(II)/
Cu(I) states, and this also aligns well with previously reported
observations.65 When the electrolyte was saturated with O2,
the [S-Cu50]/C electrode displays a different CV profile (SI,
Figure S30), and the LSV curve for [S-Cu50] (Figure 7a)
clearly reveals the obvious increase for the limited current
density from −0.86 mA/cm2 to −6.05 mA/cm2. Moreover,
under the same ORR investigation conditions, inactive pure
carbon black displays a featureless CV response in O2-saturated
electrolyte (SI, Figure S31), precluding the pseudo-electro-
chemical signals resulting from systematic errors. The above
observations suggest the [S-Cu50] is active as an ORR catalyst.
Detailed examination of the ORR performance shows that the
[S-Cu50]/C electrode displays an onset potential of 0.83 V, a
half-wave potential of 0.56 V, and an overpotential of 0.40 V
(see SI, Figure S33 for more details about the extrapolation of
the half-wave potential). Although those performance param-
eters are not comparable with the commercial ORR catalysts
like Pt/C (0.95 and 0.81 V for onset and half-wave potentials,
respectively),66 they are still superior among the synthesized
atomically precise NCs.66,67 The ORR reaction kinetics for [S-
Cu50] was investigated by the rotating ring-disk electrode
(RRDE) method (see the SI for more information), through
which, the linear Koutecký−Levich plots from 0.20 to 0.60 V
were profiled (SI, Figures S32 and S34).
Based on these findings, it was calculated that the ORR
follows an interesting four-electron pathway for each O2
molecule (Figure 7b and SI, Figure S34) since most of the
reported atomically precise NCs usually exhibit either a pure
two-electron or a combination of two-/four-electron reduction
pathways.29,68 The four-electron mechanism was further
confirmed by the detection of small amounts of H2O2 (less
than 5%), which is a two-electron pathway product during the
electrochemical catalytic process.69,70 All in all, due to the
paucity of high-nuclearity binary copper sulfide NCs (Cu >
25), our work is, as of writing, the first report of the ORR
performance for such nanomaterials, and the four-electron
pathway makes it attractive as a model to design better-
performing nanomaterials.

A B S T R A C T   
High-efficiency enrichment of functional bacteria is one of the biggest technical barriers to the application of 
anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox). This study combined biofilm carriers and quorum sensing to ach­
ieve rapid biomass accumulation. A biofilm carrier with biomass adsorption and quorum sensing regulation 
functions was developed for the rapid start-up of the reactor and enrichment of anammox bacteria (AnAOB). The 
nitrogen removal rates of the three reactors were further tested, and the carrier mechanism was explored. The 
start-up time of the reactor with the modified carriers was 28 days, which was ~44 % shorter than that of the 
reactor without carriers. After 90 days of operation, the absolute abundance of total AnAOB was 3.2 times that in 
the control group. The modified carrier promoted the rapid adhesion of biomass through the hydrophobic cavity 
of cyclodextrin and induced rapid proliferation, enhancement of activity, and extracellular protein secretion of 
AnAOB through quorum sensing signaling molecules. Finally, a special hydrophobic biofilm with granular sludge 
characteristics was formed on the modified carrier through the self-assembly of hydrophobic biomass particles. 
Overall, we propose a novel approach to enrich anammox bacteria involving a combination of quorum sensing 
and biofilm carriers.   
1. Introduction 
Anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox) is regarded as the most 
promising biological nitrogen removal (BNR) method owing to its high 
performance, cost efficiency, and environmental friendliness [1]. 
Anammox uses anammox bacteria (AnAOB) to remove ammonium and 
nitrite concurrently [2], and is used to treat wastewater with high 
ammonia content, such as anaerobic digestion effluent, landfill leachate, 
and livestock wastewater [3]. Disappointingly, the widespread appli­
cation of anammox is limited by its start-up difficulties, as AnAOB is 
hard to enrich with a typical generation time of 2.1–11 days [4], much 
longer than other denitrifying bacteria. Strategies for AnAOB enrich­
ment have received considerable attention in industrial and laboratory 
settings. Among the many strategies, various biofilm carriers are most 
popular [5], as they retain biomass by forming biofilms on the surfaces 
of carriers. Anammox biofilm carriers can be categorized into inorganic 
and organic types. Volcanic stone, zeolite, and slag are common inor­
ganic carriers [6–8], whereas common organic carriers include 
nonwoven fabrics, organic polymer fillers, and biochar [9–11]. Addi­
tionally, gel carriers containing polyvinyl alcohol and sodium alginate 
as the main components are also gradually emerging [12]. Typically, 
these carriers increase the adhesion between the carrier surface and 
microorganisms by enhancing their physical contact by adjusting the 
specific surface area, surface charge, and hydrophilicity [5]. Although 
carriers based on this strategy can effectively enrich the biomass, they 
lack selectivity for AnAOB and biological activity to promote anammox 
metabolism. 
Quorum sensing (QS) is the process of microbial communication 
through extracellular signaling molecules to share information about 
cell density and regulate gene expression [13]. N-acyl homoserine lac­
tones (AHLs) are vital quorum sensing (QS) signaling molecules in gram- 
negative bacteria [14]. Some AHLs have recently been shown to pro­
mote AnAOB metabolism, including anammox activity, growth rate, and 
extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) synthesis [15–17]. These 
properties suggest that AHLs are ideal active substances for the enrich­
ment of AnAOB in wastewater treatment systems. A range of such 
studies and applications have been reported in recent years. Tang et al., 
recently reported that the addition of exogenous AHLs to the reactor 
influent reduced the reactor start-up time by up to 20 % [17]. However, 
existing studies have often overlooked the potential of AHLs to promote

biofilm formation [18], which may be associated with biofilm carriers 
and synergies. AHLs can promote the propagation of microorganisms 
and EPS synthesis to accelerate the formation of biofilm [19] and new 
biomass can be effectively retained by the carrier. Notably, biofilms are 
the site of most microbial metabolism [20]. Thus, increasing the amount 
of AHLs in biofilms will prove a more effective AHLs application 
strategy. 
Here, we propose a novel application method immobilizing AHL on 
biofilm carriers to efficiently enrich AnAOB by the synergistic effect of 
both. Cyclodextrin (CD), a cyclic compound, serves as a container for 
loading signaling molecules and slowly releasing them into the biofilm. 
Based on this, we developed a CD-AHL-modified porous ceramic biofilm 
carrier to promote anammox biofilm formation. The novel carrier con­
sisted of an internal sponge-like ceramic with immobilized CD-AHL in­
clusions on the surface. Three anaerobic bioreactors were used: one as a 
control and the other filled with unmodified and modified carriers to 
verify the effectiveness of the carrier. The mechanism of action of the 
novel carriers was determined by analyzing the morphology, EPS frac­
tion, bioactivity, and the biofilm microbial community in the reactor. 
The results indicate that the novel carrier, through the synergistic action 
of quorum sensing and cyclodextrin, shortened the reactor start-up time 
and effectively enriched AnAOB. The obtained results can provide new 
insights for the rapid cultivation of AnAOB, presenting a feasible option 
for the rapid start-up and stable operation of anammox systems. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Synthesis of carriers 
Polyurethane sponges have an excellent pore structure and are 
suitable biofilm carriers for long-term anammox operation. However, 
their smooth surface limits microbial adhesion; therefore, a ceramic 
carrier substrate with a polyurethane sponge structure was developed in 
this study to improve adhesion. Porous ceramics were fabricated using 
the replica method [21]. In this method, polyurethane sponges with 40 
pores per linear inch (PPI) were selected as frames. Medical stone (a 
natural silicate mineral) powder was configured into a ceramic slurry 
with 40 % water content. The frame was soaked in a ceramic slurry to 
ensure that its surface was in complete contact with the slurry. The 
excess ceramic slurry was discharged from the skeleton gap using a 
roller press to ensure that only one layer of ceramic slurry remained on 
the surface. After the organic frame was vaporized at a high tempera­
ture, the ceramic slurry was sintered at a high temperature, ultimately 
inheriting the shape of the frame. The specific synthesis method and 
XRD characterization are detailed in the supporting information (Text 
S1, Figs. S1, S2, and S3). 
Silane coupling agents are classical modifiers used to bind inorganic 
surfaces to organic matter [22]. 3-aminopropyl triethoxysilane (KH550) 
was used to modify the porous ceramics for the subsequent introduction 
of AHLs. Briefly, KH550 (150 mL) and water (50 mL) were dissolved in 
300 mL of ethanol in a flask and stirred under a nitrogen atmosphere for 
1 h to completely hydrolyze the silane. The ceramics obtained in the 
previous step were immersed in the solution at 25 ◦C for 3 h, then 
washed thrice with distilled water and once with NaCl solution (0.02 M). 
Fig. S4a shows the chemical formulae of the reactions involved in this 
step. 
Beta-cyclodextrin (β-CD) is a cyclic compound that can coat, grad­
ually release, and stabilize various molecules [23], including AHLs. In 
this study, β-CD was grafted onto the modified carrier obtained in the 
previous step for the encapsulation and sustained release of AHLs. Using 
a previously-described method [22,24], carboxymethyl-β-CD (5 g), EDC 
(1-Ethyl-3- [3-dimethylaminopropyl]carbodiimide hydrochloride) (10 
g), and NHS (N-Hydroxysuccinimide) (8 g) were configured in a 200-mL 
aqueous solution, and the carboxyl groups were activated by slow stir­
ring for 60 min (Fig. S4b). The modified ceramics were submerged in the 
above solution for 3 h and then washed three times with distilled water 
and once with NaCl solution (0.02 M), Fig. S4c details the chemical 
formulas of the reactions involved in this step. Finally, N-hexanoyl-DL- 
homoserine lactone (C6-HSL) (50 mg) was dissolved in methanol to form 
a concentrated 10-mL solution, then distilled water was added until the 
volume was 100 mL. The carriers grafted with β-CD were immersed in 
the above solution for 3 h to form stable inclusion complexes of β-CD and 
C6-HSL. The coated carriers were then washed thrice with distilled 
water and once with NaCl solution (0.02 M). Finally, carriers modified 
with CD inclusion complexes were obtained by freeze drying. The IR 
spectra of the initial and modified carriers were recorded over the fre­
quency range of 4000–1200 cm−1 using a Bruker Tensor FT-IR spec­
trophotometer (Bruker, Karlsruhe, GER), which showed that the CD was 
successfully grafted onto the surface of the modified carriers. The 
detailed procedure and IR spectrogram results are presented in Text S2 
and Fig. S5, respectively. 
2.2. Bioreactor operation 
Three 1-L anaerobic reactors were operated at 35 ◦C for 90 d to 
determine the effect of modified carriers on the start-up time of the 
anammox reactor and the microbial community. The reactors were 
operated in continuous influent mode using synthetic wastewater as the 
influent. The HRT and chemical composition of the wastewater are 
detailed in supporting information (Text S3, Figs. S6 and S8). Reactor 
one (R1) and reactor two (R2) were used as control groups, with no 
carriers in R1 and unmodified ceramic carriers in R2. Reactor three (R3) 
was used as the experimental group with the modified carriers. Sludge 
containing AnAOB was seeded with 0.2 g of volatile suspended solids 
(VSS) L-1. After 90 d, the 16S rRNA sequences of the biological samples 
were extracted and analyzed. For details, see supporting information 
Text S4. 
The concentrations of ammonia–nitrogen (NH4
+-N), nitrite nitrogen 
(NO2
–-N), nitrate-nitrogen (NO3
–-N), and VSS were determined using the 
standard methods of the American Public Health Association (APHA, 
2017). pH was measured using a pH meter (Mettler Toledo, Columbus, 
OH, USA). 
2.3. Batch reactor operation 
After the modified carrier was found to efficiently enrich the 
anammox bacteria, batch tests were used to explore the relevant 
mechanisms. Due to the heterogeneity of ceramics, it was difficult to 
conduct quantitative research using controlled variable methods. To 
investigate the effects of surface modifiers on biofilm formation, we used 
glass sheets with the same main components as the ceramic substrate. 
Multiple empty 500-mL rubber-capped bottles of the same size were 
operated at 35 ◦C for 30 d. The reactor was intermittently fed with 
synthetic wastewater, and the composition of the influent is detailed in 
Text S5. Four groups of reactors were used for the intergroup control 
tests. Group 1 (G1) used untreated glass sheets as carriers, whereas 
Group 2 (G2) used glass sheets treated with KH550. Group 3 (G3) used 
G2 carriers grafted with β-CD, and Group 4 (G4) used G3 carriers 
embedded with AHLs. The treatment method is provided in detail in 
Text S6. Sludge containing AnAOB at a concentration of 0.2 g VSS L-1 
was used as seeding material. After 10 and 30 d of incubation, the glass 
sheets were removed. 
2.4. Microbial adhesion test 
To clarify the adsorption capacity of different modified carriers on 
sludge, a series of batch experiments were carried out to test microbial 
adhesion. Equal volumes of the four modified carriers from G1 to G4 
were each placed in a 100-mL beaker. The anammox sludge was 
removed from the seed reactor and diluted with distilled water to a 
concentration of 0.2 g VSS L-1 and the pH was adjusted to 7.5. Next, 80- 
mL sludge suspension samples were added to each beaker, and the glass

2.5. Special activity of anammox (SAA) test 
To determine the effect of the modified carriers on anammox activ­

Biofilm samples were extracted from G1-G4 batch reactors, rinsed thrice

ratio of the substrate degradation rate to the biomass concentration

2.6. Extraction and composition analysis method of EPS 
Consistent with the methods used in previous studies [26,27], 8-mL 
biomass samples were placed in 10-mL centrifuge tubes, which were 
filled with PBS solution up to a total volume of 10 mL, and centrifuged at 
2000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected as slime and sedi­
ments at the bottom were collected and resuspended in 10 mL of PBS and 
centrifugation at 5000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected as 
loosely bound EPS (LB-EPS). Subsequently, following ultrasonic 
extraction, tightly bound EPS (TB-EPS) was obtained and the sediments 
were collected and resuspended in 10 mL of PBS. The mixture was 
treated at 20 kHz and 5.59 W mL−1 for 1 min under ultrasonic condi­
tions, followed by centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 20 min. The super­
natant was collected as TB-EPS. The particles in the slime, LB-layer, and 
TB layer solutions were subsequently removed using 0.45-m pore-size 
polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membranes. 
The excitation-emission matrix spectroscopy (EEM) measurement 
method was adapted from previous studies [28]. To eliminate the inner- 
filter effect of fluorescence, the EPS samples were diluted with deionized 
water until their absorbance at 200 nm was less than 0.05 [29]. The 
fluorescence index (FI) and humus index (HIX) values were calculated 
according to the method described by Gabor et al [30]. PARAFAC 
analysis is a three-way method for simulating the EEM fluorescence data 
and was conducted according to a previous study [28]. Extracellular 
polysaccharides (PS) and proteins (PN) were determined using the 
anthrone method with glucose as the standard and the Lowry method 
with bovine serum albumin as the standard [31], respectively. Each 
group of EEM tests conducted 3 parallel experiments, while PS and PN 
tests involved 5 parallel experiments. 
2.7. Extraction and detection of AHLs 
The method of extracting AHLs from biofilms was based on the 
research of Wang [16]. Specifically, the carriers were cultured for 10 
and 30 d and centrifuged at 10000 × g at 4 ◦C for 5 min to separate 
carriers and biofilms. The collected biofilm samples (2 g) were ultra­
sonically extracted with 20 mL of ethyl acetate at 30 ◦C under 150 W for 
1 h and repeated thrice. HLB columns (Waters, Milford, Mass, USA) were 
used to extract the AHLs. Briefly, 3-mL methanol and 3-mL ultra-pure 
water-activated columns were used, and the extract was added to the 
column. Water containing 5 % methanol and 2 % ammonium hydroxide 
was added to the column to remove impurities. Finally, 5 mL of 
analytical-grade methanol was used for elution. The elution solution was 
concentrated by blowing nitrogen and suspended in 0.2 mL of methanol. 
Finally, all the samples were analyzed using an LC-MS (Q TOF6230, 
Agilent) system with a C18 column (ZORBAX SB-C18, Agilent). The 
detailed information is presented in Fig. S7. Each group was conducted 
with 3 parallel experiments. 
2.8. Electron microscopic observation 
Biofilm samples of < 2 mm3 were obtained by cutting the biofilm 
surface using a medical blade and immersing it in a 2.5 % glutaralde­
hyde solution for 2 h. A Field emission scanning electron microscope 
(Merlin, Zeiss, Oberkochen, GER) was used to observe the morphology 
of the biofilm surface. A 3D color microscope (VHX 5000, Keyence 
Corporation, Osaka, Japan) was used to monitor biofilm topology on 
various glass substrates. Each group was conducted with 3 parallel 
experiments. 
2.9. Data processing 
All statistical analyses were performed using R (v3.6.2) unless 
specified otherwise. Significant differences are indicated as follows: * p 
< 0.05, ** p < 0.01, and *** p < 0.001. A redundancy analysis (RDA) 
was performed using CANOCO 5.0. Pairwise distances between each 
biofilm parameter were computed using the ggcor package in R. To 
identify the relationship between biofilm parameters and modified 
carrier function parts, partial Mantel correlations were computed using 
the ggcor package in R. 
3. Results 
3.1. Shortened reactor start-up time with the modified carriers 
Exogenous C6-HSL and C8-HSL can shorten the start-up time of 
anammox reactors when supplied continuously [17]. The three reactors 
were operated for 90 d to determine whether the modified carriers could 
shorten the start-up time. Based on the anammox reaction equation, the 
maximum theoretical nitrogen removal rate (NRRtm) considering only 
anammox is calculated to be 0.85 times the nitrogen loading rate (NLR), 
serves as a reference to assess the degree of anammox, details are shown 
in Text S9. The NRRtm and the nitrogen removal rates (NRRs) are shown 
in Fig. 1. The operation of the reactors was divided into three phases 
based on the NRR fold changes in R3 compared to that in R1 and R2. 
In phase I (d 1–10), the NRR of the R1 gradually increased from 0.2 
to 0.6 gN/L d-1 (Fig. 1a), and the NRR fold-changes of all reactors had no 
significant difference (Fig. 1b and c). In this stage, the NRR ratios of R3 
to R2 were almost all less than 1, indicating that the modified carriers 
had no significant effect on the NRR. 
In phase II (d 11–40), the trend of the NRR fold-changes in the three 
reactors shifted as the reactor loading rate continued to increase. The 
NRR of the R1 increased from 1.25 to 2.25 gN/L d-1, and the average 
value of the NRR fold-changes of the R3/R1 and R3/R2 increased by 
22.22 % and 11.11 %, respectively (Fig. 1b and c). The NRR of R3 was 
significantly higher than that of R1 and R2 (p < 0.05). Notably, due to 
the degradation of nitrate (Fig. S8), R3 experienced instances of NRR 
exceeding NRRtm on certain days during this phase. In this study, the 
reactor was considered as successfully started up when the NRR reached 
approximately 2.5 gN/L d-1 (the mean of the NRR values of the control 
reactor in phase III); therefore, the control group R1 took 50 d, while the 
R2 and R3 groups took 52 and 28 d, respectively. The results showed 
that the start time of the R3 group was the shortest, 44 % and 46 % 
shorter than those of the R1 and R2 groups, respectively. 
In phase III (day 40–90), the nitrogen loading rate (NLR) of influent 
water was no longer increased to study the influence of the modified 
carriers on reactor stability. During this phase, due to the nitrite con­
centration reaching 240 mg/L, led to nitrite inhibition on AnAOB. 
Consequently, NRRs in all three reactors exhibited a noticeable decrease 
on the 41st day, followed by a gradual increase, and the NRR of R1 
increased from 2.00 to 2.71 gN/L d-1. The ammonium and nitrite 
removal rates of the three reactors also increased slowly and reached the

3.2. AnAOB enrichment using modified carriers 
After 90 d of reactor operation, mature carmine biofilms formed on 
the surfaces of the unmodified carriers in R2 and the modified carriers in 
R3 (Figs. 2 and S9), indicating successful enrichment of AnAOB. To 
investigate the effect of the modified carriers on the abundance of 
AnAOB, the microbial community structure of the three groups of bio­
reactors was analyzed using 16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing 
technology. The results of the microbial community analysis are shown 
in Fig. 3. 
At the phylum level, Chloroflexi, which feeds on soluble microbial 
products and EPS [32], accounted for more than 30 % of the relative 
abundance as the dominant phylum, followed by Proteobacteria, Bac­
teroidetes, and Planctomycetes which contain all currently known 
AnAOB [33] (Fig. 3c). Analysis of the microbial community revealed 
that after 90 d of reactor operation, the absolute abundance of AnAOB 
was the greatest in R3 with modified carriers, showing a 3.20- and 3.00- 
fold increase in abundance than that of R1 and R2, respectively (Fig. 3b), 
aligning with R3′s excellent nitrogen removal capability over the past 
90 days. AnAOB of the Candidatus Kuenenia Stuttgartiensis (Ca. K. 
stuttgartiensis) and Candidatus Brocadia fulgida (Ca. B. fulgida) were 
identified at the species level (Fig. 3a). Unlike in the inoculated sludge, 
the main anammox species was Ca. K. stuttgartiensis, the dominant 
anammox species in reactors R2 and R3, became Ca. B. fulgida after 90 
d. The absolute abundance of Ca. B. fulgida in the R3 reactor which had 
modified carriers was 10.67 and 3.35 times higher than that in R1 and 
R2, respectively (Fig. 3b). The microbial RDA showed that the modified 
outer layer had a significant positive correlation with Ca. B. fulgida but 
was poorly correlated with Ca. K. stuttgartiensis (Figs. 3d, S13). In 
addition, Ca. B. fulgida had a good positive correlation with carriers, 
which was contrary to Ca. K. stuttgartiensis (Figs. 3d, S13).

3.4. Effect of modified carrier on microbial adhesion and biofilm 
generation 
Microbial attachment to a carrier is the first and the most crucial step 
in biofilm formation. To investigate the effects of the modified carriers 
on microbial adhesion, the microbial adhesion abilities of the four types 
of glass sheet carriers were tested individually. The amount of microbial 
adhesion is displayed in Fig. 5b, indicating that the biomass adsorption 
capacity of the glass sheets after the different treatments varied signif­
icantly. On the G1 carriers, almost no microbial adhesion was visible. In 
contrast, the treated glass sheets showed varying degrees of increased 
microbial adhesion compared to the control group (12, 60, and 40 times 
for G2, G3, and G4, respectively). G3 showed the highest microbial 
adherence, with biomass granules uniformly attached to the surface of 
the glass sheets. The G4 group also demonstrated a significant (P < 0.01) 
increase in microbial adhesion compared to the control group, albeit 
with high variance. Unexpectedly, CDs alone could capture biomass in a 
water environment to the carrier surface at a rapid rate, which has not 
been previously reported. Contrary to expectations, AHLs hindered the 
adsorption of CDs and had no positive effect on microbial adhesion. 
Based on previous research, the biofilm topology on various sub­
strata [34] was performed using 3D color microscopy. The 2D topog­
raphy and corresponding height line profiles revealed differences in the 
profiles of the different surfaces (Fig. 5d). The 3D topography of the bare 
substrata was obtained over an area of 1770 μm × 1332 μm (Fig. 5c). In 
contrast to the flat surfaces of G1 and G2, the biofilms of G3 and G4 were 
composed of flat bases and ovate bulges. The arithmetic mean height 
(Sa) is used to reflect the surface roughness of the object surface [35]. 
(Fig. 5e) showed that the roughness values of the G3 and G4 biofilms 
were significantly higher than those of G1 and G2 biofilms. The root 
mean square height (Sq) and maximum height (Sz) were used to describe 
the degree and maximum gap of surface fluctuations [35], respectively. 
In contrast to G1 and G2, the larger Sq and Sz values of G3 and G4 
indicated that the roughness of the biofilm surface was mainly due to 
large-scale fluctuations. Kurtosis (Sku) and Skewness (Ssk) were used to 
respectively describe the shape features of the surface roughness sources 
and the degree of symmetry of the surface heights about the mean plane, 
and the interpretations of these changes are shown in Fig. 5e. The Sku 
values of both G3 and G4 were approximately ≤3 and Ssk > 0, indi­
cating that the smooth, protruding ovate structure was the main source 
of biofilm roughness. Finally, groups G3 and G4 accumulated signifi­
cantly more biofilm volume. 
3.5. Effect of carrier functional surfaces on anammox EPS fractions 
AHL-mediated quorum sensing is closely associated with microbial 
anabolism. Previous studies have shown that AHLs promote EPS syn­
thesis in anammox sludge by modulating amino acid and uridine 
diphosphate (UDP) pathways, thereby increasing the particle size of the 
granular sludge [17]. Therefore, the effect of functional surface com­
ponents on EPS composition was investigated. After 30 d of operation, 
glass sheets in the batch reactors were randomly selected as test subjects. 
The PN and PS contents of the total EPS on the surfaces of different 
sheets were detected. The results showed significant differences in the 
biomass of the different glass sheets (Fig. 6a and b). The PS/PN in groups 
G1-G4 were 1.06, 0.34, 0.17, and 0.125, respectively. The main 
component of EPS in groups G2, G3, and G4 was extracellular PN, which 
is similar to the results of previous studies [28,36]. Compared with the 
control group (G1), extracellular PN in the experimental groups G2, G3, 
and G4 increased by 26 %, 116 %, and 215 %, respectively. The higher 
polysaccharide content in G1 samples may be attributed to the hydro­
philic surface of the glass, which promotes the adhesion of extracellular 
PS. The EEM fluorescence spectra of the three EPS fractions from various 
anammox biofilm samples were investigated using the PARAFAC model. 
The three decomposed EPS fractions had characteristic peaks at Ex/Em

4.2. Regulatory properties of modified carriers on microbial communities 
Quorum sensing selectively affects microorganisms sensitive to 
signaling molecules, thereby altering bacterial and fungal communities 
[47]. Our study is the first to report the change in the dominant 
anammox species under AHL intervention. Inoculation sludge had Ca. K. 
stuttgartiensis as the dominant species, accounting for 90 % of the total, 
whereas after 90 d of culture, Ca. B. fulgida became the new dominant 
species in R3, accounting for 72 % of the total (Fig. 3b). In anammox 
reactors, a shift in AnAOB species is common. For example, the domi­
nant species shifted from Ca. B. fulgida to Ca. Brocadia. sp. 40 [48], from 
Ca. B. fulgida to Ca. K. stuttgartiensis [49], from Ca. Brocadia sp. to Ca. 
K. stuttgartiensis [50] and Ca. B. anammoxidians to Ca. Anammox­
oglobus propionicus [51]. Diverse anammox species compete for the 
same resources (substrates or living spaces), thereby eliminating inferior 
species (or declining numbers) from the habitat. Previous studies 
suggested that salt and oxygen tolerance, proliferative capacity, and 
substrate-binding ability are the primary determinants of competition 
among AnAOB species [51]. In this study, the microbial RDA analysis 
showed that the modified outer layer was significantly positively 
correlated with Ca. B. fulgida but poorly correlated with Ca. K. stutt­
gartiensis (Figs. 3d, S13). According to metagenomic analysis, both Ca. 
K. stuttgartiensis and Ca. B. fulgida harbor HDTS genes responsible for 
AHLs synthesis [52] and exhibit enhanced metabolism and proliferation 
in response to C6-HSL treatment [15,16]. A recent metagenomic anal­
ysis suggested that quorum sensing may be responsible for the trans­
formation of the dominant anammox bacteria from Candidatus Kuenenia 
to Candidatus Brocadia [53]. Our study verified this conjecture at the 
experimental level for added AHLs. These results suggest that Ca. B. 
fulgida may have a lower QS threshold than Ca. K. stuttgartiensis and 
other species that are more sensitive to signaling molecules could 
become the dominant species under exogenous AHLs treatment. Further, 
this experiment demonstrated the potential of CDs to shape biofilms. 
Therefore, the selective adsorption of biological particles by CD may 
facilitate the transformation of dominant microorganisms.

The effects of the modified carriers showed clear differences across 
the three stages of reactor operation (Fig. 1). In the first phase, there was 
no difference in the NRR between groups R2 and R3. As the carrier was

used to optimize the spatial distribution of sludge in the reactor, the 
mass transfer efficiency between the sludge and water improved [54], 
both of which were higher than that of the R1 group. In the second stage, 
with the shortening of the HRT and an increase in the reactor loading 
rate, the NRRs of the three groups were significantly different. β-CD was 
significantly correlated with the concentration of AHLs on day 30, and 
only the G3 biofilm had a higher concentration of AHLs on day 30 than 
on day 10, suggesting that endogenous AHLs had been stored and 
accumulated in cavity β-CD [55] (Fig. 4b). Compared with G3, G4 
showed that CDs-AHLs released AHLs into the environment through 
decomposition reactions at the initial stage, and the reaction developed 
in a positive direction. CDs absorb AHLs originally present in the envi­
ronment, causing quorum sensing quenching [56]. When the amount of 
AHLs was low, the reaction direction was reversed such that they were 
re-released into the biofilm to activate microbial quorum sensing [55]. 
AnAOB benefited significantly from quorum sensing only when the 
concentration of AHLs was maintained at a certain level for a long time 
(Fig. 7). Exogenous AHLs counteract the negative effects of β-CD on 
quorum sensing, and β-CD prolongs the environmental half-life of AHLs, 
like other drugs [57], leading to a synergistic effect between the two. 
In the later stage of reactor operation (day 60–90), the NRR of both 
R1 and R3 remained stable and closely approached the NRRtm. The 
average removal rates of ammonium and nitrite in R3 are significantly 
higher than the other two groups (P < 0.05), reaching 93.93 % and 
92.92 %, respectively, surpassing those in R1 (0.98 %, 1.14 %) and R2 
(2.01 %, 2.45 %). Consequently, even in the absence of exogenous 
signaling molecules, the modified carrier maintained a nitrogen removal 
advantage during long-term operation. This advantageous performance 
can be attributed to two legacies of signaling molecules. Firstly, R3 
enriched the highest abundance of AnAOB. Even though the NRR had 
already approached the theoretical maximum, it continued to remove 
more nitrogen within the same time, thereby enhancing wastewater 
treatment efficiency. Secondly, under the regulation of signaling mole­
cules, the EPS of the modified carrier exhibited a higher content of PN 
(Fig. 6a). Proteins have been identified as vital and dominant fractions 
in various anammox biofilms [36,44,58]. Extracellular proteomic 
analysis showed that the initial anammox colonies exhibited higher 
expression of proteins associated with metabolic potential and func­
tional activity than the mature and isolated biofilms [59]. These results 
indicate that modified vectors delay the aging rate of surface biofilms by 
upregulating the expression of functional proteins, such as extracellular 
enzymes with catalytic activity. Therefore, even when exogenous 
signaling molecules are depleted, the modified carrier exerts a persistent 
impact on the stable operation of the reactor. 
5. Conclusions 
In this study, we developed a biofilm carrier with the ability to 
release quorum-sensing signaling molecules that benefit AnAOB for the 
first time. This novel carrier efficiently enriched AnAOB at 3.2 times the 
speed achieved without carrier and significantly shortened the reactor 
start-up time. Surprisingly, the efficacy analysis revealed that cyclo­
dextrin has significant potential for rapid biofilm formation. Cyclodex­
trin performs in multiple roles throughout the biofilm formation process, 
ultimately resulting in the formation of previously unreported particu­
late biofilms. We propose a mechanism for the expansion of this unique 
biofilm by using sludge particles as building blocks for hydrophobic self- 
assembly. Rapid enrichment of difficult-to-culture microorganisms has 
long been a challenging issue, and cyclodextrin seems to provide a new 
solution. In addition, our modified carrier caused a shift in the dominant 
AnAOB species. The role of exogenous AHLs in shaping the AnAOB 
community and the potential of Ca. B. fulgida in quorum sensing were 
discussed, although the specific mechanisms involved require further 
investigation.

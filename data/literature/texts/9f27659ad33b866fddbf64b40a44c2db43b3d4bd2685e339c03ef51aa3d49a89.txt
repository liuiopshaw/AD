A B S T R A C T
As scientific advancements continue apace, piezotronic effect enhanced catalytic sterilization technology shows 
immense potential. This review covers the fundamental principles and future prospects of this technology. Piezo- 
catalysis boosts activity by harnessing electric fields and potential differences from piezotronic materials under 
stress. Piezo-photocatalysis combines light and mechanical stress for dual excitation, while piezo-nanozymes 
merge piezotronic effects with nanotechnology for exceptional performance. These mechanisms enhance cata­
lyst functionality, and pioneer new catalytic sterilization frontiers. In practical applications, piezotronic effect 
regulated catalytic techniques exhibit outstanding performance. For instance, in treating infected wounds, me­
chanical stimulation of piezotronic materials markedly increases catalytic activity, effectively eradicating 
pathogens. Antibacterial fabrics leverage the piezotronic effect for self-cleaning and long-lasting sterilization, 
significantly improving their antibacterial properties. In water disinfection, piezotronic catalysis generates 
reactive oxygen species, swiftly and efficiently eliminating bacteria. Antibacterial implants employ piezo- 
nanozymes to achieve sustained antibacterial effects in vivo, effectively preventing implant-related infections. 
This review also summarizes recent advancements in piezotronic effect enhanced catalytic sterilization materials 
and proposes future research directions. As technology progresses, piezotronic effect enhanced catalytic steril­
ization holds broad application prospects in medical and environmental protection fields, poised to play a crucial 
role.
1. Introduction
Bacterial infections have long posed a formidable threat to human 
health, with millions of lives lost each year due to pathogen-induced 
illnesses [1–3]. The profound impact of these infections extends 
beyond individual health, straining healthcare systems and impeding 
economic growth. Despite the extensive implementation of disinfection 
strategies, including chlorination, ozonation, and the use of antibiotics, 
these approaches often entail significant chemical consumption and the 
production of harmful disinfection by-products [4–6]. Moreover, the 
escalating reliance on antibiotics has exacerbated bacterial resistance, 
giving rise to “superbugs” that critically undermine medical progress 
[7–9]. Consequently, the development of innovative antibacterial 
technologies is not only urgent but essential for effectively and safely 
eradicating bacterial pathogens, ensuring a healthier future for pop­
ulations worldwide [10–12]. Such advancements could revolutionize 
the landscape of infection control and significantly mitigate the burden 
of bacterial diseases.

Photocatalytic antibacterial technology utilizes the conversion of 
light energy from nature into chemical energy to inhibit and kill bacteria 
[36,37]. Under light irradiation, the electron-hole pairs generated on the 
surface of the catalyst can react with H2O and O2 to produce antibac­

catalysis under certain conditions [40]. To further enhance the effi­

various strategies, such as loading noble metals on photocatalysts and 
doping elements [41,42]. Recently, some researchers have suggested 
combining piezotronic materials with photocatalytic semiconductor 
materials to design heterogeneous piezotronic composite materials 
[43–45]. These composites can effectively promote the separation of 
electron-hole pairs under light irradiation, thereby improving the effi­
ciency of photocatalytic antibacterial action [46–48]. Yu et al. intro­

Nanozymes, which mimic enzyme activities, can regulate the levels 
of ROS produced [50–52]. Excessive ROS can damage cell membranes, 
proteins, and nucleic acids, killing pathogenic microorganisms,

zymes have shown broad application prospects in antibacterial and 
antiviral treatments and adjuvant therapies [55–57]. The piezotronic 
effect can further enhance their enzyme activity, thereby increasing 
their bactericidal effect. Sui et al. reported that sulfur-doped graphdiyne 
(GDY) nanosheets exhibited piezotronic responsiveness under ultra­
sound (US), enhancing enzyme-mimicking activity and demonstrating 
excellent bactericidal effects [58].

2. Piezotronic effect and piezotronic materials
Piezoelectricity was first discovered by P. Curie and J. Curie in 1880 
when they observed that certain materials generate an electric charge in 
response to mechanical stress [59,60]. The term "piezo" refers to the 
application of pressure, while "electricity" corresponds to the movement 
of electrons, thus materials exhibiting piezoelectricity are known as 
piezotronic materials [61,62]. Piezoelectricity is associated with 
non-centrosymmetric distribution of positive and negative charges 
within the unit cell of a material [63,64]. When an external force is 
applied along a certain direction, causing the material to deform (such 
as stretching, compressing, or bending), the dipole moments of the unit 
cells change, leading to the movement of charged particles, which 
generates net charges on the material [65,66], creating a potential dif­
ference and an electric field [67]. When piezotronic materials produce 
charges in response to an external force, this phenomenon of converting 
mechanical energy into electrical energy is known as the direct piezo­
tronic effect [68,69]. Conversely, when piezotronic materials undergo 
deformation in response to an electric field, converting electrical energy 
into mechanical energy, this phenomenon is known as the converse 
piezotronic effect [70,71].
Piezotronic materials include inorganic piezotronic materials, 
organic piezotronic materials, composite piezotronic materials, and 
biological piezotronic materials [67]. Common inorganic piezotronic 
materials include BTO, SrTiO3 (STO), ZnO, MoS2, and hydroxyapatite 
[72–75]. For instance, ZnO possesses a non-centrosymmetric crystal 
structure, which naturally induces a piezotronic effect when the mate­
rial is subjected to strain [76]. The wurtzite crystal structure exhibits a 
hexagonal configuration with significant anisotropy along the c-axis and 
perpendicular to the c-axis. Simply put, Zn2⁺ cations and O2⁻ anions are 
tetrahedrally coordinated, with the centers of the positive and negative 
ions overlapping. When stress is applied to the vertices of the tetrahedra, 
the centers of the cations and anions displace relative to each other, 
generating a dipole moment (Fig. 3a) [77]. The cumulative effect of 
dipole moments produced by all the units in the crystal creates a pie­
zotronic field, leading to a macroscopic potential difference along the 
strain direction of the crystal, known as the piezotronic potential 
(Fig. 3b). When dipole moments accumulate throughout the unit cell, an 
electric potential is generated along the stress direction, forming the 
piezotronic potential. This internal piezotronic potential persists under 
applied stress, with its magnitude primarily determined by the intensity 
and direction of the strain. As shown in Fig. 3c(i,ii), in a c-axis ZnO 
nanowire (NW) with a length of 1.2 μm and a hexagonal side length of 
100 nm (space group P63mc), a tensile force of 85 nN generates a po­
tential difference of approximately 0.4 V between the two ends. As the 
mechanical force increases, the degree of deformation in the ZnO 
nanorod also enhances (Fig. 3c(iii–vi)). The piezotronic potential can 
serve as the driving force for electron flow in an external load after 
mechanical deformation, which is the fundamental principle of piezo­
tronic modulation of catalytic performance [78].
Bao et al. utilized the finite element method with COMSOL Multi­
physics to simulate the distribution of piezotronic potential and eluci­
date the relationship between piezotronic potential and piezocatalytic 
efficiency [79,80]. In all simulations, the polarization axis of BTO was 
aligned along the z-axis (Fig. 3d) [79]. The isostatic pressure simulation 
resulted in axial compression of the similar BTO-1 nanowire, with the 
piezotronic potential continuously distributed along the polarization 
axis within the NW (Fig. 3e). When a small additional pressure was 
applied to the side surface, the BTO NW could easily bend due to the 
pressure perturbation (Fig. 3f). A clear proportionality between the 
maximum potential difference and the magnitude of isostatic pressure 
can be observed by comparing Fig. 3g and h. In a typical simulation, for 
a BTO-1 NW with width (W) = 200 nm, length (L) = 10 μm, and pressure 
(P) = 1×108 Pa, the maximum potential difference was 11.2 V (Fig. 3e). 
Similar values for BTO-2 (W = 200 nm) and BTO-3 (100 nm) nano­
particles were 0.224 V (Fig. 3h) and 0.112 V (Fig. 3i), respectively. This 
suggests that by adjusting the pressure, the piezocatalytic performance 
can be effectively improved.
Organic piezotronic materials, such as polyvinylidene fluoride 
(PVDF), derive their piezotronic effect from the orientation and align­
ment of molecular dipole moments [81]. The dielectric properties of 
PVDF originate from the difference in electronegativity between F and H 
atoms, generating dipole moments in the F→H direction. The α-phase 
lacks piezoelectricity due to the net cancellation of dipole moments 
resulting from the tightly packed chains within the unit cell. In contrast, 
the β-phase exhibits the highest piezotronic effect due to the parallel 
alignment of dipole moments, leading to the greatest net electric dipole 
moment. Liu et al. fabricated ordered and disordered PVDF piezotronic 
nanofiber membranes using electrospinning technology and enhanced 
their piezoelectricity through annealing treatment to increase the 
β-phase content. Furthermore, they synthesized PVDF/Ag nanofiber 
membranes, which are capable of generating dynamic piezotronic po­
tential under normal body movements [82]. This potential promotes 
fibroblast proliferation and migration, collagen deposition, angiogen­
esis, and re-epithelialization, thereby significantly accelerating wound 
healing in mice. Vukomanovi´c et al. prepared fully organic piezotronic 
biodegradable films composed of poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA) and 
demonstrated their antimicrobial efficacy against Gram-positive Staph­
ylococcus epidermidis (S. epidermidis) and Gram-negative E. coli [83].
Composite materials can include combinations of different inorganic 
materials or inorganic materials with organic materials, such as Li- 
doped Na0.5K0.5NbO3 and BTO-doped hydrogels. Biological piezotronic 
materials include bones, dentin, muscles, and nerves. For example, bone 
tissue in the human body exhibits piezotronic phenomena, where the 
generated negative charges can promote the function of osteoblasts and 
new bone formation. This biological piezotronic effect plays a crucial 
role in bone development and fracture healing.
3. Piezotronic effect modulates catalytic performance
The ability of piezotronic modulation to reshape energy band 
structures offers a pivotal foundation for advancing antibacterial tech­
nologies. On polarized piezotronic surfaces, bound charges are balanced 
by screening charges, maintaining electrical neutrality (Fig. 4a). This 
charge distribution alters the band structure, with negative charges 
causing upward band bending and positive charges driving downward 
bending (Fig. 4b). Piezo-catalysis, activated through low-frequency vi­
brations or ultrasonic waves, induces polarization and establishes in­
ternal electric fields, effectively separating carriers to react with 
substances in water and generate potent ROS. Building on this, piezo- 
photocatalysis synergizes piezotronic and photocatalytic effects, using 
mechanical stress or acoustic excitation to enhance carrier separation. 
Concurrently, piezo-nanozyme employ multi-catalytic nanomaterials to 
amplify ROS production, meeting the rigorous demands of antibacterial 
treatments. The following sections delve into the principles and recent 
advances of piezo-catalysis, piezo-photocatalysis, and piezo-nanozyme 
in the antibacterial field.
3.1. Piezo-catalysis
ROS, such as hydroxyl radicals (•OH), superoxide (•O2−), and singlet 
oxygen (1O2), are among the most potent redox species in aqueous 
phases [84]. In recent years, piezo-catalysis has emerged as a novel 
advanced oxidation process that significantly enhances catalytic per­
formance through low-frequency vibration or high-frequency US, 
inducing polarization and establishing an internal electric field in pie­
zotronic catalysts [85,86]. In piezo-catalytic redox reactions, electrons 
and holes are continuously separated and attracted to opposite surfaces 
[87,88]. In aqueous solutions, these surface carriers can engage in 
oxidation or reduction reactions with H2O or dissolved substances, 
generating homogeneous ROS and achieving efficient piezo-catalytic 
disinfection [30,89].

3.2. Piezo-photocatalysis
Piezotronic-enhanced 
photocatalytic 
antibacterial 
technology 
merges the piezotronic effect with photocatalytic processes, markedly 
improving antibacterial efficacy [98,99]. In this framework, piezotronic 
materials such as ZnO nanoparticles generate surface charges under 
mechanical stress or acoustic excitation due to the piezotronic effect 
[100]. Concurrently, these nanoparticles, when illuminated, produce 
higher-energy electrons and holes that engage in photocatalytic re­
actions, resulting in the generation of highly oxidative ROS and radicals, 
including •OH and •O2-. These species are effective in disrupting mi­
crobial cell structures, thereby providing robust microbial inactivation 
[87,101]. By harnessing the piezotronic effect to modulate photo­
catalytic activity, this technology demonstrates exceptional perfor­
mance in microbial eradication and holds significant promise for 
applications in healthcare, water treatment, and environmental purifi­
cation [88].
Yu et al. ingeniously introduced BTO piezotronic material between 
TiO2 and Au in a Schottky structure, forming a coaxial TiO2/BTO/Au 
heterostructure with a BTO layer of approximately 7 nm (Fig. 6a) [49]. 
The spontaneous polarization of BTO causes band bending, expanding 
the depletion layer between TiO2 and BTO and lowering the contact 
energy levels between Au and BTO. This facilitates the transfer of hot 
electrons from Au to BTO and photoinduced electrons from BTO to TiO2, 
accelerating the transfer of holes from TiO2 to BTO and enhancing 
electron-hole separation (Fig. 6b). The generated photoinduced carriers 
react with surface H2O and O2 to produce more ROS, leading to bacterial 
oxidative stress and achieving antibacterial effects. Additionally, Liu 
et al. introduced BTO piezotronic material at the interface between 
p-type semiconductor Ag2O and n-type semiconductor TiO2 (Fig. 6c), 
forming an electric field induced by polarized charges [102]. Negative 
charges attract holes generated by TiO2, while positive charges attract 
electrons generated by Ag2O, promoting directional carrier transport 
and efficient separation (Fig. 6d and e). This is the first report of using 
ferroelectric materials as an intermediate layer to regulate carrier 
transport at Schottky and p-n junctions. Piezotronic effect modulation 
successfully enhances carrier transport at metal-semiconductor and 
semiconductor-semiconductor interfaces, further improving photo­
catalytic performance and providing a solid foundation for antibacterial 
treatment.
Wu et al. used electrostatic interactions to encapsulate positively 
charged BiFeO3 nanoparticles on negatively charged Ti3C2 nanosheets 
(Fig. 6f), forming a ferroelectric polarization and Schottky-junction that 
accelerates interfacial charge transfer and constructs interfacial charge 
transfer channels (Fig. 6g) [103]. Ultrasonic vibration drives 
BiFeO3/Ti3C2 to generate cavitation bubbles and ferroelectric polariza­
tion, with the former’s implosion causing local sonothermal effects and 
sonoluminescence, triggering electronic transitions in the material to 
produce photoinduced e- and h+. The latter induces the formation of a 
piezotronic field at the interface, driving polarized charges and photo­
induced charges to transfer between interfaces, effectively separating 
hole and electron. The increased ultrasonic strain of BiFeO3/Ti3C2 
generates more space charges, and ferroelectric ultrasonic interface 
engineering provides a greater driving force for separating e- and h+, 
increasing the imbalance of surface charge states. Under US, more 
charges participate in ROS generation (Fig. 6h), allowing for the rapid 
and effective treatment of bacteria-induced osteomyelitis within 20 min.
Zhang et al. proposed a synergistic treatment of osteomyelitis using 
US and near-infrared (NIR) light, leveraging the piezotronic and pyro­
electric properties of BTO nanotubes (NTs) [104]. NIR or US treatment 
reduces 
BTO 
NTs 
spontaneous 
polarization, 
inducing 
pyro­
electric/piezotronic fields and band bending. The synergistic effect en­
hances charge transfer and electron-hole pair separation, improving 
pyroelectric-piezotronic catalysis and increasing ROS production. 
Simultaneously, band bending causes an irreversible conductive circuit 
from bacteria to BTO NTs, leading to bacterial membrane electron loss 
and metabolic imbalance. Under BTO NTs pyroelectric-piezotronic 
therapy, 99.8 % of bacteria are killed.
3.3. Piezo-nanozyme
Many nanozymes exhibit properties similar to natural enzymes and 
have been developed to promote ROS generation and glutathione (GSH) 
depletion 
[105,106]. 
However, 
relying 
solely 
on 
artificial 
enzyme-mediated therapies to produce ROS often falls short of meeting 
the demands of antibacterial treatment [107]. Therefore, developing 
novel nanozymes with multiple catalytic activities to increase ROS 
generation and GSH depletion is crucial for enhancing SDT [108,109].
Yang et al. synthesized vanadium vacancy-regulated BiVO4 nano­
particles, including vanadium vacancy-rich BiVO4 (Vv-r BiVO4) and 
vanadium vacancy-poor BiVO4 (Vv-p BiVO4), using a hydrothermal 
method by controlling reaction time and temperature (Fig. 7a) [110]. 
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that Vv-r BiVO4 
nanoparticles possess a narrower bandgap and higher holes concentra­
tion, and exhibit piezotronic properties (Fig. 7b). Under ultrasonic 
stimulation, the piezotronic effect-induced internal electric field pro­
motes electron-hole separation and alters the band structure. Subse­
quently, holes react with H2O2 to generate •OH, endowing the material 
with peroxidase (POD)-like enzyme activity.
Cai et al. developed a novel piezotronic nanozyme by combining 
UIO-66 with Au nanoparticles (UIO-66/Au) (Fig. 7c) [95]. The incor­
poration of Au not only facilitates charge separation and inhibits charge 
recombination but also modulates the charge distribution within UIO-66 
(Fig. 7d). Under ultrasonic pressure, UIO-66/Au nanoparticles exhibit 
internal polarization, leading to band bending. The band bending effect 
reduces the distance between the band edges and the redox potential of 
ROS, thereby enhancing electron-hole pair separation and promoting 
their reactions with nearby molecules (e.g., O2 and H2O) to generate 
more ROS (Fig. 7e). The dual nanozyme activities (CAT and POD) of 
UIO-66/Au nanoparticles are partially enhanced under ultrasonic irra­
diation. The tight interaction between Au and UIO-66 nanoparticles 
allows UIO-66 to generate an inherent electric field under US, ampli­
fying the piezotronic effect and directly stimulating the catalytic per­
formance of Au nanozymes. Consequently, the intrinsic nanozyme 
catalytic activity of UIO-66/Au nanoparticles is significantly enhanced. 
Additionally, US, as an exogenous energy source, may increase the 
likelihood of collisions and interactions between H2O2 and the catalytic 
centers (Au). Thus, nanozymes exhibit significant dual enzyme activities 
under ultrasonic irradiation.
The piezotronic effect can generate surface positive and negative 
charges, thereby activating the surface redox reactions of nanozymes 
and enhancing enzyme activity. Wang et al. focused on the impact of the 
positive and negative charges generated by the piezotronic effect on 
piezotronic-enhanced nanozyme activity [111]. When exposed to ul­
trasonic radiation, BTO undergoes mechanical deformation, producing 
induced surface charges. Fig. 7f shows the distribution of these charges 
on the sides of the cubic particle, with red and blue representing positive 
and negative charges, respectively. The presence of positive and nega­
tive charges profoundly affects the POD nanozyme reaction, inducing 
the accelerated decomposition of H2O2 molecules on the MoS2 surface 
into •OH. Reaction energy data (Fig. 7g) show that the uncharged MoS2 
surface lowers the vacuum decomposition barrier from 3.93 eV to 
2.27 eV. Additionally, when the surface is positively and negatively 
charged, the barrier values decrease to 1.85 eV and 0.93 eV, respec­
tively. It is noteworthy that •OH adsorption is unstable, and two sepa­
rate •OH units may be the final products of the catalytic reaction. To

4. Practical applications of piezotronic effect modulates 
catalytic performance
Piezo-enhanced catalytic bactericidal technology demonstrates 
broad application potential [112]. The charges generated through the 
piezotronic effect significantly enhance the catalytic activity of nano­
materials, thereby increasing their efficacy in killing bacteria and other 
pathogens [113]. This provides an efficient and environmentally 
friendly sterilization method. This technology is not only applicable to 
skin treatments and antimicrobial fabrics but also holds promise for 
water treatment, anti-infection in medical device implants, and other 
areas [114–116]. It has immense potential for applications in public 
health, safety, and environmental protection.
4.1. Treatment of infected wounds
Bacterial infections represent a significant global health challenge, 
imposing a heavy burden on public health [117]. Effective eradication of 
bacterial biofilms and inhibition of bacterial growth are crucial for 
combating infections. However, traditional treatments, such as surgical 
debridement and antibiotic therapy, can lead to tissue damage and 
increased bacterial resistance, thereby limiting their efficacy [118]. 
Considering these limitations, researchers and healthcare professionals 
are seeking more effective and lower-risk alternative therapeutic stra­

considerable attention due to their unique ability to generate electric 
fields and induce charges when subjected to mechanical pressure, of­
fering new research directions and therapeutic possibilities.

4.2. Antibacterial fabrics
Antibacterial fabrics present significant potential within the textile 
industry, primarily through the use of piezotronic effects to enable self- 
cleaning and prolonged antibacterial functions [126,127]. By embed­
ding piezotronic materials into the fabric, an electric field is generated 
when the fabric is subjected to mechanical stress [128]. This electric 
field activates the catalysts present on the fabric, further enhancing its 
antibacterial properties [129]. This mechanism allows piezotronic 
antibacterial fabrics to effectively inhibit bacterial growth while main­
taining cleanliness and comfort, thus offering substantial market value.
Liu et al. utilized electrospinning technology to incorporate polar­
ized LiNbO3 (LN) nanoparticles and graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) 
into silk fibroin (SF) nanofibers (NFs), fabricating a novel piezotronic 
film [130]. When an external force is applied to the film, the polarization 
change in LN induces a detectable current signal in the external circuit, 
with electrons flowing from the bottom to the top of the film. Upon 
removal of the force, electrons return from the top to the bottom, 
resulting in an opposite current signal (Fig. 9a). This piezotronic film 
exhibited excellent filtration and bactericidal properties against 
airborne bacteria (Fig. 9b and c). The degradation performance of the 
g-C3N4/LN/SF composite fiber membrane was evaluated using enzyme 
systems and soil burial experiments, revealing rapid degradation in both 
enzyme systems and natural environments (Fig. 9d-f).
Shan et al. demonstrated several fabrics with high antibacterial ef­
ficiency by leveraging the piezotronic effect of ZnO NW arrays and their 
physical interactions with bacteria [131] In the bent state, the piezo­
tronic effect of ZnO NWs generates interface electrons, which, when 
transferred between the bacteria and NWs, inhibit bacterial growth and 
lead to their death (Fig. 9g). Approximately 70 % of bacterial death is 
attributed to electron transfer, with the remaining 30 % due to the 
physical piercing by the NWs. The fabric exhibited significant adhesion 
and antibacterial performance against S. aureus and E. coli (Fig. 9h and 
i), achieving a bactericidal rate of up to 99.99 %, indicating its vast 
potential for practical applications.

Zhu et al. constructed a BTO/GDY/Au NFs heterostructure, 
leveraging the synergistic effects of piezo-catalysis and plasmonic pho­

sponge, significantly enhancing piezotronic photocatalytic activity and 
effectively inhibiting bacterial proliferation. When BTO contacts GDY, 
forming a Z-scheme heterojunction, electrons flow from BTO to GDY. 
Due to the different Fermi levels between GDY and Au nanoparticles, 
charge carriers at the GDY/Au interface redistribute, causing electrons 
to diffuse from GDY to the Au nanoparticles. Upon illumination of 
BTO/GDY/Au NFs, electron-hole pairs form on the valence band (VB) 
and conduction band (CB) of BTO and GDY, respectively. The electrons 
in BTO’s CB transfer to GDY’s VB to recombine with holes, leaving net 
positive charges in BTO to reduce OH- to •OH (Fig. 10i). Under localized 
surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) effects, Au nanoparticles generate 
numerous hot electron-hole pairs upon illumination. The Fermi level 
difference leads to the transfer of CB electrons from GDY to the electron 
cloud around Au nanoparticles, where they recombine with hot holes. 
Consequently, Au nanoparticles serve as active sites, catalyzing the 
decomposition of H2O2 into •OH, thereby enhancing the catalytic ac­
tivity of BTO/GDY/Au NFs. Further exploring its potential application at 
the equipment level, Fig. 10j illustrates the wastewater treatment pro­

300 min.

that active oxygen is generated through water flow over the piezotronic 
material, leading to effective inactivation of bacteria on the micro­
plastics’ surfaces.

5. Summary and perspectives
The integration of piezotronic effects with catalytic processes has 
provided novel pathways for bacterial control. This review compre­
hensively covers the fundamentals of piezotronic effects and piezotronic 
materials, exploring the mechanisms by which piezoelectricity modu­
lates catalytic performance. These mechanisms encompass piezo- 
catalysis, piezo-photocatalysis, and piezo-nanozymes. The study high­
lights the broad potential of these mechanisms in practical applications, 
including the treatment of infected wounds, antibacterial fabrics, water 
disinfection, and antibacterial implants.
Detailed investigations into these applications reveal that piezo­
tronic effect-modulated catalytic technologies exhibit outstanding per­
formance in practice. For instance, mechanical stimulation by 
piezotronic materials at infection sites significantly enhances catalytic 
activity, effectively eliminating pathogens. Antibacterial fabrics 
leverage piezotronic effects to achieve self-cleaning and prolonged 
antibacterial action. Water disinfection technologies utilize Piezo- 
catalysis to generate ROS, rapidly and efficiently eradicating water­
borne bacteria. Antibacterial implants employ piezo-nanozymes to 
deliver sustained antibacterial effects in vivo. Table 1 summarizes the 
latest advancements in piezotronic effect enhanced catalytic antibacte­
rial materials. As science and technology continue to evolve, piezotronic 
effect-enhanced catalytic sterilization technology emerges as a prom­
ising frontier, particularly within the realms of medicine and environ­
mental protection. Future investigations and applications may be 
directed toward the following dimensions:
5.1. Development of new piezotronic materials
Currently, piezotronic materials mainly focus on traditional mate­
rials such as barium titanate and lead zirconate titanate. However, these 
materials may have issues with biocompatibility and environmental 
friendliness in practical applications. Future research should aim to 
develop new piezotronic materials, such as lead-free piezotronic mate­
rials, composite materials, and organic piezotronic materials. These new 
materials need not only excellent piezotronic properties but also 
outstanding biocompatibility, environmental friendliness, and stability 
to meet the demands of various practical applications.
5.2. In-depth study of the mechanisms of piezotronic effect regulation
The specific mechanisms by which the piezotronic effect regulates 
catalytic performance, especially at the nanoscale, are not yet fully 
elucidated. Future research should strengthen basic research on the 
mechanisms of piezotronic effect regulation, using advanced charac­
terization techniques (such as atomic force microscopy, e- microscopy, 
etc.) and theoretical simulations (such as DFT, molecular dynamics 
simulations, etc.) to reveal the specific roles of the piezotronic effect in 
different catalytic reactions. This will help design piezo-catalytic ma­
terials with superior performance and guide practical applications.
5.3. Design of multifunctional composite materials
Single-function piezotronic materials may face some limitations in 
practical applications; therefore, the design of multifunctional com­
posite materials is an important direction for future development. By 
combining piezotronic materials with other functional materials (such 
as photocatalytic materials, magnetic materials, antibacterial materials, 
etc.), multiple functions can be synergistically realized. For example, 
piezotronic-photocatalytic composite materials can use both mechanical 
energy and light energy for catalytic reactions, improving sterilization 
efficiency; piezotronic-magnetic composite materials can achieve mag­
netic field-regulated catalytic performance, enabling more precise con­
trol and application.

5.4. Optimization and verification of application technologies
Although piezotronic effect enhanced catalytic sterilization tech­
nology has achieved significant progress in laboratory research, its 
performance in practical applications still needs further verification and 
optimization. Future research should strengthen performance testing of 
piezotronic materials in practical application environments, including 
their stability in biological systems, durability in complex environments, 
and interference resistance. Additionally, studies should explore how to 
integrate piezotronic materials with existing medical devices and envi­
ronmental protection equipment to achieve widespread clinical and 
industrial applications.
5.5. Evaluation of biosafety and environmental impact
The application of piezotronic materials in medicine and environ­
mental protection must consider their biosafety and environmental 
impact. Future research should strengthen the study of the compatibility 
of piezotronic materials in biological systems, assessing their potential 
toxicity and side effects. At the same time, the degradation behavior and 
environmental impact of piezotronic materials in the environment 
should be studied to ensure the sustainability and safety of their 
applications.
5.6. Industrialization and market promotion
The ultimate goal of piezotronic effect enhanced catalytic steriliza­
tion technology is to achieve industrialization and market promotion. 
Future research should focus on the feasibility assessment of the tech­
nology, cost control, and development of large-scale production tech­
niques. Additionally, collaboration with enterprises and governments 
should be strengthened to promote technology transfer and application, 
and to establish relevant standards and regulations to ensure product 
quality and safety.

technology has broad development prospects in the future. Through the 
development of new materials, in-depth mechanism research, design of

nologies, evaluation of biosafety and environmental impact, and pro­

important role in medicine, environmental protection, and other fields, 
becoming an effective means to solve bacterial infections and environ­
mental pollution problems. We look forward to seeing more break­
throughs in the practical application of piezotronic effect enhanced 
catalytic sterilization technology in the near future, making greater 
contributions to human health and environmental protection.

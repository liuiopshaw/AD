A B S T R A C T
The introduction of additives during the production of wood composites may disrupt the development of bonding 
strength between wood and adhesive, potentially compromising the structural performance of wood composites. 
This study investigates the impact of incorporating a natural wood preservative into 3-ply southern yellow pine 
(SYP) plywood on its bonding strength. The formation of β-cyclodextrin and trans-cinnamaldehyde (βCD-tCN) 
inclusion complex as a natural wood preservative was confirmed by Fourier transform infrared and X-ray 
diffraction analyses, with an inclusion yield ranging 78 % to 120 %. The differential scanning calorimetry 
analysis found the curing of pMDI with wood was not significantly affected by the βCD-based additives. The 
influence of βCD-tCN addition on the bonding strength of a wood composite was assessed by a shear strength test 
of the 3-ply plywood, following ASTM D906-20. The average shear strength of all panels ranged 1.53 N/mm2 to 
2.07 N/mm2, exceeding the minimum requirement of 1.00 N/mm2 for cross-layer bond lines according to EN 
16351-2015. Statistical analysis indicated no significant differences between the bonding strength of pristine 
pMDI and pMDI containing βCD-tCN complex. The combined results suggest that βCD-tCN could be effectively 
implemented in the manufacturing of wood composite products.
1. Introduction
Wood composite products (WCPs) comprise wood elements, such as 
veneer, fibers, particles, or wood strands consolidated with natural 
polymers, synthetic resins, or inorganic binders (Stark & Cai, 2021). In 
recent years, WCPs have gained unprecedented attention in the building 
industry because they offer more environmental benefits than other 
building materials such as concrete, steel, masonry, or plastics (Bergman 
et al., 2014; Wimmers, 2017; Winchester & Reilly, 2020). Since the late 
1990s, numerous exemplary tall wood buildings and structures have 
been constructed worldwide, showcasing the benefits of wood as a 
construction material including faster construction speed and a positive 
environmental impact (Wimmers, 2017). The WCPs are considered 
highly sustainable due to their carbon neutrality, low embodied energy, 
and ability to be produced from various wood species (Milner, 2009; 
Venkatesh et al., 2013). Moreover, their cost-effectiveness and versa­
tility make them suitable for various applications in construction and 
design (Venkatesh et al., 2013). Specifically, engineered wood products 
such as glue-laminated timber (GLT/glulam) and cross-laminated tim­
ber (CLT) are viable alternatives to steel and concrete in prefabricated 
walls and floors, beams, and columns (Brandner et al., 2016; Hassan & 
Johansson, 2018; Neupane et al., 2023; Sathre & Gustavsson, 2009), 
while oriented-strand boards (OSB) are predominantly employed in 
roofing, subflooring, and wall sheathing panels (Copak et al., 2021; 
Doudak & Smith, 2009; Hornus et al., 2020; Santos et al., 2019). The 
essential requirements for these building materials include durability, 
mechanical performance, and cost-effectiveness (Alabi & Fapohunda, 
2020; Asdrubali et al., 2017). However, despite their excellent proper­
ties and promising environmental sustainability, WCPs exhibit limited 
resistance to biological degradation, fire, and dimensional changes 
caused by moisture, which can deteriorate their mechanical perfor­
mance and shorten their service life. Thus, protecting WCPs from 
degradation to retain their structural integrity is essential for making 
them competitive in the building and construction industries.

A wide range of additives are often incorporated into the 
manufacturing process of WCPs to improve their performance and 
prolong their service life. Targeted properties include biological resis­
tance (Bayatkashkoli et al., 2016; Cai, Lim, Kim, & Jeremic, 2020; 
Khademibami et al., 2020), fire protection (Khademibami et al., 2020; 
Seo et al., 2016), weathering resistance (Clausen, 2012; Sedhain et al., 
2024; Sivrikaya et al., 2017), aesthetic enhancement (Kielmann et al., 
2018; Sandberg, 2016), and mitigation of water sorption and associated 
thickness swelling (Kwon & Ayrilmis, 2015; Onat et al., 2014). Gener­
ally, the most widely used and commercially available wood additive for 
enhancing the durability of WCPs involves the impregnation of wood 
with water-borne preservatives containing copper- and boron-based 
compounds (Alade, Naghizadeh, Wessels, & Tyhoda, 2022). However, 
these preservative additives have often been reported to impede the 
development of bond strength between the wood components and resin 
(Wu et al., 2021). Besides the known concern about the disposal of 
treated products after service life due to their toxicity to human health 
and the environment, the inorganic materials in the preservatives may 
reduce the contact between resin and wood components at the molec­
ular level (Tascioglu, 2002), thereby inhibiting the development of bond 
strength in the product (Pizzi, 1994).
Lim et al. (Lim et al., 2020) studied the bonding performance of CLT 
manufactured from southern yellow pine (SYP) pretreated with 
micronized copper azole type C (MCA-C) via a modified full-cell process 
at different retention levels. The treated SYP lumbers were bonded using 
melamine formaldehyde (MF), resorcinol formaldehyde (RF), and one- 
component polyurethane (PUR). The MCA-C treatment at a retention 
level of 1.00 g/m3 lowered the block shear strength (BSS) of the CLT 
regardless of the adhesive system, while the delamination rate increased 
with the retention level for CLT assembled using MF and RF. The study 
concluded that PUR provided an overall better bonding quality than MF 
and RF for MCA-C treated wood. Quin et al. (Quin et al., 2024) recently 
evaluated the bonding performance of CLT post-treated with Cu-based 
preservative as opposed to pre-treating the individual lumber before 
fabrication. The BSS of post-treated CLT was significantly lower than 
untreated CLT.
Similar observations have been reported for other WCPs such as 
laminated veneer lumber (LVL) and particleboards. Wu et al. (Wu et al., 
2021) reported vacuum-impregnated wood veneers with a compound 
protectant composed of manganese chloride, ammonium chloride, 
phosphoric acid, and boric acid. The wood veneers were dried at 60 ±
5 ◦C until a constant weight was achieved, allowed to cool down to room 
temperature in a glass dryer, and then kept in a vacuum chamber. The 
protectant was introduced into the vacuum chamber under −0.09 
megapascal (MPa) for 60 min. The compound protectant improved fire 
and decay resistance but also weakened the bonding strength of 
plywood. ¨Ozçifçi et al. (¨Ozçifçi et al., 2007) reported that a boric acid 
treatment negatively affected the strength of LVL and concluded that the 
boric acid treatment could not be recommended for LVL exposed to 
bending or shear stress. Bayatkashkoli et al. (Bayatkashkoli et al., 2016) 
investigated the incorporation of copper salicylate and zinc salicylate as 
additives into particleboard to improve biological resistance against 
Anacanthotermes vegans termites. The additives showed improved pro­
tection against the termites but caused a reduction in the modulus of 
rupture (MOR) and internal bond (IB) strength of the particleboard. In 
another study, Xu et al. (Xu et al., 2009) applied wax as a sizing agent to 
improve the dimensional stability of bagasse particleboards. The use of a 
wax positively influenced long-term water absorption and thickness 
swelling properties but also exhibited slightly adverse effects on me­
chanical performance. Developing alternative additives that are less 
environmentally invasive and more compatible with WCPs will further 
cement their acceptance as materials of choice in green building 
applications.
Essential oils (EOs), natural hydrophobic aroma compounds pro­
duced by plants, play dual roles in aiding plant growth and defending 
against competitors, pathogens, and predators. These compounds 
possess excellent antioxidant, antibacterial, and antifungal properties, 
along with their natural aroma. Since plant EOs are recognized as safe by 
the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), (U.S. Food and Drug 
Administration, 2024), the incorporation of EO-based additives into 
wood-based products has recently increased and shown promising re­
sults. Liu et al. (Liu et al., 2024) reported improved aesthetics of wood 
by coating it with a sustainable fragrance based on peppermint essential 
oil microcapsules. Bessike et al. (Bessike et al., 2023) showed the pro­
tection of a hardwood species, Triplochiton scleroxcylon, against termites 
by treating it with Jatropha curcas seed oil in combination with different 
leaf extracts. Particularly, our group has recently reported the treatment 
of wood cubes with various EOs complexed with pristine, methylated, or 
hydroxypropylated beta-cyclodextrin (βCD). In these studies, βCDs 
served as a controlled-release carrier for the EOs (Cai et al., 2019; Cai, 
Lim, Nicholas, & Kim, 2020). The βCD-EOs complexes suppressed the 
biological decay of wood from both brown and white rot fungi, main­
taining the original appearance of the wood. Furthermore, an allyl iso­
thiocyanate (AITC, mustard oil)-based βCD-EO inclusion complex was 
integrated into the manufacturing process of OSBs (Cai, Lim, Kim, & 
Jeremic, 2020). The βCD-AITC inclusion complex as an additive was 
dry-blended with wood strands before being mixed with the wood resin, 
polymeric methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (pMDI). While the decay 
resistance of the treated composites was improved, the IB strength 
decreased with the addition of the βCD-AITC complex at loadings of up 
to 10 %.
Although the IB strength is often used to determine the mechanical 
performance of reconstituted wood composite products, such as OSB, 
particleboard, and medium-density fiberboard, it is believed to repre­
sent only the strength of the weakest layer or point in the panel (Suzuki 
& Miyagawa, 2003). Moreover, the IB strength depends on a number of 
variables, such as the moisture content of furnish (Magalh˜aes et al., 
2021), mixing and blending conditions, and wood or strand dimensions 
(Farrokhpayam et al., 2016; Fehrmann et al., 2024), thereby obscuring 
the direct assessment of the influence of the βCD-EOs additive on the 
bonding strength between wood and resin.
In this study, we investigated the influence of incorporating a βCD- 
EO additive as a natural wood preservative into southern yellow pine 
(SYP) plywood on its bond shear strength with pMDI resin. This study is 
based on our hypothesis that the βCD-EO based wood additive may 
negatively influence the bond strength of pMDI in wood composite 
products, thereby lowering its mechanical performance. trans-Cinna­
maldehyde (tCN), an EO derived from cinnamon, was complexed with 
βCD to form a βCD-tCN inclusion complex through a sonochemical 
process. With βCD-tCN loadings at 3 %, 5 %, and 7.5 % in pMDI resin, the 
influence of βCD-tCN addition on the bond strength development was 
examined by changes in viscosity and curing behavior, assessed by 
rheometry and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), respectively. 
Furthermore, a shear strength test, following ASTM D906-20 (ASTM 
Standard D906-20, 2020), was conducted for 3-ply SYP plywood con­
structed by using pMDI resin and βCD-tCN as the additive. The per­
centage of wood failure in the bonded joints of the plywood was 
estimated according to ASTM D5266-13R20 (ASTM Standard D5266- 
13R20, 2020) and further analyzed by using Fiji-ImageJ.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Materials

mol, Thermo Scientific Chemicals), trans-cinnamaldehyde (tCN, 99.0 % 
purity, Acros Organics), and acetonitrile (>99.9 % purity, Fisher Sci­
entific) were used without further purification. Polymeric methylene 
diphenyl diisocyanate (pMDI, Rubinate 1840) was sourced from 
Huntsman International LLC, USA, and southern yellow pine (SYP) ve­
neers were supplied by Winston Plywood and Veneer, Louisville, MS,

2.2. Preparation of the βCD-tCN inclusion complex
In a typical example, an aqueous βCD solution (3 %) was prepared by 
dissolving 30.60 g of βCD in 1020 mL of distilled water at 60 ◦C, 
continuously stirring with a magnetic stirrer at 400 rpm. The solution 
was allowed to cool down to approximately 30 ◦C and then complexed 
with 13.57 mL of tCN at a molar ratio of 1:4 (βCD:tCN) using a 750 W 
high-power ultrasonicator (TM-750, Tekmar, Cincinnati, OH, USA) 
operating at an amplitude of 75 % in a 9.0 s pulse and 0.9 s standby 
mode for 2 min. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm 
for 5 min to separate the inclusion complex from non-incorporated tCN 
and βCD, followed by rapid freezing by liquid nitrogen, and then freeze 
drying in Labconco Freeze Dryer (FreeZon 4.5 Plus, Kansas City, MO, 
USA). The freeze-dried βCD-tCN complex was stored in a desiccator until 
ready for use and further analysis.
2.3. Characterization of the βCD-tCN inclusion complex
The formation of the βCD-tCN complex was qualitatively confirmed 
by attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) 
spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. The ATR-FTIR 
spectra of pristine βCD, tCN, and the βCD-tCN complex were obtained 
in the range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 using a 
PerkinElmer spectrometer (Spectrum Two, PerkinElmer, Inc., Waltham, 
MA, USA). All the spectra were baseline-corrected in the Spectrum® 
Quant software (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) and then normalized 
over the total spectral area using OriginPro 2023b (OriginLab Corpo­
ration, Northampton, MA, USA). Similarly, the X-ray diffractograms of 
pristine βCD and βCD-tCN complex were acquired in a diffractometer 
(SmartLab, Rigaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) using Cu-Kα radiation (λ 
= 1.5418 Å) in a 2θ range from 5◦to 60◦with 0.02◦steps at 2◦/min.

lating the ratio between the measured quantity of tCN encapsulated in 
the βCD-tCN complex and the theoretically maximum amount of tCN 
that could be present in the complex, as shown in Eq. (1). The βCD has 
been reported to form an inclusion complex with essential oils at a molar 
ratio of 1:1 (Zhang et al., 2007). Therefore, the theoretical maximum of 
tCN in the βCD complex is based on a molar ratio of 1:1 (tCN:βCD).

For the ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) spectrophotometry methodol­
ogy, 2.5 mg of the βCD-tCN complex was dissolved in 5 mL of acetoni­
trile (AC) containing de-ionized water (DI) at a mass ratio of 1:9 (DI:AC). 
The mixture was first vortexed for 5 min (Vortex-Genie 2, SI-0236, 
Scientific Industries, USA), then ultrasonicated in a bath (FS30-130 W, 
Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) for 5 min to ensure total libera­
tion of the encapsulated tCN in the complex. The mixture was then 
centrifuged using an Avanti Centrifuge (Model J-E, Beckman Coulter 
Inc., USA) at 6000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was analyzed by 
UV–vis (UV-3101PC, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) at room tempera­
ture. The amount of encapsulated tCN in the complex was calculated 
using a calibration curve, representing a linear relationship between 
various known concentrations of tCN and their corresponding absor­
bance at 287 nm in UV–vis (See Supplementary Fig. S1).
A thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, SDT Q600, TA Instruments, 
New Castle, DE, USA) was also employed to estimate the inclusion yield 
of the complex and further study its thermal stability. The thermal 
profiles of pristine βCD, tCN, and βCD-tCN complex were generated by 
heating approximately 8.0 ± 0.5 mg of each in an alumina pan from 
room temperature to 600 ◦C at a rate of 10 ◦C/min under a nitrogen 
purge flow of 100 mL/min. Except for the tCN, all samples were freeze- 
dried to a constant weight before the analysis. The amount of encap­
sulated tCN in the complex was derived from the ratio of the residual 
weight percentage of βCD-tCN in TGA at 120 ◦C and 220 ◦C, assuming 
only the tCN in the complex would experience weight loss within this 
temperature range. Similar to the UV–vis method, the inclusion yield of 
the complex was estimated by using Eq. (1).
2.4. Viscosity measurements
The βCD-tCN complex was blended with pMDI as an additive at 3 %, 
5 %, and 7.5 % loading using a mechanical stirrer (Stir-Pak 04555-00, 
Cole-Parmer, USA) operating at 600 rpm for 3 min. The mixture was 
subjected to vacuum treatment (AccuTemp-09K, Across International, 
Livingston, NJ, USA) at approximately 30 inHg (635 mmHg) for 30 min 
to remove entrapped air bubbles. The steady-state flow of pure pMDI, 
pMDI containing βCD (as controls), and pMDI with βCD-tCN were 
measured at room temperature using a 40-mm parallel plate over a shear 
rate range of 0.30 to 10.0 Hz on a rheometer (DHR2, TA Instruments, 
New Castle, DE, USA).
2.5. Curing behavior of pMDI resin using differential scanning calorimetry 
(DSC)
The influence of βCD-tCN as an additive on the curing behavior of 
pMDI was examined by evaluating the interaction between pMDI resin, 
βCD, SYP wood particles, and βCD-tCN using a differential scanning 
calorimeter (DSC, Q2000, TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA). Three 
samples of 1) pMDI and wood, 2) pMDI, βCD, and wood, and 3) pMDI, 
βCD-tCN, and wood were obtained by mixing the constituents at an 
equal weight ratio. About 10.0 ± 0.5 mg of each sample was sealed in an 
aluminium pan with a pierced lid and then subjected to the following 
heating regimes at 10 ◦C/min under a constant nitrogen flow of 50.0 
mL/min. The first thermal cycle consisted of heating from 20 ◦C to 
120 ◦C, followed by cooling to −30 ◦C to erase the thermal history of the 
sample. The second thermal cycle involved heating from −30 ◦C to 
250 ◦C, followed by subsequent cooling to 20 ◦C. Data for the DSC an­
alyses were extracted from the second thermal cycles.
2.6. Construction of 3-ply southern yellow pine (SYP) plywood samples
The bonding strength of pMDI containing βCD-tCN as an additive was 
determined following the ASTM D906-20 standard test method (ASTM 
Standard D906-20, 2020) for strength properties of adhesives in 
plywood-type construction in shear by tension loading. A 30.48 cm ×
30.48 cm (12.00 in × 12.00 in) 3-ply lay-up plywood was constructed 
with 3.20 mm thick SYP veneers, such that the fiber direction of the core 
veneer was perpendicular to the top and bottom surface veneers. A 
single-surface glue line application rate and procedure was adopted 
where only the under sides of the top and bottom surface veneers were 
coated with 250.0 g/m2 of pMDI containing βCD-tCN at 3 %, 5 %, and 
7.5 % loadings. Approximately 23.3 g of adhesive was evenly spread out 
and applied onto each 12" by 12" veneer using a 10.16 cm wide plastic 
roller. The procedure was repeated with pMDI and pMDI containing βCD 
at 3 %, 5 %, and 7.5 % as controls. The panels were pressed in a hy­
draulic hot press (Dieffenbacher North America Inc. Windsor, ON, 
Canada) at 175 ◦C and 1.60 MPa for 3 min and later conditioned at 22 ◦C 
and 50 % relative humidity for 7 days before testing.
2.7. Shear strength measurements in tension loading
Ten 2.54 cm × 8.255 cm (1.00 in × 3.25 in) specimens were prepared 
from each 30.48 cm × 30.48 cm (12.00 in × 12.00 in) panel for each 
treatment according to the ASTM D906-20 standard test method for 
strength properties of adhesives in plywood type construction in shear 
by tension loading (ASTM Standard D906-20, 2020) (see Fig. S2). Each 
specimen was carefully placed in the jaws of a universal testing machine 
(S4-600KN, Instron Corporation, MA, USA) and aligned parallel to the 
center line of the jaws. Each specimen was subjected to shear by tension 
loading in an environmentally controlled laboratory (22 ◦C and 50 % 
relative humidity) at a speed rate of 2.032 mm/min. The average shear

2.8. Wood failure estimation
The visual estimation method following the ASTM D5266-13R20 is 
the traditional method of calculating the wood failure percentage in 
adhesive-bonded joints (Alade, Naghizadeh, & Wessels, 2022; ASTM 
Standard D5266-13R20, 2020). However, this method has been re­
ported to be subjective and lead to inaccurate and inconsistent estima­
tion due to the inability of the human eye to distinguish between glue 
and wood consistently (Kl¨ausler et al., 2014; Zink & Kartunova, 1998). 
Therefore, the visual analysis was augmented with a Fiji-ImageJ-assisted 
method adapted from Alade et al. (Alade, Naghizadeh, & Wessels, 
2022). The authors reported an improvement in the consistency of wood 
failure estimation compared to the ASTM D5266-13R20 standard 
method. In this study, high-quality images of the specimens were 
captured after the shear strength test using a full high-definition (HD) 
camera and then imported images into Fiji-ImageJ software. The wood 
failure areas were mapped and measured, and the wood failure per­
centage (WFP) was calculated as the ratio of the wood failure area to the 
glued area tested under shear.
2.9. Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using OriginPro, version 2023b. A 
one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) at a 5 % level of significance (p 
< 0.05) was employed to establish if the addition of βCD-tCN had a 
significant impact on the shear strength of pMDI resin. The normality of 
the data sets was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The assumption of 
homogeneity of variance was tested using Levene's test. Since this 
assumption was met, multiple means were compared using the Tukey 
post-hoc test to evaluate significant differences among panels with 
different loadings of βCD-tCN additives at a 95 % confidence interval (C. 
I.).
3. Results and discussions
3.1. Formation of inclusion complex
The formation of βCD-based inclusion complexes can be achieved by 
a number of methods, such as kneading, spray drying, co-precipitation, 
freeze-drying, co-heating, and ultrasonication (Cai, Lim, Nicholas, & 
Kim, 2020; Cheirsilp & Rakmai, 2017; Hogenbom et al., 2021; Kfoury 
et al., 2014; Waghulde et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022). However, the 
kneading method is unsuitable for large-scale production (Pereva et al., 
2016; Waghulde et al., 2023), while co-precipitation is time-consuming 
with low yield (Waghulde et al., 2023). Spray drying is limited to 
thermostable molecules (Zhang et al., 2022) while the freeze-drying 
method is well-suited for water-soluble guests (Cao et al., 2017). On 
the other hand, ultrasonication utilizes a combination of mechanical, 
thermal, and shock wave effects to increase the penetration rate of guest 
and host molecules (Zhang et al., 2022). The ultrasonication method 
offers advantages such as quick processing time and scalability, making 
it particularly appropriate for the manufacturing of wood products 
where a large quantity of additives is required.
The infrared (IR) spectra of pristine βCD, tCN, and the βCD-tCN in­
clusion complex produced via the ultrasonication method are presented 
in Fig. 1. The IR spectrum of βCD exhibited a broad absorption band in 
the 3000–3600 cm−1 region, which is attributed to the O–H stretching 
vibrations of the oligosaccharide (Barba et al., 2015; Rachmawati et al., 
2013), and peaks at 2920 cm−1 due to asymmetric/symmetric stretching 
of C–H, and at 1640 cm−1 corresponding to the H–O–H deformation 
bands of water present in βCD (Barba et al., 2015; Galv˜ao et al., 2015). 
Other peaks at 1153 cm−1 and 1029 cm−1 indicate C–O and C–O–C 
stretching vibrations, respectively (Barba et al., 2015; Galv˜ao et al., 
2015; Rachmawati et al., 2013). The IR absorption peaks of tCN 
involved aromatic C–H stretching at 3100–3000 cm−1, aldehydic C–H 
doublet stretching at 2812 and 2744 cm−1, C––O stretching at 1668 
cm−1, and C––C stretching at 1625 cm−1. In comparison, the IR spectrum 
of βCD-tCN exhibited a slight reduction in absorbance for the broad 
O–H band region, which was attributed to the substitution of high- 
enthalpy water molecules in the cavity of βCD, as a host, with the hy­
drophobic tCN molecules, as a guest, upon complexation (Cai, Lim, 
Nicholas, & Kim, 2020; Del Valle, 2004; Szejtli, 1998). The benzene ring 
in tCN was entrapped into the cavity of βCD via hydrophobic in­
teractions, causing the absorption peaks of alkene stretching vibrations 
significantly weakened (see the inset of Fig. 1). Due to intermolecular 
hydrogen bonding between tCN and βCD, the broad O–H band in βCD- 
tCN narrowed and shifted to a slightly higher wavenumber centered at 
3345 cm−1 compared to βCD (the inset of Fig. 1) (Deng et al., 2022; Tian 
et al., 2022). In addition, other prominent peaks of tCN at 1448 and 
1121 cm−1 were present in the IR spectrum of βCD-tCN with reduced 
intensity. These collective observations strongly indicate the formation

The XRD patterns of pristine βCD and βCD-tCN are shown in Fig. 2. 
The diffraction pattern of pristine βCD exhibited sharp and intense peaks 
at 4.5◦and 9.0◦. These peaks are indicative of a cage-type molecular 
packing of βCD with a herring-bone arrangement where the cavity of 
each βCD molecule is blocked by neighboring molecules (Saenger et al., 
1998; Uyar et al., 2009). In the X-ray diffraction pattern of βCD-tCN, the 
peaks observed in pristine βCD disappeared and instead the new peaks 
showed up at 7.3◦, 12.0◦, and 18.8◦. These new peaks indicate the for­
mation of the βCD-tCN inclusion complex, suggesting the transformation 
of the crystalline structure of βCD from a cage to a channel type (Abarca 
et al., 2016; Uyar et al., 2009). As shown in Schematic S1, in the 
channel-type arrangement, βCD molecules are believed to align and 
stack on top of each other, creating long cylindrical channels that 
accommodate guest molecules (Harada et al., 1995; Saenger et al., 
1998). The change in the packing of βCD crystals confirms the formation 
of the inclusion complex, further corroborating the FTIR results.
3.2. Quantification of inclusion yield of βCD-tCN
The inclusion yield of βCD-tCN was estimated to be 135.0 ± 14 % 
and 78.0 ± 3 % by using UV–vis and TGA methods, respectively, using 
three replicates (see Figs. S1 and S3; Tables S1 and S2). The higher in­
clusion yield estimated by UV–vis than TGA may be due to the presence 
of loosely bound tCN molecules to βCD during UV–vis measurements. A 
similar observation was made by Cai et al. (Cai, Lim, Nicholas, & Kim, 
2020) while quantifying the inclusion yield of methyl-βCD-essential oil 
complexes prepared by a co-precipitation method. In contrast, such 
loosely bound tCN can be selectively excluded in the TGA method (Cai, 
Lim, Kim, & Jeremic, 2020), which will be elaborated below. The TGA 
thermograms (TG) and their first derivative curves (dTG) of pristine 
βCD, tCN, and βCD-tCN are shown in Fig. 3. Since the substantial weight 
loss of pristine tCN began at 120 ◦C with an 8 % weight loss that 
accelerated until reaching its maximum at 175 ◦C, we suspect that the 
weight loss of βCD-tCN between 100 ◦C and 120 ◦C was associated with 
loosely bound and possibly unencapsulated tCN within the βCD-tCN 
complex. As such, only the weight loss between 120 ◦C and 220 ◦C was 
considered when calculating the inclusion yield using the TGA data, 
thereby resulting in the inclusion yield of 78.0 % after deducting 20.0 % 
for loosely bound tCN to βCD.
3.3. Thermal stability of βCD-tCN complex
The TG and dTG curves of pristine βCD, tCN, and the βCD-tCN 
complex are shown in Fig. 3. A rapid weight loss was observed in the TG 
curve of tCN with a maximum dTG peak at 175 ◦C. tCN completely 
evaporated at 180 ◦C with a residue <0.10 %. A weight loss of 3.5 % was 
observed in βCD before 100 ◦C, probably due to the evaporation of 
water. Significant weight loss was observed for βCD at 300–350 ◦C, 
indicating thermal degradation of polysaccharide. The βCD-tCN showed 
a similar level of weight loss of 2.8 % before 100 ◦C. The dTG of βCD-tCN 
exhibited three peaks centered at 115 ◦C, 205 ◦C, and 290 ◦C in addition 
to the main thermal degradation peak centered at 325 ◦C. Since the 
weight loss of pristine tCN occurred between 100 ◦C and 180 ◦C, the dTG 
peak of βCD-tCN at 115 ◦C can be attributed to loosely bound tCN to 
βCD, considering the volatility of the encapsulated guest molecules is 
known to decrease significantly (Szejtli, 1998). In fact, the dTG peak of 
βCD-tCN at 200 ◦C suggested such delayed volatility of tCN encapsulated 
within the cavity of βCD. In practice, plywood and reconstituted wood 
composites such as particleboards and oriented strand boards are nor­
mally hot-pressed at temperatures <200 ◦C (Alawode et al., 2022; Ala­
wode et al., 2023; Asafu-Adjaye et al., 2022; Carvalho et al., 2014; Han 
et al., 2007). Therefore, tCN in the βCD-tCN inclusion complex is ex­
pected to maintain thermal stability during hot pressing so that it can be 
integrated into the manufacturing process without compromising its 
potency.

of pMDI with bio-based additives has been reported in the literature 
(Alawode et al., 2022; Alawode et al., 2023; Asafu-Adjaye et al., 2022; 
Chen & Yan, 2018). However, we note that the viscosity of the pMDI 
mixtures with βCD and βCD-tCN additives remained well below 1.0 Pa⋅s, 
which is often considered the maximum viscosity limit for spraying in 
the production of particleboard panels (Carvalho et al., 2014). This 
suggests that the βCD-tCN inclusion complex holds potential for inte­
gration into the manufacturing process of WCPs.
3.5. Curing behavior of pMDI, wood, βCD-tCN, and their physical 
mixtures
The DSC thermograms of wood, βCD, βCD-tCN, pMDI, and their 
physical mixtures at the second heating cycle are shown in Fig. 5. Wood, 
βCD, βCD-tCN, and their mixtures with pMDI displayed broad endo­
thermic peaks between 70 ◦C and 150 ◦C indicating dehydration (Abarca 
et al., 2016; Cai, Lim, Kim, & Jeremic, 2020; Pinto et al., 2005). The βCD 
exhibited a major endothermic peak at 223 ◦C with a shoulder peak at 
219 ◦C, displaying the melting of βCD crystals. For βCD-tCN, the position 
and shape of these peaks slightly changed, which can be ascribed to the 
transformation of the molecular packing of the βCD crystals from a cage 
type to a channel type after complexation, as discussed in the XRD 
analysis.
The thermogram of pMDI showed a broad endothermic peak that 
ranged from 110 ◦C to 190 ◦C with Tonset and Tpeak at 122.5 ◦C and 
149 ◦C, respectively, which represented the onset temperature and the 
central position of the endothermic peak. This broad endothermic peak 
was attributed to the curing of 4,4′-MDI and possibly two other pMDI 
isomers including 2,2′-MDI and 2,4′-MDI. Compared to pristine pMDI, 
the endothermic peak shifted toward a lower temperature range for 
pMDI-wood, pMDI-wood-βCD, and pMDI-wood-βCD-tCN. Specifically, 
their Tonset and Tpeak decreased by >10 ◦C, as summarized in Table 2. 
The reduced curing temperature of pMDI with wood and the additives 
implies an increased reactivity of pMDI with water in wood, βCD, and 
βCD-tCN (Harper et al., 2001a, 2001b; Kawalerczyk et al., 2023). 
However, the difference in Tonset and Tpeak between pMDI-wood and 
pMDI-wood with the βCD-based additives was less pronounced, sug­
gesting the curing of pMDI with wood was not substantially affected by 
the addition of the βCD-based additives.
The DSC thermograms of pMDI-wood-βCD and pMDI-wood-βCD-tCN 
were compared to those of βCD and βCD-tCN to examine the possible 
interactions between pMDI and the βCD-based additives. For pMDI- 
wood-βCD, the disappearance of a shoulder peak at 219 ◦C suggests that 
the curing of pMDI has a minor influence on the melting of βCD crystals. 
Surprisingly, the melting peak of βCD crystals completely disappeared 
for pMDI-wood-βCD-tCN, as shown in violet in Fig. 5. This complete 
absence implies a strong molecular-level interaction between pMDI and 
the βCD crystals in βCD-tCN, basically interrupting the molecular 
packing of the βCD crystals. The enhanced interaction between pMDI 
and βCD-tCN also agrees with the more homogeneous mixture they 
formed compared to the one with pMDI and βCD, as shown in Fig. S4b. 
Additionally, it is possible that the release kinetics of tCN in βCD-tCN in 
wood composite products involving pMDI could differ significantly 
compared to other environments due to the molecular interaction be­
tween pMDI and βCD-tCN, which would warrant a separate study to 
investigate further.

3.6. The shear strength of pMDI resin containing additives and their 
respective wood failure
The shear strength of 3-ply SYP plywood panels constructed by using 
pMDI with and without the βCD-based additives are presented in Fig. 6. 
The panel with pMDI only had a mean shear strength of 2.07 N/mm2, 
while the shear strength of the panels with pMDI and the βCD-based 
additives ranged between 1.53 and 1.94 N/mm2. The shear strength of 
the panel with pMDI was an order of magnitude greater than that of 
plywood boards made with neat pMDI reported by Alawode et al. 
(Alawode et al., 2022). The authors observed resin bleeding through the 
veneer surface and attributed the poor performance of pMDI panels to 
the low viscosity of pMDI resin which caused starved joint during 
pressing. The authors coated each veneer with 250.0 g/m2 of resin, 
while a single-surface application was employed in this study. No resin 
bleeding was observed during pressing in this study, which indicates 
that the difference in the resin application method may have caused the 
disparity.
The addition of βCD and βCD-tCN to pMDI resulted in an overall 
reduction in the shear strength of the plywood panels. When βCD was 
added to pMDI, the panels with a 3 % βCD loading exhibited the lowest 
shear strength. The shear strength increased by 24 % when βCD loading 
was increased from 3 % to 5 %, but then decreased by 14 % at a 7.5 % 
loading. This inconsistent reduction pattern compared to the pristine 
pMDI can be attributed to the inhomogeneous dispersion and associated 
sedimentation of βCD in pMDI, as shown in Fig. S3b. In contrary, the 
panels made with pMDI and βCD-tCN showed a gradual reduction trend 
in shear strength as the additive content increased, with the 3 % βCD- 
tCN loading having the highest shear strength. The ANOVA results in 
Table 3 indicate that the βCD-based additives significantly affected the 
shear strength of the panels with pMDI. Despite the overall reduction in 
shear strength, the shear strength of the panels with the βCD-based 
additives was not significantly different from that of the panels with 
pMDI at a significance level of 0.05, except for the panels with 3 % and 
7.5 % βCD loadings, as shown by the statistical grouping letters in Fig. 6. 
This overall reduction in shear strength may be due to the increased 
viscosity of pMDI with the βCD-based additives, which would require 
optimization for real-world applications. It should be noted that all the 
panels made with pMDI along with the βCD-based additives in this study 
exceeded the minimum requirement of 1.00 MPa for characteristic shear 
strength for cross-layer bond lines according to EN 16351-2015 standard 
(European Standards EN16351, 2015).
In addition to the shear strength of adhesives, wood failure per­
centage (WFP) is another important index governing the commercial 
acceptability of adhesives (Shmulsky et al., 2021). After the shear 
strength tests, the WFP of the plywood panels with pMDI and the βCD- 
based additives was estimated by visual and Fiji-ImageJ-assisted as­
sessments, as summarized in Table 4. The visual assessment indicated 
that 50 % of the panels constructed with pristine pMDI had wood failure 
with an average WFP of 67 %, within the fail-pass range of 30–85 % 
(ASTM Standard D5266-13R20, 2020). The Fiji-ImageJ-assisted assess­
ment showed a higher number of panels with wood failure. For the 
panels made with pMDI and βCD, both visual and Fiji-ImageJ-assisted 
evaluations revealed that >80 % of panels exhibited adhesive failure 
with an average WFP below the fail-pass range, indicating the adhesive 
did not function properly. This poor adhesive performance could be due 
to increased adhesive reactivity caused by the interaction between the 
high-enthalpy water molecules in the cavity of βCD and highly reactive 
4,4′-MDI monomers. Adhesive penetration plays a vital role in bond 
performance and accelerated adhesive curing reactions can affect 
bonding quality including penetration depth and bonding strength 
(¨Ozparpucu et al., 2022). Specifically, premature adhesive curing can 
lead to interfacial bond failure due to insufficient time for the adhesive 
to penetrate the porous structure of wood, which is essential for 
achieving strong mechanical interlocking (Kamke & Lee, 2007). The 
negative influence of the additive is evident in the higher adhesive 
failure and the lower shear strength observed in panels manufactured 
with pMDI and βCD. On the contrary, the panels with pMDI and βCD-tCN 
showed similar WFP compared to the panels with pMDI only, except for 
the 5 % βCD-tCN loading. Overall, the panels with pMDI and βCD-tCN 
exhibited fewer instances of adhesive failure compared to those with 
pMDI and βCD, indicating βCD-tCN was more compatible with pMDI. 
This trend aligns well with the shear strength results. Wood failure re­
sults did not vary significantly between the visual and Fiji-ImageJ- 
assisted assessments.

wood preservative, on the bonding strength development between wood 
and adhesive. Three-ply SYP plywood panels were constructed using 
pMDI along with the βCD-based additives, and their shear strength was 
analyzed to assess the additive's influence on the adhesive. The βCD-tCN 
inclusion complex was synthesized by a sonochemical process and its 
formation was confirmed by FTIR and XRD analyses. The βCD-tCN 
complex showed superior compatibility with pMDI compared to neat 
βCD, as evidenced by minimal sedimentation and a viscosity profile 
ranging 0.30 to 10.00 Hz. Integration of the βCD-based additives into 
pMDI resulted in increased viscosity yet remained within an acceptable 
limit (1.00 Pa⋅s) for spray application. A shear strength test by tension 
loading following ASTM D906-20 indicated no significant difference in 
shear strength between plywood panels made with pristine pMDI and 
those incorporating the βCD-tCN additives. This finding was consistent 
with the DSC analysis, which confirmed that the addition of βCD-tCN did 
not hinder the curing process of pMDI with wood. Moreover, wood 
panels with pristine pMDI and pMDI with the βCD-tCN additive dis­
played similar wood failure patterns and percentages. Additionally, all 
panels exceeded the minimum requirement of 1.00 MPa for character­
istic shear strength for cross-layer bond lines. The collective findings of 
this study demonstrate that the βCD-EO inclusion complex holds 
promise for integration into the manufacturing process of WCPs using 
pMDI, and possibly other resin systems, offering added value with a 
focus on sustainability.

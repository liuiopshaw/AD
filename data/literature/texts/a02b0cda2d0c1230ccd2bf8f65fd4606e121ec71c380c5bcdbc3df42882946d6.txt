ABSTRACT: Vancomycin is one of the last lines of defense
against certain drug-resistant bacteria-caused infections. How-
ever, the high susceptibility to drug resistance and high toxicity
seriously limit the application of vancomycin. Nanoantibiotics
provide opportunities to solve these problems. Herein, we
present mercaptophenylboronic acid (MBA)-modified gold
nanoclusters with well-defined molecular formulas and
structure (Au44(MBA)18) and excellent antibacterial activities
against various drug-resistant bacteria such as vancomycin-
resistant Enterococcus faecalis (VRE). Au44(MBA)18 interacts
with bacteria by first attaching to teichoic-acid and destroying
the cell wall and subsequently binding to the bacterial DNA.
Au44(MBA)18 could be administered via multiple routes and has a high biosafety (500 mg/kg, no ototoxicity), overcoming the
two major shortcomings of vancomycin (sole administration route and high ototoxicity). Our study is insightful for curing
infections caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria using nanoantibiotics with high biosafety.
KEYWORDS: antibacterial gold nanoclusters, nanoantibiotics, vancomycin-resistant bacteria, ototoxicity, multiple administration modes

ecently, the risk of bacteria-caused tissue infection
poses an increasing threat on human health due to the
rapid rise of bacterial drug resistance.1−4 As an
antibiotic with powerful capability to kill most pathogenic
bacteria, vancomycin is considered as the last line of defense
against Gram-positive bacterial infections. However, vancomy-
cin has disadvantages including high ototoxicity and
nephrotoxicity, high susceptibility to the generation of drug
resistance, and single route of administration (intravenous
injection at very low rate). Thus, the clinical application of
vancomycin is seriously limited. This is a dangerous sign,
which means that the only effective antibacterial drug faces the
problem of being unusable, posing a great threat to human
health and life.5,6
Nanomaterials are powerful to fight against bacteria
especially multidrug-resistant superbugs, due to their ultralarge
surface area, tunable surface modification, and high bio-
activities.7−10 The emerging ultrasmall nanoclusters (NCs)
exhibit unique molecule-like physicochemical properties, such
as well-defined structures, molecular magnetism, and strong
luminescence, which are beneficial for biomedical applica-
tions.11−14 NCs are widely used in the fields of biomedicine,
biosensors,15 cancer therapy,16,17 drug delivery,18 and infection
therapy.19,20 Moreover, the fluorescence of NCs can be used to
monitor real-time interactions between the nanomaterials and
bacteria, providing a promising platform for exploring the
antibacterial mechanism. In addition, NCs can be modified by
antibacterial agents to achieve a synergistic antibacterial
effect.21−23 Thus, NCs are ideal platforms for developing
various antibacterial agents with simple modifications.24−27

trials. For example, nanosilver fluoride has been used to treat
dental caries. This nanodrug has entered clinical phase 3 (data

Antimicrobial Property of Au44(MBA)18. We tested the
antimicrobial property of Au44(MBA)18 by measuring their
minimal inhibition concentration (MIC) against Gram-positive
bacteria and their multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains, setting
vancomycin (Van) and oxacillin (Oxa) as positive controls that
are effective against Gram-positive bacteria. Van and Oxa are
glycopeptide antibiotics that can effectively inhibit the Gram-
positive and drug-sensitive bacterial strains but cannot inhibit
some MDR strains (Figure 3A). Due to the strong
nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity, vancomycin is only used to
treat serious infections when other antibiotics fail.32−34 By
comparison, Au44(MBA)18 effectively inhibits both sensitive
and MDR bacterial strains with an MIC of no more than 13
μg/mL (Figure 3A). However, Au44(MBA)18 cannot inhibit
any Gram-negative bacteria even at a concentration of 64.85
μg/mL (Table S1). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
images show that Au44(MBA)18-treated bacteria merge
together or lose their normal structure, indicating the apparent
disruption of the bacteria (Figure 3B). TEM further confirmed
the destruction of the bacterial cell wall for both VRE and
methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) treated by
Au44(MBA)18 (Figure 3C). By comparison, GNCs-treated
bacteria have an intact double-layered cell wall structure,
implying that the bare GNCs have no antibacterial activity. We
further investigate the permeability of cell membranes by
staining bacteria with SYTO 9/PI and counting the number of
live/dead bacteria. Fluorescence images show that the
permeability of the Au44(MBA)18-treated bacteria cell mem-
brane increases (Figure S4), indicating the disruption of the
membrane.

Biosafety and Biodistribution Evaluation. We eval-
uated the blood biocompatibility of Au44(MBA)18 by a
hemolysis test. There is no hemolytic phenomenon on rat
erythrocytes treated with different concentrations of
Au44(MBA)18 (Figure S6A), indicating that Au44(MBA)18 has
a wide range of safety in direct or indirect blood-contacting
applications. The morphologies of cells cultured with
Au44(MBA)18 are in quadrangular shapes, and the proliferation
after 3 days of culture is the same as in the control group,
demonstrating that Au44(MBA)18 has no toxic effects on cell
viability and growth (Figure S6B). Moreover, we also find that
the human aortic fibroblast cells (HAF) cells treated by
Au44(MBA)18 are in good growing condition (Figure S7A).
Au44(MBA)18 also did not damage Huh-7 and PLC cells at a
concentration as high as 128 μg/mL (Figure S7B). A large
number of literature reports indicate that vancomycin has
concentration-dependent toxicity in different cell lines,
especially at high concentrations.36−38 Although vancomycin
is a clinically used drug, it may cause irreversible cell death. In
recent years, due to the excessive use of vancomycin,
vancomycin-caused ototoxicity has emerged, which leads to
hearing loss and vestibular dysfunction. For treating MDR
bacteria-caused infections, a combination of antibiotics is often
used. The enhancement of aminoglycoside ototoxicity by
vancomycin is inevitable. The toxicity of materials will directly
hinder their applications after entering the clinic. We
investigate the ototoxicity of Au44(MBA)18 by measuring the
auditory brainstem response (ABR) in mice. Compared with
the control, the thresholds of mice after intraperitoneal
injection of Au44(MBA)18 exhibit an insignificant change
under all pure tone stimuli, which means Au44(MBA)18 does
not show an obvious influence on hearing function (Figure
4A,B). To further evaluate the ototoxicity, the cochlea tissue is
dissected from mice after administration of Au44(MBA)18 for 7
days for immunofluorescence staining to observe the hair cells
of the cochlea. The apical, middle, and base turns of the

hair cells) and DAPI (cell nuclei), respectively. The hair cells
treated byAu44(MBA)18 show normal shapes (Figure 4C). We
further count the surviving hair cell number per 100 mm of
cochlea length in three turns, and there is no significant
decrease in the Au44(MBA)18-treated group compared with the

In the clinic, different modes of administration have different
impacts on the efficacy of the same drug. Van can only be used
intravenously, and the injection time is more than 1 h. To
determine the suitable administration route for infection
treatment, we explore the biodistribution of Au44(MBA)18 in
mice by different administration modes through a NIR-II
small-animal imaging system (Figure 5). In intravenous
administration, Au44(MBA)18 mainly reaches the liver at 12
h, spreads to the spleen at 24 h, and still remains in the liver
and spleen at 48 h. In intraperitoneal administration,
Au44(MBA)18 mainly reaches the intestines at 12 h, remains
in intestines at 24 h, and spreads to the liver at 48 h (Figure 5).
Au44(MBA)18 mainly reaches the lungs through inhaled
droplets at 12 h (Figure 5) in nasal drip administration. The
biodistribution of Au44(MBA)18 is different from some
nanomaterials that can enter the brain via the inhalation
administration mode. For instance, nanoplastics (80 nm) could
distribute in the lungs and brain of mice through aerosol
inhalation.39 The retention of Au44(MBA)18 in different organs
relies on the administration modes (Figure 5). For abdominal
infections, the long retention time of Au44(MBA)18 in the
intestines implies that intraperitoneal administration is suitable.
We evaluate toxicology of Au44(MBA)18 in vivo by histological
analysis and biochemical indicators. Blood biochemical indexes
in all groups show no significant difference compared with
those of the control group, indicating that Au44(MBA)18 has no
effects on the function of the liver and kidney (Tables S2 and
S3). Hematoxylin−eosin (HE) staining results showed no
structural damage of the major organs, especially the intestines,
even after 14 days (Figure S10), which verifies that
Au44(MBA)18 causes no adverse effect in the long term.
Thus, Au44(MBA)18 exhibits excellent biocompatibility in vitro
and in vivo.

CONCLUSIONS
Addressing the disadvantage of vancomycin in clinical
application, we present a mercaptophenylboronic acid-
modified gold nanocluster, Au44(MBA)18, as a nanoantibiotic
for dealing with multidrug-resistant bacteria especially
vancomycin-resistant E. faecalis. Au44(MBA)18 kills Gram-
positive bacteria by first attaching to teichoic acid on the
bacterial surface and subsequently internalizing and binding
with bacterial DNA, demonstrating a definite antibacterial
mechanism. Au44(MBA)18 shows no ototoxicity and systematic
toxicity, which is the major obstacle hindering the application
of vancomycin. Moreover, Au44(MBA)18 could be adminis-
tered via multiple routes, which is superior to vancomycin,
with only one administration mode. Also, the biodistribution of
fluorescence of Au44(MBA)18 can be traced in vivo. Thus,
Au44(MBA)18 has the potential to replace vancomycin as a
theranostic agent for dealing with untreatable bacterial
infections in the clinic. Au44(MBA)18 shows an excellent
antibacterial effect only against Gram-positive bacteria. In our
future research, we aim to overcome this limitation by
exploring ligand modifications that can broaden the anti-
bacterial spectrum. Our goal is to achieve effective eradication
of more drug-resistant bacterial strains.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Materials. Mercaptophenylboronic acid (Mw = 153.99) and
tetrachloroauric acid (HAuCl4·3H2O) were purchased from Sigma
(USA). Sodium borohydride (NaBH4) and triethylamine were
obtained from Aladdin (Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.,
Shanghai, China). Other chemicals and reagents were all reagent
grade.
Preparation and Characterization of Au44(MBA)18. We used
the one-pot synthesis method to prepare Au44(MBA)18 by the
reduction of HAuCl4·3H2O with NaBH4. We mixed the MBA (5 mM,
20 mL), HAuCl4·3H2O (20 mM, 2.5 mL), and triethylamine (1 M, 3
mL) in water and added 1 mL of NaBH4 (4.3 mg dissolved in 1 mL of
0.2 M triethylamine solution) with vigorous stirring (800 rpm). The
sulfhydryl groups of MBA can concurrently bind to GNCs via Au−S
bonds. After 3 h of reaction at room temperature, we collected and
ultrafiltered the Au44(MBA)18 with a centrifuge (HeraeusMultifuge
X1R, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) three times with double-
distilled water at 8000 rpm for 20 min to remove the unreacted
chemicals. Using the same synthetic conditions, we synthesized GNCs
like those of the control group with the addition of glutathione
(GSH). The morphologies of NCs were characterized by TEM
(Tecnai G2F30, FEI, USA). The zeta potential of the NCs was tested
on a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern, England). We
measured the chemical elements of the NCs by XPS (ESCALAB
250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The concentration of the
NCs was tested by inductively coupled plasma analysis (ICP, iCAP
6300, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). We injected GNCs and
Au44(MBA)18 (200 μg/mL) into the high-resolution mass spectrom-
etry system (Q Exactive, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a
peristaltic pump for ESI-MS analysis.
Antimicrobial Property of Au44(MBA)18. We evaluated the
antimicrobial property of Au44(MBA)18 by measuring its MIC, which
was performed by incubating the samples with different bacteria for
24 h at 37 °C. Bacteria were cultured in Luria−Bertani (LB) medium
(pH 7, 5g/L yeast extract, 10g/L casein tryptone, and 10g/L NaCl).
The original concentration of the bacteria was 1 × 106 colony-forming
units per milliliter (CFU/mL). To further explore antibiotic
mechanisms, we incubated Au44(MBA)18 with different bacteria on
a shaking bed at 37 °C for 4 h at a rotation rate of 260 rpm. After the
incubation, the bacteria were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde treated
by ethanol dehydration for SEM (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%,
and 100% in sequence). The samples were cut to superthin slices and
stained with 2% uranyl acetate and 0.2% lead citrate for TEM

Toxicology Evaluation of Au44(MBA)18. We evaluated the blood
compatibility of Au44(MBA)18 by hemolysis assay.43 We collected rat

Au44(MBA)18 to different concentrations (128, 32, 8, and 2 μg/mL)
and incubated them with an erythrocyte suspension for 3 h at 37 °C.
We used a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite M200) to measure the
optical density of the samples at 540 nm (OD540 nm). We added 10%

the Au44(MBA)18-treated cells by an inverted microscope (DMI
6000B, Leica, Germany) and laser confocal microscopy (Nikon
Confocal A1R+Symp64) after staining the cells with Calcein AM/PI
(Dojindo, Japan).
BALB/c mice (female, approximately 20 g in weight, SPF grade)
obtained from HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd., Beijing, were employed to
assess the toxicity of the Au44(MBA)18. We injected Au44(MBA)18
(500 mg/kg) into the mice via the abdomen, setting saline as a
control. We collected blood at different time points (24, 48, and 72 h)
for liver, kidney, immunity, and blood routine analysis. We conducted
dissections of the heart, brain, lung, liver, spleen, kidney, and intestine
specimens for HE staining, followed by microscopic observation. We
injected Au44(MBA)18 (20 mg/kg) into mice via different
administration routes. We dropped 10 μL of Au44(MBA)18 in the
nostrils of mice and observed that the droplet was inhaled by mice
immediately. At a predetermined interval of 2, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h, we
anesthetized the mice with 2% isoflurane and imaged them by a small-
animal NIR II imaging system (NIROPTICS, series III 900/1700) to
confirm the biodistribution of Au44(MBA)18 in different admin-
istrations. We dissected the temporal bones of the administrated mice
and fixed them in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C with
rotation and decalcified them in EDTA for 48 h for immunohis-
tochemistry analysis. We microdissected the organs of Corti and the
cochlea tissue and then permeabilized them in PBS containing 5%
BSA and 1% Triton X-100 for 1 h. The permeabilized samples were
then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. In this
study, rabbit anti-myosin 7a (Proteus Biosciences, No. 25-6790) was
used as the primary antibody, diluted to 1:500 in a solution of 5%
BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS. Subsequently, the samples
underwent three PBS rinses and were incubated with secondary
fluorescent antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit, diluted at
1:200, Abcam, No. ab150073) for 1 h at room temperature in the
dark to detect the primary antibodies. Cell nuclei were simultaneously
stained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, D9542). Imaging was performed
at 10 and 20× magnification using a confocal microscope (Nikon
Confocal A1R+Symp64).
We injected Au44(MBA)18 (200 mg/kg) into the mice via tail vein,
setting saline as a negative control and vancomycin (200 mg/kg) as a
positive control. We also injected Au44(MBA)18 (200 mg/kg) into the
mice via abdominal injection, setting saline as the negative control
and gentamicin (50 mg/kg) as the positive control. We recorded and
analyzed ABR signal with a customized system (mouse auditory
brainstem response test system). The mice were anesthetized
(solution mixed with Zoletil 50 40 mg/kg and dexdomitor 0.1 mg/
kg, 0.004 mL/10 g intraperitoneal injection) after administration for 7
days. The record and reference electrodes were respectively inserted
subcutaneously at the cranial vertex, below the left pinna; another
ground electrode was inserted into the muscle at the right thigh. To
assess hearing capability, the mice were exposed to pure tone stimuli
ranging from 4 to 32 kHz (4, 8, 12, 16, 24, or 32 kHz) at sound
pressure levels (SPLs) varying from 10 to 90 dB in increments of 10
dB. The ABR threshold was determined as the lowest stimulus
intensity demonstrating an identifiable wave. The mice’s hearing
thresholds were averaged within each experimental group for analysis.
Vestibular function of the Au44(MBA)18-administered mice was
evaluated by vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) test, and the signal was
recorded by a customized system (mouse vestibular function
comprehensive test system, from Shenzhen Giant (Ju’An) Tech-
nologies Co., Ltd., No. GAT-MVOR943). The mice were mounted
on the platform of the VOR system and recorded by video. The
platform rotated at 0.5, 0.8, 1.6, and 3.2 Hz at an angular velocity of
40°/s. In all rotation modes, the camera continuously recorded the
rotation stimulus for more than 90 s. The eye movement amplitude
was obtained and calculated as the study reported before by the
customized software.44 Gains were determined by computing the ratio
of eye velocity/platform velocity within 90 ms of the start of the
rotation.
Animal Model and Histological Analysis. We evaluated the
therapeutic effect of Au44(MBA)18 using an acute intra-abdominal
infection model in mice. We diluted a bacterial suspension of E. f. (1
× 107) and VRE (5 × 107) in a 5 wt % yeast solution and injected 500
μL of the mixed bacteria solution into the peritoneal cavity of the
mice to build the acute peritonitis model. Mice were divided into 6
groups (n = 25). For the E. f. infection, we injected Au44(MBA)18 (10
mg/kg) into the abdominal cavity of the mice, setting Van (10 mg/
kg) as a positive control. For the VRE infection, we injected
Au44(MBA)18 (20 mg/kg) and the same concentration of Van to
evaluate the survival conditions of the mice. We recorded the body
weight and observed the states of the mice for 72 h to evaluate their
survival. At different time intervals (12, 24, and 48 h), we euthanized
5 mice of each group randomly and dissected the organs. We ground
the organs (the lung, spleen, and kidney) in PBS and spread the
diluted solution on LB agar plates to count the number of the bacteria
in the tissues. The organs were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde
(PFA) for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, sectioned for histological
examination, and subjected to HE staining. We collected blood at
different time points (24, 48, and 72 h) to assess the functions of the
liver, kidney, and immune system and performed blood routine
analysis.

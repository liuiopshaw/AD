A B S T R A C T
The development of cotton fabrics with multifunctional properties such as superhydrophobicity, self-cleaning, 
and antibacterial activity without compromising intrinsic characteristics like flexibility, breathability, and 
comfort remains a significant challenge. In this study, a multifunctional superhydrophobic cotton fabric with 
amplified antimicrobial activity was fabricated by integrating multiple bactericidal mechanisms into a single 
platform. The process involved the deposition of copper-doped mesoporous silica nanoparticles (CuMSNs) onto 
cotton fabrics (CF), followed by in situ synthesis and deposition of copper stearate (CuSA) to impart durable 
superhydrophobicity. The incorporation of copper in two forms, embedded within the mesoporous structure for 
sustained release and long-term efficacy, and as copper stearate for immediate contact killing, produces a 
complementary dual-action effect that enhances both antibacterial performance and durability. Surface char­
acterization by scanning electron microscopy, elemental analysis, and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy 
confirmed the successful surface modifications. The modified cotton exhibited excellent superhydrophobicity 
with a water contact angle of 152◦and a sliding angle of 7◦, along with outstanding antibacterial activity, 
achieving 99.99% bacterial reduction against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains after 24 h. Self- 
cleaning and air permeability tests further demonstrated the material's suitability for practical and protective 
applications. This multifunctional coating offers sustained antibacterial performance and robust durability, 
making it highly promising for advanced medical textiles and protective fabrics.
1. Introduction
Cotton fabrics (CF) occupy a vital position in both daily life and in­
dustrial applications due to their softness, comfort, renewability, eco- 
friendly nature, and excellent air-permeability and breathability [1,2]. 
Among their various uses, one of the most significant is in the production 
of medical textiles, including hospital garments, bed linens, and staff 
uniforms. The increasing incidence of hospital-acquired infections 
(HAIs), which develop after a patient's admission to healthcare facilities 
such as hospitals and nursing homes, imposes a substantial social and 
economic burden on individuals and healthcare systems [3]. Chemi­
cally, cellulose is the primary component of cotton fibers, and the 
abundance of hydroxyl groups makes the fabric surface highly hydro­
philic. Cotton can easily absorb water and other liquids, which makes it 
susceptible to staining and the accumulation of contaminants [4]. 
Moreover, this hydrophilic and porous nature, combined with its ability 
to retain moisture, allows cotton-based hospital textiles to act as reser­
voirs for pathogenic microorganisms, thereby promoting the growth and 
transmission of bacteria and fungi [5,6]. Consequently, there is a 
growing demand for developing advanced cotton fabrics with durable 
antibacterial and self-cleaning properties to enhance hygiene and safety 
in medical environments.
To mitigate these limitations, researchers have focused on devel­
oping CF endowed with antibacterial and self-cleaning properties [7]. 
Inspired by nature, surfaces exhibiting a water contact angle (WCA) 
above 150◦and a sliding angle (SA) below 10◦demonstrate excellent 
self-cleaning efficiency by repelling water and contaminants [8,9]. 
Achieving such superhydrophobicity typically requires the introduction 
of hierarchical micro−/nanostructures combined with low-surface- 
energy compounds [10]. Several modification techniques, including 
sol–gel [11], dip [12] and spin coating [13], chemical vapor deposition 
[14], and plasma treatment [15], have been explored, among which dip-

Stearic acid, a saturated fatty acid containing a long C18 hydrocar­
bon chain, plays a critical role in reducing the surface free energy of 
solid materials when combined with hierarchical micro/nano-structured 
substrates. Its nonpolar tail forms a densely packed hydrophobic layer 
through self-assembly, effectively repelling polar liquids such as water 
and limiting surface wettability. When applied to roughened cotton 
substrates, stearic acid facilitates the formation of a Cassie–Baxter 
wetting state, in which air pockets are trapped between the liquid 
droplet and the surface asperities, thereby minimizing the actual solid­
–liquid contact area and significantly lowering adhesion of water or 
contaminants [17–19]. Consequently, contact angles exceeding 150◦
can be achieved, conferring superhydrophobic and self-cleaning prop­
erties that prevent both moisture penetration and microbial coloniza­
tion. Previous studies have reported that trilayer coatings incorporating 
stearic acid on CF exhibit durable water repellency along with decreased 
bacterial adherence, even after repeated washing cycles [20]. These 
findings highlight the synergistic influence of hierarchical surface ar­
chitecture and hydrophobic surface chemistry in achieving dual func­
tionality, superhydrophobicity, and antibacterial resistance in treated 
cotton materials. It should be noted that, however, superhydrophobicity 
alone has thresholds; at high bacterial inoculate, repellency can fail due 
to increased adhesion forces, underscoring the need to integrate biocidal 
agents for comprehensive protection [21].
To achieve a significant enhancement in the antibacterial perfor­
mance, the introduction of metal particles proves to be highly effective. 
Among applied metal ions as antibacterial agents (silver (Ag), copper 
(Cu), zinc (Zn), titanium (Ti), and gold (Au) and their metallic oxides), 
Cu and Cu-based materials have shown great potential in efficiently 
killing both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria [22]. When the 
materials release Cu ions, it triggers the production of excessive Reactive 
Oxygen Species (ROS), which leads to cell damage and oxidative stress 
in the bacteria, ultimately causing their death. Furthermore, when in 
direct contact with bacteria, they can also destroy the bacterial cell 
membrane [12,23]. To achieve prolonged efficacy, mesoporous silica 
nanoparticles (MSNs) serve as ideal reservoirs for controlled release of 
copper ions or nanoparticles, enabling sustained antimicrobial action 
[24,25]. With high surface areas and tunable pores, MSNs can be 
functionalized for hydrophobic modification while encapsulating 
metals, allowing simultaneous repellency and programmed release 
[24,26]. Studies on modified mesoporous silica demonstrate altered 
release kinetics through pore size, surface chemistry, and topology ad­
justments, enhancing antibacterial effects against model bacteria 
[25,27,28]. The combined effects of stearic acid induce repellency and 
surface roughness on one hand, and contact-mediated bacterial inacti­
vation from copper enrichment, and sustained release activity from 
mesoporous carriers on the other hand, result in additive or even syn­
ergistic antibacterial efficiency [29]. Moreover, such hierarchical sur­
face decoration on CF demonstrates excellent mechanical and chemical 
durability, maintaining both superhydrophobicity and antimicrobial 
function after repeated washing, mechanical abrasion, and exposure to 
solvents or variable pH conditions [30,31]. The tunability of these 
coatings through controlled metal ion loading, adjustable pore archi­
tecture, and responsive gating mechanisms enable precise regulation of 
antibacterial dosage and release duration. This versatility renders them 
highly suitable for applications in medical textiles, protective clothing, 
and self-cleaning functional surfaces [26,32].
This study presents a novel strategy for developing a multifunctional 
superhydrophobic and antibacterial coating on CF by integrating mul­
tiple bactericidal mechanisms within a single platform. The process 
begins with the deposition of MSNs onto the CF surface, where copper 
ions are incorporated into the silica framework, while (3-aminopropyl) 
triethoxysilane (APTES) is employed as a coupling agent to strengthen 
surface binding. Subsequently, the fabric surface is coated with copper 
stearate (CuSA), synthesized in situ via the reaction between copper ions 
and stearic acid, to impart durable superhydrophobicity. Our super­
hydrophobic antibacterial coatings are expected to substantially reduce 
bacterial attachment by limiting protein adsorption and preventing 
initial bacterial adhesion. Surface chemistry, mediated through steric 
and electrostatic repulsion, along with surface morphology [5,6], is the 
primary mechanism responsible for decreasing the adhesion force be­
tween bacteria and the surface. Meanwhile, if bacteria come into contact 
with the surface or partially adhere to it, copper, chosen for its dual 
contact- and release-based antibacterial activity, is incorporated in two 
complementary forms. The first involves embedding copper ions as 
network modifiers within mesoporous silica nanoparticles (Cu-MSNs) to 
enable controlled ion release, while the second entails in situ formation 
and deposition of CuSA on cotton fibers for direct contact killing. This 
dual incorporation is expected to amplify the durability and overall ef­
ficacy of antibacterial activity. Collectively, this multifunctional coating 
offers tunable antibacterial efficacy and adjustable durability, making it 
suitable for diverse applications requiring long-lasting and efficient 
antimicrobial protection.
2. Experimental
2.1. Materials and reagents
Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, ≥98%), tetraethyl 
orthosilicate (TEOS, ≥98%), ammonia solution (NH₃, 28%), ethyl ace­
tate (EA, ≥99.8%), copper (II) chloride dihydrate (CuCl₂⋅2H₂O, 99%), 
stearic acid (SA, 95%), ethanol (EtOH, 99.5%) and (3-aminopropyl) 
triethoxysilane (APTES, 99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and 
Scientific Thermo Fisher. All the materials were of analytical grade and 
used without additional purification. Cotton fabric (100%) was also 
purchased from Fabricville, Canada and used as the cotton fabric 
substrate.
2.2. Material synthesis
2.2.1. Synthesis of CuMSNs by microemulsion-assisted sol-gel method
Following established protocols with slight modifications, CuMSNs 
were synthesized using a traditional microemulsion-assisted sol-gel 
approach [27]. A binary SiO₂-CuO system was created using EA, CTAB, 
and water-based microemulsion droplets as a soft template. Initially, 
CTAB (0.56 g) was dissolved in 26 mL of deionized water under 
continuous stirring to form a clear solution. EA (8 mL) was then intro­
duced to initiate droplet formation. After 30 min, ammonium hydroxide 
(28%) was gradually added to raise the pH to approximately 10.5. 
Fifteen minutes later, TEOS (3 mL) and CuCl2⋅2H2O (30 mg) were added 
to the mixture every 30 min. Following 4 h of mixing, a blue precipitate 
was obtained, collected via centrifugation, and washed three times with 
water and ethanol. The product was then dried, and the organic tem­
plate and nitrates were eliminated through calcination at 680 ◦C for 6 h, 
using a heating rate of 2 K/min (Scheme 1a).
2.2.2. Preparation of modified CF with CuMSNs and SA (CF@CuMSN- 
CuSA)
To increase the availability of hydroxyl groups on the fabric surface, 
the CF were pretreated as follows. First, the fabrics were soaked in 
deionized water for 5–10 min. They were then immersed in a water bath 
at 90–100 ◦C containing Tween 20 (2 g/L) and sodium hydroxide 
(NaOH, 10 g/L) for 30–60 min. After treatment, the samples were rinsed 
thoroughly, first with hot water and then with cold water until the pH of 
the rinsing solution was neutral. Finally, all fabrics were dried at 60 ◦C 
[33]. The multifunctional superhydrophobic cotton fabric was prepared 
using a two-step process. In the first step, CuMSNs were coated onto 
prewashed CF using APTES as a coupling agent. Specifically, 150 mg of 
CuMSNs were dispersed in 30 mL of deionized water via sonication. A 4 
× 4 cm2 piece of cotton fabric was then immersed in the dispersion and 
kept at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, 1 mL of APTES was

2.3. Testing and characterization
2.3.1. Material Characterization
The surface morphology of CF subjected to various treatments was 
examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) equipped with 
energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (JSM-6480 LV SEM, JEOL, 
Japan) operating at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. Elemental 
composition was analyzed with an EDAX silicon drift EDS detector. 
Functional group changes in the treated fabrics were identified using 
Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Cary 630, Agilent, USA) 
in attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode, within the spectral range of 
400–4000 cm−1. The static water contact angle (WCA) and sliding angle 
(SA) were measured using a Krüss™ DSA100 goniometer (KRÜSS Sci­
entific, Hamburg, Germany) at 25 ± 0.5 ◦C. A 4 μL droplet of demin­
eralized water was placed on the fabric surface, and the WCA was 
calculated using Advanced drop shape analysis software (version 
1.16.0). The Kruss system was equipped with a tilting stage to measure 
SAs. A 35 μL water droplet was utilized to ensure a sufficient gravita­
tional component to overcome localized pinning and contact angle 
hysteresis inherent in the hierarchical cotton surface [34,35]. The stage 
was tilted at a constant rate of 60◦/min up to a maximum inclination of 
60◦.The SA was recorded as the stage angle exactly 0.5 s before the onset 
of droplet movement. Measuring the size and distribution of CuMSNs 
were evaluated by analyzing the diameters of 100 particles from SEM 
images using ImageJ software. The crystalline structure of CuMSNs was 
analyzed by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) using an Equinox 3000 
diffractometer with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.541874 Å), oper­
ating at 40 kV and 30 mA, over a 2θ range of 20◦to 80◦. Surface 
roughness was measured using an optical profilometer (Profilm3D 
Filmetrics®, USA) with white light interferometry (WLI) to measure 
surface profiles. For each sample, six randomly selected locations were 
assessed.
2.3.2. Air permeability
The air permeability of both coated and uncoated fabrics was eval­
uated using an air permeability tester in accordance with ISO 9237. 
Specimens were prepared with a test area of 6 cm2 and conditioned by 
drying at 60 ◦C for 24 h prior to measurement. The instrument test head 
provided a circular test area in which air was passed through the fabrics 
under a pressure differential of 100 Pa. The air permeability was 
recorded directly from the instrument output [36].
2.3.3. Water vapor transmission rate (WVTR)
The WVTR of coated and uncoated CF was determined in accordance 
with ASTM E96/E96M-22 (The Water Method). Circular fabric speci­
mens were conditioned at 21 ± 1 ◦C and 65 ± 2% RH for 24 h prior to 
testing. The specimens were sealed onto test dishes containing distilled 
water, ensuring an exposed test area. The assemblies were placed in an 
environmental chamber maintained at 21 ± 1 ◦C and 50 ± 2% RH, for 
24 h. The dishes were weighed at the start and end of the test to 
determine the mass gain due to water vapor transmission. Each mea­
surement was performed in triplicate, and results are reported as mean 
± standard deviation.
The WVTR (g⋅m−2⋅day−1) was calculated as: 
WVTR = 24M/At
(1) 
where, M is the mass gain during the test period (g), t is duration of the 
test (h) and A is exposed test area of the fabric (m2) [37,38].

The surface protection properties were evaluated under controlled 
laboratory conditions (21 ± 1 ◦C, 65 ± 2% relative humidity). Square

for each group) were used. To assess liquid repellency and antifouling, 
droplets of green-dyed water, tea, milk, and juice were applied to the 
fabric surface. Additionally, a buoyancy test was conducted by placing 
specimens in a beaker of deionized water to observe floating/sinking

2.3.5. Antibacterial performance test
All microbiological dishes were sterilized using an autoclave at 
120 ◦C for 30 min. After sterilization, sterile nutrient agar medium was 
poured into the Petri dishes. The bacterial strains of Escherichia coli 
(E. coli, ATCC 25922) as Gram-negative and Bacillus cereus (B. cereus, 
ATCC 25923) as Gram-positive strains were selected. To assess the 
antibacterial performance of fabrics, two different methods were 
employed as described below:
2.3.5.1. Colony count formation test. The antibacterial properties of 
untreated and treated CF were evaluated using the ISO-
20,743 standard method. Test and control fabric samples were cut 
into 1 × 1 cm2 pieces (4 for each group) and sterilized using UV irra­
diation for 1 h on each side. In a biological safety cabinet, each fabric 
piece was placed in a sterile glass vial. Then, 200 μL of both bacterial 
solutions (1.5 × 105 CFU/mL) were inoculated onto each sample. For the 
initial (0-h) group, 10 mL of physiological saline solution containing 
sodium chloride and polysorbate 80 was added to each vial, vortexed for 
20 s, and 40 μL of the resulting solution was transferred to 360 μL of 
physiological saline. Serial dilutions (1–4) were prepared, and 100 μL of 
each was spread onto the agar plates, four plates per sample (agar plates 
for t = 0). The plates were incubated at 37 ◦C for 24 h, after which the 
bacterial colonies were counted. The vials were also incubated for 24 h 
and the above procedure was repeated to prepare 24-h groups. The 
antibacterial rate was respectively calculated using the following for­
mula equations: 
Antibacterial rate (%) = (Wt −Qt)/Wt × 100
(2) 
where Wt represents the initial number of bacteria (CFU/mL), and Qt 
represents the number of viable bacterial (CFU/mL) after contact with 
the treated cotton fabric for 24 h [41–43].
2.3.5.2. Bacteria adhesion assay. An increase in surface hydrophobicity 
reduces the contact area between a contaminated droplet and a surface. 
The sliding angle reflects the ability of a (contaminated) droplet to roll 
off an inclined surface, which can help minimize or prevent bacterial 
adhesion. To evaluate this property, according to the previous literature 
[44], we assessed the extent of bacterial adhesion to the cotton surfaces 
after exposure to bacterial droplets. If bacteria remained on the surface, 
it was considered contaminated; if the droplet rolled off completely, the 
surface was deemed uncontaminated. For the experiment, 100 μL of 
bacterial suspension was applied to fabric samples positioned horizon­
tally. After remaining in this position for 5 min, the samples were tilted 
at an angle of 45◦(or 90◦for non-hydrophobic fabrics). The fabrics were 
then incubated in nutrient broth tube, and bacterial growth was evalu­
ated after 24 h. To assess contamination, 100 μL from each tube was 
extracted and analyzed for optical density (OD), which quantifies light 
scattering caused by bacterial presence. An increase in OD corresponds 
to higher bacterial concentration. Measurements were taken using a 
Gensys 20 spectrophotometer.
2.3.6. Durability
The washing durability and mechanical stability of CF@CuMSN- 
CuSA was evaluated about their antibacterial performance and water 
repellency. Washing resistance was tested in accordance with a modified 
AATCC 61-1A method. Fabric samples were laundered in a 2% sodium 
carbonate and 5.0 g/L standard soap solution (liquor ratio 1:50) at 40 ◦C 
for 30 min per cycle, with evaluations after 5, 10, 15, 20 and 50 cycles 
[45]. After each washing stage, samples were rinsed thoroughly with 
distilled water, air-dried, and conditioned prior to testing. Mechanical 
durability was assessed by abrasion on P280 sandpaper under a 25 g 
load. At each durability checkpoint, antibacterial activity and WCA of 
CF@CuMSN-CuSA was measured.
2.3.7. Ion release study
The time-dependent copper ion release behavior of the functional­
ized CF was investigated by measuring the concentration of copper ions 
release into the laundering water. The prepared samples were immersed 
in deionized water at 40 ◦C under constant orbital shaking to simulate 
standardised laundering conditions. To mimic skin contact, the release 
behavior was further assessed in an acidic artificial sweat solution (pH 
5.5), prepared according to ISO 105-E04, at 37 ◦C. At specific time in­
tervals (1, 3, 7, 12, 24, 48, 72, 120, and 168 h), the release media was 
collected and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh 
media. All the aliquots were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min and 
filtered through a 0.22 mm membrane. The collected aliquots were then 
diluted using nitric acid solutions (2 v/v%) and analyzed by ICP-OES to 
determine the concentration of Cu. The release values were calculated 
by accounting for the mass removed during each sampling interval. All 
the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (n = 3) 
[46,47].
2.3.8. Statistical analysis
The data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Sta­
tistical evaluation was conducted using one-way or two-way analysis of 
variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test, using GraphPad 
Prism version 8.4.3. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statis­
tically significant.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Fabrication process and characterization
The CF@CuMSN-CuSA was fabricated through initial deposition of 
CuMSNs onto CF followed by in situ formation of CuSA. First, the 
nanoparticles were prepared using a microemulsion-assisted sol–gel 
method with copper ions incorporated as network modifiers, followed 
by comprehensive characterization. The FTIR spectrum of CuMSNs 
exhibited characteristic silicate bands at approximately 450 and 800 
cm−1, corresponding to Si–O–Si bending and symmetric stretching vi­
brations, respectively (Fig. S1a). A weak shoulder observed at ~950 
cm−1 was attributed to the stretching vibration of Si–O–NBO (non- 
bridging oxygen bonds) [27]. The amorphous nature of CuMSNs was 
confirmed by its XRD pattern, which displayed a single broad peak 
centered at 2θ = 23◦(Fig. S1b). SEM analysis (Fig. 1a) revealed that the 
CuMSNs possessed a spherical morphology with an average diameter of 
185 ± 25 nm. EDS analysis (Fig. S1c) verified the successful incorpo­
ration of Cu ions into the silicate framework, corresponding to 2 wt% Cu 
loading. Elemental mapping further demonstrated the uniform distri­
bution of Si, Cu, and O throughout the nanoparticles (Fig. 1b). TEM 
imaging revealed the radial mesostructured of the synthesized CuMSNs 
(Fig. 1c). The use of mesoporous nanoparticles was an integral part of 
the architectural design to promote sustained copper ion release, 
thereby improving the long-term durability of the antibacterial perfor­
mance. To further investigate the porosity of the CuMSNs, BET/BJH 
measurements were conducted. As shown in Fig. S1d, the N2 adsorption- 
desorption measurements exhibit a Type IV isotherm with a character­
istic hysteresis loop, confirming a mesoporous structure. The calculated 
BET specific surface area was found to be 338 m2/g. Furthermore, the 
BJH pore size distribution curve shows a narrow peak centered at 
approximately 3.69 nm, providing quantitative evidence of the meso­
pores (Fig. S1e).

The synthesized nanoparticles were subsequently applied to the 
surface of cotton fabric (CF). Fig. 1d1-3 present the SEM images of

evident natural twist. To evaluate the influence of silica nanoparticles on

was immersed in a copper and SA solution. SEM images (Fig. 1g1-3) show 
the formation of interwoven micro-nano sheets that grew across the 
fabric surface, significantly increasing surface roughness. The develop­
ment of this hierarchical architecture is attributed to the in-situ forma­
tion of copper stearate (CuSA), which utilizes the hydroxyl groups of the 
CuMSN nanoparticles as active nucleation sites. As shown in Fig. 1f4, the 
initial deposition of spherical CuMSNs provides a base layer of nanoscale 
roughness. Upon subsequent functionalization, these nanoparticles act 
as templates for the growth of an interwoven network of micro-nano

and 4 μm, while the thickness remains consistently in the nanoscale 
range (300 ± 80 nm). This hierarchical, multi-scale morphology is 
essential for surface superhydrophobicity, confirming the critical role of 
the CuMSN nanoparticles as nucleation seeds for the engineered 
topography. The formation of these multi-scale structures could not be 
observed in the absence of nanoparticles.

The FTIR analysis was applied to evaluate the changes in the cotton 
surface chemical groups in each step, as shown in Fig. 3a. The CF dis­
played distinct O–H stretching vibrations near 3300–3500 cm−1 and 
C–O–C stretching at around 1030 cm−1, characteristic of cellulose, in 
agreement with previous reports on raw cotton [49]. After modification 
of CF with copper and SA (CF@CuSA), the FTIR spectrum exhibited two 
weak bands corresponded the aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations of SA 
in the region of 2800–3000 cm−1. The stretching vibration band of C––O 
of SA showed a shift from 1690 cm−1 to 1640 cm−1 [50]. It could be 
related to its interaction with copper ions [51], demonstrating the suc­
cessful formation of CuSA on the cotton surface. However, the weak 
intensity of the absorption bands suggests that the surface loading is not 
high. Decoration of CF with CuMSNs introduces a strong Si–O–Si 
stretching band around 1000–1100 cm−1, which might be overlapped 
with the C–O–C stretching of CF in the same region. The significant 
reduction of the O–H groups of CF could be assigned to the more in­
teractions between nanoparticles and hydroxyl groups of cellulose 
compared to the CuSA alone [52]. The spectrum of CF@CuMSN also 
showed a distinct peak around 790 cm−1, which could be attributed to 
the Si–O–Cu stretching vibrations associated with the copper-silicate 
structures [27]. The FTIR spectrum of CF@CuMSN-CuSA exhibits the 
combined features of both the CuMSNs and CuSA modifications. 
Notably, the broad absorption band of hydroxyl groups of CF is sub­
stantially diminished, which is consistent with extensive interactions 
between the native cellulose hydroxyl groups and the silanol groups 
from Cu-MSN, as well as the different chemical groups in APTES and 
CuSA during successive surface modification steps. Sharp aliphatic C–H 
stretching bands appear in the 2800–3000 cm−1 region, attributed to the 
incorporation of long-chain fatty acid moieties of SA. Importantly, the 
carbonyl band is shifted to approximately 1600 cm−1 which could be 
related to the formation of new amide bond between amine groups of 
APTES and carboxyl groups of stearic acid and may also coordination of 
carboxylate groups with Cu ions and copper stearate formation. More­
over, the presence of the Si–O–Cu vibration, evidenced by an absorption 
band near 800 cm−1, supports the presence of CuMSNs. Together, these 
spectral features confirm the stepwise and effective surface modification 
of the cotton surface with CuMSNs and CuSA as well, resulting in a fabric 
with integrated superhydrophobic and antibacterial properties.
The crystalline structure and phase composition of the modified 
cotton fabrics were investigated using XRD (Fig. 3b). The diffraction 
pattern of the pristine CF exhibits characteristic peaks of Cellulose at

(200), and (004) crystalline planes, respectively [53]. Upon the depo­
sition of CuMSN, the characteristic cellulose peaks remain dominant, 
indicating that the bulk crystalline structure of the cotton fibers was 
preserved. Notably, a diffraction peak appears at low angles (2theta <
5◦), indexed as the (100) reflection, which confirms the presence of an 
ordered mesoporous silica framework associated with the CuMSN 
modification [54]. The final CF@CuMSN-CuSA composite shows the 
successful in situ formation of the copper stearate complex. The dif­
fractogram reveals a series of intense, sharp, and equidistant diffraction 
peaks at low 2theta values (2theta = 3.8◦, 5.7◦, 7.6◦, and 9.5◦), indexed 
as the (001), (002), (003), and (004) planes of crystalline CuSA. These 
reflections are indicative of a highly ordered lamellar structure, typical 
of long-chain metal carboxylates [55]. The XRD results and SEM ob­
servations reinforce one another, offering multi-scale validation of the

3.2. Surface chemistry and water repellency
Fig. 4a and Table 1 present the images of the pristine CF after each 
modification step, showing both the visual appearance changes and the 
corresponding WCA and SA measurements. These measurements were 
performed to evaluate the wettability evolution of the fabric surface 
through the multi-step functionalization process. The surface of pristine 
CF was completely wetted due to the abundance of hydrophilic –OH 
groups in cellulose, resulting in a WCA of around 0◦. Functionalization 
with CuMSNs alone temporarily increased the water contact angle 
(WCA) to approximately 126◦, likely due to nanoparticle-induced sur­
face roughness. Droplet penetration occurred rapidly, indicating poor 
water repellency stability. This fast absorption can be attributed to the 
hydrophilic amine and hydroxyl functionalities of APTES-modified 
MSNs present on the surface.
Upon coating CF@CuMSN with CuSA, the surface exhibited super­
hydrophobic behavior, characterized by a water contact angle (WCA) of 
152◦and a sliding angle (SA) of 7◦. This result highlights the crucial role 
of the initial coating with CuMSNs layer in providing a suitable foun­
dation for subsequent CuSA deposition. As discussed earlier, the 
CuMSNs may introduce multiple active sites that facilitate new chemical 
interactions and enhance surface roughness. In contrast, when CF was 
modified directly with CuSA without the presence of CuMSNs, the WCA 
increased only to 134◦, and the SA decreased to 16◦, indicating a less 
hydrophobic surface. Therefore, the hierarchical structure formed by 
sequential CuMSNs and CuSA modification is essential because CuMSNs 
act as a foundation and contribute to nanoscale roughness, while the 
CuSA coating provides a low-surface-energy layer. The synergistic effect 
of these two factors results in a stable and significantly enhanced 
superhydrophobic surface. According to the Cassie–Baxter model, such 
hierarchical structures trap air pockets between the liquid droplet and 
the solid surface, minimizing the solid–liquid contact area and thereby 
increasing the contact angle. Moreover, the incorporation of organo­
silane groups (from APTES) and metallic nanoparticles (Cu-based) pro­
vides chemical heterogeneity that facilitates robust anchoring of 
hydrophobic layers [58]. Therefore, the dual effect of topographical 
modification and chemical functionalization plays a vital role in 
achieving durable superhydrophobic performance in CF@CuMSN-CuSA 
composites.
We can go deeper on the journey of molecules during these surface 
modifications. Fig. 4b showed the possible interactions between 
different molecules during this journey. The pristine cotton fabric is 
hydrophilic due to abundant OH groups. CuMSNs were anchored at the 
fabric surface in the presence of APTES as a crosslinker [59]. The ethoxy 
groups of APTES are hydrolyzed to form hydroxyl groups, which interact 
with the silanol groups of the CuMSNs and the surface of the fabric. 
During high temperature curing at 100 ◦C, condensation reactions 
occur, promoting strong interfacial bonding. Additionally, CuMSNs 
undergo direct condensation and become chemically anchored to the CF 
surface, thereby enhancing the overall durability of the coating. Sub­
sequently, the superhydrophobic fabric was fabricated by treating the 
previously modified cotton fabric with SA, using the dip-coating 
method. SA can interact with hydroxyl and amine functional groups 
through various interactions. The hydroxyl groups present on CuMSNs 
can form hydrogen bonds with the silanol and amine groups of hydro­
lyzed APTES [60]. In addition, APTES can attach to the fabric surface via 
free silanol or amine groups, enabling multiple bonding pathways 
among APTES, CuMSNs, and the cellulosic structure of pristine CF. Upon 
SA treatment, the carboxyl groups of SA may form new amide bonds 
with the amine groups of APTES, coordinate with copper ions, and 
interact with free hydroxyl groups on the cellulose structure and 
CuMSNs.

3.4. Antifouling, stain resistance, and self-cleaning performance
Hydrophilic cotton fabrics readily absorb water and attract con­
taminants such as dust and pollutants. Recent studies have shown that 
inducing superhydrophobic characteristics in cotton not only signifi­
cantly reduces water uptake but also prevents the adhesion of fouling 
agents and microorganisms, consequently enhancing both antifouling 
and self-cleaning capabilities [64]. The antifouling performance of CF 
and CF@CuMSN-CuSA was assessed by applying various liquids (green- 
dyed water, juice, milk, and tea) onto the fabric. As illustrated in Fig. 6a, 
CF@CuMSN-CuSA demonstrated strong antifouling properties. While 
the untreated CF was instantly penetrated by all the liquids, droplets on 
CF@CuMSN-CuSA maintained a spherical shape, indicating excellent 
liquid repellency and resistance to foulants. Further evaluation of anti­
fouling behavior was conducted under continuous liquid contact, as 
shown in Fig. 6c and Movies S1 and S2. When placed separately in a 
beaker of water, the untreated CF quickly sank, whereas CF@CuMSN- 
CuSA remained afloat on the water surface, confirming its enhanced 
antifouling capability.
Self-cleaning and dirt-repellent features are critical for super­
hydrophobic fabrics, as they help prevent surface contamination. To 
assess this property, CF and CF@CuMSN-CuSA were tested using soil 
and blue dye as representative pollutants (Fig. 6 b and d). As shown in 
Fig. 6b and Movie S3 and S4, when exposed to soil powder, CF@CuMSN- 
CuSA easily sheds the particles upon rinsing with water, leaving the 
fabric clean and intact. In contrast, untreated CF failed to remove the soil 
effectively, as the particles adhered firmly to the surface and penetrated 
the fabric. The evaluation with dye further confirmed this trend 
(Fig. 6d). When dye powder was deposited onto the fabrics, the subse­
quent addition of deionized water dissolved the dye. On CF@CuMSN- 
CuSA, the rolling water droplets quickly carried the dye solution 
away, preserving the fabric's original appearance due to its micro/ 
nanostructures and low surface energy. Conversely, CF retained a visible 
blue stain even after repeated rinsing, indicating poor self-cleaning 
ability. Overall, these findings demonstrate the outstanding multifunc­
tional protective performance of CF@CuMSN-CuSA, emphasizing its 
suitability for applications where maintaining cleanliness is essential.

hierarchical CuMSN-CuSA coating acts as a highly stable reservoir, of­

reduction in performance and continues to deliver effective protection.

more, the inherent tunability of our architecture, along with the ca­
pacity to load different cargos into the mesoporous carrier, combined 
with its green, fluorine-free synthesis, offers a significant advantage over 
conventional platforms. Overall, this multifunctional coating provides 
an optimal balance between environmental sustainability and long-term 
functional stability, while offering tunable antibacterial performance 
and adjustable durability, making it a promising candidate for medical 
textiles and protective fabrics.

4. Conclusion
This study successfully demonstrates the fabrication of multifunc­
tional cotton fabrics that unite superhydrophobicity, self-cleaning, and 
robust antibacterial activity while preserving key textile properties. The 
dual application of copper, both as a sustained-releasing agent within 
Cu-MSNs and as a superhydrophobic contact-active CuSA barrier, cre­
ates a complementary bactericidal mechanism, resulting in exceptional 
and durable antibacterial efficacy. The modified fabric achieves a WCA 
of 152◦and a sliding angle of 7◦, signifying high superhydrophobicity 
and strongly inhibiting bacterial adhesion and colonization on the 
textile surface. Even when bacteria did adhere, the dual-action mecha­
nism enabled a remarkable 99.99% bacterial reduction against both 
Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains. Crucially, owing to the deep 
structural integration of the active agents within the inter-fiber spaces, 
this potent efficacy and morphological stability were successfully 
maintained even after 50 rigorous laundering cycles and 200 severe 
mechanical abrasion cycles. Importantly, air permeability measure­
ments indicate only a minor (~5%) decrease in breathability after 
treatment, confirming the material's compatibility with everyday textile 
requirements. The combination of physiologically safe, controlled cop­
per ion release (strictly regulated below cytotoxicity thresholds) and 
direct contact-killing, alongside minimal compromise of comfort and 
breathability, positions this innovative coating as a promising candidate 
for advanced protective textile applications, particularly for short-term 
or indirect-contact scenarios. Future studies will expand this evalua­
tion to include fungal and other clinically relevant microbial species, as 
well as comprehensive in vitro and in vivo biosafety assessments 
required for prolonged, direct-contact medical applications (such as 
wound dressings), to further validate its versatility.

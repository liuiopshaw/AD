Smart Bactericidal Capsules Based on Cationic Luminescent
Nanoclusters for Controllable Treatment of Drug-Resistant
Bacterial Infection
Yixiao Li, Tianyi Wang, Jiaxin Zhang, Gleb B. Sukhorukov, Lianbing Zhang, Yumeng Xue,
and Li Shang*
Eﬀective treatment of drug-resistant bacteria infected wound has been a
longstanding challenge for healthcare systems. In particular, the development
of novel strategies for controllable delivery and smart release of antimicrobial
agents is greatly demanded. Herein, the design of biodegradable
microcapsules carrying bactericidal gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) as an
attractive platform for the eﬀective treatment of drug-resistant bacteria
infective wounds is reported. AuNC capsules are fabricated via the
well-controlled layer-by-layer strategy, which possess intrinsic near-infrared
ﬂuorescence and good biocompatibility. Importantly, these AuNC capsules
exhibit strong, speciﬁc antibacterial activity toward both S. aureus and
methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA). Further mechanistic studies by
ﬂuorescence confocal imaging and inductively coupled plasma mass
spectrometry reveal that these AuNC capsules will be degraded in the S.
aureus environment rather than E. coli, which then controllably release the
loaded cationic AuNCs to exert antibacterial eﬀect. Consequently, these AuNC
capsules show remarkable therapeutic eﬀect for the MRSA infected wound on
a mouse model, and intrinsic ﬂuorescence property of AuNC capsules enables
in situ visualization of wound dressings. This study suggests the great
potential of microcapsule-based platform as smart carriers of bactericidal
agents for the eﬀective treatment of drug-resistant bacterial infection as well
as other therapeutic purposes.

1. Introduction
The diseases and infections caused by
bacteria are seriously threatening human
health, especially severe infectious wound
can cause sepsis or even amputation.[1–4]
For a long time, antibiotics are the main
clinical choice to ﬁght against pathogenic
bacteria, but the abuse of them has led to the
appearance of drug-resistant bacteria.[5–7]
To solve this urgent problem, a variety
of nanomaterials have been developed as
a new generation of antibacterial agents
recently.[8–11] In particular, nanomaterials
with abundant positive charges on their
surfaces have been found to possess supe-
rior sterilization capability.[12–14] Not only
do these cationic nanoagents exhibit high
antibacterial activity due to strong elec-
trostatic interactions with bacterial mem-
branes, but they are also less likely to cause
bacterial drug-resistance owing to the in-
volvement of multiple bactericidal path-
ways. Consequently, these cationic nanoa-
gents are considered as promising alterna-
tives for the treatment of drug-resistant bac-
terial infection-related diseases. However,
there are still several challenges in the practical application of
these cationic nanomaterials. For example, the complicated bi-
ological environment around the infected wounds may easily
cause the aggregation of cationic nanomaterials due to strong
electrostatic interactions or the presence of harsh chemicals,
which can greatly impose their antibacterial activity and ther-
apeutic performance.[15,16] In addition, nonspeciﬁc, uncontrol-
lable exposure of cationic nanomaterials may also trigger toxic
side eﬀects on normal cells and healthy tissues.[17,18] Hence, it is
necessary to develop eﬃcient strategies that can not only enhance
the biological environmental stability of these cationic nanoa-
gents but also release them on demand in a controllable manner,
which is important to advance the further applications of these
bactericidal nanoagents.
Microcapsules prepared by the layer-by-layer self-assembly
method have proved to be an eﬀective means for delivering bioac-
tive molecules.[19–21] The structure of microcapsules can be cus-
tom designed depending on the application requirements, which
can be degraded under speciﬁc stimulus and release loaded

Gold nanoclusters (AuNCs), composed of a few to hundreds of
gold atoms, exhibit ultrasmall size (less than 2 nm), distinct ﬂu-

Recent studies showed that cationic AuNCs possess strong, broad
spectrum antibacterial activity, and less tendency to cause drug
resistance.[27–29] In the present work, we report the fabrication
of smart microcapsules loaded with cationic, near-infrared lumi-
nescent AuNCs, which possess good stability and exhibit speciﬁc
antimicrobial eﬀects toward S. aureus and methicillin-resistant
S. aureus (MRSA) in a controllable manner. Furthermore, in vivo
application of these smart AuNC capsules was demonstrated by a
mouse model of MRSA-infected wound. Our results suggest the
great potential of microcapsules for the delivery and controllable
release of antimicrobial cationic AuNCs as well as other nanoa-
gents toward advanced healthcare applications.
2. Results and Discussions
2.1. Synthesis and Characterization of AuNC Capsules
AuNC capsules were synthesized via the strategy illustrated in
Figure 1a. First, hyaluronic acid (HA) and cationic bovine serum
albumin AuNCs (cBSA-AuNCs) are alternately adsorbed onto the
surface of CaCO3 template, followed by introducing BSA and
tannic acid (TA) as the out-layer to provide the support for the
capsule structure.[31] Finally, CaCO3 templates are removed by
ethylenediamine tetra acetic acid (EDTA), yielding hollow AuNC
capsules. As shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information),
spherical CaCO3 templates with the size of 4.4 ± 0.8 μm were
ﬁrst synthesized via the well-established approach.[32] Fluores-
cent cBSA-AuNCs were prepared by chemically engineering the
surface of BSA-AuNCs with ethylenediamine dihydrochloride
(EDA), which can convert part of surface carboxyl groups of BSA
into amine groups (Figure S2, Supporting Information).[33,34] As
shown in Figure 1b, cBSA-AuNCs possess an absorption peak
at 280 nm originating from the template protein (BSA). Mean-
while, they possess strong red ﬂuorescence in the near-infrared
(NIR) spectral range with the emission peak at 665 nm, which is
promising for biological imaging.[28,35] The size of cBSA-AuNCs
is 1.83 ± 0.49 nm, as measured from transmission electron mi-
croscope (TEM) images. As the key bioactive components of
the AuNC capsules, the ultrasmall size of these cBSA-AuNCs
(<2 nm) endow them with highly active surface and distinct ﬂuo-
rescent properties. Moreover, the Zeta potential of cBSA-AuNCs
in aqueous solution is positive with a value of 15.1 ± 1.1 mV,
which enables their strong interactions with negatively charged
molecules (i.e., HA and BSA) based on electrostatic interactions
for driving the layer-by-layer assembly process.
Zeta potential measurement was employed to monitor the
preparation process of AuNC capsules. As shown in Figure S3
(Supporting Information). The CaCO3 template is positively
charged (13.8 ± 1.5 mV), which allows the assembly of negatively
charged HA during the ﬁrst step. Further assembly of diﬀerent
components, including cBSA-AuNCs and BSA, resulted in the al-
ternative change of the Zeta potential, indicating the successful
assembly driven by electrostatic interactions. The resulted AuNC
capsules are negatively charged (−13.8 ± 1.1 mV), which can en-
dow them with a good stability in the biological environment by
the synergistic eﬀects of surface charge and steric hinderance
from biomolecules on the surface layers. We note that the com-
position and layers of AuNC capsules have been optimized to en-
sure suﬃcient AuNC loading and good colloidal stability.
As shown in Figure 1c, as-prepared AuNC capsules possess
similar ﬂuorescence property as cBSA-AuNCs with the emission
peak at 665 nm, indicating the successful loading of AuNCs in the
capsules. Moreover, a characteristic absorption peak at 280 nm
in the absorption spectrum can be attributed to the presence of
BSA in the capsules. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) im-
ages showed that these AuNC capsules are hollow spheres as
expected (Figure 1d), also conﬁrming the complete dissolution
of the CaCO3 templates by EDTA. Note that the size of AuNC
capsules was measured to be 6.5 ± 1.7 μm (Figure 1e), which
is slightly larger than the CaCO3 template. The thickness of the
polymer layers in these capsules was calculated to be ≈1.1 μm.
Energy disperse spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping was
further employed to characterize the distribution of AuNCs in
the capsules. As shown in Figure 2a–e, the presence of Au as
well as other main elements indicates the eﬃcient and homo-
geneous distribution of cBSA-AuNCs inside the capsules. X-ray
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis revealed that the bind-
ing energies of Au 4f7/2 and Au 4f5/2 in cBSA-AuNCs are 84.4 and
88.0 eV, respectively, while those in AuNC capsules blueshift to
83.8 and 87.7 eV, respectively (Figure S4, Supporting Informa-
tion). This change is likely caused by the altered local environ-
ment of Au on the surface of cBSA-AuNCs upon interacting with
the nearby components in the capsules. Further measurement by
inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-
OES) revealed that the Au content in the as-prepared AuNC cap-
sules is 0.8%. Moreover, the intrinsic ﬂuorescence property of
AuNC capsules enables the direct visualization via ﬂuorescence
imaging. Indeed, as seen in Figure 2f, strong red ﬂuorescence
from well-dispersed AuNC capsules can be clearly observed. The
average size of these ﬂuorescent capsules was measured to be
6.5 ± 0.8 μm based on confocal images, which is consistent with
SEM results above.
Moreover, the hemocompatibility and cytotoxicity of AuNC
capsules were tested to evaluate their biocompatibility. As shown
in Figure 2g, the color of the supernatant in the presence of
AuNC capsules is transparent without obvious hemoglobin out-
ﬂow even till the concentration up to 2200 μg mL−1. Quanti-
tative analysis further showed negligible hemolysis (<2.0%) of
the tested AuNC capsule groups. The viability of L929 cells to-
ward AuNC capsules was evaluated by CCK-8 assay. As shown
in Figure 2h, with the concentration ranging from 0 to 2200 μg
mL−1, the cell viability is all higher than 80%, indicating a low
cytotoxicity. Taken the results together, these AuNC capsules
possess good biocompatibility and negligible leakage of loaded

2.2. Antibacterial Eﬀects and Mechanism of AuNC Capsules
To evaluate the antimicrobial activity of AuNC capsules, E. coli
(Gram-negative bacteria), S. aureus (Gram-positive bacteria), and
MRSA (drug-resistant species of S. aureus) were chosen the
model microbes. Herein, we note that AuNC capsules main-
tained good stability in the LB medium without aggregation or
degradation, as conﬁrmed by the confocal imaging experiments
(Figure S5, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure 3,
with the treatment of 275 μg mL−1 AuNC capsules to S. au-
reus and MRSA, the number of colonies signiﬁcantly decreased
by 50% compared with the control group. When further in-
creasing the concentration of AuNC capsules to 1100 μg mL−1,
the survival rate of bacteria decreased to less than 3%. The re-
sults suggest a remarkable antibacterial eﬀect of AuNC cap-
sules against Gram-positive bacteria. In stark contrast, AuNC
capsules did not exhibit obvious antibacterial eﬀect against E.
coli, where the bacteria grew normally in the presence of AuNC
capsules. To further demonstrate the role of the cationic AuNCs
during the antibacterial process, the antibacterial eﬀects of cap-
sules without AuNCs were also examined. As shown in Figure S6
(Supporting Information), the survival rates of E. coli, S. aureus
and MRSA were 100%, 13.9%, and 12.3% under the treatment of
1100 μg mL−1 capsules without AuNCs, which are much higher

could only be observed from the capsules rather than the bac-

The release of AuNCs from the capsules was further conﬁrmed
by quantitatively analyzing the confocal images in Figure 4a–i. As

the concentration of Au in the supernatant of AuNC capsule-
bacterial debris mixture was measured by inductively coupled
plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). As shown in Figure 4k,
the concentrations of Au from the samples treated with S. aureus
and MRSA debris are much higher than that from E. coli debris,
and the release kinetics of AuNCs from capsules is very fast in
the ﬁrst 2 h. Importantly, without the external stimulation of bac-
teria, AuNC capsules can maintain a good stability, as supported
by a much lower concentration of Au from the negative control
(0.46 μg L−1) than that of the bacteria group. The diﬀerence of
the capsules’ degradation behaviors among diﬀerent bacteria are
likely stemming from the pH of diﬀerent bacterial microenvi-
ronments. The present AuNC capsules are mostly constructed
via electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding, which are
strongly inﬂuenced by their surrounding pH. E. coli is known to
produce acetic acid which could metabolically acidify their local
microenvironment.[41,42] However, the colonization of S. aureus
leads to a relatively alkaline microenvironment.[43,44] Control
experiments showed that the capsules would dissolve and release
AuNCs under the alkaline environment, as supported by the ﬂuo-
rescent signal from AuNCs in the supernatant of AuNC capsules
dispersed in alkaline solution (Figure S11, Supporting Informa-
tion). Also, S. aureus can secrete hyaluronidase to break down
HA in the capsules,[45] which can further damage the capsules’
structure and promote the release of cationic AuNCs as well
as TA.
Taken the results together, a possible antibacterial mechanism
of AuNC capsules is proposed in Figure 4l. Cationic AuNCs
loaded in the microcapsules can maintain good stability in com-
plex medium in the absence of external stimulation. When enter-
ing the bacterial environment, AuNC capsules would be speciﬁ-
cally degraded under the alkaline environment of Gram-positive
bacteria (S. aureus and MRSA) rather than Gram-negative bacte-
ria. Consequently, the structure of AuNC capsules would collapse
and the loaded AuNCs are released, which then annihilate the
bacteria through a contact-killing eﬀect via disrupting the mem-
brane integrity, as conﬁrmed by the SEM and confocal images.
Moreover, the released TA from degraded capsules can also exert
synergistic antibacterial eﬀect with cationic AuNCs.
2.4. In Vivo Treatment of MRSA-Infected Wound
Considering the distinct antibacterial property of AuNC capsules
toward MRSA, their in vivo therapeutic eﬀect on MRSA-infected
wound was investigated. Mice were randomly divided into con-
trol group and AuNC capsule-treated group. Round wounds were
made on the back of all mice and further infected by MRSA
(Figure 5a). The photographs of wounds were recorded at pre-
determined time after the treatment. As shown in Figure 5b,c,
the treated wounds manifested a faster closure on day 3 (35.7%)
than that of control group (23.0%), especially no excessive exu-
date was observed. After 14 days of treatment, the wound defect
was fully closed in AuNC capsule-treated group, while the defects
in the control group remained unhealed.
To evaluate the in vivo anti-infection ability of AuNC capsules,
bacteria from wound tissues on day 3 and day 7 were collected.
As shown in Figure 5d, the antibacterial eﬀect of AuNC capsules
was signiﬁcant, as the bacteria from the wound were almost
eliminated completely. Quantitative analysis revealed that the
survival rate of bacteria was reduced to 16.7% and 0.6% for the
AuNC capsule group on day 3 on day 7 (Figure 5e), respectively,
indicating strong in vivo antimicrobial eﬀect of AuNC capsules.
Moreover, given the intrinsic ﬂuorescence feature of AuNC
capsules, images of wound areas under UV lamp were recorded
at diﬀerent time points (Figure 5f). The images showed that red
ﬂuorescence of AuNC capsules could be clearly visualized at the
wound area, which gradually decreased with the consumption of
the materials. The ﬂuorescence signal almost disappeared when
the wound was scabby on day 3. The attractive ﬂuorescence
property of AuNC capsules can be harnessed to monitor the
remaining dosage of materials at wound sites, which can provide
important information during the wound healing process and en-
able the development of self-monitoring and bioimaging-guided
therapy.
As a typical inﬂammatory cytokine, immunoﬂuorescence
staining of TNF-𝛼and IL-6 at the wound bed was performed
(Figure 6). Compared with the control group, AuNC capsule-
treated group showed signiﬁcantly reduced the expression level
of TNF-𝛼and IL-6 on day 3 and day 7. The positive expression rate
of TNF-𝛼in the AuNC capsule-treated group was 39.3% on day
3 and further declined to 9.7% on day 7 (Figure 6b). Similarly,
the expression rate of IL-6 in the AuNC capsule-treated group
was 46.4% and 6.0% on day 3 and 7, respectively (Figure 6d).
These results indicate that AuNC capsules could accelerate the
wound healing process by eﬀectively shortening the inﬂamma-
tion phase.
Moreover, histological analysis of newly formed skin during
the wound healing process was carried out through hematoxylin
eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining to further vali-
date the extent of wound healing. After 21 days, intact epiderma
covered the wound sites in both control and AuNC capsule-
treated group. However, the tissue structure in AuNC capsule-
treated group is thinner and better deﬁned than that of the con-
trol group. Particularly, cutaneous appendages (hair follicles and
sweat glands) could be observed in the AuNC capsule-treated
group on day 21 (Figure 7a). This observation indicates that the
re-epithelialization and regeneration of skin were complete af-
ter 21 days. Quantitative analysis of the epidermal thickness fur-
ther veriﬁed that AuNC capsules eﬀectively promoted the for-
mation of granulation tissue and achieved epithelial crawling
(Figure 7b). The Masson’s trichrome staining results showed that
the arrangement of collagen ﬁbers in the AuNC capsule group
was more compact and ordered compared with the control group
(Figure 7c), which suggests that AuNC capsules can enhance the
collagen deposition. Herein, both H&E and Masson’s trichrome
staining results demonstrate promising therapeutic eﬀects of
AuNC capsules toward infectious wound by promoting the re-
epithelialization and collagen synthesis process.

4. Experimental Section
Reagents:
All chemicals used were analytical grade. Deionized water
(18.25 MΩ cm, Molecular Company) was used in all preparations. BSA,
gold (III) chloride trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O), and TA were from Sigma-
Aldrich (Milwaukee, USA). Calcium chloride (CaCl2), sodium carbonate
(Na2CO3), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-
ethyl carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), and EDA were from Aladdin
(Shanghai, China). HA was from Ourchem (Beijing, China). EEDTA was
from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Hoechst 33 342 was from In-
vitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc Company. Propidium iodide (PI) was
from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Company. Cell Counting
Kit-8 (CCK-8) was from Dojindo Molecular Technologies Company. The
strain of E. coli is BNCC 336 902, S. aureus is BNCC186335 and MRSA is
ATCC43300.
Characterization Instruments:
Fluorescence spectra were taken on a
FLS 980 spectroﬂuorometer (Edinburgh Instruments, UK). UV–vis absorp-
tion spectra were recorded with a U-3900H spectrophotometer (Hitachi,
Tokyo, Japan). TEM was performed on a Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN (FEI, Eind-
hoven, the Netherlands) equipped with a thermally assisted ﬁeld-emission
gun, which was operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. TEM sam-
ples were prepared by spraying a dispersion of AuNC capsules onto a Cu
grid covered by a holey carbon ﬁlm. Zeta potential was measured in water
solution by Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern, UK). XPS was carried out on an
Axis Ultra DLD XPS spectrometer (Kratos, Manchester, UK), using Al K𝛼
X-ray radiation (1486.6 eV) for excitation. All spectra were referenced to
the C1s peak at 284.8 eV. SEM images were taken from a NOVA450 SEM
(FEI, Eindhoven, the Netherlands), and samples were prepared by spray-
ing a dispersion of bacteria onto a Si slice. Confocal ﬂuorescence images
were taken by a SP8 ﬂuorescence confocal microscope (Leica, Germany).
ICP-OES was carried out on an Agilent 5110. ICP-MS was carried out on
an Agilent 7800.
Synthesis of Cationic BSA-Stabilized AuNCs (cBSA-AuNCs):
First,
BSA-stabilized AuNCs were synthesized according to the reported
procedures.[30] HAuCl4 solution (10 mm, 5 mL) was added to 5 mL BSA
solution (50 mg mL−1) under vigorous stirring at 37 °C. After 2 min, 0.5 mL
NaOH solution (1 m) was introduced and the mixture was stirred at 37 °C
for 12 h. Then the product was puriﬁed by triple centrifugation ﬁltration
with a molecular weight cut-oﬀ(MWCO) of 30 kDa (Millipore, Amicon,
Ultra-15) and redispersed in 5 mL water. To prepare cBSA-AuNCs, EDA
(2.5 m, 5 mL) and EDC (40 mg) were further added to the solution of
BSA-AuNCs (50 mg mL−1, 5 mL). The reaction solution was stirred at
room temperature for 2 h before puriﬁed by triple centrifugation ﬁltration
(MWCO: 30 kDa). The ﬁnal product was redispersed in water for later use.
Synthesis of AuNC Capsules:
The AuNC capsules were synthesized by
a layer-by-layer method over CaCO3 templates. CaCO3 cores were synthe-
sized by mixing an identical volume of 0.33 m CaCl2 and 0.33 m Na2CO3,
and stirring for 30 s. The formed CaCO3 particles were collected by cen-
trifugation (4500 rpm, 2 min) and redispersed in water. Then the solu-
tion of diﬀerent components (HA, cBSA-AuNCs, BSA, and TA) were added
in the CaCO3 solution and shaken for 10 min followed by the washing

Cell Culture and Cytotoxicity Assay:
The cytotoxicity of AuNC capsules
was assayed with mouse ﬁbroblast cells (L929). Cells were cultured in Dul-
becco’s modiﬁed eagle medium (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), supplemented
with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin and streptomycin under
37 °C and 5% CO2. The cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a cell den-
sity of 1.0 × 105 cells per well in 200 μL culture medium and incubated
for 24 h. Then the solutions of AuNC capsules with diﬀerent concentra-
tions were added to the 96-well plates and incubated for another 24 h. Six
wells with only complete medium were used as the blank control. Next the
medium was removed and 100 μL CCK-8 (5%) solution was added to each
well. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was read on a Microplate Reader
(Tecan) and analyzed. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.
Hemolysis Assay:
To further evaluate the biocompatibility of AuNC
capsules, hemocompatibility experiment was carried out. First, red blood
cells were separated from fresh mouse blood and washed with phosphate-
buﬀer saline (PBS) for four times via centrifugation at 4 °C (1200 rpm,
10 min). Subsequently, the cells were redispersed in PBS for further use.
0.5 mL cell dispersion was gently mixed with 0.5 mL AuNC capsules at dif-
ferent concentrations. Cells incubated with deionized water and PBS were
used as the positive control and negative control, respectively. All samples
were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min before centrifugation at 1200 rpm for
10 min. Then the absorbance of supernatants was detected at 540 nm by a
Microplate Reader. The hemolysis ratio of AuNC capsules was calculated
by the following formula
Hemolysis ratio = AS −AN
AP −AN
× 100%
(1)
where AS, AN, and AP represent the absorbance of the sample, the negative
control and the positive control, respectively.
In Vitro Antimicrobial Test of AuNC Capsules:
Both liquid and solid LB
culture medium were used for the antimicrobial test. Liquid LB culture
medium was prepared by mixing 10 g NaCl, 5 g yeast extract, 10 g tryptone,
and 1 L sterile water and adjusting pH to 7 by 1 m NaOH. The solid LB
culture medium needs another 1.5 g agar for 100 mL liquid. Initially, 10 μL
seed solution of bacteria was transferred to 5 mL liquid LB culture medium
and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Then the bacteria were collected and
redispersed in PBS for later use. Subsequently, 160 μL liquid LB medium,
20 μL AuNC capsules at predetermined concentration and 20 μL diluted
bacterial suspension (106 CFU mL−1) were mixed in the 96-well plate. The
solution was then incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. Three parallel samples were
set for each concentration and negative control samples were prepared
without adding AuNC capsules. Afterward, the solution was diluted 104
times and spread onto an agar plate. All the plates were incubated under
37 °C and collected after 18 h. The number of grown colonies was counted
for further quantitative analysis.

The samples for the ICP-MS measurement were prepared as follows.
First, 5 μL seed solution of bacteria was transferred to 5 mL liquid LB cul-
ture medium and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Then 100 μL fresh bacteria
solution was mixed with 50 mL LB medium for an additional 2 h incuba-
tion. Subsequently, all the bacteria were collected and redispersed in 5 mL
water for later ultrasonic pulverizing. The solution of AuNC capsules was
then added in the bacterial emulsion and incubated at 37 °C for diﬀer-
ent times. AuNC capsules dispersed in water with the same concentration
was used as the negative control. Afterward, the mixture was centrifuged
at 4500 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was taken for vacuum concen-
tration. The concentrated liquid (about 1 mL) was then digested by 1 mL
aqua regia (VHCl:VHNO3 = 3:1) and diluted to 6 mL by deionized water. To
evaluate the degradation of AuNC capsules in the bacteria environment,
the content of Au in the sample was measured by ICP-MS.
Live/Dead Bacteria Staining Experiment:
Hoechst 33 342 and PI were
used to stain all and dead bacteria, respectively. First, 160 μL liquid LB
medium, 20 μL AuNC capsules (11 mg mL−1), and 20 μL diluted bacterial
suspension (106 CFU mL−1) were mixed in 96-well plate, and incubated
at 37 °C for 6 h. Then the bacteria were isolated by centrifugation and
washed 2 times. Collected bacteria was incubated with Hoechst 33 342
and PI (both diluted with PBS, 1:1000) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark
before observed by confocal microscopy. The signal of Hoechst 33 342 was
excited by 405 nm and collected in the range of 425–470 nm. The signal of
PI was excited by 552 nm and collected in the range of 600–650 nm.
SEM Analysis:
The morphology of treated bacteria was observed by
SEM. Bacteria were incubated and collected similar as the live/dead bac-
teria staining experiment. Then the treated bacteria were washed by PBS
and ﬁxed by 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Next, the bacteria were
washed by PBS before dehydrated with diﬀerent concentrations of ethanol
(10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%). Then the bacterial dispersion
was dropped onto the silicon wafer and dried. Samples were subjected to
the gold-spray treatment before further observation.
In Vivo Treatment of the Infectious Wound:
Female Kunming mice (5–6
weeks, 18–20 g) purchased from Vital River Lab Animal Technology Co.,
Ltd. (Beijing, China) were used to construct the infectious wound model.
All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the animal
care and use guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of
Northwestern Polytechnical University (#202 201 127). The mice were
anesthetized and round surgical incisions were made on their back with
a diameter of 8 mm. Then, the wounds were covered with 100 μL of
bacterial suspension (108 CFU mL−1) of MRSA. After 24 h incubation, all
mice were randomly divided into 2 groups: control and AuNC capsules.
AuNC capsules were mixed with sodium alginate and applied to the
wound. The sodium alginate without capsules was used as the control.
Then the wound area was recorded on day 3, 7, 14, and 21 after the
treatment. The wound tissues were collected at diﬀerent times and ﬁxed
with 4% formaldehyde solution for H&E staining and Masson’s trichrome
staining. In addition, bacteria from the wound region were collected on
day 3 and 7, which were spread on agar plates and incubated at 37 °C
for 18 h to evaluate the in vivo antibacterial ability of AuNC capsules.
Moreover, wound tissue slices were treated with anti-TNF-𝛼antibody and
anti-interleukin 6 (IL-6) antibody, followed by staining with the correspond-
ing ﬂuorescence-labeled secondary antibodies and DAPI (cell nucleus
dye). The stained tissue sections were observed under confocal mi-
croscopy, and the relative ﬂuorescence intensity was quantiﬁed by ImageJ
software.
Statistical Analysis:
Each experiment was repeated at least three times
and the data were displayed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Diﬀer-
ences between groups were determined by unpaired two-tailed Student’s
t-test. Diﬀerences between groups were determined by t-test. Statistical
signiﬁcance was set as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01.

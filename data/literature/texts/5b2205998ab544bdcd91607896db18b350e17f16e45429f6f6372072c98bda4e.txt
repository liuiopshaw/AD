Embedding ultrasmall Ag nanoclusters in Luria-Bertani extract 
via light irradiation for enhanced antibacterial activity

ABSTRACT 
Ultrasmall silver nanoclusters (Ag NCs) with rich surface chemistry and good biocompatibility are promising in antibacterial 
application, however, further development of Ag NCs for practical settings has been constrained by their relatively weak 
antibacterial activity. Using the nutritionally-rich medium for bacteria (e.g., Luria-Bertani (LB) medium) to coat active Ag NCs could 
further improve their antibacterial activity. Here, we provide a delicate design of a highly efficient Ag NCs@ELB antibacterial agent 
(ELB denotes the extract of LB medium) by anchoring Ag NCs inside the ELB species via light irradiation. The as-designed Ag 
NCs with bacterium-favored nutrients on the surface can be easily swallowed by the bacteria, boosting the production of the 
intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS, about 2-fold of that in the pristine Ag NCs). Subsequently, a higher concentration of 
ROS generated in Ag NCs@ELB leads to enhanced antibacterial activity, and enables to reduce the colony forming units (CFU) of 
both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria with 3–4 orders of magnitude less than that treated with the pristine Ag NCs. In 
addition, the Ag NCs@ELB also shows good biocompatibility. This study suggests that surface engineering of active species (e.g., 
Ag NCs) with nutritionally-rich medium of the bacteria is an efficient way to improve their antibacterial activity. 
KEYWORDS 
Ag nanoclusters, LB broth, light irradiation, antibacterial agent, reactive oxygen species 
 
1 Introduction 
Silver (Ag) based materials have historically been applied to 
combat a wide-spectrum of bacteria due to their unique 
antibacterial and non-drug resistant attributes [1–6]. In the 
5th century BC, ancient Greeks had demonstrated that Ag 
container could be used to keep water fresh for years [2]. In 
1893, Swiss botanist Nageli experimentally confirmed that 
trace amount of Ag+ ions can kill bacteria [7]. Since then, 
Ag-containing compounds were used to treat many ailments, 
including burns, pneumonia, typhus, and wounds [1]. With 
the advent of the Nano era, various Ag nanoparticles with much 
better wide-spectrum antimicrobial efficiency than their bulk 
analogues have been developed and incorporated into many 
consumable products [1, 3, 5, 6]. More recently, in view of 
growing infestations of various superbugs, increasing research 
efforts have been devoted to the design of Ag-based nanomaterials 
as non-drug resistant antimicrobial agents [1–2, 8–10].   
Recently, ultrasmall metal nanoclusters (NCs for short; < 3 nm) 
with higher surface atoms, adjustable surface/metallic chemistry, 
and unique molecular-like properties [11–29], have emerged 
as a new type of functional materials in fields of catalysis  
[30–35], environment protection [36], biomedicine [37–39], etc. 
Interestingly, several types of Ag NCs have been successfully 
designed as wide-spectrum antibacterial agents with improved 
bacterial killing effect, and show promising progress in 
antibacterial application [8, 40–46]. For example, our group 
recently developed two strategies to fabricate Ag NC-based 
antibacterial agents for improving bacterial killing performance: 
(1) processing the oxidation states of the Ag core of Ag NCs 
from Ag0 to Ag+ [40], which endows both the intact Ag NCs 
with surface Ag+ ions and the dissociated Ag+ ions from Ag 
NCs with good antibacterial activity; and (2) grafting antibiotic 
molecules on the surface of Ag NCs [43], which makes possible 
synergistic antibacterial functions from the active compounds. 
However, several limitations of such Ag NC-based antibacterial 
agents, including low endocytosis efficiency, passive Ag+ ion 
release [10], and poor targeting to the bacteria [39, 47], remain 
unresolved. The limitations of Ag NC-based antibacterial agents 
have curtailed their further development in the antibacterial 
field [1, 48, 49].  
We hypothesized that if we can encapsulate Ag NC-based 
antibacterial agents inside a sacrificial nano-vehicle that could 
be preferably swallowed and digested by the bacteria, we might

improve the bacterial targeting and endocytosis efficiency of 
the antibacterial agents, further boosting their bacterial killing 
efficacy. Here we report the design of a highly efficient Ag 
NCs@ELB antibacterial agent (ELB denotes the extract of 
Luria-Bertani (LB) medium) by encapsulating the Ag NCs in 
the ELB species via light irradiation. It is known that the ELB is 
mainly composed of nutrients (e.g., tryptone and yeast extract) 
of bacteria, and thus they could be preferably swallowed  
and digested by the bacteria via endocytosis. Therefore, the 
as-designed Ag NCs@ELB may have multiple features favored 
by antibacterial applications: (1) Ag NCs@ELB could be selectively 
swallowed by the bacteria through endocytosis; and (2) Ag+ 
ions could be efficiently released from Ag NCs@ELB within 
the bacteria, which can be triggered once the sacrificial ELB 
shell was digested by the bacteria. These features endow the 
Ag NCs@ELB antibacterial agents with multiple functions of 
targeted delivery and smart release of Ag+ ions [9], subsequently 
improving their antibacterial activity. As expected, the 
as-designed Ag NCs@ELB exhibits enhanced bacterial killing 
activity, and enables to reduce CFU with 3–4 orders of magnitude 
less than that treated with the pristine Ag NCs. 
2 Experimental 
2.1 Synthesis of Ag16(GSH)9 NCs 
Aqueous solutions of L-glutathione (GSH 20 mM) and AgNO3 
(20 mM) were prepared with ultrapure water. An aqueous 
solution of NaBH4 (~112 mM) was freshly prepared by dissolving 
43 mg of NaBH4 in 10 mL of 0.2 M NaOH solution. In a 
typical synthesis of Ag16(GSH)9 NCs, aqueous solutions of 
GSH (0.375 mL, 20 mM) and AgNO3 (0.125 mL, 20 mM) 
were mixed in water (4.4 mL) under vigorous stirring to form 
Ag(I)-GSH complexes at room temperature, followed by adding 
50 μL of the fresh-prepared NaBH4 solution (112 mM) drop by 
drop. After ~ 5 min reaction, the solution changed to deep red, 
which was incubated without stirring at room temperature for ׽ 
4 h for complete decomposition of the deep-red Ag NCs into 
colorless Ag(I)-GSH complexes. Afterwards, 50 μL of NaBH4 
solution (112 mM) was introduced into the colorless solution 
under vigorous stirring, and the solution turned light brown 
after reaction of 30 min. Finally, the solution was incubated 
without stirring at room temperature for ~ 8 h for the formation 
of Ag16(GSH)9 NCs. The as-prepared Ag NCs were purified via 
a PD-10 desalting column and stored at 4 oC for later use. 
2.2 Synthesis of Ag NCs@ELB antibacterial agent 
In a typical synthesis of Ag NCs@ELB, 0.1 mL of the as- 
synthesized Ag16(GSH)9 NC solution (5–6 mM) was mixed with 
0.9 mL of LB medium. Afterwards, the mixture was irradiated 
by a cold daylight lamp for 1 h. It should be noted that the species 
in LB medium (e.g., tryptone and yeast extracts) have abundant 
amino groups, and could interact with Ag16(GSH)9 NCs under 
light irradiation, which may cleave Ag16(GSH)9 NCs into smaller 
ones via amino groups of ELB, resulting in the formation of 
Ag NCs@ELB antibacterial agent. 
3 Results and discussion 
We chose a well-defined Ag16(GSH)9 cluster (GSH denotes 
glutathione, Fig. 1(a)) as the model antimicrobial agent. This 
compound was synthesized according to the reported “cyclic 
reduction-decomposition” method [41]. As shown in Fig. S1 in 
the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM), the ultraviolet– 
visible (UV–vis) absorption spectrum of the Ag NCs shows a 
dominant peak at 489 nm, which is the characteristic absorption 
of Ag16(GSH)9 NCs [50]. Transmission electron microscopy 
(TEM) images (Fig. S2 in the ESM) suggest that the as-synthesized 
Ag NCs have a core size below 2 nm. Subsequent encapsulation 
of Ag NCs in ELB was carried out via a light–irradiation-assisted 
anchoring strategy (Scheme S1 in the ESM). Briefly, an aqueous 
solution of Ag16(GSH)9 NCs was mixed with the LB medium, 
followed by irradiation under a simulated sunlight for 1 h (see 
results of optimization on the light irradiation time in Fig. S3 
in the ESM). Such irradiation treatment could facilitate the 
encapsulation of Ag NCs inside the LB species. It is known 
that Ag nanomaterials are light sensitive, and selected light 
irradiations might control their morphology, size, and structure 
[51, 52]. Moreover, such structural modulation could be further 
improved in the presence of species containing rich functional 
groups (e.g., –NH2 and –COOH, which have moderate 
interactions with Ag) [53]. 
To shed light on the encapsulation chemistry of Ag NCs in 
ELB species, we first studied the roles of light irradiation and 
ELB. As shown in Fig. S4 in the ESM, no obvious changes were 
seen in the solution color of Ag NCs without light irradiation 
(Ag NCs + ELB), which is different from the samples after 
light irradiation for 1 h (Ag NCs@ELB). In comparison, the 
solution color of Ag NCs in water (Ag NCs + H2O) was almost 
unchanged under the same light irradiation condition, which 
suggests that the ELB species have strong interactions with Ag 
NCs under light irradiation. We reasoned that the ELB species, 
such as tryptone and yeast extract, could interact with Ag NCs 
and partially etch Ag NCs into smaller species by using their 
functional groups (e.g., carboxyl and amino groups) under light 
irradiation. The as-generated smaller Ag NC species could bind

This speculation is supported by Fourier transform infrared 
(FTIR) spectroscopy and TEM analysis. As shown in Fig. S5 in 
the ESM, while obvious N–H stretching vibration (~ 3,412 cm−1) 
and in-plane N–H bending vibration (~ 1,637 cm−1) of –NH2 
groups are observed in both ELB species and the non-light 
irradiated mixture of Ag NCs and LB medium (Ag NCs + ELB) 
[54, 55], similar vibrations are mostly suppressed in the light 
irradiated mixture of Ag NCs and LB medium (Ag NCs@ELB). 
The FTIR result suggests that the Ag NCs could markedly interact 
with the –NH2 groups of ELB species under light irradiation 
condition, facilitating the formation of Ag NCs@ELB. The 
existence of Ag NCs in the irradiated mixture is further verified 
in the corresponding TEM images. The light irradiated sample 
(Ag NCs@ELB) was subjected to dialysis (molecular weight 
cut-off of 10 K) for 24 h (the dialysis water was refreshed 
for 3 times) prior to the TEM analysis. The purpose of dialysis 
is to remove the free Ag NCs (if any; the molecular weight of 
Ag16(GSH)9 NCs is < 4.5 K) [41] from the light irradiated 
sample. As shown in Fig. S6 in the ESM, a number of Ag NCs 
with core size of < 1 nm were seen in the TEM image, which 
not only corroborates the anchoring of Ag NCs inside the 
ELB species, but also confirms the etching of larger Ag NCs 
(~ 2 nm, Fig. S2 in the ESM) into those smaller ones. It should 
be mentioned that the ELB species could not be seen in the TEM 
images due to their light contrast. Hence, scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM) and elemental mapping analysis were applied 
to characterize the size/morphology of the Ag NCs@ELB. The 
results reveal that the ELB exhibits near-spherical shape with 
a diameter of ~ 340 nm, and a number of Ag NCs are evenly 
embedded into one ELB structure (Fig. S7 in the ESM). 
The as-fabricated Ag NCs@ELB antibacterial agent (Fig. 1(a), 
bottom panel) shows better bacterial killing efficacy as compared 
to the pristine Ag16(GSH)9 NCs (Fig. 1(a), upper item). In this 
study, we chose two gram-positive bacteria Staphylococcus aureus 
(S. aureus) and Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis), and two gram- 
negative bacteria Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Pseudomonas 
aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) [40]. The antibacterial activity of Ag 
NCs@ELB was evaluated by plate counting analysis. As shown 
in Fig. 1(b), while a large number of colonies of the four bacterial 
strains could be observed on LB agar even after treating with 
Ag NCs (top panels), colony formation of similar strains is 
completely inhibited after Ag NCs@ELB treatment (bottom 
panels). Based on counting the number of bacteria colonies, 
it is found that the bacterial killing activity of Ag NCs@ELB is 
enhanced by around 3–4 orders of magnitude compared to that 
of the pristine Ag NCs (Fig. 1(c)). This observation is in good 
accordance to the beneficial impacts of sacrificial ELB nano- 
vehicle on the bacterial killing efficacy of Ag NCs. In addition, 
the Ag NCs@ELB exhibits excellent stability, and maintains 
superior antibacterial activity after storage of 6 months at room 
temperature (Fig. S8 in the ESM).  
To further investigate the bacterial killing activity of the Ag 
NCs@ELB, we studied the correlation between the dosage of 
Ag NCs@ELB and the bacterial growth kinetics in LB medium. 
Corresponding time-course bacterial growth curves with 
different antibacterial dosages are plotted in Fig. S9 in the ESM. 
The data suggests that the Ag NCs@ELB would inhibit the 
growth of both gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria at a 
threshold concentration of 0.3 mM (on the basis of Ag atoms), 
while complete inhibition of the bacteria growth could be 
achieved at a concentration of 0.6 mM or above. In order to 
compare the bacterial killing performance of Ag NCs@ELB and 
Ag16(GSH)9 NCs, the antibacterial efficacies of both samples 
are evaluated at a concentration of 0.6 mM. As depicted in Fig. 2,  
while Ag NCs (red curves) exhibit improved antibacterial activity 
compared to the control group (ultrapure water, black curves), 
Ag NCs@ELB (blue curves) could completely inhibit the growth 
of both gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria, corroborating 
its good antibacterial activity. To further identify the active 
species in ELB that contribute to the enhanced antibacterial 
activity, we also examined the composition of LB broth. It is 
known that the LB broth consists of tryptone (1 w/v%), yeast 
extract (0.5 w/v%), and NaCl (1 w/v%). Tryptone, yeast extract, 
and NaCl was separately mixed with Ag NCs, followed by light 
irradiation to initiate the encapsulation reaction. The resulting 
Ag NCs@tryptone and Ag NCs@yeast extract show comparable 
antibacterial efficacy to Ag NCs@ELB (Fig. S10 in the ESM). 
Similar evaluation was conducted on NaCl incubated Ag 
NCs, the irradiated and non-irradiated samples exhibit similar 
bacterial killing efficiency, which is much lower than that of 
Ag NCs@ELB. 
Next we would like to find out whether the light irradiation 
itself (in the first 1 h) could directly kill the bacteria [56]. We 
could divide the bacterial killing process of the Ag NCs@ELB 
into two stages: (I) light irradiation directed formation of Ag 
NCs@ELB and concurrent bacterial killing in the first 1 h; and 
(II) non-light involved bacterial killing of Ag NCs@ELB in the 
subsequent 11 h. The antibacterial activities of Ag NCs@ELB 
in such two stages were measured by plate counting analysis.

and Ag NCs@ELB (blue column) showed apparent bacterial 
activity, while irradiation itself did not show any antibacterial 
activity (grey column). Specifically, Ag NCs@ELB (fabricated 
by light irradiation on the mixture of Ag NCs and ELB species)

function of light irradiation is mainly to induce the anchoring 
of Ag NCs on the ELB species, which excludes the possibility 
of the light irradiation-induced bacterial killing. Therefore, 
the formation of Ag NCs@ELB should be the main reason for 
the observed antibacterial enhancement. In addition to light 
irradiation, we also exclude Ag(I)-GSH complexes (the dominant 
decomposition product of Ag16(GSH)9) as the key factor to the 
antibacterial activity enhancement. As shown in Fig. S11 in the 
ESM, after mixing Ag(I)-GSH complexes with LB medium and 
irradiating under a simulated sunlight for 1 h, the resulting 
sample exhibits comparable antibacterial performance to the 
pristine Ag NCs. This result, on one hand, confirms that the 
Ag16(GSH)9 NCs were etched into those smaller sized Ag NCs 
instead of Ag(I)-GSH complexes by ELB species under light 
irradiation; on the other hand, implies that the Ag(I) in 
Ag(I)-GSH complexes might be fully bonded with the thiol 
group of GSH and there are no other sites to interact with ELB 
that could exhibit the synergistic effects [57]. 
It is known that the oxidation states of Ag nanomaterials are 
crucial to the bacterial killing activity [3, 40]. Thus, we carried 
out X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to examine 
the oxidation states of Ag species in pristine Ag NCs and Ag  
NCs@ELB. As shown in Fig. S12 in the ESM, the Ag 3d3/2 and 
Ag 3d5/2 binding energies of Ag NCs and Ag NCs@ELB were 
centered at ~ 373.83 and ~ 367.84 eV, respectively, and no 
perceptible difference between these two samples was observed. 
This result indicates that the oxidation states of Ag species is 
not responsible for the enhancement of the bacterial killing 
activity of Ag NCs@ELB.  
Owing to the preferential uptake of the nutrient ELB by a 
wide spectrum of bacteria, the Ag NCs@ELB could be easily 
swallowed by the bacteria, surfacing as a significantly enhanced 
uptake by the bacteria. Moreover, the bacterial killing activity 
of the Ag NCs@ELB that swallowed by the bacteria could be 
effectively triggered once its ELB shell was digested by the 
bacteria. This hypothesis is supported by the internalization 
analysis of Ag NCs and Ag NCs@ELB. As shown in Fig. S13 in 
the ESM, TEM and elemental energy dispersion spectrometry 
(EDS) analyses provide direct evidence that the Ag NCs@ELB 
antibacterial agents are endocytosed by bacteria (black dots 
in Fig. S13(a) in the ESM). In addition, inductively coupled 
plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) was employed to detect 
and quantify the intracellular Ag content in S. aureus strain after 
12 h of incubation with Ag NCs@ELB and the pristine Ag NCs. 
As displayed in Fig. 3(c), the S. aureus strain treated by Ag 
NCs@ELB has an approximately 4-fold higher uptake of Ag 
species (0.133 μg per unit mass of cells with OD600 = 0.2) than 
those treated by their Ag NC counterpart (0.034 μg per unit 
mass of cells).  
Ag-based antibacterial agents could induce the generation 
of reactive oxygen species (ROS) to kill bacteria [1, 41, 58]. To 
verify the underlying antibacterial mechanism of Ag NCs@ELB, 
we measured the level of ROS in the S. aureus strain after 
exposure to Ag NCs@ELB by using dichlorofluorescein (DCF) 
dye as indicator. The S. aureus strain treated by Ag NCs@ELB 
shows approximately 2-fold increase in the oxidized DCF 
concentration (Fig. 3(d), blue column) compared to that treated 
by Ag NCs (red column), and both values are higher than the 
ROS level in the control group (bacteria treated by ultrapure 
water). Both bacterial uptake results and the ROS analysis 
confirm that the Ag NCs encapsulated in ELB species could 
markedly affect their cell uptake behavior, and much more Ag 
NCs@ELB could be internalized into the cells when compared 
to the pristine Ag NCs, thus inducing more ROS to kill the 
bacteria. 
The cytotoxicity of the Ag NCs@ELB was examined on HeLa 
cells and mouse fibroblast cells (L929) using the MTT (i.e., 
thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide) kit (Fig. 4 and Fig. S14 in 
the ESM, respectively). As shown in Fig. 4(a), the Ag NCs@ELB 
with a concentration of 0.6 mM shows no obvious cytotoxicity 
with HeLa cell viabilities of 87.7% at 12 h and 79.2% at 24 h, 
which are similar to the pristine Ag NCs with HeLa cell 
viabilities of 89.4% at 12 h and 84.5% at 24 h. In contrast, AgNO3 
of the same concentration shows severe cytotoxicity with HeLa 
cell viabilities of 8.7% at 12 h and 6.3% at 24 h. Similar results 
were also observed for L929 cells (Fig. S14(a) in the ESM). 
LIVE/DEAD viability assay was also performed using HeLa 
cells and L929 cells. In the assay, the cells that appear green 
are alive while those red cells are dead ones with damaged 
membranes. As seen in Figs. 4(b)–4(d) and Figs. S14(b)–S14(d) 
in the ESM, both HeLa cells and L929 cells remained alive when 
treated with ultrapure water (control), Ag NCs@ELB, and Ag 
NCs, while almost all those cells treated by AgNO3 were 
dead. These results suggest that Ag NCs@ELB possesses good 
biocompatibility, despite its strong bactericidal effect on both 
gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria.

4 Conclusions 
In summary, we have developed an efficient and highly 
biocompatible Ag-based antibacterial agent by encapsulating Ag 
NCs inside the sacrificial ELB species made possible by facile 
light irradiation. The as-designed Ag NCs@ELB has improved 
the antibacterial activity to about 4 orders of magnitude higher 
compared to that of the reference Ag NCs, toward both gram- 
positive and gram-negative bacteria. We have shown that the 
delicate architecture design of Ag NCs@ELB could greatly 
improve the bacterial uptake of Ag NCs, resulting in an 
enhanced intracellular ROS generation after digestion of the 
sacrificial ELB species in bacteria. The sacrificial ELB vehicle 
together with its synergistic effects with Ag NCs have been 
identified as the key factors to improve the antibacterial activities 
of Ag NCs@ELB. In addition, the Ag NCs@ELB shows good 
biocompatibility which is comparable to the pristine Ag NCs. 
We have demonstrated in this study that the nutrient or food 
(i.e., ELB) of the bacteria can be utilized to encapsulate Ag 
NCs via a light irradiation strategy, which could transform the 
role of ELB from the delicious food to the favorite “poison” of 
the bacteria.

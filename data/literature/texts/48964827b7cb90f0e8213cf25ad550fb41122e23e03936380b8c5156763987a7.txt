ORIGINAL PAPER
Exploring a monothiolated β-cyclodextrin as the template
to synthesize copper nanoclusters with exceptionally increased
peroxidase-like activity
Yaping Zhong1 & Chun Deng1 & Yu He1,2 & Yili Ge1,2 & Gongwu Song1,2
Received: 20 April 2016 /Accepted: 17 July 2016 /Published online: 16 August 2016
# Springer-Verlag Wien 2016
Abstract The authors describe a method for increasing
the peroxidase-like catalytic properties of copper
nanoclusters (Cu-NCs) that are used in non-enzymatic as-
says. The Cu-NCs were prepared by utilizing 6-thio-β-
cyclodextrin as both the template and as effective modu-
lators for increasing the peroxidase-like properties of the
Cu-NCs. The β-CD-coated Cu-NCs have an average di-
ameter of 2 nm, are stable in aqueous solutions, display
strong fluorescence with excitation/emission peak wave-
lengths of 360/450 nm, and possess peroxidase-like cata-
lytic activity which makes them a useful enzyme mimic.
We have applied the findings to non-enzymatic photomet-
ric determination (at 650 nm) of (a) H2O2 in the concen-
tration range of 0.02 to 10 mM using the β-CD/Cu-NC
assisted oxidation of tetramethylbenzidine by H2O2, and
(b) glucose in the concentration range of 0.04 to 20 mM
after addition of glucose oxidase and formation of H2O2.
The detection limits (at an S/N ratio of 3) are 0.2 μM for
H2O2 and 0.4 μM for glucose. The β-CD coating is found
to result in a strong increase in the reaction rate, probably
because the cavity of β-CD acts as a pocket for the
recognition and catalysis of substrate. Hence, the binding
specificity becomes similar to that of natural enzymes.

Introduction
Natural enzymes, exquisite biocatalysts mediating every bio-
logical process in living organisms, are able to accelerate the
rate of chemical reactions up to 1019 times for specific sub-
strates and reactions [1, 2]. However, problems such as sensi-
tivity of catalytic activity to environmental conditions, low
operational stability due to denaturation and digestion, as well
as the high costs in preparation and purification largely ham-
pered their applications [3]. To circumvent the aforemen-
tioned limitations, tremendous efforts have been made to de-
velop biomimetic catalysts. Since the initial discovery of mag-
netic nanoparticle-based catalytic capability, a significant
amount of effort has been focused on imitating peroxidase
activity with various nanomaterials because of their controlled
synthesis, low cost, tunable catalytic activities, and high de-
gree of stability under stringent conditions [4–9].
Nevertheless, the binding affinity and substrate specificity of
most nanomaterial-based peroxidase mimics remain lower
than those of natural enzymes. Therefore, the enhancement
of the catalytic performance of nanomaterial via rational de-
sign and engineering was very significant.
Metal nanoclusters (NCs) exhibit great potentials as func-
tional building blocks in a wide range of applications such as
optoelectronic nanodevices, biosensors, nanoelectronics, and
catalysts, because of their unique size-dependent optical and

electronic properties that differ from those of atoms and bulk
materials. The superior catalytic activity of metal nanoclusters
resulted substantially from their high surface-to-volume ratio
and high surface energy [10–14]. The size-dependent catalytic
activity of ultra small gold and silver nanoclusters has been
extensively studied and emerging as promising nanocatalysts
[15–23]. Nevertheless, previous reports for the synthesis and
catalytic activity of copper nanoclusters are still scarce for the
difficulty in preparing highly stable and extremely tiny parti-
cles [24, 25].
β-Cyclodextrin (β-CD) can form host-guest complexes with
a wide range of hydrophobic guest species through noncovalent
interactions [26]. Previous works report β-Cyclodextrin serve
as a modulator to greatly improve the catalytical activity of
nanomaterial, because they can form complexes with hydro-
phobic compounds to offer a pocket for the substrate recogni-
tion [27–29]. However, there are only limited work focusing on
the synthesis and application of β-cyclodextrin based metal
nanoclusters, especially for copper nanoclusters [30–32].
Herein, we explore a strategy for increasing the peroxidase-
like catalytic rate of Cu-NCs in non-enzymatic sensors by
utilizing mono-6-thio-β-CD as both the template for synthesis
of Cu-NCs and the effective modulator for increasing catalytic
rate of Cu-NCs (Scheme 1). Compared with other Cu-NCs,
The preparation of β-CD protected Cu-NCs with high good
fluorescence and catalytic ability is very simple, repeatable
and efficient. On the basis of its attractive catalytic activity,
we expect that β-CD protected Cu-NCs become a promising
substitute for peroxidase with great potential in biochemical
analysis. Therefore, we apply the β-CD protected Cu-NCs as
peroxidase mimetics to detect H2O2 and glucose as the model
system. With the aid of β-CD, the catalytic rate of Cu-NCs is
greatly increased because the cavity of β-CD offered a pocket
for substrate recognition and catalysis. The β-CD engineered
on the exterior surfaces of Cu-NCs to increase the binding
affinity and specificity which are similar to those that are
exposed in natural enzymes. Our finding payed the way for
engineering β-CD on the surface of metal nanoclusters to
improve the catalytic performance.
Experimental
Reagents and materials
Glucose, maltose, lactose, fructose, glucose oxidase (GOx)
and horseradish peroxidase (HRP) were purchased from
Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China, http://www.
aladdin-e.com/). Mono-6-SH-β-CD was purchased from
Shandong Binzhou Zhiyuan Bio-Technology Co., Ltd.
(Shandong, China, http://m.bzzysw.com/). Copper nitrate
trihydrate (Cu (NO3)2 · 3H2O), hydrazine hydride (N2H4)
(50 wt% aqueous), TMB (tetramethylbenzidine) and H2O2
solution (30 wt% aqueous) were purchased from Sinopharm
Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China, http://www.
sinoreagent.com/). All chemicals were of at least analytical
grade and used as received. Milli-Q water (18 MΩ cm) was
used throughout the experiments.
Measurements and characterization
The UV–Vis absorption measurements were performed on a
Lambda 35 UV analyzer (Perkin-Elmer, America). The fluo-
rescence intensity was recorded on a LS55 fluorescence spec-
trometer (Perkin-Elmer, America). The TEM images of the
prepared samples were examined using a TecnaiG20 transmis-
sion electron microscope (FEI, American). Fourier Transform
Infra-Red (FTIR) spectra were taken with a spectrum on a
FTIR spectrophotometer (Perkin-Elmer, America) at room
temperature. The Raman spectra were recorded on a
LabRAM HR Raman Spectrometer (Jobin Yvon, France).
Preparation of water-soluble β-CD protected Cu-NCs
Typically, 2 mL of 5 mM 6-SH-β-CD and 250 μL of 20 mM
Cu(NO3)2 solution were first dissolved in 1 mL of Milli-Q
water by stirring for 10 min at 45 °C, and then 1.6 mL of
0.1 M NaOH was added and homogenized by stirring for
2 min. Next, 15 μL of N2H4 (50 wt%) was added, and the
mixture was heated at 45 °C for 5 h. The final solution was
stored at 4 °C in refrigerator for use in the next step. The Cu-
NCs were purified via 8000 Da MWCO dialysis bag in double
distilled water for 24 h to remove unreacted Cu2+. Factors
affecting formation of β-CD protected copper nanoclusters
including the concentration of each reactant, reaction time
and reaction temperature were studied to obtain the optimal
synthetic conditions.
Peroxidase-like activity of β-CD protected Cu-NCs
The reaction kinetics measurements were carried out by re-
cording the absorption value at 652 nm for TMB with a 1 s
interval in time drive mode. Experiments were conducted by
using 300 μL β-CD protected Cu-NCs in 0.02 M phosphate
buffer (pH 6.0) containing 0.5 mM TMB or 10 mM H2O2.
The Michaelis–Menten constant was calculated using
Lineweaver–Burk plots of the double reciprocal of the
Michaelis–Menten equation. The absorption value at 652 nm
of each substrate concentration was measured three times in
our experiments.

Cu-NCs and HRP
The influence of reaction buffer pH on the Cu-NCs and HRP
relative activity was investigated in 0.02 M phosphate buffer

H2O2 and glucose detection using β-CD protected Cu-NCs

typical colorimetric analysis for glucose detection were car-
ried out using 50 μL of 100 ng mL−1 GOx and 50 μL of
glucose with different concentrations in 0.02 M phosphate
buffer (pH 6.0) incubated at 37 °C in water bath for 1 h.
Then, 50 μL of 5 mM TMB, 300 μL Cu-NCs, and 100 μL
of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) were added into the above
solution, and the final mixture was further incubated at 30 °C
for 1 h. The control experiments were carried out with malt-
ose, lactose, and fructose, instead of glucose.
Results and discussion
Synthesis and characterization of β-CD protected Cu-NCs
To obtain the optimal synthetic conditions, the influence of
reactants concentration including N2H4, NaOH, Cu (NO3)2, 6-
SH-β-CD and reaction time, reaction temperature on the fluo-
rescence intensity of β-CD protected Cu-NCs were investi-
gated respectively by varying one synthetic condition and
keeping the other synthetic conditions constant (Fig. S1 to
S6). Typically, 2 mL of 5 mM 6-SH-β-CD and 250 μL of
20 mM Cu(NO3)2 solution were first dissolved in 1 mL of
Milli-Q water by stirring for 10 min at 45 °C, and then
1.6 mL of 0.1 M NaOH was added and homogenized by
stirring for 2 min. Then, 15 μL of N2H4 (50 wt%) was added,
and the mixture was heated at 45 °C for 5 h. Finally, the Cu-
NCs were purified via dialysis to remove unreacted Cu2+. The
formation of water-soluble and blue-emitting Cu-NCs was
characterized using TEM, FTIR, fluorescence, absorption
spectra and Raman spectra. The as-prepared β-CD protected
Cu-NCs was diluted 50 times for TEM detection. The TEM
image showed the β-CD protected Cu-NCs displayed an av-
erage diameter of 2 nm (Fig. 1a). The optical properties of Cu-
NCs were characterized by fluorescence and absorption spec-
tra (Fig. 1b and c). The maximum fluorescence excitation and
emission wavelength of β-CD-protected Cu-NCs were
360 nm and 450 nm (Fig. 1b). Compared to the absorption
peak of β-CD at 220 nm, a new absorption peak at 270 nm
was presented in the absorption curve of the β-CD protected
Cu-NCs (Fig. 1c). This multiple band optical absorption was
due to inter-band electronic transitions of the Cu-NCs from
discrete energy levels [33]. Furthermore, there was no charac-
teristic surface Plasmon resonance (SPR) peak of large Cu
nanoparticles at around 600 nm. Compared with the FTIR
spectra of mono-6-SH-β-CD, the peak positions of the char-
acteristic absorptions of –OH (3375 cm−1 and 1415 cm−1) and
–C–O–C(1030 cm−1) were shifted to 3356 cm−1, 1371 cm−1
and 1030 cm−1 in the FTIR spectra of β-CD protected Cu-
NCs (Fig. 1d). Compared with the Raman spectra of mono-6-
SH-β-CD, the characteristic Raman absorption peak of –SH at
around 2570 cm−1 disappeared in the Raman spectra of the β-
CD protected Cu-NCs which demonstrated the mono-6-
SH-β-CD combined on the surface of obtained Cu-NCs
(Fig. S7). The long time stability of β-CD protected Cu-NCs
was also investigated. The fluorescence intensity of the Cu-
NCs changed less than 5 % of its initial intensity after one
month’s storage in the refrigerator which indicated its excel-
lent aqueous stability (Fig. S8).

Catalytic oxidation of TMB by β-CD protected Cu-NCs
Similar to the enzyme horseradish peroxidase (HRP), the
peroxidase-like activity of β-CD protected Cu-NCs was eval-
uated for the oxidation of TMB in the presence of H2O2. In the
control experiments, TMB was colorless and displayed no
absorbance in the presence of H2O2 or β-CD protected Cu-
NCs (Inset of Fig. 2a and b). However, upon the addition of
both H2O2 and β-CD protected Cu-NCs, the TMB solution
exhibited a deep blue color and a maximum absorption peak at
around 652 nm, which results from the oxidation products of
TMB [20] (Inset of Fig. 2c and Fig. 2). The control experi-
ments using TMB in the absence of Cu-NCs or H2O2 showed
no oxidation reaction, indicating that both the components are
required for the catalytic reaction. The catalytic performance
of β-CD protected Cu-NCs was originated from the electronic
structures of Cu-NCs. Cu(0) and Cu(I) are reported to be both
existed in the Cu-NCs structure and they can broke up the O-O
bond of H2O2 which adsorbed on the surface of Cu-NCs into
double OH• radical [34–36].
The catalytic activity of the β-CD protected Cu-NCs,
like horseradish peroxidase (HRP), is dependent on pH.
We measured the peroxidase-like activity of β-CD
protected Cu-NCs by varying the pH from 2 to 10. The
maximum catalytic activity of β-CD protected Cu-NCs
was obtained at pH 6.0 (Fig. S9). We also investigated
the influence of dose of β-CD protected Cu-NCs on the
initial velocity of the peroxidase-like activity of β-CD
protected Cu-NCs. Result showed that, the maximal initial
velocity was obtained when volume of β-CD protected
Cu-NCs was 300 μL (Fig. S10). Further, we studied the
influence of TMB concentration on the peroxidase-like
activity of β-CD protected Cu-NCs to obtain the best
catalytic effect. From the results, the maximal
peroxidase-like activity was obtained when the TMB con-
centration reached 0.6 mM (Fig. S11). Thus, 300 μL of β-
CD protected Cu-NCs, pH 6 and 0.6 mM of TMB were
chosen as the optimal catalytic conditions.

Relative activity comparison between β-CD protected
Cu-NCs and HRP
pH-dependent studies of the catalyzed oxidation of TMB
showed that as the pH value of the buffered solution increased
from 2.0 to 10.0, the β-CD protected Cu-NCs showed stable
and high catalytic activity to oxidize the substrate(Fig. 4). β-
CD protected Cu-NCs exhibited high pH and thermal stability
compared with the natural HRP enzyme. As shown in 4a, the
pH stability of β-CD protected Cu-NCs and HRP was inves-
tigated. To test this, we first incubated both HRP and theβ-CD
protected Cu-NCs at a range of pH values (2–10) for 2 h and
then measured their activities under standard conditions
(pH 6.0 and 37 °C). The β-CD protected Cu-NCs were found
to remain stable over a wide pH range, from 2 to 10. In con-
trast, HRP only showed good activity after treatment at pH
values of 6.0. In addition, we investigated the thermal stability
of β-CD protected Cu-NCs. The activity of HRP was
completely inhibited under heating (temperature higher than
40 °C, 5 min) mainly because its own structure was destroyed,
whereas the activity of the β-CD protected Cu-NCs remained
almost the same as before heating. These results further con-
firmed that natural enzymes can easily lose catalytic activity
when the environment changes, whereas artificial enzymes
have excellent stability under stringent conditions.
H2O2 and glucose detection using β-CD protected Cu-NCs
as peroxidase mimic
The absorbance change depended on the concentrations of
H2O2 (Fig. S12a). Under the optimized conditions, the absor-
bance at 652 nm was proportional to H2O2 over the concen-
tration range from 0.02 to 0.1 mM with a correlation coeffi-
cient of 0.998 (Fig. S12b), 0.1 to 1 mM with a correlation
coefficient of 0.983 (Fig. S12c), 1 to 10 mM with a correlation
coefficient of 0.996 (Fig. S12d). The detection limit (at an S/N
ratio of 3) was 0.2 μM. In the inset of Fig. S11a, an obvious
color change (from blue to colorless) appeared due to the
oxidation of TMB when the H2O2 concentrations decreased,
which proved that the β-CD protected Cu-NCs were potential
as visual sensor for H2O2.

This colorimetric method can be extended to detect glucose

We compared the experimental results using different bio-
sensors to detect hydrogen peroxide and glucose in the recent
years [38–45] (Table S1). Although the introduction of β-CD
had largely increased the peroxidase-like catalytic rate of Cu-
NCs, results showed that our method had the limitation of
narrower linear range which put forward the direction of our
future efforts.
To evaluate the specificity of β-CD protected Cu-NCs, we
measured the absorbance responses of three glucose analogs,
lactose, fructose and maltose. Remarkably, the three glucose
analogs at the concentration of 20 mM did not increase the
absorbance while 8 mM of glucose caused a remarkable in-
crease in absorbance (Fig. 6). Moreover, the blue color change
induced by glucose can be easily distinguished by bare eyes.
Conclusions
In conclusion, we synthesized β-CD protected copper
nanoclusters by using hydrazine hydrate as reductant. The cop-
per nanoclusters exhibits high optical properties and excellent
catalytic activity compared with other copper nanoclusters. The
peroxidase-like catalysis of Cu-NCs shows the Michaelis–
Menten kinetics, which is similar to that of HRP. On the basis
of its unique and attractive catalytic activity, a simple and se-
lective colorimetric assay for H2O2 and glucose is developed.
Compared with natural enzymes, Cu-NCs as a mimic peroxi-
dase shows several advantages such as ease of preparation, low
cost, as well as high stability and activity under harsh conditions
which makes it promising as enzymatic mimics in biotechnol-
ogy and clinical diagnosis applications. This work will facilitate
the study of NCs as peroxidase mimics and open up a wide
range of potential applications for NCs in biotechnology, catal-
ysis, and medical diagnostics.

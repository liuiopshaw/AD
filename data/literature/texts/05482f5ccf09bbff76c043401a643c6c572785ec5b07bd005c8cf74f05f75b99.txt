Design of a Molecular Hybrid of Dual Peptide Inhibitors 
Coupled on AuNPs for Enhanced Inhibition of Amyloid 
β-Protein Aggregation and Cytotoxicity
Neng Xiong, Yanjiao Zhao, Xiaoyan Dong,* Jie Zheng, and Yan Sun*

Aggregation of amyloid-β protein (Aβ) is a pathological hallmark of Alzheimer’s 
disease (AD), so the inhibition of Aβ aggregation is an important strategy for the 
prevention and treatment of AD. Herein, we proposed to design molecular hybrids of 
peptide inhibitors by combining two peptide inhibitors, VVIA and LPFFD, into single 
sequences and examined their effects on Aβ42 aggregation and cytotoxicity. The hybrid 
peptides exhibit increased but moderate inhibitory activity as compared to their two 
precursors. By conjugating the peptides onto gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), however, 
the inhibition activity of the corresponding peptide@AuNPs against Aβ42 aggregation 
and cytotoxicity is greatly improved. Among them, VVIACLPFFD (VCD10)@AuNP 
is the most effective, which increases cell viability from 48% to 82% at a dosage 
as low as 0.1 nmol L−1 (NPs) or 40 nmol L−1 (peptide). The superior capacity of 
VCD10@AuNPs is considered due to its branched dual-inhibitor sequence, and its 
special surface orientation and conformation. These structural features promote its 
synergetic interactions with Aβ on AuNP surface, leading to strong inhibitions of 
Aβ oligomerization and fibrillation and the cytotoxicity caused by the aggregation 
species. The findings suggest that potent inhibitors can be derived by hybridization of 
multiple peptide inhibitors with the hybrid products coupled onto nanoparticles.

The fibrillation and deposition of amyloid proteins in 
human tissues are pathologically linked to many different

dysfunction and death.[15,16] Therefore, it is envisioned that 
targeting the early aggregation species of Aβ could be prom-
ising therapeutic strategies for preventing or delaying the 
onset of AD.[17]
Some critical regions in Aβ sequence have strong regu-
lation in controlling the early conformational transition and 
the following oligomerization toward Aβ fibrillation. Among 
those, Aβ16-22 (KLVFFAE), namely, the central hydrophobic 
cluster, has been reported to be primarily responsible for 
Aβ self-assembly and aggregation.[18,19] A bulk of pep-
tide mimetics have been designed including Soto’s β-sheet 
breaker peptide of LPFFD.[20] The C-terminal region of 
Aβ1-42 (Aβ42) is also critical for controlling Aβ42 oligomeri-
zation.[21] It was reported that as compared to Aβ1-40 (Aβ40), 
the additional C-terminal I41 and A42 residues of Aβ42 
increase its conformational stability[22,23] and the adverse 
possibility to form more toxic oligomers[24] that induce higher 
cell death.[25–27] The direct use of Aβ39-42 (VVIA) fragment 
was reported to efficiently inhibit Aβ42-induced toxicity by 
generating nontoxic heterooligomers.[28] Therefore, peptide 
inhibitors designed in a self-complementary fashion to these 
key amyloidogenic regions are considered favorable. More-
over, the design principle by molecular dynamics studies sug-
gests that the strong binding affinity between the artificially 
designed peptides and the critical regions of Aβ often lead 
to potential hits of Aβ inhibitors. Guided by this design prin-
ciple, several peptide inhibitors have been found to interfere 
with Aβ aggregation.[29,30] Other screening methods such as 
phage display[31] have been used to screen and design dif-
ferent cyclic[32] and non-natural peptide[33] as Aβ inhibitors. 
These studies, together with the biocompatibility and speci-
ficity of peptides, make peptide inhibitors promising for drug 
candidates. However, peptide inhibitors are susceptible to 
degradation in vivo and have self-assembly tendencies.[34] 
Besides, most peptide inhibitors exhibited considerable inhi-
bition of Aβ aggregation only at high dosages. To overcome 
these problems, N-methyl groups,[35,36] D-amino acids,[37] and 
even hybrid peptide derivatives[38] were introduced into pep-
tide inhibitors to improve their bioavailability and inhibition 
efficiency.
Recent studies have emphasized the use of nanomaterials, 
especially nanoparticles (NPs) in the imaging, biosensing, and 
therapeutic applications of protein-misfolding diseases.[39,40] 
Specifically, the fibrillation processes of Aβ can be accel-
erated or retarded by some NPs at low dosages.[41–44] The 
anti-amyloid capacity of NPs could be further enhanced by 
grafting anti-amyloid molecules onto NPs surface, such as 
peptides,[45] polymers,[46] epigallocatechin-3-gallate,[47] and 
curcumin.[48] Such surface modifications facilitate binding 
specificity and affinity between NPs and Aβ, and help to 
remedy the biochemical properties of the conjugated mole­
cules. Our previous work on peptide-functionalized poly 
(lactic-co-glycolic acid) NPs demonstrated that upon con-
jugation with NPs, the cell toxicity of peptide inhibitors 
(LVFFARK) was eliminated and inhibition efficiency was sig-
nificantly enhanced.[49] Among various NPs, gold nanoparti-
cles (AuNPs) have attracted substantial attention because of 
their low cytotoxicity,[50] unique optical properties suitable for 
detection/imaging,[51] well-established synthesis methods,[52] 
and the potential capability to cross the blood brain barrier 
by coupling various shuttle peptides.[53,54] Besides, AuNPs can 
be readily functionalized by peptides via stable AuS bond 
between the thiol group of cysteine and AuNP surface.[55] 
Negatively charged AuNPs have shown inhibitory ability to 
prevent Aβ fibrillation and Aβ1-40-induced cytotoxicity.[56] A 
number of studies have shown that CLPFFD-capped AuNPs 
improved not only their biocompatibility and stability, but 
also their interactions with Aβ.[57,58] Moreover, the surface 
properties of NPs can be altered by conjugated peptides, 
especially the multiple peptides from different functional 
regions.[59,60]
Herein, we proposed a novel hybridization design 
strategy to conjugate peptide inhibitors derived from dif-
ferent Aβ regions (i.e., LPFFD, VVIA, and both) onto the 
AuNPs, yielding different peptide@AuNPs conjugates. Spe-
cifically, CLPFFD (CLD6), CVVIA (CVA5), and two hybrid 
peptides of LPFFDCVVIA (LCA10) and VVIACLPFFD 
(VCD10) combining CLD6 and CVA5 in different ways 
were conjugated onto the AuNPs (15 nm in size) separately 
to fabricate the conjugates (Figure 1). As reported previ-
ously, both LPFFD and VVIA enable to interact with Aβ 
to disrupt the early aggregation of Aβ, thus exhibiting mod-
erate inhibitory capacities against Aβ42 aggregation.[21,61] We 
have extensively examined the inhibitory effects of the pep-
tide@AuNPs on Aβ42 aggregation and cytotoxicity. Then we 
compared the inhibitory abilities between peptide@AuNPs 
conjugates and free peptide inhibitors (i.e., in the absence 
of AuNPs), characterized the surface properties of the con-
jugated peptides on AuNPs, and correlated their structural 
characters of the peptide conjugates to their Aβ inhibition 
effects. The aggregation kinetics of Aβ40 was also examined 
in the presence of the most effective hybrid-peptide@AuNPs. 
Finally the interactions between peptide@AuNPs and Aβ42 
were probed. Based on these results, we proposed a mecha-
nistic model to elucidate the synergetic effects of the hybrid-
peptides@AuNP on the inhibition of Aβ42 aggregation and 
toxicity.

2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Design and Characterization of Peptide@AuNP Conjugates
Aβ fragments derived from some critical regions have 
often been used as templates to develop effective inhibi-
tors against Aβ aggregation. Our previous work has dem-
onstrated that NPs conjugated with a peptide inhibitor can 
remedy the toxicity and instability issues of both NPs and 
conjugated peptides.[49] Inspired by our proof-of-concept 
observations, we designed four short peptides, CLPFFD, 
LPFFDC, CVVIA, and VVIAC, based on β-sheet breaker 
peptides LPFFD and VVIA. Introduction of cysteine res-
idue at the end of these short peptides allowed them to con-
jugate onto AuNP surface via Au-S chemistry, while either 
N-terminal or C-terminal residues of their parent peptides 
were possibly exposed to NP/solution interface. It is also 
interesting to examine the synergetic inhibition effect of 
the combination of the two different peptides onto AuNPs 
on Aβ amyloid formation. So, we designed two hybrid 
peptides of VVIACLPFFD (VCD10) and LPFFDCVVIA 
(LCA10) by combining VVIA and LPFFD in different ways. 
Upon conjugation, the bifurcate hybrid peptides would be 
divided by cysteine anchored on AuNP surface, displaying a 
branched conformation. Table S1 (Supporting Information) 
summarizes the designed short peptides and the seven cor-
responding peptide-conjugated AuNPs; for simplicity only 
three of them are illustrated in Figure 1.[62] Though all the 
designed peptides were soluble in aqueous solution, upon 
conjugation of the peptides with AuNPs, LDC6@AuNP, 
VAC5@AuNP, and LCA10@AuNP showed poor stability 
in aqueous solutions and aggregated immediately into 
insoluble precipitates, while CLD6@AuNP, CVA5@AuNP, 
and VCD10@AuNP retained their solubility and stability 
in aqueous solutions (Table S1, Supporting Information). 
The results confirmed that the solubility and stability of 
NPs were influenced by peptide sequences and the posi-
tion of charged and hydrophobic groups, consistent with 
previous studies.[58] Because LCA10@AuNP was not avail-
able, AuNP functionalized with an equimolar mixture of 
CLD6 and CVA5 (CVA5&CLD6@AuNP) was additionally 
synthesized and used as alternative control (Table S1, Sup-
porting Information). Below, we only selected soluble and 
stable NPs of CVA5@AuNP, CLD6@AuNP, VCD10@AuNP, 
and CVA5&CLD6@AuNP for subsequent experiments 
(Table S1, Supporting Information).
The peptide@AuNP conjugates were exhaustively char-
acterized by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy 
(1H NMR), electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS), 
UV–vis, and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) to confirm 
the success of peptide conjugation onto citrate-reduced 
AuNPs (named as bare AuNPs). Specifically, compara-
tive analysis of 1H NMR spectra of both peptide@AuNP 
conjugates and free peptides revealed that upon conjuga-
tion, peptide@AuNP conjugates displayed typical peaks 
of aromatic protons at 7.00–7.50 ppm corresponding to FF 
(phenylalanine) residues and the peaks of amide protons 
at 7.50–8.50 ppm corresponding to all residues (Figure S1, 
Supporting Information). It was also observed that some 
peaks at 7.00–8.50 ppm were partially broadened; this is 
probably due to the steric congestion of some protons 
residing in too close proximity to gold surface.[63] Another 
complementary method, EELS, was used to confirm the 
presence of SAu bonds on the surface. EELS spectra in the 
region of 90–240 eV (Figure S2, Supporting Information) 
show that as compared to bare gold, all peptide@AuNP 
samples presented a clear edge shape change at the S L2,3 
edge (165 eV) after subtraction, which is indicative of the 
presence of sulfur atoms at the Au surface. Both NMR and 
EELS data confirmed that the peptides were successfully 
conjugated onto AuNPs.
UV–vis spectra also show that the binding of peptides to 
AuNPs induced minor changes in the absorption spectra of 
AuNPs (Figure S3a, Supporting Information). As compared 
to the maximum absorption peak of bare AuNPs at 521 nm, 
the maximum absorption peaks of all the peptide@AuNP 
samples shifted to 523–527 nm. As shown in transmission elec-
tron microscopy (TEM; Figure S3b, Supporting Information), 
the average diameter of the AuNP nucleus was 15 ± 1.2 nm. 
Meanwhile, the zeta potential value dropped once different 
peptides were conjugated onto the AuNP surface (Table 1), 
because of the successful exchange and replacement of the 
negatively charged citrates with the peptides, which also con-
firmed the conjugation. However, these peptide@AuNPs with 
lower zeta potentials were more stable than bare AuNPs in 
aqueous solution as observed in our subsequent experiments, 
which was likely due to the steric stabilization produced by 
the peptide molecules.[64]
The number of peptides per AuNP was quantified by a 
weight loss analysis using TGA. During the heating process 
of TGA, the thermograms show that bare AuNPs were 
very stable without significant weight loss (Figure S4a, Sup-
porting Information). Conversely, the peptide@AuNPs 
largely retained their weights under 200 °C, and experi-
enced a major decrease in weight due to the degradation 
of the conjugated peptides when the temperature increased 
to 250 °C (Figure S4b–d, Supporting Information). At a 
low plateau of >400 °C, the overall weight loss was 2.573% 
for CLD6@AuNP, 3.076% for CVA5@AuNP, and 2.148% 
for VCD10@AuNP, respectively. Based on the different 
weight losses, the number of peptide molecules coupled to 
each AuNP could be calculated (see Equation (1) in the 
Experimental Section). The coupled number was estimated 
and listed in Table 1.

2.2. Inhibitory Effects of Peptides and Peptide@AuNPs 
on Aβ42 Aggregation
First, we examined the inhibitory effects of free peptides 
(LPFFD, VVIA, LCA10, and VCD10) on Aβ42 fibrillation 
at different molar ratios of peptide: Aβ = 0.2:1, 1:1, and 2:1 
using ThT (Thioflavin T) fluorescence assays.[65] To make a 
fair comparison, we first used the maximum intensity of ThT 
fluorescence obtained from pure Aβ42 incubation (a total 
amount of amyloid fibrils being formed) to normalize other 
ThT profiles. In Figure 2a, when incubating LPFFD with 
freshly prepared Aβ42 solution (25 µmol L−1) at three dif-
ferent peptide: Aβ ratios, LPFFD showed a very weak inhi-
bition effect on Aβ42 fibrillation, as evidenced by only 6% 
decrease in the maximum ThT intensities. Such weak inhi-
bition on Aβ42 aggregation was also insensitive to LPFFD 
concentrations.[21,66] Similar to LPFFD, VVIA also exhibited 
weak, dose-independent inhibition ability against Aβ42 fibril-
lation, in which the maximum ThT intensities decreased by 
12% at 24 h. We further tested the effects of an equimolar 
mixture of LPFFD and VVIA on the formation of Aβ42 
fibrils. The results indicate that it had weak inhibitory effects 
similar to LPFFD and VVIA, and showed no obvious syn-
ergetic inhibition effect. A hybrid peptide of LCA10 did not 
show high inhibition activity either. However, another hybrid 
peptide of VCD10 was found to inhibit Aβ42 fibrillogenesis 
in a dose-dependent manner. When a 0.2 or 1 molar ratio of 
VCD10 (i.e., 5 or 25 µmol L−1) was added, ThT fluorescence 
intensity was only decreased by 7% and 10% at 24 h, respec-
tively. When the molar ratios of VCD10:Aβ was increased 
to 2:1, moderate inhibition on Aβ42 amyloid formation was 
observed with the ThT fluorescence intensity being decreased 
by 20%. Taken together, VCD10 exhibited better inhibitory 
effect than its precursors (LPFFD and VVIA) and ana-
logues (LCA10) under the same concentrations, suggesting 
the sequence-dependent inhibitory activity of the peptides. 
Though the hybrid peptides exhibited the moderate inhibi-
tion abilities, we expect that the peptides@NP conjugates 
could efficiently affect Aβ aggregation when carefully con-
trolling binding residues (particularly negatively charged Asp 
and aromatic Phe and Pro) being displayed on the NP sur-
face. Besides, our previous study also showed that peptide@
polymer NP conjugates exhibited higher inhibition capacity 
for Aβ aggregation and toxicity than pure peptides, probably 
because the presence of NPs would increase the binding pos-
sibility and affinity of peptide@NP conjugates to Aβ.
Next, we examined whether peptide conjugation on 
AuNPs would improve their inhibition activity. As the ThT 
emission might be quenched by other factors like the sur-
face plasmon resonance absorption of AuNPs, we performed 
a confirmatory experiment and excluded the interference 
of AuNP nucleus in fluorescence measurements (Figure S5, 
Supporting Information). ThT profiles in Figure 2b show 
that bare AuNPs as a control exhibited very weak inhibitory 
effect on Aβ42 aggregation. Even at the highest concentration 
examined (3.5 nmol L−1), the final ThT fluorescence inten-
sity of the AuNP/Aβ42 mixture decreased by only ≈13% at 
24 h, relative to that of pure Aβ. Different from bare AuNPs 
and free peptides (Figure 2a), all peptide@AuNP conjugates 
apparently inhibited Aβ42 aggregation in a dose-dependent 
manner, reflecting the conjugation effect on Aβ42 aggrega-
tion. Particularly, at the highest concentration of the pep-
tide@AuNP conjugates studied (3.5 nmol L−1), Aβ42 fibril 
formation was significantly reduced by 47% by CVA5@
AuNP, 60% by CLD6@AuNP, 74% by VCD10@AuNP, and 
51% by CVA5&CLD6@AuNP. Clearly, as compared to 
free peptides, the peptide@AuNP conjugates remarkably 
enhanced the inhibition activity on Aβ42 fibril formation. 
Among them, VCD10@AuNP was the most effective con-
jugate against Aβ42 fibrillation, which had much stronger 
inhibition activity than other peptide@AuNPs, especially 
CVA5&CLD6@AuNP. The different inhibitory abilities of 
the peptide@AuNPs might be a result of the differences in 
the conjugated peptide sequences.[67,68] The branched conju-
gation of VVIA and LPFFD fragments on VCD10@AuNP 
facilitated the binding recognition and affinity between NPs 
and Aβ42 species, in contrast to the random conjugation of 
CVA5 and CLD6 on CVA5&CLD6@AuNP that did not 
show strong inhibitory effect.[69]
Additionally, we examined the effects of the peptide@
AuNPs on Aβ42 fibrillation kinetics by ThT (Figure S6, 
Supporting Information). It can be seen that though all 
the peptide@AuNPs reduced the final Aβ fibril forma-
tion to different extents depending on their concentrations, 
they appear not to delay the lag time (<1 h; see Figure S6f, 
Supporting Information) of Aβ42 fibrillation. The lack of the

The inhibition effect of the peptide@AuNP conjugates on

AuNP and CVA5@AuNP. Conversely, co-incubation with

2.3. Effect of Peptides and Peptide@AuNPs on the Secondary 
Structure of Aβ42
As the presence of AuNPs might also change the interaction 
mode of the conjugated peptides, far-UV CD spectroscopy 
was applied to gain insights into the effects of the peptides 
and peptide@AuNPs on the conformational transition of Aβ42 
upon aggregation. As a control, VCD10 and its precursors of 
LPFFD and VVIA presented very weak circular dichroism 
(CD) signals both at 0 and 24 h (Figure S9, Supporting Infor-
mation), indicating that the short peptides could hardly form 
obvious secondary structures in solution, thus will not inter-
fere in the subsequent experiments. The initial secondary 
structure of Aβ42 was random coil with a representative nega-
tive minimum of ≈197 nm (Figure 5a). After 24 h incubation 
this peak disappeared; instead, a positive peak at 195 nm and 
a negative minimum at 215 nm appeared (Figure 5b). This 
clearly indicates that Aβ42 underwent structural transition 
from initial random coil to β-sheet structure, consistent with 
previous studies.[6] The incubation of free peptides with Aβ42 
showed a similar conformational transition from random 
coils to β-sheet structures (Figure 5a,b). Only VCD10 slightly 
reduced the β-structure content of Aβ42, as evidenced by the 
shift of negative minimum from 215 to 209 nm (Figure 5b). 
So, VCD10 and its precursors of LPFFD and VVIA did not 
affect the conformational transition of Aβ42.
Differently, incubation of Aβ42 with 1 nmol L−1 pep-
tide@AuNPs showed a global reduction in overall CD sig-
nals as compared to those of Aβ42 alone at both 0 and 24 h 
(Figure 5c,d). This indicates nonspecific adsorption of Aβ42 
on all the AuNPs. Particularly, the CD signal of Aβ42 incu-
bated with bare AuNPs was greatly reduced at 0 h, sug-
gesting fast adsorption of Aβ42 on AuNPs, consistent with 
DLS results (Figure 4). Figure 5d shows the CD spectra of 
Aβ42 co-incubated with the peptides@AuNPs at 24 h. It can 
be seen that the conformational changes of Aβ42 were par-
tially suppressed by CLD6@AuNP and VCD10@AuNP, as 
indicated by the shift of negative minimums from ≈216 to 
≈208 nm (Figure 5d). Such shift corresponds to the reduction 
of β-structures and a higher proportion of random coils.[70] 
The results indicate the inhibitory effects of CLD6@AuNP 
and VCD10@AuNP on Aβ42 fibrillation.

the peptide@AuNP binds to Aβ to form more toxic com-

lower cytotoxicity following similar mechanisms, while the 
CVA5&CLD6@AuNP-induced Aβ42 species might be dif-

weakened the NP-Aβ interactions.
2.5. Exploring the Role of Conjugated Peptides on AuNPs
When peptides are conjugated on different surfaces, they 
generally undergo conformational changes that are different 
from the changes in bulk phase.[68] The altered conformations 
of conjugated peptides will definitely affect their interactions 
with Aβ species. Herein, we used high-resolution transmis-
sion electron microscopy (HRTEM) and CD to determine 
the conjugation state of the peptides on AuNPs. The HRTEM 
images (Figure S12, Supporting Information) show that the 
thickness of the conjugated peptides on AuNPs was 1.2 nm 
for CVA5@AuNP, 1.2 nm for VCD10@AuNP, and 1.7 nm for 
CLD6@AuNP. Our molecular modeling also showed that in a 
fully extended state, the length of peptides would be 1.44 nm 
for CVA5, 1.8 nm for CLD6, and 1.8 nm for VCD10 (VCD10 
presents two branches of CVA5 and CLD6 on AuNP sur-
face after conjugation, thus its extended length should be 
similar to that of CLD6). By comparison of the theoretical 
values with the HRTEM data, it is concluded that CVA5 and 
CLD6 largely adopted extended conformations, but VCD10 
did not because of the presence of a large length difference 
(0.6 nm). Moreover, given a diameter of AuNP nucleus of 
15 ± 1.2 nm (Figure S3, Supporting Information) and the 
number of the peptides conjugated on AuNPs (Table 1), the 
average separate distance between two conjugated peptides 
was estimated to be 0.90 nm for CVA5, 1.22 nm for CLD6, 
and 1.65 nm for VCD10. This indicates that CVA5 and CLD6 
were more densely packed on AuNP than VCD10, and such 
high packing density would induce steric effects between 
conjugated peptides, which in turn limited their inhibitory 
activity of Aβ42 aggregation. Different from densely packed 
CVA5 and CLD6, VCD10 had a large separate distance of 
1.65 nm between the conjugated neighboring peptides, which 
provided large space for VCD10 to adopt a tilted orienta-
tion. Such a titled orientation of VCD10 could also account 
for its shorter height of 1.2 nm on AuNPs as compared to a 
fully extended height of 1.8 nm. Instead, CVA5 and CLD6 
were likely to stand straight on AuNP surface, with a fully 
extended structure.

did not produce any CD signal. In contrast, CLD6@AuNP 
showed a β-rich structure with a broad negative peak from 
215 to 225 nm; CVA5@AuNP showed a dominant α-helix 
structure with a double minimum at 208 and 222 nm; while 
CVA5&CLD6@AuNP exhibited both α-helix and β-sheet 
structures, which seems simply a mixture of CLD6@AuNP

2.6. Mechanistic Discussion
The cell viability assays in Figure 6 have shown that the 
peptide@AuNPs exhibited favorable inhibitory activity at 
0.1–0.5 nmol L−1. From the coupled numbers per AuNP listed 
in Table 1, it is estimated that the peptide concentrations 
were in the range of 40–650 nmol L−1. Since Aβ42 concentra-
tion was 25 µmol L−1, it is obvious that the molar ratios of the 
peptides to Aβ42 were only 0.0016–0.026. This was extremely 
lower than the free peptide concentrations used to inhibit 
Aβ42 aggregation used in this work (Figure 2a), as well as 
in the literatures,[38,79] where generally stoichiometric con-
centrations of peptide inhibitors with Aβ were employed to 
achieve appreciable inhibitory effects. Thus, it is convincing 
that the conjugated peptides were extremely more effec-
tive than free peptides, particularly for VCD10@AuNP that 
was highly effective at 0.1 nmol L−1 (NPs) or 40 nmol L−1 
(peptide). Based on the findings, we proposed a mechanistic 
model to interpret the inhibitory effects of the peptide@
AuNPs on Aβ42 aggregation and cytotoxicity (Figure 8).
Aβ fibrillation is a complex self-assembly process, 
involving conformational transition, structural reorganiza-
tion, and kinetic equilibria between Aβ species of different 
sizes, structures, and fibrillation pathways.[80] As illustrated in 
Figure 8, when peptide@AuNPs were co-incubated with Aβ42 
monomers in solution, it is no doubt that they would inter-
fere with Aβ fibril formation equilibria at different aggrega-
tion stages to achieve their inhibitory activities. Irrespective 
of the mechanistic details, the possible interpretation for the 
inhibitory mode of these peptide@AuNPs is likely to be the 
combination of (i) a peptide segment that can recognize and 
interact with Aβ species to prevent β-sheet formation of Aβ, 
(ii) a biocompatible AuNP that provides solid supports to 
stabilize conjugated peptides and facilitate Aβ binding, and 
(iii) a synergetic effect formed by the conjugated peptide 
ligands that adopted favorable conformations, and the AuNP 
surface that restricted the binding orientation between the 
peptide ligands and Aβ species.
First, the conjugated peptides on AuNPs change the sur-
face charge and hydrophobicity of AuNP accordingly,[64] and 
such surface modifications are expected to affect Aβ. Liao 
et al. found that negatively charged AuNPs exerted some 
inhibitory effects on Aβ fibrillation.[56] In our cases, we found 
that hydrophobic CVA5@AuNP (−11.8 ± 2.4 mV) had much 
lower zeta potential than CLD6@AuNP (−28.1 ± 2.0 mV) 
and VCD10@AuNP (−24.5 ± 3.5 mV) (Table 1). Among 
them, however, VCD10@AuNP was the most effective Aβ42 
inhibitor, while CVA5@AuNP and CLD6@AuNP exhib-
ited similar, weaker inhibitory activity (Figures 2b and 6). 
Besides, the high zeta potential of CVA5&CLD6@AuNP 
(−32.2 ± 4.4 mV) might be caused by the accumulation of 
randomized conjugated charged peptides (CLPFFD) on 
NP surface, or the formation of NP clusters, which could be

Differently, VCD10 was coupled on AuNP surface as a 
branched hybrid sequence. The collective data indicate that 
the branched conformation of conjugated VCD10 is critical 
for achieving the superior inhibitory ability of VCD10@
AuNP. First, according to the conformational analysis as 
discussed above, both negatively charged Asp and hydro-
phobic Val were located on the outer termini of the ligand, 
so they are likely exposed on the NP/solution interface, 
while the presence of the negatively charged and hydro-
phobic groups on NP surfaces will facilitate the inhibition of 
Aβ fibrillation.[69] Second, the steric effect of VCD10 could 
be amplified by the branched conjugation, resulting in more 
loosely conjugated VCD10 with a tilted orientation (Table 1 
and Figure 7). Such a configuration allowed VCD10 to largely 
expose its two binding sequences of hydrophobic VVIA and 
negatively charged LPFFD to Aβ in solution, and allowed 
VCD10 to form more randomized structures. This exposure 
enhances Aβ-VCD10@AuNP interactions via self-comple-
mentary effect. Moreover, VCD10@AuNP inhibited Aβ oli-
gomerization at the early stages (Figure S14b, Supporting 
Information) and the fibrillation (Figure 2 and Figures S6 
and S14, Supporting Information), induced Aβ to form amor-
phous species with altered secondary structures (Figures 3 
and 5), and retained its monodispersity when co-incubating 
with Aβ (Figure 4). These findings suggest that VCD10@
AuNP not only interfered with Aβ oligomerization at early 
stages, where the structure transition of Aβ monomers or oli-
gomers was affected, but also eventually prevented Aβ from 
aggregating into large fibrils. Therefore, the combination of 
LPFFD and VVIA into VCD10@AuNPs indeed enhanced 
the synergetic interactions between VCD10@AuNP and 
small Aβ species through multiple interactions, including 
hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions and hydrogen 
bonding. The enhanced Aβ-VCD10@AuNP interactions 
would alter the structure of Aβ species, Aβ aggregation equi-
librium and pathways, thus inhibiting Aβ aggregation and 
cytotoxicity. Moreover, considering that small, soluble Aβ oli-
gomers are the primary toxic species generated at the early 
stage of Aβ fibrillation,[9] strong interactions of VCD10@
AuNP with Aβ monomers and oligomers to form VCD10@
AuNP/Aβ complexes would also convert toxic Aβ42 species 
into nontoxic or less toxic VCD10@AuNP/Aβ complexes 
(Figure 6). Both effects interpret the fact that VCD10@AuNP 
protected SH-SY5Y cells from Aβ-induced toxicity.

3. Conclusions
In this work, different peptide fragments (CVVIA, CLPFFD, 
VVIACLPFFD, etc.) derived from Aβ were conjugated onto 
AuNP surface in different ways to form different peptide@
AuNPs, which were then used as novel nanosized inhibi-
tors against Aβ42 aggregation and toxicity. Upon synthesis, 
all the peptide@AuNPs showed less or nontoxicity to cells. 
Different from the weak inhibitory activity of free peptides, 
all the peptide@AuNPs inhibited the aggregation of Aβ42 
and reduced the Aβ-induced cytotoxicity significantly at 
the molar ratios of peptides to Aβ42 as low as 0.0016–0.026. 
Particularly, among the peptide@AuNPs, VCD10@AuNPs 
(VVIACLPFFD), combining VVIA and LPFFD sequences 
into a single one, was demonstrated as the most effective 
inhibitor that increased cell viability from 48% to 82% at 
0.1 nmol L−1 (NPs) or 40 nmol L−1 (peptide). Such a superior 
inhibitory capacity of VCD10@AuNPs was likely attributed 
to its branched hybrid sequence from VVIA and LPFFD, 
tiled conjugated orientation, and special conformation on 
AuNP surface. These structural characters would promote its 
synergetic interactions with Aβ, thus leading to strong inhibi-
tions on Aβ oligomerization and fibrillation. The findings sug-
gest that hybrid of multiple peptide inhibitors coupled onto 
nanoparticles would be a promising strategy for the design of 
effective amyloid inhibitors.
4. Experimental Section
Materials: Gold (III) chloride trihydrate (HAuCl4·3H2O), sodium 
citrate, dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO), hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP), 
and ThT were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Aβ42, 
Aβ40, and peptide ligands LPFFD (LD5), VVIA (VA4), CLPFFD (CLD6), 
VVIAC (VAC5), CVVIA (CVA5), LPFFDCVVIA (LCA10), and VVIA-
CLPFFD (VCD10) (>95% purity) were purchased from GL Biochem 
(Shanghai, China). Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) 
and fetal bovine serum were obtained from Gibco Invitrogen 
(Grand Island, NY, USA). All other chemicals were the highest 
purity available from local sources.
Preparation of AuNPs: Citrate-reduced gold nanoparticle (bare 
AuNP) was synthesized by the modified Frens method.[84] An 
aqueous solution (100 mL) of HAuCl4 (1 mmol L−1) was refluxed 
and stirred for 10 min, and a hot aqueous solution (10 mL) of 
sodium citrate (38.8 mmol L−1) was added to the vortex of the stir-
ring solution. The reflux and stirring was continued for a further 
30 min and the color of the solution changed from yellow to wine-
red. After the solution was cooled, it was filtered through 0.45 µm 
Millipore filters to remove any precipitates. The resulting bare AuNP 
solution was stored at 4 °C till further use. The monodispersity and 
size of the bare AuNP were observed by transmission electronic 
microscopy (Tecnai G2 F20 TEM system, FEI Inc., Netherlands).
Conjugation of Peptide Inhibitors onto AuNPs: Bare AuNP was 
functionalized with six peptides: the sequence CLPFFD (CLD6@
AuNP), LPFFDC (LDC6@AuNP), CVVIA (CVA5@AuNP), VVIAC 
(VAC5@AuNP), VVIACLPFFD (VCD10@AuNP), and LPFFDCVVIA 
(LCA10@AuNP), an equimolar mixture of CLPFFD and CVVIA 
(CVA5&CLD6@AuNP) was also employed. Peptide-functionalized 
AuNPs were prepared by adding aqueous solution of respective 
peptides (1.28 mmol L−1) into bare AuNP (5 nmol L−1) at a volume 
ratio of 1–10, and the solution was stirred for 2 h at room tem-

the number of peptides conjugated on AuNPs. Functionalized 
AuNPs were filtrated by 0.45 µmol L−1 filter to remove precipitates 
and then dialyzed (membrane molecular weight cut off = 10 000) 
against 1.2 mmol L−1 sodium citrate for 3 d to remove residual 
peptides. The resulting purified AuNP solutions were stored at 4 °C 
till further use.
Characterization of Peptide-Functionalized AuNPs: The struc-
tural properties (e.g., morphology, size, and zeta potential) of 
all synthesized AuNPs were determined by HRTEM (Tecnai G2 
F20 TEM system, FEI Inc., Netherlands) and Malvern Nanosizer 
2000 (Malvern Ins., UK). In HRTEM study, the purified AuNP solu-
tions were lyophilized, resuspended in ethanol and sonicated for 
10 min, and then the solutions were dropped onto carbon film. 
The AuNP samples were successively photographed using STEM 
(scaning transmission electron microscopy) and HRTEM modes 
with a field-emission gun operating at 200 kV. In zeta poten-
tial study, AuNPs were diluted to appropriate concentrations, 
transferred into a zeta potential cup, and measured at 25 °C. In 
UV–vis study, the absorption spectra of purified AuNP solutions 
were obtained via UV–vis spectroscopy measurements (Lambda 
35, PerkinElmer, USA). The diameter of synthesized AuNPs was 
calculated using Gatan DigitalMicrograph software. The molar 
concentrations of all synthesized AuNPs were estimated using the 
absorbance of the surface plasmon resonance peak and the molar 
extinction coefficient of the 15 nm diameter AuNPs.[85]
Estimation of the Number of Peptide Ligands Conjugated on 
Each AuNP: First, to confirm the absence of residual free peptides, 
the purified AuNP solutions were centrifuged at 16 000×g for 
30 min and the NP precipitated. The supernatants were checked 
by 1H NMR (500 MHz Bruker, USA) after the addition of 10% D2O 
(v/v). Free peptides were not detected (data not shown).
In order to check the conjugation of peptide ligands, NMR 
and EELS (electron energy loss spectra) analyses were performed. 
In the NMR analysis, the purified AuNP solutions were concen-
trated by ultrafiltration, then 10% D2O (v/v) was added to obtain 
the sample. The sample was then checked by 1H NMR (500 MHz 
Bruker, USA). In EELS analysis, the purified AuNP solutions were 
lyophilized, the resulting brown nanopowders were resuspended 
in ethanol and sonicated for 10 min, and then the solutions were 
dropped onto carbon films. The EELS spectra were obtained by 
Tecnai G2 F20 TEM system and processed using the Gatan Digital-
Micrograph software.

by TGA (thermo gravimetric analyzer, 7 HT thermobalance, Perki-
nElmer, USA). Before analyses, the purified AuNP solutions were

was vacuum dried at 120 °C for 2 h to remove residual and physical 
adsorbed water. Then the samples were ramped from room temper-
ature to 900 °C at 5 °C min−1 under nitrogen purging at 20 mL min−1.

Preparation of Aβ Monomer: Aβ42 and Aβ40 monomer were pre-
pared as reported in the literature.[67] First, Aβ proteins were dis-
solved in HFIP solutions to a final concentration of 1.0 mg mL−1. 
The solution was in quiescence at least 2 h, and then sonicated 
for 30 min to destroy the pre-existing Aβ aggregates. Thereafter, 
the solution was centrifuged at 15 000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to 
remove the existing Aβ aggregates. About top 75% of supernatant 
was collected and the solvent was removed by vacuum freeze-
drying overnight. Before use, Aβ was dissolved in 10.0 mmol L−1 
NaOH, centrifuged at 16 000×g at 4 °C for 20 min to remove the 
aggregates, then about top 75% of the supernatant was collected 
to produce Aβ monomers. Finally, the Aβ monomers were diluted 
with 100.0 mmol L−1 phosphate buffer solution (PBS) (10 mmol L−1 
NaCl, pH 7.4) to a final concentration of 25 µmol L−1.
ThT Fluorescent Assay: The inhibition effect of inhibitors was 
investigated by the intensities of ThT florescence. Aβ42 or Aβ40 
were incubated in 100.0 mmol L−1 PBS (10 mmol L−1 NaCl, pH 
7.4) in the presence of 25 µmol L−1 ThT at 37 °C, with various con-
centrations of inhibitors. In the seeded Aβ42 aggregation experi-
ments, Aβ42 seed (mature fibrils) obtained by incubation for 48 h 
was added into the incubation solutions at a molar ratio of 5%. 
The incubation solutions were divided into aliquots in a 96-well 
microplate. The final volume in each well was 200 µL. ThT fluores-
cence intensities were measured by a plate reader (Infinite M200, 
Tecan, Austria) at 25 °C. Periodic shaking at 300 rpm was executed 
every 10 min. The quenching effect of AuNPs on ThT fluorescence 
was also checked. In the experiment, Aβ42 were incubated at 
above conditions for 24 h, and then various AuNPs were added 
into the Aβ42 solutions. The ThT fluorescence was recorded after 
Aβ42 was mixed well with the particles. ThT emission was moni-
tored at 480 nm with the excitation at 440 nm. The slit width was 
20 nm. The fluorescence intensity of the contrast without Aβ42 was 
subtracted as background from each read with Aβ42. At least three 
independent measurements were performed and the data were 
presented as mean ± SD. Analysis of variance was carried out for 
statistical comparisons using t-test, and p < 0.05 or less was con-
sidered to be statistically significant.
Transmission Electron Microscopy: The morphology of Aβ42 
aggregates with or without inhibitors was studied using TEM. The 
incubation solutions were diluted to appropriate concentrations, 
dropped onto Formvar carbon-coated copper grids (400 mesh), 
and air-dried for 2 min. Then the samples were stained with 2% 
phosphotungstic acid for 5 min, exceeding solutions were removed 
by filter paper. Finally, the stained samples were examined and 
photographed using a JEM-100CXII TEM system (JEOL Inc., Tokyo, 
Japan) with an accelerating voltage of 100 kV.
CD Spectroscopy: CD spectra of free peptides, peptides@
AuNPs, and Aβ42 co-incubated with the inhibitors were measured 
using a J-810 spectrometer (Jasco, Japan) at 25 °C. A quartz cell with 
1 mm path length was used for far-UV (190-260 nm) measurements 
with 1 nm bandwidth at a scan speed of 100 nm min−1. The CD 
spectra of solutions without AuNPs/Aβ42 were subtracted as back-
ground. All spectra were the average of three consecutive scans 
for each sample. The proportion of secondary structures was cal-
culated and compared using an online analysis (http://dichroweb.
cryst.bbk.ac.uk/html/home.shtml).[70]
Dynamic Light Scattering: The size and the size distribution of 
Aβ42 aggregates and AuNPs were measured using a Malvern Nano-
sizer 2000 (Malvern Ins., UK). The incubation solutions of Aβ42 
with and without AuNPs were diluted to the appropriate concentra-
tions, transferred into a clear disposable zeta cell, and measured 
at 25 °C. Three measurements of each sample were employed at a 
single backscatter angle of 173°.
Cell Viability Assay: To examine the cytotoxicity of synthesized 
AuNPs and their effects on Aβ-induced cytotoxicity, cell viability 
assays were performed using MTT assays and LDH release assays.
The MTT assay is a colorimetric assay for assessing cell via-
bility. Human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells were obtained from the 
China Cell Bank (Shanghai, China) and maintained in high glucose 
DMEM supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum, 2 mmol L−1 
glutamine, 100 U mL−1 penicillin, and 100 U mL−1 streptomycin 
at 37 °C under an atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2. Cells were 
plated at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates with 
90 µL fresh medium. After incubated for 24 h, aged Aβ42 aggre-
gates with different concentrations of inhibitors (10 µL each 
well) were added into the plates and the cells were incubated for 
another 48 h. At select time point, the incubation medium was 
removed, the cells were washed with PBS at pH 7.4, and then the 
culture medium was replaced with FBS-free (fetal bovine serum 
free) medium. After that, 10 µL MTT solutions (6.0 mg L−1) were 
added into each well and incubated for another 4 h. The culture 
medium was removed by centrifugation and the precipitated cells 
were lysed using DMSO. After the purple crystals were dissolved 
completely, the cell viability was calculated from the absorbance 
signals measured by a plate reader (Infinite M200, Tecan, Austria) 
at a 570 nm wavelength. The wells containing medium only were 
subtracted as the background from each reading. The cell viability 
data were normalized as a percentage of the control group without 
Aβ42 and inhibitors.
Cell death was quantitatively assessed by measuring the 
release of LDH. Briefly, SH-SY5Y cells were plated at a density of 
5 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates with 90 µL fresh medium. 
After incubated for 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with 
FBS-free medium, and then aged Aβ42 aggregates with different 
concentrations of inhibitors were added into SH-SY5Y cells. After 
treatment with Aβ42 species for 48 h, the LDH leakage assay 
was performed. Prior to assay, the cells were incubated with 1% 
(V/V) Triton X-100 in FBS-free medium at 37 °C for 1 h to obtain 
a representative maximal LDH release as the positive control with 
100% toxicity. Extracellular LDH leakage was evaluated by using 
the assay kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s 
instructions. Briefly, cells in 96-well plates were centrifuged at 
400×g for 5 min, 80 µL culture supernatants were collected from 
each well, and 40 µL reaction buffer supplied in the kit was then 
added. The leakage of LDH was assessed at a test wavelength 
of 490 nm with 630 nm as a reference wavelength after mixing 
for 30 min at room temperature; the absorbance of AuNPs was 
subtracted.
